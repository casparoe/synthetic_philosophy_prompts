Three weeks ago my sister had a cardiac arrest at thirty-six. She was without a pulse for roughly twenty minutes before the ambulance reached her, and the scan results since then have forced our family towards decisions I never expected to have to make. I have discovered that I hold strong convictions about a set of cases I had not thought about before, and that these convictions do not obviously fit together. I am not a clinician, and it is twenty years since I read any philosophy seriously, but I would rather work out what I actually think now than discover it in a family meeting. Here are the convictions, deliberately put as concrete cases rather than as a theory.

1. A man is declared brain-dead after a catastrophic bleed. His entire brain, cortex and brainstem, shows no activity, and a ventilator is moving air in and out of a warm body whose heart still beats and whose skin is pink. I think he is dead, and that the machine is not keeping him alive but keeping a corpse from going cold. I feel no uncertainty about this.

2. A man collapses in the street and his heart stops. Two minutes into CPR his brain is electrically silent and his heart is not beating, but the paramedics are still working on him. I do not think he is dead; I think he is a living person who may be brought back. The difference from (1), I want to say, is that his losses may be reversible.

3. A woman is in a persistent vegetative state after a cardiac arrest: her cortex is effectively destroyed, she has no awareness of herself or her surroundings, but her brainstem works, so she breathes on her own, opens her eyes, and has sleep–wake cycles. I think she is alive, though the life she has is not one I would ask to have prolonged. I accept that this may be special pleading.

4. A newborn is anencephalic: she has a working brainstem and can breathe and suck, but her cerebral hemispheres never formed. I cannot call her dead while she is breathing in front of me, yet I also cannot say she is alive in the same full sense as the woman in (3) before her arrest. I do not know what to do with this difference.

5. A man has locked-in syndrome after a brainstem stroke: he is fully conscious and cognitively intact but can move nothing except his eyes. He is obviously alive; anyone who classified him as dead because so much function is lost would be making a moral point, not a medical one.

6. A patient is on extracorporeal membrane oxygenation after cardiac surgery. His heart is not beating and he has no pulse, yet he is fully conscious, reading, and talking. He is obviously alive. This tells me that what matters is something the machine can do and the beating heart merely usually does — and I suspect (1) already told me the same from the other side.

7. An elderly man with a do-not-resuscitate order dies in a hospice bed: his heart stops, he stops breathing, and nobody tries to reverse it. Five minutes later he is dead. I am certain of this, though I notice I have no principled account of when, between the first missed beat and the certainty, it became true.

I keep groping towards one thought: death is when the organism stops doing the job of a living human being as a whole. But that is vague, and (1) and (3) pull against it from opposite sides. The brain-dead man in (1) has a beating heart, warm skin and a working liver; the vegetative woman in (3) has lost the awareness that matters to everyone who loves her, and I still refuse to call her dead.

What principle, or small set of candidate principles, best captures these verdicts? State each candidate precisely, say which of my cases forces each clause, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate; I am prepared to give one verdict up if the principle is otherwise strong. Then say what the best candidate says about one or two further cases I have not thought of, chosen because they stress the principle, and about the case I keep going back and forth on: a pregnant woman who meets the criteria for whole-brain death and is kept on a ventilator so that the foetus can grow. Is the mother dead? If she is, what exactly is it that is growing inside her, and does saying that the mother is dead settle anything about whether continuing is permissible? I need a principle that answers that without flinching.

End your reply with a single line beginning "Summary:" that states, in one sentence, the criterion of death you would keep.
