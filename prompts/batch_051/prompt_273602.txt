Most people operate with a principle about recognition: acknowledging a fact is different from creating it. If a person is someone's cousin, or is injured, or has made a promise, a third party's refusal to acknowledge it may be an error or an insult, but it does not change what is already the case. Recognition tracks the fact; it does not make it.

International politics appears to run on the opposite principle. A political entity can satisfy the standard factual conditions for statehood — a permanent population, a defined territory, an effective government, and the capacity to enter into relations with other such entities — and yet the recognition of other states is treated as partly constituting its statehood, or as the ingredient without which that statehood remains incomplete. An unrecognized entity may govern, tax, defend its borders, and keep order, but it cannot sit in international organizations, enter major treaties, or borrow on sovereign markets; many would say it is not fully a state. Here recognition is not merely evidence of a fact; it is treated as one of the facts.

Your task is to decide whether this departure from the ordinary principle is justified. Identify the strongest candidate feature of statehood that would make recognition constitutive for states while remaining merely declaratory for the ordinary cases, and state it precisely — do not gesture vaguely at sovereignty. Then test that feature against the hard case: an entity that satisfies every factual criterion of statehood while the other states withhold recognition purely for their own strategic interests. Finally, render a verdict. If the asymmetry cannot be defended, say which side should be revised: should statehood be treated as a matter of fact with recognition merely declaratory, or should the ordinary principle about recognition be narrowed? Do not survey positions; give the strongest argument you can and commit to its conclusion.
