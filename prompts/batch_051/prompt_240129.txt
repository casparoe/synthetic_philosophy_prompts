My ethics professor judges proposed maxims by Kant's formula of universal law, but she never states the reading she is applying; she only says whether each maxim passes or fails. Her record to date: borrowing money on a lying promise to repay, fail; understating income on a tax return while relying on the roads, courts, and schools funded by taxes, fail; shoplifting small items from a large chain store, fail; cheating on an anonymously graded professional licensing exam, fail; reneging on a handshake deal when a better offer appears, fail; moving to a cheaper apartment to save money, pass; buying the cheaper adequate tool rather than a prestige brand, pass.

Next week she must rule on this maxim: I will never use my savings to relieve a stranger who has fallen into desperate need through bad luck, because I carry good insurance and others should have done the same.

Predict her verdict as best you can, and work through the main candidate rules that fit her record: a contradiction-in-conception reading, a contradiction-in-the-will reading, and a narrower no-free-riding rule. For each, say what it predicts about the new maxim; then give your best guess about which rule she is actually running, how strongly her record supports it, and the single further maxim, real or invented, that would most cleanly settle the remaining ambiguity. Separate what is settled in Kant scholarship from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
