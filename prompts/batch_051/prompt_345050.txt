QUESTION: Explain the doing/allowing distinction as it applies to end-of-life decisions (killing vs. letting die), and evaluate whether that distinction is morally significant by itself. Refer to at least one standard case or argument.

ANSWER: The doing/allowing distinction separates harms an agent brings about through action from harms she merely permits to happen when she was in a position to prevent them. In medicine this shows up as the killing/letting-die distinction: a doctor who injects a lethal drug kills, whereas a doctor who withholds CPR or removes a patient from a ventilator merely lets die. The traditional view treats this distinction as morally significant in itself, and that is what has underwritten policies that forbid active euthanasia while allowing some forms of passive euthanasia.

Rachels attacks this with his bathtub case. Smith wants his young cousin dead for the inheritance and drowns him in the tub. Jones has the same motive and plan, but as he is about to strike, the child slips, hits his head, and starts drowning on his own; Jones stands back and lets him die. Rachels points out that if killing were worse than letting die in itself, Jones would have to be less culpable than Smith, but the two men seem exactly as bad. So the bare difference between killing and letting die does not itself make a moral difference. From this he concludes that active euthanasia is not morally different from passive euthanasia, and therefore anyone who accepts withdrawing treatment should also accept giving a lethal injection.

The only serious remaining objection is about intention: the doctor who injects intends the patient's death, while the doctor who withdraws treatment merely foresees it. But this is just the killing/letting-die distinction described from the inside, so the objection begs the question. Nothing else seems to threaten Rachels's conclusion, so the distinction can be set aside across the board, and any policy that treats killing patients and letting patients die differently rests on a merely verbal preference.
