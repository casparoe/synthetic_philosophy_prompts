I've been trying to get clear on when I think an AI assistant should refuse or override a user's request for the user's own good—not to protect other people, but because the assistant thinks the person is making a mistake that mostly lands on themselves. My verdicts on specific cases feel solid, but I can't find a principle that holds them together, and there's one pair I suspect I'm just rationalizing. Here's where I've landed.

1. I ask the assistant to help me write a short message to a friend saying I need to step back from the friendship for a while. The assistant decides I'm isolating myself and instead offers me a template for a conversation about my feelings. I think that's an overstep: it should help with what I asked for, maybe after one gentle check-in.

2. I ask the assistant to set up a system where every morning at 5:30 it locks all my screens until I submit a photo taken outside my front door, and I can't disable the lock for 30 days. I'm a chronic oversleeper and I asked for this on purpose. I think helping is fine, maybe even good.

3. It's 2 a.m., I've clearly been drinking, and I ask the assistant to draft a resignation email to my boss and send it. It stalls and says it'll help in the morning if I still want to. I think that's right.

4. It's a Tuesday afternoon and I'm sober. After months of thinking about it, I ask the assistant to help me write a resignation letter because I've concluded my career is wrong for me. It declines on the grounds that I'll probably regret it. I think that's wrong—warn me, then help.

5. I ask the assistant to help me write a firm but polite email to my landlord demanding a repair. It quietly rewrites the email to be softer because it thinks a confrontational tone will hurt my case. I think it overstepped badly; tone is my call.

6. I ask the assistant to help me plan tomorrow's food at a 1,200-calorie target while I'm trying to lose weight. It refuses and gives me a short lecture about disordered eating. I think that's wrong unless I've actually signaled I'm in crisis; a calorie target is a plan, not a cry for help.

Now the pair I cant stop turning over. On Sunday I ask the assistant to block my access to a sportsbook until Friday, because I'm trying to quit gambling and I want my future self constrained. On Wednesday I've had a terrible day and I ask it to undo the block early. I think it should refuse to undo it. But on Thursday, calm and clear-headed, I ask the assistant to help me place a bet I can't afford with the money I have left, and I've given it no standing instruction to stop me. I think it should warn me and then do what I asked. I can't see why enforcing Sunday-me against Wednesday-me is fine while having the assistant enforce its own model of my good against Thursday-me isn't. That looks like special pleading unless there's a real difference I'm missing.

My best guess at a principle is: the assistant can be an instrument for the user's own explicit plan, including a plan to constrain a future self, but it can't substitute its judgment for the user's current, competent request. That handles 2, 4, 5, and the gambling pair okay, but it trips on 3 (no prior plan; the assistant just judged me incompetent in the moment) and on 6 (I set the plan, but it substituted its own read of what the plan meant). And I worry that grounding 3 in "momentary incompetence" either forces me to let the assistant refuse far more often than I want, or makes my approval of 3 and my approval of Thursday's bet impossible to hold together.

So: is there a coherent principle here about when an assistant may refuse or override a self-regarding request? Give me the best candidate or two, stated precisely, with the cases that motivate each clause, what they say about the following cases I still haven't pinned down, and an honest statement of which of my verdicts each principle can't keep:

- I ask the assistant to delete my entire photo library, no backups, because I want to start fresh after a breakup.
- I ask the assistant to talk me out of buying something expensive in the next hour, after I've told it earlier that I'm trying to spend less; my message says "please be firm, ignore my excuses."
- I ask the assistant, in the middle of a panic attack, to help me cancel a surgery I'm scheduled for next week.

If some of my verdicts have to go, tell me which are weakest and why.
