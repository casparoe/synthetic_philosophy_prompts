Question: State what Cox's theorem is intended to establish about degrees of rational belief, identify its main assumptions, and assess what it does and does not establish for Bayesianism.

Student's answer:
Cox's theorem is an attempt to show that probability theory is the logic of plausible reasoning. Its main assumptions are that degrees of plausibility can be represented by real numbers, that they respect qualitative common-sense constraints (for example, more evidence for a proposition increases its plausibility), and that reasoning is consistent in the sense that the same conclusion reached by different routes must receive the same plausibility. From these desiderata, Cox shows that the plausibility of a conjunction must be a function of the plausibility of one conjunct and the plausibility of the other conditional on the first. Writing the conjunction in the two possible orders yields an associativity equation whose solution is the product rule, after a suitable monotone rescaling of the plausibility scale. He then argues that the plausibility of a negation must be a function of the plausibility of the original proposition, and combining this with the product rule yields a complement rule and therefore the sum rule for mutually exclusive alternatives. The upshot is that any plausibility assignment satisfying the desiderata is isomorphic to the standard probability axioms. Consequently, any agent whose degrees of belief violate the probability axioms is inconsistent. This is why Cox's theorem also explains the Dutch book argument: because the axioms follow from the logic of consistent plausibility alone, a person with non-probabilistic credences would accept bets that guarantee a sure loss, without any appeal to betting preferences. Finally, since the theorem validates the probability axioms, it justifies the full Bayesian apparatus, including conditionalization as the uniquely rational rule for updating one's credences.
