I have spent this term working through the demandingness objection to consequentialism, and I have become dissatisfied with the way I slide between my theoretical sympathies and my considered verdicts. I want to stop advocating a position and instead see whether my verdicts, taken together, add up to any position worth advocating. Let me set them out as plainly as I can. In each case, take the stated facts as given, however artificial.

The pond. On my way to give a lecture I pass a shallow ornamental pond in which a small child has fallen and is drowning, with no one else around. Wading in would ruin an expensive suit and make me miss the lecture. I regard it as morally required that I wade in; a colleague who refused would have done something seriously wrong, not merely something ungenerous.

The further donation. I already make a fixed monthly gift to an evidence-backed anti-malaria charity. The charity asks for a one-time additional donation of six hundred dollars, roughly the price of the suit in the first case, which would in expectation avert a child's death. I do not feel that making this further donation is required. I would admire someone who gave, but when I decline I do not feel I have failed in a duty. This troubles me, because I cannot state what separates the two cases.

My sister. My sister needs surgery that will prevent an otherwise permanent and severe disability. It costs fifteen thousand dollars, which I would have to draw from my retirement savings. I think I am required to pay it, and I would judge myself harshly if I kept the money.

The sabbatical. I have saved for years for a year abroad to learn a language I need for a book. If I gave the money instead to the same anti-malaria charity, it would in expectation save several lives. I do not think I am required to cancel the year, and I would not blame a colleague for going.

The kidney. A transplant coordinator approaches me about non-directed donation: a stranger will very likely die within the year without a kidney, and I am a suitable donor. Donating carries real surgical risk and a recovery period. I do not think I am required to donate; I regard non-directed donors as heroic rather than as simply doing what duty requires.

The burning car. I come upon a crashed car that has caught fire, with the driver conscious and trapped. I could likely pull him free, but with a substantial chance of severe burns or permanent impairment. I do not think I am required to enter the fire, and I would not condemn someone who called emergency services and stayed back.

The coffee. Each day I buy a coffee that costs about four dollars more than making coffee at home, and I could give the difference to the anti-malaria charity. I do not think each such purchase is wrong, and I do not think I am bound to forgo every small pleasure whose redirected cost would, in the aggregate over time, contribute to saving lives. Yet this confidence sits uneasily beside my certainty in the pond case.

The student. A talented student asks whether she is morally obligated to take a high-paying consulting job she would dislike, so that she could give away a third of her income to effective charities, rather than take the modest nonprofit job she wants. I tell her I do not think she is obligated to take the consulting job. But I cannot give her a principled explanation of why she may decline to earn resources that would, if earned and given, do vastly more good.

That is the full set of considered reactions. Colleagues offer me distinctions intended to reconcile them: the beneficiary is in front of me or far away, identified or statistical, the harm is certain or probabilistic, I am the only possible helper or one among many, the cost is money or bodily risk. None of these distinctions, as I have tried to state it, carves the cases at the right joints. The closest I can come to a principle is roughly this: beneficence becomes strict duty only when my act is the last practical means of preventing a serious harm to a particular person, at a cost that does not undermine my own central projects. That shape of view would make the pond and my sister obligatory while leaving the kidney and the burning car supererogatory. But it seems to leave the further donation optional only because the beneficiary is anonymous, and I do not know whether that is a moral reason or a confession of bias.

Two further cases remain unsettled for me, and I would like your principle to decide them. First, the named child: the charity offers me a one-time sponsorship in which I receive the child's name, photograph, and diagnosis; my six hundred dollars funds the whole treatment; and if I decline, the child is not treated. Is this required in the way the pond is, or optional in the way the further donation is? Second, my daughter: she could undergo an experimental treatment costing eighty thousand dollars that has a one-in-four chance of restoring her mobility, while the same money would with near certainty save several children's lives through the anti-malaria charity. May I spend the money on my daughter's slim chance, and does your principle say anything general about partiality?

What I am asking you for is this. State the best principle, or a small set of rival principles, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible; make it precise enough to apply to new cases, and say which clause is motivated by which case. Then apply it to the two unsettled cases. Finally, be explicit about which of my stated verdicts it cannot accommodate, and whether you would advise me to overturn those verdicts rather than complicate the principle. If you think a verdict should go, tell me what was likely distorting my reaction.
