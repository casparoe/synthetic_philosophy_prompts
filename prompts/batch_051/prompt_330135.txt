I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so bear with me if I mangle the jargon. The book I'm drafting has a neuroscientist character who has talked herself into epiphenomenalism about consciousness: experiences are real, they're caused by the brain, but they never cause anything — not even her own thought that she's conscious. The whole novel turns on the puzzle this creates for her: her conviction, and every sentence she says about her own experience, is itself a physical event with physical causes, so if the feeling itself is causally inert, her judgment can't have been caused by the very experience it's about. And then why would she be entitled to believe it? I want that scene to be sharp, not dorm-room skepticism.

So here's what I need from you: the whiteboard specification. What does a theory of phenomenal judgment — the process by which a creature judges, reports, and maybe knows about its own experiences — have to get right before we let it call itself adequate? Adequate here means good enough to settle whether a thoughtful epiphenomenalist can coherently trust her own conviction, and good enough not to make a hash of ordinary first-person talk.

Give me the requirements list. For each requirement, say what phenomenon, case, or distinction forces it, and mark it non-negotiable or just a preference of one camp. In particular, make sure the list keeps these apart, or says what happens if you refuse to:
- the experience itself, versus the judgment or report about the experience — I gather people collapse these and pretend they've solved the problem;
- the cause of a judgment, versus its content;
- a judgment being true, versus its being justified or known.

Also bring in the real dissociation cases, where the report and the experience come apart — blindsight, anosognosia, whatever else is solid — because those seem to kill the tidy idea that a report of an experience is just caused by the experience. Keep it to things a well-read non-specialist could check; don't invent studies.

Then the part I actually want to see: tell me which single requirement does the most work to exclude existing contenders. And is there a subset of these requirements thats jointly unsatisfiable, so the list itself is the result? The obvious tension looks like it's between (a) physical causal closure of the brain, (b) the idea that a zombie duplicate with no experiences would make word-for-word the same judgments, and (c) the claim that our actual self-reports amount to knowledge in any ordinary sense. But dont just gesture at it — lay out whether that's really a contradiction, or whether giving up one of the three costs more than the other two. Skip the scorecard of existing theories; the criteria and the cases that force them are what the character would actually use in the scene, and that's the part I'm worried about getting wrong.
