I teach a course in deontological ethics, and in our unit on the doctrine of double effect (DDE) we have been generating trolley variants to test how much of the moral work is really done by the distinction between harm one intends and harm one merely foresees. Our baseline cases are these. In the ordinary switch case, the agent redirects the trolley onto a side track where one person will be killed; standard DDE reasoning counts this as permissible because the one person's death is a foreseen side effect of diverting the trolley, not the route by which the five are saved. In the footbridge case, the agent pushes a heavy person onto the track so that his impact stops the trolley; standard DDE reasoning counts this as impermissible because the person's death is the very means of stopping the trolley. And the loop case, in which the agent only flips a switch but the trolley loops back toward the five unless it is stopped by striking the one person on the loop, is classed with the footbridge: the collision with the one is causally what saves the five, so the death is intended as a means.

In last week's seminar, a student offered a variant I cannot classify. At the switching station there are two conflicting maintenance records: an older diagram showing the side line as a short dead-end spur with a sandbank just beyond the point where a lone track worker is standing, and a newer diagram showing the sandbank removed and the spur reconnected as a loop onto the main line. The signalman cannot inspect the rails in time and regards each diagram as equally likely. On the dead-end layout, the sandbank alone would stop the trolley after it has struck the worker, so the worker's death would be a side effect, as in the switch case. On the loop layout, the worker's body—he is a large man in heavy gear—would be the only thing that derails the trolley before it re-enters the main line, so his death would be the means, as in the loop case. Throwing the switch saves the five and kills the one under either layout. The student's challenge was this: does DDE yield a determinate verdict? If permissibility is fixed by what the agent intends, and intention is constrained by belief, then what does a signalman who is fifty-fifty between the two diagrams intend when he throws the switch? If permissibility is instead fixed by the actual rails, then a signalman using the outdated diagram and one using the accurate diagram occupy causally identical situations, yet the theory would seem to judge the same physical act as permissible for one and impermissible for the other. Work through how DDE should handle the case, what each resolution commits the theory to concerning intention, belief, and actual causal structure, and whether the case leaves the intended/foreseen distinction intact or shows it doing less work than DDE needs it to do.
