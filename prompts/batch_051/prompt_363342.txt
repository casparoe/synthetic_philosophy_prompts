For the written exercise on universalizability, I want you to think through a mechanism rather than rehearse the standard objections to the generalization test. Consider the following proposal, which is meant to operationalize the family of tests associated with Kant's Formula of Universal Law, Hare's universal prescriptivism, and the generalization argument.

A residential college replaces its ad hoc decisions about exceptions to house rules with a Generalization Code. When a resident wants to do something that a standing rule forbids — say, to exceed a shared-resource quota or to claim a room outside the normal schedule — they must file a petition. The petition states the act as a maxim: anyone in circumstances C may do X. The description C must contain no proper names, no dates, and no predicate whose only function is to pick out the petitioner. The maxim is posted for forty-eight hours, during which any ten residents may force a hearing.

At the hearing, a panel of nine randomly selected residents votes on two questions, in order.

The first is a question of conception: would the universal practice of X in C make impossible the practice or institution that the standing rule protects? If everyone claimed the room whenever they judged their need urgent, would the scheduling system — or the very notion of a claim under it — collapse? If the panel answers yes, the petition is denied, and no second vote is taken.

If the petition survives, the panel proceeds to the question of willing. Each panelist is asked whether they can will that everyone in C do X. Voting is by secret ballot. Before voting, each panelist is assigned, in imagination, the position of the person worst off in the world in which the maxim is universally acted on. A no ballot defeats the petition only if the dissenter submits a written ground, and only if the panel judges that the ground is not special pleading on the dissenter's own behalf.

If both votes pass, the exception is granted, and it is automatically recorded as a precedent: any resident later in circumstances materially identical to C may do X without a hearing.

The Code is meant to hold the description of the maxim, the judge's impartiality, and the treatment of like cases fixed, rather than leaving those things to good faith. Walk through how it would actually operate once residents have had a year to adapt to it. What will petitioners learn to do with C? How will the order of the two questions be exploited? What effect will the precedent clause have on how panelists vote and on how broadly petitioners draft? Where do the familiar failure points of universalization — description-relativity, thresholds, free-riding, coordination — re-enter inside the Code's machinery? And does the Code turn out to select for universalizable maxims in the intended sense, or for something closer to drafting skill and political maneuvering? Conclude by saying which of the failure modes you identify could be fixed by amending the Code, and which cannot be fixed from within it.
