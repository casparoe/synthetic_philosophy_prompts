Here's an asymmetry that bugs me. If a man gets violent because of a brain tumor or a seizure, we don't blame him — we treat him, maybe restrain him, but we don't resent him or say he deserves it. If a man robs a store and we learn he grew up in abuse and poverty with a brain that never got a chance, we still send him to prison and talk about what he deserves. Somewhere along the causal story, the excuses run out. Is there a principled place to draw that line, or is the real difference just that a tumor shows up on a scan and a bad upbringing doesn't? Caruso thinks the line is drawn in the sand: nobody has the kind of control that would make just deserts make sense, so punishment should be re-described as quarantine — containment and prevention, no retribution. Dennett thinks there's a genuine difference between a competent agent and a broken mechanism, and that abandoning blame would cost us something real. Give me the strongest Dennett-style principle that keeps some version of the asymmetry, test it against Caruso's slide toward treating everyone as a patient, and then say whether the asymmetry should be kept, narrowed, or given up. Also separate what's actually settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this — don't present a contested view as the consensus.
