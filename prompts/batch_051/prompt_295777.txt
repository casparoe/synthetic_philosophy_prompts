I read a lot of early Greek history for fun, and I've developed a policy for the sources that I keep applying without really checking it. The policy has two parts. First, on any point where Herodotus and Thucydides speak about the same event or period, I go with Thucydides, because he presents himself as writing about what he lived through and claims he checked reports against people who were there, whereas Herodotus is constantly relaying what the Persians say or the Greeks say, and he often says straight out that he reports a story without believing it. Second, when a much later author such as Diodorus or Plutarch supplies a vivid detail that no earlier source has — a deathbed speech, a neat moral, a dramatic exit line — I refuse to use it unless archaeology or a near-contemporary source backs it, even when serious modern historians repeat the detail. My worry is that I may just be trusting the ancient author who sounds most like a modern historian, and that I've quietly turned a sound source-critical method into a blunt rule for deciding who to ignore. I also notice I still use Herodotus wholesale for the Persian Wars because there is often no one else, which feels a little inconsistent. Is the policy defensible as stated, or is it mostly bias with a method-shaped cover? Where would it reliably lead me astray — are there cases where I should prefer Herodotus over Thucydides, or where a late source preserves an older strand of tradition that my rule throws away? And is there a tighter version of the policy, say about when to trust someone who advertises a method and when to treat late additions as evidence about the tradition rather than about the events?
