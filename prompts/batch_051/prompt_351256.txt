In my logic course we have been working through Lewis Carroll's dialogue between Achilles and the Tortoise, at least in its usual schematic form. The Tortoise accepts P and accepts if P then Q, but refuses to accept Q. Achilles tries to force the conclusion by adding a bridge conditional to the effect that if the two accepted premises are true, Q must be true. The Tortoise accepts the bridge but still withholds Q, and the exchange is supposed to continue indefinitely. Our instructor has offered two morals of the original: first, that a rule of inference cannot be added as just another premise; second, that the Tortoise is simply missing the disposition to infer in accordance with modus ponens, and no further premise can supply it.

I want to think through a variation I have not seen discussed. Suppose the Tortoise does apply modus ponens reliably in her ordinary reasoning. Her only special policy is this: for a disputed step, she will not draw the conclusion until she has accepted exactly three bridge conditionals above that step, not two and not four. Number the bridges in the usual way: bridge one is if the original premises are true, Q must be true; bridge two is if the original premises and bridge one are true, Q must be true; and so on. Once bridge three is on the table, she stops balking and immediately accepts Q.

Working through this version step by step: does Achilles now succeed in forcing Q at the third bridge, and if he does, has he justified the move or merely satisfied a policy whose own status is unexplained? Does the first moral, that rules are not premises, survive the variation intact, or does this case suggest that the puzzle was never really about the infinity of the chain? Does the second moral, that the Tortoise lacks the inferential disposition, survive, given that she now demonstrably has the disposition and exercises it after three bridges? Finally, if the Tortoise is challenged to justify the stopping rule itself, why exactly three, does the regress reappear at a higher level, and what would that show about whether the original puzzle can be dissolved by pointing out that the chain is finite?
