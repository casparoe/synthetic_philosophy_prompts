I hold a version of the ancient atomist picture of matter, and I want to know how exposed it is. The view's core claims are these: (1) being material is fundamentally a matter of excluding other material from the region one occupies — not of having mass or charge, and not primarily of being solid to the touch; (2) matter and void are both real, with the void understood not as a mere absence but as the capacity of the world to receive and separate bodies; and (3) plurality and motion cannot be accounted for without both. By "impenetrability" I mean only the minimal exclusion thesis: two material bodies cannot both wholly occupy one and the same region at one time. I treat this exclusion as close to the mark of the material, constitutive of what it is for two bodies to be distinct. I am aware modern physics complicates the picture — fields, particles passing through matter, the quantum vacuum — but I want to know whether those complications erode the view or merely refine it.

What would have to be true for this position to be false, or badly weakened? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want each one classified as something a committed defender should concede counts against the view, something they can dismiss as a misdescription of the view, or something that qualifies the view without killing it. Include considerations from physics, from the metaphysics of space and matter, and from the theory of concepts — for instance, the possibility that impenetrability is not a mark of the material but a derivative, system-dependent feature, or the possibility that "matter" and "void" are not independent categories but a package that must be accepted or rejected together. I am not looking for stock objections; I want the precise exposure of the position.

Approach this as a philosopher of physics with historical command of atomism and a firm grip on contemporary debates about matter, space, and fields. Take time to brainstorm candidate defeaters across the three domains before writing, but present only your final, organized answer. Structure it exactly as follows:

1. **The view in its strongest form** (roughly 100 words). Restate it charitably, distinguishing the minimal exclusion thesis from any stronger claim you will not defend.
2. **What physics would have to show** (roughly 250 words). The empirical or theoretical results that would most damage the view, and what the world would look like if each obtained.
3. **What metaphysics and the theory of concepts would have to show** (roughly 250 words). Conceptual pressures, such as that exclusion is derivative, that "material" should be characterized in some other way, or that the void is an incoherent posit.
4. **What a defender must concede** (roughly 200 words). Which of these a committed atomist should themselves treat as counting against the view, as opposed to criticisms only a hard-headed opponent would press.
5. **Ranking the defeaters** (roughly 200 words). From fatal at the top to merely awkward at the bottom, with a one-line justification for each.
6. **Unfalsifiability audit** (roughly 150 words). Has the position been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if so, is that a problem for a view of this kind? Be honest.
7. **What would change my mind** (roughly 150 words). Say which single consideration is most likely to move you off your verdict, and where in your own reasoning a mistake is most plausible.
