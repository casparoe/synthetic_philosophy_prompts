I cover the social psychology and history of mass violence for a science-and-ideas magazine, and I am working on a reported essay about how middle-aged Hamburg policemen with no prior history of killing came to shoot thousands of Jewish civilians in occupied Poland as Reserve Police Battalion 101. I want to avoid rehearsing the ordinary-men-versus-willing-executioners debate as a spectator, so before I write I have set down six commitments I currently hold, and I suspect they may not cohere. They are: (1) that the immediate situation is the chief engine of such conduct—obedience to authority, conformity to one's unit, fear of letting comrades down, and the stepwise escalation of small complicity into killing; (2) that situations of this kind are not natural facts but are engineered, and institutional design—whether an officer offers an opt-out, how killing is divided among units, how often men rotate—changes outcomes dramatically; (3) that culture nonetheless matters as an enabling condition, because the same barracks-level pressures would not have produced genocide in any society; a reservoir of eliminationist ideas had to be available for ordinary men to draw on; (4) that individual character predicts only the tails—the early refusers and the enthusiastic sadists—but tells us little about the compliant majority; (5) that a complete causal explanation of how these men became killers is compatible with continuing to hold them blameworthy; and (6) that had I been a middle-aged reservist in that battalion in 1942, I would probably have done what most of them did.

I cannot tell whether this is a coherent position or a fragile alliance of views whose standard justifications pull against one another. Audit it for me. Please reply with exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. For each item, identify a genuine tension or near-tension among my commitments—or an apparent tension that dissolves on inspection—say how severe it is, and state what would have to give. Rank the five by severity, most severe first.
