I help run a small community garden and sit on its pest-management committee. I don't have any background in philosophy or biology, so I've had to think my way through this on my own, mostly by staring at our log of past decisions and noticing that I can't tell which principle we've actually been applying. I'd like you to act as an applied ethicist who works on environmental ethics and the moral status of insects. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming the candidate principles that could fit our past decisions, including principles that fit the past equally well but would point in different directions on the new case.

Our past decisions:

1. Aphids on the shared rose arbor. We approved water jets and an insecticidal soap spray.
2. Mosquito larvae in a rain barrel. We approved a bacterial larvicide (Bti) that targets mosquito and fungus gnat larvae but leaves most other organisms alone.
3. Cabbageworms eating through a plot-holder's kale. After floating row covers failed, we approved a bacterial spray (Bt) that targets caterpillars specifically.
4. White grubs causing cosmetic damage in the shared lawn. We rejected a broad-spectrum grub insecticide and recommended beneficial nematodes and lawn-care changes instead.
5. A ground wasp nest beside the children's play area. We approved removing it with a targeted aerosol applied directly into the nest, after the children were clear.
6. Scale insects on an overwintering potted lemon in the greenhouse. We rejected a systemic neonicotinoid, the kind that gets taken up by the plant and can harm pollinators, and told the owner to try horticultural oil and physical removal first.
7. Aphids on a plot-holder's tomato plants. We approved insecticidal soap after a strong water spray failed, and required spraying at dusk and avoiding open flowers.

The new case before us: A plot-holder's storage onions are heavily infested with thrips. The onions are a meaningful part of that household's winter food. Hand-removal, insecticidal soap, and two applications of a plant-derived oil spray (neem) have failed. The plot-holder now asks permission to use a broad-spectrum contact insecticide, applied in the early morning to the onion bed only. The bed sits about twenty feet from the garden's pollinator strip, and the onions will not be allowed to flower.

If we want to be consistent with our own log, what should we decide? Walk me through the candidate rules that fit our past decisions, say what each rule would require for the new case, identify the closest precedent, and, if our precedents genuinely underdetermine the decision, say exactly what principle we would be adopting with each choice. Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the ethics of pest control, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Write the answer in American English.
