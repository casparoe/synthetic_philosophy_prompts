I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on phenomenology of technology, and I have a small theory I'd like a comitted verdict on. Heidegger's equipment analysis in Being and Time draws the contrast between readiness-to-hand — the hammer disappearing into the work — and presence-at-hand — the hammer breaking and suddenly just sitting there as an object with properties. I think that binary misses the state I actually spend most of my life in, which I've been calling half-broken equipment, or maybe cranky equipment. These are tools that still work but only if you accomodate them: the door you have to lift while turning the key, the laptop that overheats unless you prop it up on a book, the browser extension that crashes if you click too fast, the ankle that only hurts on stairs. They're not transparent, and they're not broken. They hang in a middle state where you're still getting on with the task but some little slice of your attention is doing unpaid labor, adjusting and tiptoeing around the tool.

My claim is that this isn't just a midpoint on a spectrum between readiness-to-hand and presence-at-hand. It has a different structure, because the tool stays 'for' the task even as it announces itself, and the irritation is aimed at the tool's uncooperativeness rather than at a neutral object staring back at you. And unlike a clean breakdown, a half-broken tool steers the whole shape of your activity without ever becoming the explicit object of it: you start avoiding certain movements, planning routes around the tool's moods, and lowering your expectations of the task itself. I think this does real work — it would explain why people tolerate brokenness for ages rather than fixing it, which the clean breakdown story seems to predict they wouldn't.

So here's what I want. Be committed, not balanced-for-the-sake-of-balanced. Is this a coherent third mode, or have I just relabeled breakdown happening slowly? If there's an existing concept in the phenomenology of technology that already does this (without making me chase citations), say what it is and how close. And then be candid: what would Heidegger say is already covered by his three ways equipment can go wrong — conspicuousness, obtrusiveness, obstinacy — and where does my distinction actually collapse under its own weight?
