I grew up in a small manufacturing town, and over the last fifteen years I have watched its communal life dissolve. When I was young the town sustained a dense web of face-to-face associations—churches, a volunteer fire company, a union hall, ethnic clubs, summer leagues, neighbors who looked in on the elderly and shamed the occasional vandal. After the mill closed for good, these institutions folded one after another over the course of about a decade. What puzzles me is not that they declined while times were hard; it is that they never came back. The mill site was eventually redeveloped, new jobs and new families arrived, some of the original residents never left, and the physical setting is largely the same. Yet the place still feels atomized: people keep to themselves, informal mutual aid is rare, and the old organizations are defunct or exist only on paper. So my real question is what exactly was lost, and why adding back the ingredients—jobs, people, buildings—did not restore it.

I recently read some popular material on ecology and was struck by the idea of alternative stable states. A lake or a rangeland can persist for a long time in one self-stabilizing condition; if a disturbance pushes it past some threshold, it flips into a different condition held in place by different feedbacks, and simply removing the disturbance or reintroducing the original species often fails to bring the old state back. I cannot shake the feeling that my town went through something like this: it flipped from one stable state, a dense community with strong face-to-face ties and informal cooperation, to another stable state, private and isolated, and the old ingredients by themselves were not enough to flip it back because the feedback loops that maintained the old state—trust, habit, the expectation that someone will show up, the visibility of others' participation—had disappeared.

Please respond as a professional philosopher, and think carefully rather than settling for whether the analogy feels apt. Start by brainstorming what, on each side of the analogy, corresponds to what: the disturbance, the threshold, the feedbacks, the state itself, the attempted restoration. Then tell me where you think the correspondence is genuine and where it breaks down. In particular, I want to know whether this is a real structural similarity or whether I am being misled by the word state; what the ecological picture, taken seriously, would lead someone to predict or recommend about rebuilding communities; and whether any of those predictions strike you as false or distorting, and why. I am ultimately trying to understand whether belonging is the sort of good that, once the conditions sustaining it are gone, cannot be restored by simply reassembling the old parts.
