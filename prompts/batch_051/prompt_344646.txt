You are a professional philosopher working on social epistemology, collective memory, and the ethics of belief. I ask you to think carefully, to be candid rather than encouraging, and to spend a little time at the start disentangling the pieces of what I propose before you evaluate them. I am a novelist, not a philosopher, so where my terminology is improvised I would prefer you to correct the thought rather than hold me to the words.

For the past two years I have been drafting a novel about a town that marks its founding each summer with a historical pageant built around an event that did not happen—not in anything like the form the town recounts. Early on, the town council votes to keep a fabricated date on the monument even after the municipal archive quietly confirms the real one. Researching this pushed me into work on collective memory and historiography, and I ended up with a small theory I have been calling the two-jurisdiction account of disputed pasts. The account says that a community relates to its past on two registers: the record, which answers to evidence and is in principle revisable by strangers, and the inheritance, which answers to use and is held by the community itself. The record asks what happened; the inheritance asks who we are and what the past requires of us now. When the two conflict, I claim the quarrel is not really about the facts but about boundaries. A past-directed claim is answerable to the record exactly insofar as its function is epistemic; it is answerable to the community's memory exactly insofar as its function is constitutive—identity, obligation, commemoration, inheritance. From this I draw a stronger and more uncomfortable corollary: it can be legitimate for a community to protect a factually false memory from correction inside constitutive settings, because correcting it there is not truth-telling but a failure to recognize what kind of act is being performed. The very same sentence, placed in a textbook, a court record, or an archive catalogue, becomes simply false and should be corrected.

I want an honest assessment of this idea, not of my prose. First, reconstruct the account in its strongest form, even if that means quietly improving my statement of it. Second, tell me whether it is genuinely distinct from the familiar thought that myth and history serve different functions, or whether I have merely relabelled that thought. Third, give me the three strongest objections and say which, if any, is fatal rather than merely painful. Finally, show me the kind of case where the two-jurisdiction account tells us to do something clearly wrong—where the instruction to respect a constitutive memory licenses something awful, or where it wrongly crowds out the record. If the whole idea is not worth keeping, say that too, and tell me what I should replace it with.
