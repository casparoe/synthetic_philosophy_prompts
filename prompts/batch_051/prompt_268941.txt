Exam question: State Condorcet's jury theorem as precisely as you can, and then address two questions. First, which of the theorem's assumptions is most likely to fail in an actual legislature or jury, and why? Second, does the theorem give a decisive reason to prefer decision by a large group using majority rule over decision by a single expert? Explain.

Student answer: Condorcet's jury theorem says that when a group must choose between exactly two alternatives, one of which is objectively correct, and when each voter has the same probability p (greater than 1/2) of selecting the correct alternative and casts a vote independently of the others, then the probability that a majority selects the correct alternative is greater than p and increases toward 1 as the size of the group grows. The theorem therefore shows that a large majority is more reliable than any single voter, no matter how expert that voter is, because a single expert can only deliver a probability p whereas a large electorate approaches certainty. So the theorem provides a decisive epistemic case for majority rule over expert rule.

The assumption most likely to fail in a real legislature or jury is independence. Members hear the same witnesses, read the same briefing materials, and argue with one another before voting, which makes their judgments correlated rather than independent. A single persuasive speech or one widely shared piece of misleading evidence can push many votes in the same wrong direction. Once independence fails, the theorem's asymptotic conclusion need not hold, and the majority may be less reliable than a well-chosen expert. Indeed, deliberation tends to replace independent private information with correlated common information, so in a real deliberative body the theorem gives us little reason to trust majority rule over expert judgment; if anything, it suggests that the deliberation we value undermines the epistemic case for aggregation.
