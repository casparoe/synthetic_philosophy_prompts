I am giving a seminar next month to a workshop that mixes development economists and political theorists, defending the thesis that charter cities are best justified as a credible exit option whose main value is the discipline it imposes on incumbent governments rather than as a development intervention whose value lies in what happens inside the new city. My argument is that most of the expected welfare gains come from how the surrounding state responds—improving its own tax, regulatory, and property-rights environment to retain mobile people and capital—not from the new city's own productivity, which may be modest even on the project's own terms. The audience is not hostile, but it is skeptical in precise ways. A public choice economist will say that foot voting and interjurisdictional competition already deliver this discipline without any new legal form. A political philosopher working on the ethics of exit will say that exit without voice is a morally unserious response, one that lets the mobile abandon institutions rather than improve them for those left behind. A development economist who has watched special economic zones underperform will read Paul Romer's charter-cities idea as SEZs with better marketing and worse governance problems.

Give me a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the thesis. Order the objections roughly as they will surface, and flag how damaging each is. Phrase each objection in the questioner's own vocabulary, give a two-minute reply for each, say what I should concede up front and what I should hold, and note the point in each reply where I am most exposed. End with the question I am most hoping nobody asks, and explain why it is the dangerous one. Begin with the prep sheet itself, with no preamble, no restating of the brief, and no commentary before the first objection.
