I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the ethics and governance of emerging technologies, with about twenty students, and I have a two-hour block for a unit on frontier artificial intelligence. The unit is specifically about the case for pausing or sharply slowing the scaling of frontier AI systems—that is, the argument that the socially rational thing may be for the major labs to coordinate on a moratorium even though no single lab has a unilateral incentive to slow down. I want a structured, runnable classroom simulation that makes the students encounter this argument through their own choices rather than through a lecture. No framing talk up front: they should learn the rules, play, and only afterwards hear the concepts named.

Please design the full activity. The deliverable should include the setup, the roles, the exact rules and a payoff scheme that does not use real money, the materials I would need to prepare, a timeline with round structure, and a debrief. The students should come away seeing three things: first, the gap between what everyone prefers in advance and what everyone has an incentive to do round by round; second, why a voluntary pause is fragile absent monitoring and enforcement; and third, why the live disagreement in the room is not really about whether the risk is serious but about whether anyone can trust the other tables to exercise restraint.

The core mechanic I want is a shared risk jar. Split the class into four or five "labs," each with a visible score. Each round represents a year. Each lab secretly chooses between scaling and aligning. Scaling earns more points but adds black chips to a shared jar of white chips; aligning earns fewer points and adds nothing. At the end of each round the facilitator draws a chip; a white chip lets the round pass, while a black chip means a catastrophic event that ends the game and wipes every lab's score to zero. The gains are private but the accumulating risk is shared. Build in a frontier bonus that rewards the lab with the best recent scaling record, and a lag penalty for falling behind, so that defection is genuinely tempting even for a group that wants to be cautious. I would like you to calibrate the arithmetic so that if all labs scale every round, catastrophe becomes overwhelmingly likely before the end, while a sustained collective alignment would very likely survive—but where a single secret defector in any round profits handsomely. Include a short practice round with the jar visible before the real run so the mechanics are understood.

Structurally, I want the game to move through three phases: a phase in which labs may talk and make promises between rounds; a phase in which their choices are submitted privately on cards and no communication is allowed; and finally a proposed moratorium vote in which any lab may reject the moratorium, with no mechanism to detect or punish a rejection, so that we can watch whether the pause survives the first real temptation. The debrief should map specific moments—the first unilateral defection, the defensive scaling that follows, the broken pledge, the failure of the moratorium—onto the underlying ideas: a common-pool resource with an accumulating risk externality, the unilateralist's curse understood as the possibility that one actor's private gain is enough to trigger a shared hazard, the defensive race that follows once trust collapses, and the verification problem that arises because aligning and pausing can look indistinguishable from outside.

Finally, I want two honest notes. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle it—for instance, the tables colluding and concluding the problem is easy, a table treating total ruin as a joke and defecting for sport, or confusion about the chip arithmetic. And tell me frankly which feature of the real argument the simulation cannot reproduce. I suspect it is something like this: the real catastrophe is one-shot and unrepeatable, real actors hold different private estimates of the risk, and in the game the probabilities are supplied by me rather than genuinely contested—so the activity cannot reproduce the underlying dispute about the size of the risk itself. Correct or refine that if needed, but in either case be explicit about it.
