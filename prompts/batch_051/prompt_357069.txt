I teach an undergrad course on democracy and autocracy, and I want to make a "which position on democratic backsliding do you actually hold?" handout — the sort of thing where students get sorted by what they say about concrete cases, not by which label they already like. I'm less interested in rigging it toward a liberal-democracy-is-in-danger message and more interested in whether "democratic backsliding" is a usable analytic category in the first place.

Give me 8 to 10 questions, each a concrete mini-case (use Hungary under Orbán, Poland under PiS, Erdoğan's Turkey, and the US around 2020-21, since those are the cases everyone in the room can recognize without looking anything up). Each question should have 2 or 3 answer choices that pull apart some real fault line. Then a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto at least four positions: (1) a minimal procedural view where backsliding just means elections stop being free or turnover stops being possible, and the courts-and-media stuff is a different problem; (2) a liberal or substantive view where erosion of rule-of-law constraints, civil liberties, and independent scrutiny counts as backsliding even when elections stay competitive; (3) a sceptical view that the term is mostly a partisan cudgel used by people who lost or never accepted an election; and (4) an "inherently contested but still usable" view where the concept only does work once you say who gets to judge, and against what baseline. If you think the logical space forces a fifth or sixth leaf, add it, and if some combinations are empty, say why — because nobody coherently holds it, or just because it's unattractive or unexplored?

Make the branch questions the star: none of them may ask "do you think the term is partisan?" or use the word "backsliding" in a way that tips the answer. Also flag any two leaves your instrument genuinely can't separate — no papering over it. One thing I specifically want handled: a student who says Orbán's changes count but Trump's post-2020 behavior doesn't, or vice versa. Where does that person land, and what's the most awkward commitment of each leaf?

If the whole premise is off — say the positions dont really sort on cases, or the term is doing more than one job — tell me plainly and give me a restructured instrument instead of forcing the tree I asked for. And please write the whole thing in American English.
