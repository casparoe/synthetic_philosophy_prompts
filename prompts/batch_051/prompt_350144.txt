I have been turning over a parallel between two kinds of deference that are usually treated in separate literatures, and I cannot decide whether it is deep or superficial.

On one side is deference to practical authority: the soldier who carries out a lawful order despite private doubts, the official who implements a policy they personally oppose, the subject in Milgram's obedience studies who continues because the experimenter has assumed responsibility. On the other side is deference to epistemic authority: the patient who accepts a diagnosis they cannot independently verify, the layperson who accepts a scientific consensus, the juror who relies on expert testimony.

The structural resemblance I see is this. In both cases, someone with less information, standing, or time suspends their own judgment and acts (or believes) on the say-so of another, and in both cases the same ambivalence shows up. We think deference is sometimes a virtue: second-guessing every order is insubordination, and second-guessing every expert is arrogance. But we also think it is sometimes a vice: blind obedience on one side, credulity before authority on the other. Both cases also seem to raise the same second-order problem: how is the deferrer supposed to identify legitimate authority without already possessing the very competence that deference is meant to substitute for?

What I want to know is whether this is one normative structure or two. Do practical authority and epistemic authority instantiate a single general phenomenon — say, justified deference under asymmetric information, or a division of labor — or does the analogy break at a load-bearing point? The obvious candidate for the break is that a command demands action while testimony supplies evidence: one can obey while privately dissenting, but one cannot, at least not straightforwardly, believe at will. I would like you to say how deep that difference cuts.
