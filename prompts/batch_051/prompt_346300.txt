Last month I booked a flight and the airline offered the usual little box: for six dollars more it would offset the flight's carbon by funding efficient cookstoves somewhere far away. I paid it, and afterwards felt weirdly like I'd paid a fine to keep doing the exact thing I was doing — like buying my way out of the wrong rather then not doing it. A friend who thinks about this stuff calls it moral trade, and its got me wondering what the practice is actually assuming about how obligations work.

So take carbon offsetting as a whole practice: the checkbox, the credit, the marketing that calls the flight carbon neutral afterwards. What theory of wrongdoing or obligation does that practice presuppose? It seems to treat the harm of my flight and the benefit of someone else's new stove as swappable units that can be moved across people and places until the ledger balances. But we'd balk at that in other cases: nobody thinks you can punch someone and then make it right by funding a violence-prevention charity, or buy a permit to drive drunk because somewhere else someone has agreed to take a taxi. Maybe the difference is that carbon emissions are diffuse and there's no victim standing in front of you, but I can't tell if that's a real philosophical difference or just squeamishness. What exactly is being presupposed — that only total outcomes matter, that duties are transferable, or that a wrong has a price once the price is high enough? Does offsetting strictly presuppose one of those theories, or is it compatible with several rival views and the talk of cancellation is just loose marketing? And if we replaced that presupposed picture with one where harms aren't swappable and you can't discharge your own obligations by paying someone else to be virtuous, what would have to change in how offsets are designed and described — would the checkbox even survive?
