I have been turning over an argument about the morality of everyday conventions, and I cannot find the step that goes wrong. It seems to show that every local convention is morally binding, whatever its content, and that visitors have no dispensation. I find that conclusion absurd, but each premise looks plausible on its own. I would like to know which premise or step you reject, and what rejecting it commits you to.

Consider ordinary cases: queueing for a bus, leaving a tip in a restaurant that has table service, not crossing against the pedestrian signal. The argument runs as follows.

(1) A convention is a regularity on which members of a place rely. They plan their time, labour, and money in the expectation that it will be observed, and they sometimes would have done otherwise had they known it would be violated.

(2) If others rely on a convention in this way, then a knowing violation, where the regularity is in operation, imposes a small but real loss on them — the wasted time, the forgone income, or the frustrated expectation that would otherwise have been secured.

(3) Other things being equal, it is wrong to impose a small loss on others for one's own convenience when one could instead absorb a small cost oneself. This is what makes queue-jumping and failing to tip wrong.

(4) Neither the fact that the violator is a visitor nor the fact that they never consented changes (2) or (3). Ignorance of a readily ascertainable local rule is no excuse, and the loss imposed on locals is the same whether or not the visitor has accepted the convention; that the visitor has chosen to be present is, if anything, an additional reason to conform.

(5) These features — reliance, loss, and avoidability — are present in the same way in every convention, since a convention is by nature a regularity on which some group relies. There is no internal distinction here among conventions; any line short of full observance would have to be imported from something other than the features that generate the obligation.

(6) Therefore, by (1)–(5), everyone present in a place is morally required, other things being equal, to follow each local convention that others rely upon, whatever its content — including conventions that are pointless, wasteful, or demeaning.

(7) But this cannot be right. It would leave no position from which to criticise a convention, and it would turn etiquette into a standing moral debt owed to every practice, however indefensible.

(8) So one of (1)–(5) must be false, or the argument must trade on an ambiguity between steps.

I am most reluctant to surrender (3), since without something in its vicinity I cannot make sense of why a queue-jumper or a non-tipper has done anything wrong at all. But if (3) remains, I do not see how to block the route to (6). If you think the argument is invalid rather than unsound, identify the step at which it moves beyond what the premises support, and explain why that step looked inevitable. If you think it is valid, tell me which premise is least costly to abandon and what should replace it.
