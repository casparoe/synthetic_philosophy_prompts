My mate Ben has been bending my ear about a book he read on 'antimemes', and the way he summarised it has stuck with me, partly because I can't work out if it's profound or just a bit of a confidence trick. As he told it: a meme is anything that spreads from mind to mind, and an antimeme is its exact opposite—an idea or scrap of information that, by its very nature, tends not to spread and not to be remembered. The interesting ones aren't just boring or forgettable; they actively resist transmission. A secret is the standard example: once you know it, the knowledge itself gives you a reason not to pass it on, so the idea suppresses its own reproduction. Some antimemes are stronger still, he said—ideas that actively erase themselves, so that even after you've been told one you can't keep hold of it in your own head. The book apparently argues that whole areas of reality are invisible to us, not because anyone is covering them up, but because the facts themselves are antimemetic; it also reckons this is why organisations get clogged with information nobody acts on—the crucial facts keep failing to propagate. Ben's takeaway was that there are probably real, important truths that almost nobody knows, and the reason almost nobody knows them is just that they're antimemes, so the knowledge keeps dying out.

I've been turning this over and I dont know how much to trust. The phrase 'the exact opposite of a meme' feels glib, and I'm suspicious of the jump from 'some information doesn't spread' to 'the facts themselves are hiding from us'. But I also don't want to dismiss it just because it sounds like science fiction. Which parts of his summary are fair to the idea, which parts are subtly wrong or overstated, and does Ben's final conclusion survive once the sloppy bits are corrected?
