I'm a second-year law student taking a module that sits between criminal law and jurisprudence, and we've spent the past fortnight on the void-for-vagueness doctrine, or what our reading list calls the requirement of legal certainty in criminal law. The standard tests — fair notice and the avoidance of arbitrary enforcement — make sense in the abstract, but they now strike me as too coarse to do any real work, since they treat every vague statute as if it failed in the same way. To work out what I actually think, I've written a set of invented statutes and recorded my instinctive verdict on each.

(1) A park byelaw makes it an offence to 'annoy' any other person in the park; I think this is unacceptably vague. (2) A statute makes it an offence to engage in conduct 'liable to cause a breach of the peace'; I think this is acceptable, even though it is open-textured, because courts have built up a settled meaning over decades. (3) A road-traffic provision makes it an offence to drive 'at a speed greater than is reasonable and proper' having regard to traffic, weather and road conditions; I think this is acceptable as a basic rule of the road, although I would not want the criminal code filled with such provisions. (4) A statute criminalises the sale of 'drug paraphernalia', defined by a list of specific items followed by 'or any other object primarily intended for use with a controlled substance'; I think this is acceptable, though perhaps only because the list and the intent element do the constraining. (5) A loitering provision makes it an offence to remain 'in or about a school' without a lawful purpose; I think this is too vague — the reference to purpose does not, for me, repair the conduct element. (6) An offence of dishonestly appropriating property belonging to another, with 'dishonesty' left undefined for the jury; I think this is acceptable, oddly, even though the term is moralised and open. (7) A communications offence prohibits sending another person an electronic message that is 'seriously offensive'; I think this is too vague for online speech, largely because of the chilling effect on expression. (8) A gang statute defines a 'criminal street gang' in detail — three or more persons, an identifying sign or name, and a pattern of specified predicate offences; I think this is acceptable, and that the complexity buys precision.

I can see that these verdicts pull against one another. I am comfortable with 'reasonable and proper' speed but not with 'annoy'; comfortable with undefined 'dishonesty' but not with 'seriously offensive'; comfortable with a list-plus-purpose definition of paraphernalia but not with the 'unlawful purpose' loitering provision. Is there a single principle — about notice, discretion, chilling, the availability of an objective standard, the density of prior judicial construction, or something else — that captures all eight verdicts, or nearly all of them? If no single principle gets all eight, which of my verdicts should I give up, and why? Please state the principle precisely, indicate which of my cases motivates each clause of it, and then apply it to a ninth case I keep going back and forth on: a local council measure making it an offence to cause 'unreasonable noise' after 11 pm, with no further definition. Since the module is assessed in British English, could you please write your answer in British English throughout?
