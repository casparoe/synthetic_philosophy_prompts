I retired from a career in the natural sciences several years ago and have recently been reading philosophy of history. A line of thought keeps presenting itself to me as an argument, and I cannot tell whether it is valid with one costly premise or quietly invalid at a particular step.

(1) A scientific claim is better than a rival to the extent that it accounts for the relevant phenomena more accurately and comprehensively.
(2) Over the long run, later science has replaced earlier claims with claims that account for the same phenomena more accurately and comprehensively.
(3) So later science is better than earlier science in the respects that matter to scientific judgment.
(4) To understand a past episode in science, one must understand it from the standpoint best suited to judging the matters that episode was about.
(5) The standpoint best suited to judging any scientific matter is the standpoint of our best current science.
(6) Hence, to understand past scientists, one must assess their claims by how well they measure up to our best current science.
(7) But measuring past scientists by our current science obscures their own questions, standards, and concepts, since those are often exactly what later science discarded.
(8) Therefore, a faithful history of science would have to treat past scientists as approximations to or deviations from present science, which is a way of not taking their thought seriously as thought.

I am most inclined to keep (1) and (2), and I find (8) unacceptable. Where does the argument first fail, if it fails, and what is the cost of rejecting the step you identify? Please answer in American English.
