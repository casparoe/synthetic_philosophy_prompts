I'm not in school or anything, I just got really into how computation works a couple years back and started reading whatever I could find online. The thing that keeps bugging me is how every single attempt to make the idea of "what can be computed" precise ends up matching every other attempt. Like, there's the machine with a tape and a read-write head, there's building functions out of three or four super simple operations, there's rewriting strings according to rules, and there's even a model of a person following instructions on grid paper. Different people came up with these for totally different reasons, and some of them look at first like they should be far more powerful than the others, but anything you can compute in one you can compute in all of them, just maybe slower or with a lot more tape. And nobody has ever found a method that a reasonable person would call mechanical but that breaks the pattern.

So it feels like there are two facts tangled together here: one is the mathematical fact that all these precise systems line up, and the other is the much vaguer claim that they've actually captured what we mean by "following a procedure" in the first place. Does this whole observation, or maybe one of the halves of it, have a name? More importantly I want to know what kind of claim each half is: which part is a proveable theorem, which part isn't, and whether the unproveable part is the sort of thing that could even be true or false, or if it's more like a definition or a guess about what humans can do. I'm not just after the label, I want to know exactly where the boundary is between the proveable part and the vauge part, and what could in principle count as evidence for the vauge part.
