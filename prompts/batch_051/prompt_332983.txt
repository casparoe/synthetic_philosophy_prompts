I'm an undergraduate in a course on climate ethics and emerging technology, and our seminars keep circling around one argument about solar geoengineering — specifically sulfate aerosol injection or other forms of solar radiation management (SRM) — that nobody in the room ever operationalizes. The moral hazard objection, as we discuss it, says something like this: once SRM exists or is even publicly discussed as a fallback, governments, firms, and voters will treat a carbon-intensive trajectory as less risky, and mitigation effort will fall. But I find it hard to see how to separate that from several tamer mechanisms: people might reduce their support for near-term mitigation because information about SRM rationally lowers their estimate of the expected damages of a given emissions path, or because the mere availability of an additional policy option reminds them of uncertainty rather than making them feel protected. I set aside for this exercise the separate questions of termination shock if deployment were ever interrupted, and of who should decide about research or deployment; I want to focus only on whether the moral hazard mechanism is real and how one would know.

Could you write a short paper that treats the moral hazard concern as a question about evidence and operationalization rather than ideology? In particular: (1) restate the moral hazard claim precisely enough to be testable, and distinguish it clearly from the rational-reassessment hypothesis and from simple framing effects; (2) propose at least one concrete empirical design that could separate these readings — a survey or lab experiment, a design for a field or observational study, or some combination — with enough specificity about the manipulation, the control condition, and the outcome measure that someone could actually run it, acknowledging that public discussion of SRM is a more realistic treatment than full deployment; (3) spell out which pattern of results would favor the moral hazard reading, which would favor rational reassessment, and which would suggest a null or framing-only effect; and (4) say in detail what even a clean, decisive result would not settle — for example, whether it would justify restricting research or public discussion, whether expressed attitudes predict actual carbon policy, and whether the concept of moral hazard is even well-defined for a multi-agent, intergenerational public-goods problem of this kind. I am not asking for a literature review or citations; I want the reasoning to stand on its own, and I'll handle references separately.
