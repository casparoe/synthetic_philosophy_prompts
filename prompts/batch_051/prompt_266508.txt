I've been reading Pascal's Pensées in my spare time — I'm a backend engineer, so this is pretty far outisde my usual lane, but a friend recommended it and I've been chewing through it. I got to this bit where he's talking about justice and force. As I understand him, he's saying something along these lines: everyone thinks what's just should be obeyed, but what actually gets obeyed is what's strongest. Justice without force is powerless; force without justice is just tyranny. But justice is the kind of thing people argue about endlessly, while force is obvious and nobody disputes it. So you can't make the just strong, so instead you make the strong just.

My question is: isn't Pascal basically just making a might-makes-right claim, i.e., whatever the powerful decide to call just becomes just, end of story? If that's the idea, why do philosophers take him seriously as having something to say about justice, rather than writing him off as a cynic or an early legal realist who thinks law is just brute force with a nicer name? I've seen him brought up in discussions of political theory and it feels like there must be something I'm missing, because taken at face value the point seems almost trivially grim.
