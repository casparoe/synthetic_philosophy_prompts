The exam question was: "Explain the moral equality of combatants, the main argument for it, and the strongest challenge to that argument. Does the challenge succeed?" Here is what the student wrote: "The moral equality of combatants is the thesis that soldiers fighting on the unjust side and soldiers fighting on the just side are morally on a par: both have the same permission to kill and the same liability to be killed under the same rules. Walzer's central argument is that jus ad bellum and jus in bello are independent. Once a war is underway, the individual soldier finds himself in a situation he did not create—trained, threatened with punishment, and given only partial information—so the justice of his side's cause is not something he can be expected to settle. What he can control is how he fights, and therefore that is the only thing he is morally answerable for. The strongest challenge says this seperates a soldier's acts from their purpose: killing in an unjust war is killing in the service of an unjust end, and adding uniforms, obedience, and mutual risk cannot turn that into permissible killing. Critics of moral equality therefore conclude that unjust combatants do wrong whenever they kill. But this conclusion is too strong, because rules of war have to be symmetrical if they are to restrain anyone at all. If each side made its conduct depend on whether its own cause was just, there would be no stable rules and the laws of war would collapse. So legal and conventional symmetry between combatants is justified, and that in turn vindicates the moral equality thesis."
