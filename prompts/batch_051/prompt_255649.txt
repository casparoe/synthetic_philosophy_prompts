I retired a few years ago from a career in evolutionary biology and have since been reading philosophy of history with a small group. One debate has occupied us, in one form or another, for most of a year, and I have come to suspect that the real problem is not that the evidence is insufficient but that the question is being misdiagnosed. The question is whether history has a direction — whether the familiar image of the moral arc bending toward justice picks out something real. One side holds that, beneath the reversals, the long run of human history shows a cumulative expansion of moral and legal inclusion, and that this pattern is not a mere accumulation of accidents but reflects something about how history is structured, whether one cashes that out in providential, dialectical, or broadly rational terms. The other side holds that this is retrospective projection: the record can be emplotted just as convincingly as decline, and the claim survives only because its defenders revise what counts as justice whenever the record fails them. The striking thing is that the two sides agree on nearly all the particular facts — the wars, the famines, the abolitions, the expansions of rights — and yet neither has moved an inch.

My training makes me want to ask what observation would discriminate between the two views, and here the answers are evasive. When I ask the first side what would count against the arc, I am told that even a century of catastrophe would delay rather than refute it; when I ask the second side what would count for it, I am told that any apparent trend is an artifact of choosing the endpoint and the metric after inspecting the record. In my old field we would have called this a dispute about the null model: if the endpoint and the measure are selected after looking at the data, an upward trend is almost guaranteed. Having spent a career in a field that labored to expel cosmic teleology from its explanations, I am also alert to the thought that the first side's direction is teleology in historical dress; but I do not want to convict them on that resemblance alone, and the group's impasse has outlasted several attempts to resolve it by that charge. I suspect the two sides may not be making the same kind of claim at all — one may be offering a retrospective interpretation or a moral commitment, the other an empirical prediction — or that they differ about whether large-scale historical explanation may legitimately appeal to direction in the first place. I would like a diagnosis of the structure of this impasse rather than a verdict on the question itself: is it verbal or substantive, empirical or normative, well-posed or not? And given that diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction that dissolves the apparent disagreement, an operationalization that would let the two sides actually test something, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why?
