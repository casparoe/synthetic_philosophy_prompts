Before I read further in the literature on biological individuality, I want a statement of what an adequate theory in this area is supposed to deliver. Write the desiderata for a theory that tells us what makes a living assemblage one organism rather than many. It should be able to handle at least these cases: a clonal aspen grove connected by a shared root system, a Portuguese man-of-war built from functionally specialized zooids, a honeybee colony with reproductive division of labor, a lichen composed of a fungus and a photosynthetic partner, and a human body whose microbial residents can outnumber its own cells. For each requirement, give the underlying consideration or the case that forces it. Then flag which requirements are truly non-negotiable, which are preferences of a particular research program rather than adequacy conditions, and which pairs of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. I am less interested in a scorecard of existing positions than in the criteria themselves and in the places where the criteria pull against one another.

Thomas Henry Huxley argued early on that what counts as an individual is not a fixed fact of nature but depends on the level of organization at which one chooses to describe the living thing; I want the desiderata to take that thought seriously without letting it collapse into the view that individuality is merely conventional.
