I've been reading Keynes's A Treatise on Probability for a seminar, and I keep coming back to an analogy my instructor used to explain his idea of the weight of arguments. The picture is a balance: probability is where the needle leans, while weight is how much material is sitting in the pans. Two different bodies of evidence can both favour a hypothesis to the same degree — the needle is at the same angle — but one of them is backed by a much bigger pile of evidence. Keynes is supposed to have thought this second dimension matters for practical reasoning: we rightly feel more confidence in riding a train when the bridge's safety has been checked many times than when it has been checked once, even if our best estimate of the probability that it will collapse is numerically the same in both cases. The analogy is being used to support the conclusion that weight of evidence is a genuine epistemic factor distinct from probability, and that it should influence rational choice. What does it quietly import from physical weighing — additivity, a common unit, independence of the items, the idea that any extra evidence adds weight regardless of its content — that Keynes wouldn't or shouldn't want to claim? Once those disanalogies are made explicit, does the conclusion that weight matters over and above probability still hold? Could another analogy do better?
