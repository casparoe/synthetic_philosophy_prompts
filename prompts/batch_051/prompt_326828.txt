So I'm a novelist, and I've been stuck for weeks on the premise of a new book — or maybe it's the whole point of the book, I can't tell anymore. Seven years from now, an autonomous container ship runs down a small fishing boat in the fog in the North Sea. Two people die. The ship's collision-avoidance system had been retraining itself across the entire fleet, and in the accident it pulled a maneuver that nobody can reconstruct from the logs with confidence: the remote operator in Rotterdam (a human is required in the loop for regulatory reasons) couldn't override in time, and the engineers, the company, and the regulator all followed the standards that existed. The family wants a culprit. The law keeps looking for one and finding a process instead.

I have two characters who've been fighting about this for months. Abel, a criminal law scholar, is a responsibility-gap realist: our thick notions of blame need foresight and control, and no human had either here, so the choice is either admitting nobody is responsible or scapegoating someone for doing their job competently. Bina, a moral philosopher, is a distributed-responsibility theorist who thinks the gap dissolves if you stop hunting for a single guilty mind: responsibility was always spread across organizations and accidents, and what matters is forward-looking answerability, compensation, and fixing the risk, not desert.

What I want from you is the document a genuine adverserial collaboration between Abel and Bina would produce after both of them tried in good faith to write something they could sign — the kind of thing they'd send to the (fictional) accident commission. Draft it. Give me: (1) a numbered list of claims both can sign, about what actually happened in cases like this, what blame requires, and what the law can and can't do; (2) a disagreements table, each row one precise point of difference, with each side's view phrased so its own side would accept the wording; (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions that, if settled, would actually move one of them; and (4) for each crux, a concrete case or bit of evidence (real or invented) both would agree bears on it, with Abel's and Bina's predictions written down in advance. Where I suspect the cruxes are: whether generic foreseeability — knowing the system could surprise you, even if not how — is enough to ground blame; whether a corporation can be a moral agent beyond legal fiction; and whether the real issue is about facts or about which kind of responsibility we are even allowed to ask for after an accident. If part of their argument is just verbal, say which part and why. Make both positions as strong as you can — I'm already drowning in strawmen.
