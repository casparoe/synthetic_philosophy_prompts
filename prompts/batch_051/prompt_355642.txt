Over the past year I have been reading on my own about the split-brain operations performed on patients with severe epilepsy, and about two competing pictures of conscious experience. I would like to use the split-brain patients as a real case to compare the two pictures, because the case seems to put pressure on both.

The first picture is what is often called the Cartesian theater: the idea that somewhere in the brain there is a single place, or a single moment, at which the various pieces of information come together and are then presented to an inner observer, and that this presentation is what it is like to be conscious of something. The second picture is the multiple drafts model: the idea that the brain continually produces many partial, competing versions or drafts of what is going on, each of them influencing behavior and speech, with no central stage at which one of them is finally shown to a viewer.

Here is the case as I understand it. After the corpus callosum was cut, a picture shown only to a patient's right visual field could be named aloud, while a picture shown only to the left visual field could not be named but could be selected or pointed to with the left hand. When the two hemispheres were given conflicting instructions, one hand would do one thing and the other hand another. Most strikingly, when a patient was asked to explain an action performed by the left hand, the speaking side of the brain produced a fluent, plausible explanation that did not reflect the true cause of the action.

Please take me through how each picture would handle this episode. What would each picture lead one to predict or expect about the naming, the pointing, and the invented explanations? Which features does each picture handle naturally, and which features does each have to strain to accommodate? In particular, I want the obvious fallback for the Cartesian theater addressed: instead of one theater, suppose there are two, one in each hemisphere, or suppose there is one theater that is sometimes cut off from the speech system. How well does that fallback survive the fact that the speaking side invents plausible reasons for what the other side did, rather than simply reporting ignorance? Finally, say what the episode actually establishes: is it a refutation of the Cartesian theater, a serious problem for the multiple drafts view, or something narrower than either, and, if it is narrower, state precisely what has and has not been shown.
