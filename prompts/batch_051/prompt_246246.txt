I teach an upper-level ethics seminar on utilitarianism, and I’m thinking about the final exam. I want one essay question that makes students confront weather Bentham’s method already assumes that pleasures and pains are commensurable, and how a so-called “lexical threshold” negative utilitarianism might push back. I don’t want a question they can answer by reciting the “hedonic calculus” label. The question should turn on the passage below.

Here is the passage, from Jeremy Bentham, *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation* (1789), ch. IV:

> To a person considered by himself, the value of a pleasure or pain considered by itself, will be greater or less, according to the four following circumstances:
>
> - Its intensity.
> - Its duration.
> - Its certainty or uncertainty.
> - Its propinquity or remoteness.

And a bit later:

> Sum up all the values of all the pleasures on the one side, and those of all the pains on the other. The balance, if it be on the side of pleasure, will give the good tendency of the act upon the whole, with respect to the interests of that individual person; if on the side of pain, the bad tendency of it upon the whole.

Please write the exam question, a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 25. The marking scheme should name the specific moves that separate an A from a B, and it should say where students typically go wrong on each point.

---

**Exam question**

Consider the following view:

*Lexical threshold negative utilitarianism*: for any person, once a pain crosses a certain level of severity, that pain is morally worse than any amount of pleasure whatsoever—no matter how intense, long, certain, or widely distributed the pleasure is. Below that threshold, ordinary trade-offs between pleasures and pains apply.

Using the two passages from Bentham above, answer both of the following:

1. Does Bentham’s method, as written, already rule out lexical threshold negative utilitarianism? Or can a proponent of the view accept Bentham’s listed circumstances and still deny that an intense pain can always be balanced by pleasure?
2. If the proponent is right that Bentham does not rule it out, can the view still count as a form of utilitarianism? Explain what your answer turns on.

---

**Model answer**

A good answer should begin by saying what Bentham’s four circumstances are: intensity, duration, certainty, and propinquity. These are not measurements of pleasure and pain in the way a thermometer measures temperature. They are dimensions along which a single pleasure or pain can be more or less valuable to a person considered by itself. Bentham then adds, when estimating an act’s tendency, the further circumstances of fecundity and purity, and for a number of persons, the circumstance of extent. But the core point is that each pleasure or pain has *a value*, and the values can be compared.

The second passage then tells us to sum the values on each side and take the balance. This looks like it presupposes that pleasures and pains share a common scale. If one side is greater, the act is good on the whole; if the other side is greater, the act is bad on the whole. That is a commensurability assumption: pain of intensity *x* can be offset by pleasure of sufficient duration, certainty, or number. Bentham is not merely saying we should compare them qualitatively; he is saying we should aggregate them into a balance.

Lexical threshold negative utilitarianism denies one particular kind of trade-off. It says there is a class of pains—those above the threshold—that cannot be outweighed by any amount of pleasure. This is not the view that intense pain is merely very bad, so that enough pleasure would still tip the balance. It is the stronger view that the ranking is lexicographic: the avoidance of severe pain comes first, and only once that is satisfied does pleasure enter the ordering.

Does Bentham rule this out? The best answer is that the text strongly suggests a cardinal, Archimedean scale because it uses the language of “sum” and “balance.” Arithmetic addition, as Bentham invokes it, has the Archimedean property: no finite quantity is so large that it cannot be outweighed by sufficiently many finite quantities. So if we read “value” as a real number and “balance” as numerical difference, then Bentham’s method does rule out a lexical threshold: an intense pain would receive a finite negative value, and enough pleasure would outweigh it. A student who stops here has understood the passage but not yet the philosophical crux.

The stronger point is that the passage does not logically force that reading. Bentham tells us to consider intensity, duration, certainty, and propinquity, but he does not say that these dimensions must combine into a single real-valued measure satisfying the Archimedean condition. A person could accept all four circumstances as relevant dimensions and still assign lexicographic priority to severity above a threshold. The summing instruction is the closest Bentham gets to ruling it out, but the notion of a “balance” could be formalised as a lexicographic ordering with a threshold. So the proponent can say: Bentham gives us a list of factors, but not a proof that all values are cardinally commensurable.

That said, a defensible answer may go the other way and argue that Bentham’s text is not just a loose list of factors. The passage tells us to sum the values and take the balance. That is a decision procedure, and the natural interpretation of such a procedure is that no single pain is lexically privileged. If a student argues that Bentham does rule it out, they should be clear that they are appealing to the summing passage, not merely to the four circumstances. They should also distinguish a textual implication from a logical entailment: Bentham may in fact have assumed commensurability, even if he did not prove it.

On the second question: a lexical threshold negative utilitarian can still count as a utilitarian. The view is welfarist, because it ranks outcomes by the pleasure and pain experienced by sentient individuals. It is consequentialist, because it evaluates acts by the value of their outcomes. It is maximising, because it says we should choose the outcome that ranks highest according to the lexicographic ordering. It is impartial in Bentham’s sense: the same threshold applies to any person’s severe pain, and extent is handled by assigning lexical priority to severe pain regardless of whose it is. The main difference from standard utilitarianism is that the ranking is not representable by a simple additive real-valued function. But that is a formal restriction, not a rejection of the utilitarian criterion. A student should say that the label “utilitarian” is best understood in terms of the structure of the normative theory—maximising welfare—not in terms of a particular mathematical representation of welfare.

The distinction that should run through the answer is comparability versus a single cardinal scale. Lexical orderings are still orderings: they compare outcomes completely and transitively. They may fail to be Archimedean, but comparability does not require that every good and bad be tradeable at some finite rate. Bentham’s passages give evidence that he wanted a single cardinal scale, but they do not show that any view denying such a scale ceases to be utilitarian.

---

**Marking scheme (out of 25)**

- **5 marks** for correctly explaining Bentham’s four circumstances and saying what they are dimensions of — intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity — rather than treating them as units of measurement or as a full decision procedure on their own. *Typical mistake:* students say Bentham measures pleasure and pain in “utils” and skip the fact that these are factors, not quantities already assigned a number.
- **5 marks** for focusing on the summing/balance passage and identifying the assumption of a single value scale. *Typical mistake:* students quote the first passage only and never notice that the second paragraph is where the commensurability pressure comes from.
- **5 marks** for defining lexical threshold negative utilitarianism accurately, including the lexicographic structure and the threshold condition. *Typical mistake:* students conflate it with the claim that only pain matters, or with ordinary negative utilitarianism, and ignore that ordinary trade-offs apply below the threshold.
- **5 marks** for taking a determinate position on whether Bentham rules it out, while distinguishing comparability from a single cardinal scale. *Typical mistake:* students assert either “Bentham obviously rules it out” or “Bentham obviously allows it” without separating textual implication from logical entailment, and without saying which passage does the work.
- **5 marks** for evaluating whether the view is still utilitarian by appeal to welfarism, consequentialism, and maximisation, rather than by appeal to a real-valued utility function. *Typical mistake:* students declare it non-utilitarian simply because not every trade-off is allowed, as if utilitarianism were defined by the existence of an additive cardinal measure.
