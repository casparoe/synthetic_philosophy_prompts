Here's one I've been kicking around, and I'd like you to actually work through it rather than hand me the standard undergrad moves.

Take Hempel's raven paradox. The puzzle normally runs on two assumptions: (i) an instance confirms a universal generalization, so a black raven confirms "All ravens are black," and (ii) confirmation respects logical equivalence, so whatever confirms "All non-black things are non-ravens" also confirms "All ravens are black." Hence the white shoe. Fine.

Now suppose we stop modeling evidence that way. Imagine confirmation is what happens in supervised learning: a learner has some hypothesis (a classifier, a decision boundary, whatever), and data points adjust it by reducing error on a task. A white shoe isnt simply an instance of "non-black thing" or "non-raven" anymore. What it confirms depends on which hypothesis is being trained, where the point sits relative to the current boundary, and what the label distribution looks like. The very same white shoe could act as positive evidence for one generalization and negative evidence for another, depending on the task.

So here's the question. Does Hempel's paradox survive that change, or was it an artifact of a proposition-and-equivalence picture of confirmation? If it survives, tell me exactly where the raven paradox reappears inside a learning setup. If it dies, isolate which premise gets killed and say whether there's a repaired version that's weaker but still worth caring about. And don't just tell me the machine-learning setup is irrelevant because confirmation was always about propositions; I want to know whether the puzzle tracks anything real once evidence is treated as a parameter update.
