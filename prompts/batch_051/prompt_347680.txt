I was down a rabbit hole after my cousin got a fine for selling a lottery ticket to a 19-year-old at his gas-station job. He swears the kid looked 25 and the state didn’t care at all—strict liability, no mens rea needed. That felt kind of weird to me, but I also didn’t want to be one of those people who thinks every old legal idea is deep just because it’s in a book. Then I was reading Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr.’s *The Common Law* (1881) and hit this line: “Vengeance imports a feeling of blame, and an opinion, however distorted by passion, that a wrong has been done.” Holmes is talking about the early roots of liability, but the line seems to be saying that punishment starts from blame and a judgment that someone did a wrong. And yet modern law is full of strict liability offenses—regulatory stuff, selling to minors, some environmental violations—where you can be punished or fined even if nobody thinks you were blameworthy. The strange thing is that many people accept the gas-station fine but would be outraged by, say, a zero-tolerance school suspension for a kid who accidentally brings a pocketknife to class. Both punish without fault, but one feels like law doing its job and the other feels like a moral mistake. So what I’m asking is: is there actually a principled justification for this asymmetry, or are we just applying inconsistent intuitions? What’s the best candidate principle that seperates the cases people accept from the ones they reject, and does it survive contact with Holmes’s point that the original logic of punishment carried a built-in blame condition? If he’s right, should strict liability offenses be kept, narrowed, or given up?
