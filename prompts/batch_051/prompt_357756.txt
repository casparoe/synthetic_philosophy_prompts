I've been rereading Du Bois's The Crisis piece "Criteria of Negro Art" (1926), and I keep trying to line my own reactions up with his claim—to paraphrase, he says something like all art is propaganda anyway, so the real question is which side it's serving, and he doesn't care much for art that isn't doing that work deliberately.

Here are my gut verdicts on cases. A quick protest poem that's honestly not that good but lands a point about police violence: fine with me, sometimes better than fine. A technically dazzling novel by a white author that uses Black suffering as atmosphere and wins all the awards: not fine. An abstract Black painter who refuses any representational racial content and says the work is just about color and form: completely fine, though Du Bois might think it's a wasted opportunity. A mural about Black history paid for by a tobacco company that uses it as PR: queasy, but I can't articulate whether the problem is the funder, the message, or the mismatch. A searing anti-racist film made by a director whose other films are overtly bigoted: I want to say the film itself is fine, but something still feels off. A gorgeous, politically empty love song that I know is just product: I enjoy it, but I feel a little ashamed, which is odd because I don't feel that about abstract painting.

So I don't think I'm straightforwardly on Du Bois's side, but I'm not purely aestheticist either. Is there a principle that gets these verdicts, or close? If not, which ones should go? And what would that principle say about the case I keep flip-flopping on: a Black artist making deliberately disengaged, luxurious art while their community is facing overt political attack? I want to say they have every right, but Du Bois's essay makes that feel like a cop-out. Where should I land?
