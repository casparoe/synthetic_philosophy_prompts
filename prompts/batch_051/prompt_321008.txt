I've been stuck on a passage in Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling, in Problema I, where he distinguishes the ethical and religious descriptions of Abraham's intended act: ethically the act is murder, religiously it's a sacrifice, and the difference is what he calls the teleological suspension of the ethical. The passage makes ordinary judgments look pretty weird to me. If my neighbor believed God told him to kill his son, nearly everyone would say he's dangerous, call the police, and describe the intended act as murder. But those same people read the Genesis story and describe Abraham's willingness as sacrifice, and call him the father of faith. That's an asymmetry: same intended act, same claimed divine command, but we switch from condemnation to veneration depending on who it is or which text it appears in. What, if anything, justifies that asymmetry? Is there a real difference in the situations that does justificatory work, or is it just the packaging? If you defend the difference, address the case where someone in the present day makes the identical claim with equal sincerity.
