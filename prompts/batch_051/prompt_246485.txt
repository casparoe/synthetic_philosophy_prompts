Like many people who work on the ethics of risk, I have settled into a view that I would like pressure-tested. The micromort — a one-in-a-million chance of death — is the right common unit for thinking about everyday mortality risks, and once one thinks in those terms, ordinary car travel comes out looking like a poor bargain: it is among the largest voluntary mortality risks most people routinely accept, and it compares unfavorably with several risks that we treat as serious enough to regulate, insure against, or avoid. A person who genuinely accepted the micromort comparisons would drive materially less than most of us do. I want to know what would count against this view. I am not asking for the routine objections one lists and then sets aside; I want the genuine defeaters. What would have to be true — about the unit itself, about the structure of the comparison, or about the nature of risk — for the view to be false, or for driving to turn out to be worth it after all? Distinguish the evidence and arguments that would be decisive from those that would merely complicate the picture. In particular, tell me whether a one-in-a-million chance of death can be treated as fungible across very different activities: voluntary versus imposed, dreaded versus ordinary, aggregated over a population versus faced by an individual, immediate versus distributed over time. Say what the framework quietly assumes about risk-risk tradeoffs and about harms other than death, and whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability. I want to know which of these defeaters a committed defender of the view should concede as counting against it, and which they may fairly dismiss. I am personally most likely to be moved by the claim that the micromort conflates a population-level rate with a first-personal prospect, so I ask that you treat that objection as a serious candidate rather than as psychological noise. End with an honest verdict on how exposed the position actually is. Keep your answer to no more than 750 words, and write in American English.
