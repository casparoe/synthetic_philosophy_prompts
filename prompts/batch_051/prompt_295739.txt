Last night a friend and I argued for three hours about whether her decision to stop a medication that would probably buy her a few more years was philosophical maturity or avoidable fatalism, and Cicero kept surfacing in the argument. I have some philosophical training, though I do not work on ancient thought; my friend is a geriatrician with strong views. She described Cicero's De Senectute as a seductive consolation written in a world without real medicine, one that prettifies decline and teaches old people to accept suffering they ought to resist. I found myself defending the dialogue, not because I believe it is right about everything, but because I suspect it makes a deeper point worth reconstructing: that resisting the diseases of old age is compatible with accepting that a human life has a final phase, and that this phase can be genuinely worth living rather than merely prolonged. We could not settle it, and I want to force the question properly.

Suppose the Cicero who wrote De Senectute, speaking through Cato the Elder, had before him an accurate briefing on modern medicine and contemporary social arrangements, and was then asked to take positions on four developments that postdate him: first, the anti-aging medicine movement and its aim of extending healthspan rather than merely lifespan, so that serious illness is compressed into a short period near the end; second, the legalization of physician-assisted dying, including cases in which a person is not terminally ill but judges their life complete; third, the growth of age-segregated retirement communities and the decline of multigenerational households; and fourth, modern age-discrimination law as a tool for keeping older people in employment and public life. For each, reconstruct the position and reasoning you think he would actually adopt, drawing out the commitments in his own arguments rather than falling back on generic commonplaces about the wisdom of age. Then say where a modern listener would find him most surprising, and where a critic would be most justified in calling his position an elegant rationalization rather than an argument.
