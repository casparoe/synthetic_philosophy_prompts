i've been trying to understand flood risk for a house my sister is about to buy. one report says a 1-in-100 annual flood chance, based on the postcode. another says 1-in-30, based on the soil type and the way it's built. at first i assumed one had to be wrong, and that there's a real number sitting behind both that each report is estimating badly. but the more i look into it, the more it seems like neither is wrong: each is a frequency in some group (all houses in that postcode, all timber-framed houses on that kind of soil), and there are hundreds of defensible groups you could use, and they give different answers. i've ended up half-convinced of what i know is a philosophical-sounding view: for a one-off event, there is no unique probability, only probabilities relative to a reference class. i like it because it explains how the experts can disagree without anyone being sloppy, and it gives a tidy story about what the numbers actually are. but i also can't shake the conviction that there is a fact of the matter about how likely this particular house is to flood, even if we can't get at it cleanly. the rain case feels the same: when the forecast says 70% chance of rain here tomorrow, there's a fact about whether that's a good bet for this day, and a forecaster who swapped the reference class and said 20% wouldn't just be answering a different question, they'd be wrong about the question i asked. if i tell my sister there's no true flood risk for her house, i don't believe myself, but the numbers seem to back me. so is the no-fact view right and the conviction about a real chance just a hangover from thinking of one-off events as having hidden causes, or is the theory overcorrecting because some reference classes really are better than others in a non-practical sense? dont pick a side for me. i want to know what it would cost to give up the intuition, what it would cost to give up the theory, and whether there's a reading on which both survive.
