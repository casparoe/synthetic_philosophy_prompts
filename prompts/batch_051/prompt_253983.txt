I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about whether academic philosophy actually makes progress. One thing I keep bumping into is that progress is easy to talk about in physics but weirdly slippery in philosophy, because there's no agreed-upon answer key. So I've been chewing on a mechanism as a thought experiment, and I'd like you to tell me how it would actually play out.

Heres the proposal. A foundation creates a market in what it calls philosophical settlement bonds. It picks a bunch of live philosophical questions -- whether there are moral facts, whether free will is compatible with determinism, whether consciousness is an illusion, whether numbers exist -- one proposition per bond. For each proposition, traders can buy and sell two assets, and both settle fifty years later using a panel of 200 randomly sampled faculty from PhD-granting philosophy departments. The panelists rate how much the field accepts the proposition on a 1-to-5 scale.

1. An endorsement bond pays $20 for each point of the panel's mean rating, so it returns somewhere between $20 and $100.

2. A consensus bond pays $100 minus $30 times the standard deviation of the ratings, floored at $0. If the panel is tightly clustered, it pays close to $100; if ratings are all over the place, it pays less.

Anyone can trade or short these bonds, including the philosophers themselves. The rough idea is that the endorsement price tracks where the field expects to end up on a question, and the consensus price tracks how much convergence it expects, so a rising and tightening pair of prices gives you something like a progress meter for that subfield.

I want an analysis of how this setup would really behave, not whether its a nice idea in the abstract. What would the prices actually track? Who would be incentivized to do what -- grad students picking a specialty, established philosophers choosing which view to defend or how aggressively to defend it, people trying to move the settlement panel, the panelists themselves? Where does the scheme break, and are those failure modes fixable? In particular, could the market look like philosophy is progressing when it isn't, or look stagnant when philosophy is actually making progress? And as part of your verdict, please say what would change your mind about it, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
