In my philosophy of mind seminar I keep hitting the same wall: students talk about qualia as if they're pointing at an obvious kind of thing, but as soon as we get to awkward cases everyone is tracking something different. So I want a working definition of qualia—not the what-it-is-like-ness dressed up in fancier words, but something that actually does sorting work. Run it against these five: (i) the throbbing pain of stubbing a toe on a chair leg; (ii) the reddish look of a ripe tomato in daylight; (iii) a blindsight subject who sincerely denies seeing anything but guesses the direction of a moving dot well above chance; (iv) Chase, a coffee grader, who says his usual blend tastes exactly the same as it always did but he no longer enjoys it; and (v) Sanborn, another grader, who says he still enjoys the blend just as much but it no longer tastes the way it used to. My suspicion is that the word is doing at least two jobs at once—the felt character of an experience, and something like one's liking of it or verdict on it—and the Chase and Sanborn pair is meant to pull those apart, though maybe you think they can't come apart and the whole posit should be quined in the Dennett way. If you think that, let the definition show it rather than just asserting it.

Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion, in British english please. Point one gives your proposed defintion. Points two through four run it against the cases above (you can group the clear ones). Point five says where the definition gives the wrong answer or no answer, and what you'd revise.
