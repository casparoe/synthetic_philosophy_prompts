I serve on my city's planning commission, where we vote on ordinances that bind everyone in the city. Over time I have settled into a policy for how I deliberate. When a fellow commissioner or a member of the public offers an argument for or against a proposed ordinance, and the argument, traced back to its final grounds, rests on a religious claim — that the proposed use of a site would violate divine law, that the land is sacred, that God requires or forbids a certain use — I set the argument aside entirely. I do not do this because I believe the religious claim is false; I genuinely have no view about whether it is false. I do it because an ordinance coerces people of every faith and of none, and I believe that only considerations all reasonable citizens could in principle share should determine how we coerce one another.

A colleague on the commission tells me this is a genetic fallacy and, worse, a way of excluding religious citizens from democratic deliberation under the guise of neutrality. I am not sure he is wrong.

Last month the policy came under real strain. One neighbor objected to a zoning change on the grounds that a small religious community has used the site for worship for decades and that developing it would desecrate a place they regard as sacred. Another neighbor objected that he had received a private revelation that the land must remain untouched. I set both objections aside when I voted, but the two cases felt different to me. The first objection has an obvious secular near-neighbor — a claim about the historical and cultural significance of a long-used site — while the second does not seem translatable into any shareable consideration at all.

I want to know whether my policy is defensible as it stands. If it is not, is the right repair to discount religiously grounded arguments rather than set them aside, to admit only those that have a shareable secular translation, or something else? How should the rule treat the neighbor whose objection rests on a community's decades-old sacred site, as against the neighbor whose objection rests on private revelation? And how would I tell, from the inside, whether I am practicing a defensible discipline of public reason rather than dressing up an antipathy to religion as a principle? Please write your answer in American English.
