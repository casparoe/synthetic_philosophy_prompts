I work as a policy analyst in a nonpartisan legislative research office. My team reviews the factual claims embedded in proposed bills, regulatory impact statements, and committee preambles, and we have to classify each claim as established, contestable, or unsupported before the bill goes to committee. We make these calls repeatedly, under deadline, and a wrong call can leave lawmakers voting on a false empirical premise. I want a reusable decision procedure for evaluating such claims, grounded in the divergence between C. S. Peirce's and William James's pragmatist accounts of truth. The procedure should be one an analyst with no philosophy training can follow in three hours per claim, and it should mark which steps are Peircean and which are Jamesian.

The passage from Peirce's 1878 essay "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" that I want the procedure to take seriously is this:

"A clear idea is defined as one which is so apprehended that it will be recognized wherever it is met with, and so that no other will be mistaken for it."

Peirce treats that kind of clearness as insufficient—a subjective feeling of mastery. So I do not want a checklist that merely tells an analyst whether a claim feels clear. Please write the procedure: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a "no" and labels the claim unsupported, where it hands the decision to a person because the claim turns on values or policy rather than on fact, and where judgment cannot be eliminated no matter how good the checklist. Then apply the procedure to two hypothetical claims: (1) a bill states that exposure to a specified pesticide in drinking water at a specified level causes neurological damage, and (2) a bill's preamble states that a traditional child-rearing practice is safe and beneficial for development. I suspect the second will return an answer my office will not like, and I want the procedure to explain why. Finally, say what the procedure cannot do.
