In public discussion of racist speech, the expression "dog whistle" has become a standard piece of argument. The picture it relies on is that a disputed remark is like an actual dog whistle: it carries a surface message that ordinary listeners hear and treat as innocuous, while also emitting a coded signal that a particular audience picks up clearly. This comparison is made to do real argumentative work: it is used to support the conclusion that whether a statement is racist cannot be settled by its paraphraseable content or by the speaker's declared intention, because the decisive facts are the hidden signal, the audience equipped to decode it, and the effect on that audience in recruiting, reassuring, or signaling common cause. Audit this analogy as an argument. Set out the correspondence explicitly: in the whistle story, what plays the role of the whistle itself, the audible portion, the ultrasonic portion, the dogs, the human bystanders who hear nothing, and the act of blowing the whistle? Then identify where the mapping breaks, separating the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from those that are merely incidental. Finally, assess how much weight the analogy can bear: does the conclusion that a statement's racism is best located in encoded uptake and audience effect rather than in surface content or avowed intent survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy doing most of the work? If a better comparison would support the same conclusion more honestly, say what it is and why.
