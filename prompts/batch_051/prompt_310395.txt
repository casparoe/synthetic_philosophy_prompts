I am trying to work out when going along with a crowd is rational and when it is not, and my judgments about particular cases keep pulling in different directions. I would like you to tell me whether a single principle captures my verdicts, and if so what it is.

Consider these cases.

(1) Two restaurants sit side by side in a city I am visiting. One is full of locals; the other has empty tables. I have no other information, and I choose the busy one. I am confident this is sensible.

(2) Six of us must choose between two suppliers, A and B. Each of us has independently consulted a different contact whose judgment we respect but who is wrong roughly a third of the time. We announce our choices aloud in a fixed order. The first two people both choose A. I am third, and my own contact recommends B. I nonetheless announce A. I think I acted rationally, but I feel as though I threw away real evidence, and I cannot say exactly why this case feels different from the restaurant case.

(3) I am on a jury. All twelve of us have heard exactly the same testimony and seen the same exhibits. In the jury room, eleven lean guilty and I lean not guilty. I do not take their numbers as a reason to switch. Caving would feel like a failure of nerve rather than rationality, because they have no information that I lack.

(4) A friend urges me to buy a stock that has doubled in price in a month on no news. He says he is buying because everyone else is buying. I would not buy. I think this is a bubble, not a case like the restaurant, but again I cannot say exactly what the difference is.

(5) A teenager takes up vaping because most of her friends vape. I would call this social conformity or pressure, not rational deference to evidence, even though she could describe it as learning from her peers.

Beyond these cases, I have a standing unease about (2). I have heard the situation described as an information cascade, but the label does not settle my unease. I think each person in the sequence is reasoning well, yet the group as a whole seems to me epistemically defective: once the first two announcements come out the same way, later announcements appear to add nothing, and the group can lock onto A even though most of the private recommendations actually favored B. I want to say both things at once — that no individual made a mistake and that the group is doing something unreliable in a way that should worry us. Is that coherent?

What principle captures my verdicts in (1) through (5)? Please state it precisely, say which case or clause motivates each part of it, and tell me what it predicts about cases I have not listed. In particular, what should it say about this one: a hurricane evacuation warning is issued, everyone on my street stays put, and they all have access to the same forecasts; I stay too, partly because the others are staying. Is my staying more like the restaurant, the jury, or something else? Finally, tell me which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether you would overturn that verdict or complicate the principle.
