I'm a software engineer, not a philosophy person, and I'm in a book club that's doing The Call of the Wild this month. I picked up C. Lloyd Morgan's Introduction to Comparative Psychology after a friend mentioned it, and the opening of the preface hit me. He writes: "MY central object in this work is to discuss the relation of the psychology of man to that of the higher animals, since such a discussion forms in my opinion the best introduction to Comparative Psychology." That sentence made me want to read London's novel through it, because the book is about Buck, a dog, but it narrates his fears, reasoning, loyalty, even something like a pull toward an older wild nature. I'm trying to figure out: does The Call of the Wild rely on exactly the kind of move Morgan is warning against, or is its doing something else? Read the novel through Morgan's lens: where does the novel attribute mental states to Buck that Morgan's project would make us suspicious of, and where does it stay safely on the "lower" side? I don't want a flat "London anthropomorphizes, end of story" take. I want a reading that takes both the novel and Morgan seriously, notices the places where the novel resists the skeptical reading, and says whether the book ends up adding anything to the philosophical question about how we should attribute minds to animals. Also, if I'm misreading Morgan or the whole framing is off, tell me that plainly instead of pretending the question works.
