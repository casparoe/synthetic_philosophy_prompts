Write a Petrarchan sonnet about what adult children owe their parents, in which the octave presents the debt model and the sestet presents the friendship model associated with Jane English, and the volta between them is the precise moment the speaker abandons the ledger. The octave should make the debt model genuinely attractive rather than a straw man: years of care, labour, worry and lost alternatives, all given before the child could consent or reciprocate, so that the grown child now seems to face a debt that can never be discharged. The volta, at line nine, should turn on the realisation that the question is not what was given in the past but what kind of relationship exists now. The sestet should render the friendship model the way English does: whatever duties there are arise from the relationship itself, much as in friendship, where one helps a friend not to repay past favours but because the friendship calls for it; where no such relationship exists, the fact of having been raised creates no standing debt, and at most the ordinary duties owed to any person in need remain. The closing line or two should say exactly what has to be given up — the metaphor of debt, the balance sheet, perhaps the idea that gratitude can be owed for a childhood one never contracted for — rather than merely gesturing at it. Avoid the jargon of moral philosophy and do not name the author in the poem; carry the argument through imagery and syntax. The poem should be accurate enough that someone who knows English's view would recognise it, and good enough as a poem that someone who does not know the debate would still feel the weight of the position.
