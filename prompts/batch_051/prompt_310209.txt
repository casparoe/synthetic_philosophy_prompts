Hey, I'm in a pragmatism class and we're reading Peirce's How to Make Our Ideas Clear, and I keep getting stuck on the passage where he defines truth. I don't have the exact wording in front of me, but it goes roughly like this: the truth is the opinion that is fated to be ultimately agreed on by all who investigate, and the real is the object represented in that final opinion. I understand he's not saying truth is whatever people believe right now, because he admits individual inquirers can get things wrong. But if the final opinion is just whatever all inquirers eventually settle on, I don't see how that isn't still making truth depend on us. Like, spose there's some claim that would be settled on only because of some cosmic accident or a bias in human cognition that never gets corrected—would Peirce have to say that's the truth? And what is fated doing here exactly? Does it mean will happen or something like would happen under ideal conditions? Also, the real being the object of the final opinion feels circular: we identify the final opinion by its getting reality right, but we identify reality by what the final opinion says. Can someone untangle this?
