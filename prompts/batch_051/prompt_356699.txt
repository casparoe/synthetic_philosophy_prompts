I'm in a seminar on religion and the state, and one thing keeps annoying me: French laïcité is always presented as strict state neutrality, as if the public sphere is a blank surface onto which no religion is allowed to write. The showpiece is the 2004 rule that keeps students in public schools from wearing conspicuous religious signs — a headscarf, a kippa, a large cross — while a small medal around the neck is fine. But the blank surface turns out to be surprisingly Christian-shaped. The national holiday calendar is mostly the Christian one (Easter Monday, Ascension, Pentecost Monday, Assumption, All Saints, Christmas), school breaks cluster around All Saints, Christmas, and Easter, Sunday is the default weekly rest, and Alsace-Moselle still runs on the old Concordat, with Good Friday and December 26 as extra public holidays and state-subsidized religious instruction. So the same state that tells a Muslim student her headscarf is an unacceptable intrusion of religion into public school life also closes the school for a Catholic feast and names the November break after All Saints.

I genuinely cant tell if there's a principled line here or whether majority Christianity just gets reclassified as culture and inherited timekeeping while minority religions get reclassified as politics and intrusion. Give me the strongest case you can that the asymmetry is justified — maybe the calendar is an inert social convention and not an act of recognition, maybe laïcité constrains individuals' conduct in schools but not the administrative skeleton, maybe the Concordat is a frozen historical exception with no live endorsement behind it. Then stress-test that case against nearby examples: if France made Eid or Yom Kippur a public holiday, would that be a religious endorsement of a kind the Christian holidays already aren't — and if so, why? Is a school closing for Easter really different in kind from a student skipping for Eid, or only different in who has to ask permission? At the end, give a bottom line: keep the asymmetry, extend it (secularize the calendar, or pluralize it), or give it up — and say what the deciding case is.
