I chair my hospital's clinical ethics committee, which must decide before Thursday whether an elective gastric bypass may proceed for a 62-year-old patient. The surgeon has a signed consent form, but several staff members have asked us to review whether that consent is valid.

The facts are these. The patient has severe obesity and type 2 diabetes and has said for years that she wants the operation. At the consent discussion, the surgeon emphasized expected weight loss and diabetes remission but did not mention the possibility of a bowel obstruction requiring further abdominal surgery, even though he knew she had previously had a bowel perforation and had said another unplanned abdominal operation was her greatest fear. Her adult son, who is her primary caregiver, was present at the visit; the patient later told a nurse that her son had told her that if she did not go through with the surgery he would have to stop caring for her and she would end up in a nursing home. No formal capacity assessment was performed before she signed. A geriatric psychiatrist who saw her afterward reports that her capacity fluctuates: on good days she can state the purpose and main risks of the operation in her own words, but on the day she signed she could repeat the information without being able to weigh the risks or recall the alternatives, and on bad days she cannot do that.

The committee is split. Some members say the consent fails the standard requirements that consent be informed, voluntary, and given by someone with decision-making capacity, and they argue that the three failures compound one another. Others reply that she has held a stable, long-standing preference for the procedure, that the omitted risk is relatively uncommon, and that a competent adult can legitimately be influenced by a family member's honest statement about the limits of his care.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete course of action—allow the surgery to proceed, suspend it pending specified safeguards, or declare the current consent invalid and require the consent process to be redone—and it should give reasons that the surgeon and the family could engage with, including an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side.
