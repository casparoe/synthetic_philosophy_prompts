Here are my verdicts about when a musical event is a performance of a work, and I want to know what principle they add up to. When a modern orchestra plays one of the late Beethoven quartets, I have no hesitation saying it is the same work as the one premiered in Beethoven's day, though every instrument and stylistic choice differs. When a jazz trio covers a Nirvana song, reharmonizes it, and changes the meter but keeps the melody and lyrics recognizable, I still call it a performance of the same song. But when a producer lifts a two-second horn stab from an old funk record and loops it under new vocals, I call that a new work, not an arrangement or performance of the old one. Now the sticky cases. If someone opens a MIDI file and hits play, letting a computer render the score data through a piano plugin, I dont want to call that a performance; it feels like playing back a recording, though no recording was ever made. Yet if a tribute band recreates a song exactly as it appears on a studio record, note-for-note, playing to a click and triggering samples of the original effects, I'm tempted to say that *is* a live performance. And when a pianist performs that piece consisting of four and a half minutes of deliberately not playing, I count it as a performance, but when a subway car sits silent for the same span I don't, even though theres no acoustic difference. Is there one principle about the work/performance relation that yields all these verdicts? If not, which would you overturn, and why? And what does your principle say about a producer who builds a track entirely from granular resynthesis of one existing recording, so it sounds new but every sound traces causally back to the old one?
