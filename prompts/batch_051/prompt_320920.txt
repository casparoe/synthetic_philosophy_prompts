I've noticed a difference in how I attribute knowledge that I can't justify offhand. If a friend tells me she knows that Lisbon is the capital of Portugal, I treat her word as enough: I don't ask her to demonstrate it, and I won't take the attribution back unless something specific undermines her claim. But if a friend tells me she knows how to swim, I don't grant that she knows how until I've seen her swim or heard from someone who has watched her. Her describing the strokes, even accurately, is no substitute. So my standard for ascribing what I'll call knowing-that is far more lenient toward testimony than my standard for ascribing knowing-how. Yet I also accept that someone with two broken arms still knows how to swim, so my insistence on a demonstration can't just be an insistence on present ability. Is there some principle that makes this difference in standards defensible, or is it an inconsistency in how I talk? Find the strongest justification you can, test it against nearby cases (the injured swimmer, someone who performs a skill without being able to describe it, someone who succeeded only by luck), and then say whether I should keep the asymmetry, drop it, or reverse it. Also tell me how confident you are in that verdict and which point in your reasoning you are least sure of.
