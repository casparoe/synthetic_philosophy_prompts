In the final paragraph of Chapter VII of the first edition of An Essay on the Principle of Population, Malthus describes a sequence in which vice, sickness, epidemics, pestilence, and ultimately famine act as checks that level population with subsistence. Consider an argument built on that passage:

(1) Malthus’s core principle is that population tends to outrun subsistence unless held back by some check.  
(2) The absence of a Malthusian catastrophe during the nineteenth and twentieth centuries was not the absence of checks but the strengthening of preventive checks—contraception, delayed marriage, urbanization, and changing norms about family size—alongside unprecedented gains in agricultural productivity.  
(3) Those modern checks belong to the same family as the checks described in the passage, even if their moral coloring has changed.  
(4) A long-run mechanism can be correctly identified while its predicted timing and specific forms are mistaken.  
(5) Therefore Malthus was right but early: the principle of population was not falsified but shown to operate through different instruments than Malthus anticipated.

Is this argument valid? If not, which step exactly fails, and what is the strongest repair at that step? In particular, does step (3) rest on an equivocation between Malthus’s own categories of vice and misery and later reconceptualizations of preventive checks?
