I've been down a lottocracy rabbit hole the last few weeks and got into an argument with a friend about how far you could actually push random selection before it turns into a wierd new form of machine politics. So here's a specific proposal—I'm not asking whether it's a good idea, I want to know how it would actually behave.

Imagine a mid-sized country amends its constitution like this. The lower house, currently 300 elected members, gets 150 additional members chosen by lot from the adult citizen rolls, serving a single three-year term. The lot-chosen members can't introduce or amend bills; they only get a up-or-down vote on bills that have already passed the elected members. A bill is killed if a majority of the whole 150 votes against it, meaning at least 76 no votes—abstentions and absences count as letting the bill pass. To keep them 'independent,' they're barred from running for any elected office for ten years after their term, and if they complete the term they get a lifetime pension set at the median national income. Their staff and briefing materials come from a new Office of the Citizen Chamber, whose director is appointed by the elected government.

Walk me through what this actually does. What does it incentivize among the elected politicians, the party machines, the lot-chosen members themselves, and ordinary people deciding whether to stay in the lottery pool? Where does it get gamed first—the selection rolls, the briefing process, the pension, the post-term lobbying ban, the absence rule? Does the up-or-down-only rule concentrate power somewhere surprising? And are the failure modes fixable without gutting the scheme, or do the fixes just create new failure modes? Write your answer in American English.
