Write a dialogue between two Warring States thinkers, a Mohist and a Confucian, arguing over whether care should be impartial or graded. Set the scene the day after a flood has destroyed a neighboring town: the academy where they are staying holds a limited store of grain and must decide how to distribute it among a stranger from the flooded town, a destitute former political rival, and the Confucian's aging mother.

The Mohist should defend impartial care as a practical norm for action, pressing the Confucian to state the principle that lets the claims of the nearest outweigh the claims of the most desperate, and charging that graded love, scaled up to clans and states, is exactly the partiality that keeps the states at war. The Confucian should defend graded love as psychologically real and morally basic, pressing the Mohist on whether someone who treated a parent and a stranger as interchangeable could love either of them genuinely, and whether impartiality would not collapse into an abstract rule the moment it had to override a concrete attachment. Each must answer the other's latest point rather than give set speeches, and each should grant small concessions where the other's arguments compel them while holding the core position. Neither should be a strawman. Include eight to twelve exchanges, and do not resolve the disagreement into a tidy synthesis; the dialogue may end with both positions sharpened and unreconciled.

Begin your response with the dialogue itself, with no preface or commentary before the first spoken line. Use the format "Mohist:" and "Confucian:" without further stage directions.
