I'm trying to think through Habermas's ideal speech situation, the idea that a discussion is only rationally legitimate when the participants are free from coercion and status differences, and what moves people is just the better argument, not power or pressure. The word I keep getting stuck on is "coercion." I want a working definition of coercion in the communicative sense (not just physical threats or a gun to your head) that gets the following cases right. Run the definition through each case and say wether it counts as coercion in that sense—or, if you think it's some other kind of failure of the ideal speech situation, say where coercion ends and the other problem begins.

- A boss asks an employee, in front of the whole team, for honest feedback on her new policy.
- A participant in a town hall stays silent because they expect everyone to mock their accent.
- A moderator gives one side twice as much time and keeps cutting the other side off.
- A speaker frames the question so the alternative they dislike sounds obviously idiotic, without threatening anyone.
- A person is paid a lot to argue for a position they don't actually hold.
- Someone says outright: agree with me or you'll lose your job.
- A debater wins people over mostly by being quicker on their feet and more articulate, even though their argument is weaker.
- A panel runs out of time and the group has to settle after hearing only the first two options.
- Two friends are arguing about a movie, and one changes their mind because the other gives reasons they find convincing.
- Someone knowingly says something false to get the group to agree.

Then, if the definition gives a weird or surprising verdict on any of these, either revise it or defend the weird verdict. And tell me which of your verdicts is least secure, and what would change your mind about it—which argument or consideration would make you redraw the line.
