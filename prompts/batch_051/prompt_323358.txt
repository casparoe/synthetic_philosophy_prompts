Last night, a friend and I spent the whole evening arguing about a question we had not seen discussed anywhere: what would C. S. Peirce have made of large language models? My friend's view was that Peirce would classify them as a mechanized version of the method of authority, since they fix belief by reproducing whatever dominates their training corpus and since users defer to their output in roughly the way people defer to institutional consensus. I did not accept that. Peirce treats inquiry as beginning only with real, living doubt, understood as an uneasy disturbance of habits of belief and conduct; a language model, I argued, has no beliefs, habits, or unease to be disturbed, so it cannot count as an inquirer in his sense no matter how competent its output looks. My friend replied that Peirce was giving a theory of method rather than a psychology of inquirers, and that the harder question is whether a machine that runs experiments, tests predictions, and revises its dispositions could satisfy his account of the method of science. We never settled it.

Please predict, as rigorously as you can, what position Peirce would have taken on this issue, drawing on the pragmatic maxim, his taxonomy of the methods of fixing belief, and his fallibilism. In particular: would Peirce regard a large language model, or any machine learning system, as an inquirer in his sense? Which of his methods of fixing belief, if any, does such a system instantiate? Under what conditions, if any, would he have accepted a machine as a participant in scientific inquiry? Defend the answer you believe he would have given, and explain where each of the two positions from last night's argument goes wrong.
