Before I read another proposal for sorting scriptural commands into cultural and transcultural, I want the full list of requirements such a theory would have to meet. Give me the desiderata: the cases it must get right or explicitly justify setting aside (head coverings, the commands to slaves and masters, dietary law), the distinctions it cannot afford to collapse (a command versus the principle it serves versus the cultural form of its application; what the text commands versus what its author merely assumes; a transcultural norm versus a culturally variable expression of it), and the historical facts about first-century audiences it cannot contradict — with the case or consideration behind each requirement, and a mark of which are non-negotiable and which are only preferences of a particular camp.

Here is the passage that makes me suspect the usual tests are too quick. Paul, in his First Letter to the Corinthians (King James Version), defending the head-covering rule, writes: "Doth not even nature itself teach you, that, if a man have long hair, it is a shame unto him? But if a woman have long hair, it is a glory to her: for her hair is given her for a covering." This appeal to nature itself is exactly the sort of grounding people usually treat as transcultural, yet most later readers treat the resulting dress code as culturally bound. I want the requirement that handles that pairing written out explicitly, with what a theory must say about appeals to nature inside a passage whose application it culturalizes. Then tell me which single requirement on your list would knock out the most popular ways of drawing the line, and, very briefly and without citations, how the tripartite division of the law, a redemptive-movement hermeneutic, and a creation-order test fare against the list.
