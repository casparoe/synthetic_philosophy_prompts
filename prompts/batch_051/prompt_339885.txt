I have come to accept a structuralist resolution of the identification problem about what numbers are. The motivating puzzle is that the natural numbers can be reduced to sets in incompatible ways: on one standard reduction 2 is a member of 4, on another it is not, and there seems to be no further fact that could settle which reduction is the real one. From this I draw the structuralist conclusion that mathematical objects are not independent relata with hidden intrinsic natures but positions in structures, and that a question like whether 2 is really an element of 4 is either context-relative or not well-posed when asked in absolute terms. I want to know what would count against this position, not as a debating exercise but as an honest map of its exposure. Which arguments, intuitions, formal results, or observations should most move me toward abandoning or substantially revising it? For each, I would like to know whether it is decisive, merely awkward, or something a well-informed structuralist would dismiss as resting on a confusion; and I would like to know which of these defeaters a committed structuralist would themselves concede are genuine costs rather than artifacts of a critic's misreading.

I am thinking of several candidates, among whatever others occur to you. Does the identification problem simply reappear one level up, as a question about which structure the natural numbers are, or about how to individuate two structurally interchangeable positions in a non-rigid structure such as the complex field with its conjugate square roots of minus one? Does the routine mixed use of mathematical vocabulary across domains — sets and numbers, ordinals and cardinals — force structuralism to treat a single expression as naming different positions in different structures in a way that distorts mathematical practice? Are there non-structural mathematical facts that mathematicians are entitled to take seriously, and if so, does their existence reveal a boundary the structuralist cannot draw without circularity? And does the view, under pressure, collapse into formalism, into a modal story on which structures are possibilities rather than objects, or into full-blooded Platonism? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which a defender would grant as a live problem, and end with an honest assessment of whether the position as I have stated it still risks anything at all — or whether it has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that would be a defect in a metaphysical view of this kind.
