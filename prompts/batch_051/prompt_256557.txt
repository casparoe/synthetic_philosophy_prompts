I've been going back and forth on Weber's Protestant ethic thesis, and I want someone to actually adjudicate it rather than summarize it. The thesis, as I understand it: ascetic Protestantism—especially Calvinism with it's predestination problem—created a psychological situation where people could never know whether they were saved, so they threw themselves into methodical worldly work as a sign of election, and because they were ascetic they couldn't spend the profits on luxuries, so they reinvested them. That, Weber says, is the spirit of capitalism, and it's what launched capitalism in the places where it took off.

But here's what's bugging me. The last few decades of economic history have made it pretty plausible that the measurable Protestant advantage came from literacy—Protestants had to read the Bible, so Protestant regions built up human capital, and human capital is what drove the growth. That mechanism is boring and mechanical, and it doesn't need any of Weber's psychology of salvation anxiety. So: does Weber's thesis survive this finding at all, or is it dead and just a good story? I want to know exactly which premise the literacy explanation attacks—does it undercut the claim that culture can be a cause here, or only the specific psychological mechanism Weber proposed? And if there's a repaired version that still has teeth, what would it have to look like to count as a real causal explanation rather than a just-so story? I'm less interested in whether Weber got the history right than in what this whole thing says about explaining outcomes with values.
