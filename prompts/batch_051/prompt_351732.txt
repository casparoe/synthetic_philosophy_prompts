Twice a week I end up in some version of the same argument, and nobody ever moves. The argument is about small failures of reliability: being late, forgetting to call when you said you would, cancelling plans at the last minute without much reason, breaking a minor promise. Everyone involved agrees on the facts. We all agree that these actions usually do little or no serious harm, that they are typically not done with bad intent, and that the person let down often feels briefly annoyed, inconvenienced, or mildly disrespected. What we cannot agree on is what sort of thing such a failure is.

One side treats these failures as genuine moral failures — small ones, but moral in the same general category as lying or breaking a serious promise — because the person creates an expectation and then fails to honor it. The other side treats them as matters of etiquette, practical competence, or personality: possibly rude, maybe immature, but not the kind of thing that makes someone morally worse. What bothers me is that this does not feel like a disagreement about evidence. No additional fact about the effects of lateness seems likely to change anyone's mind; both sides would simply absorb the new information into the position they already hold.

My working hypothesis is that the real disagreement is about what makes something a moral issue in the first place: whether morality reaches down to small, everyday failures whose individual consequences are minor, and whether creating expectations in another person can itself give rise to moral reasons, rather than only social ones. I am not confident that this is the right diagnosis.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a hidden empirical disagreement about how these small failures erode trust over time, a disagreement about standards for what counts as moral, a difference in bedrock intuitions that neither side can argue for, or something else? And given the diagnosis you defend, is there anything that would actually move one side or the other — a kind of case, a distinction, a way of reframing the question — or should we expect the argument to stay stalled? I want the diagnosis argued for concretely, not a survey of the two positions, and I want an honest answer about whether progress is possible.
