A question I keep getting stuck on. In ordinary criminal law, a private person who breaks a law to stop a greater harm still gets punished: we might sympathize, but we dont say the law made an exception for them. Yet when an executive does roughly the same thing in a crisis — suspends a process, spends money without authorization, acts outside or against the statute — we have a whole vocabulary for treating it as constitutionally fine, and we mostly decide the vocabulary after the fact: "prerogative" and statesmanship if it works, "tyranny" or "usurpation" if it doesn't. The same conduct can be praised or condemned depending on how the gamble comes out, which looks like exactly the kind of outcome-worship we'd reject everywhere else.

Locke more or less says that's the test, in the Second Treatise, chapter XIV, "Of Prerogative":

"This power, whilst employed for the benefit of the community, and suitably to the trust and ends of the government, is undoubted prerogative, and never is questioned: for the people are very seldom or never scrupulous or nice in the point; they are far from examining prerogative, whilst it is in any tolerable degree employed for the use it was meant, that is, for the good of the people, and not manifestly against it: but if there comes to be a question between the executive power and the people, about a thing claimed as a prerogative; the tendency of the exercise of such prerogative to the good or hurt of the people, will easily decide that question."

I'm not asking for the history of this idea. What I want is your best shot at defending or dismantling the asymmetry. Is there a principle that separates constitutional gaps (the rules genuinely ran out) from plain illegality (the ruler just broke the rules), and does Locke's "good or hurt of the people" test track that principle or just relabel it by results? Test whatever principle you give against hard cases: an emergency act done with good justification and bad luck, and a nakedly illegal act that happens to produce a public benefit. Then tell me whether the distinction should be kept, revised, or given up.
