hey, i'm putting together a short position paper for a philosophy of psychiatry seminar and i want a consistency audit before i have to defend any of this as a package. here's the set of views i seem to have landed on, in my own words, and i'd like you to go through them the way a careful but unsympathetic friend would.

i think at least some psychiatric categories, with schizophrenia as my go-to example, pick out real and fairly stable dysfunctions in the world, not just labels for behavior that worries other people. so i'm some kind of realist about psychiatric kinds, though i don't want to overclaim about how sharply the world is jointed.

i'm also pretty convinced by hacking's looping effects: once people are sorted into a category they take the category up, reinterpret themselves through it, and that reinterpretation feeds back into their symptoms, the prevalence figures, and the whole life-course of the condition. psychiatric kinds are interactive kinds, not indifferent kinds, the way a chemical element doesn't care what we call it.

from that i want to draw the conclusion that the dsm could never be a faithful mirror of a fixed natural landscape, because publishing a category is itself partly an intervention in the phenomenon it sorts, not just a measurement of it. the target moves underneath the classification.

but i still think validity in the strong sense is the right standard for revising the manual: a category should be kept or cut according to whether it maps onto an underlying disease process or mechanism, not because it's useful for insurance, destigmatizing, or making patients feel seen.

and here's a consequence i'm prepared to bite: if a patient community builds an identity around a diagnosis, i don't think that identity should count for much in nosology. the grief people felt when asperger's got folded into autism spectrum disorder is real pain, but it isn't evidence about whether the category tracks a real mechanism.

one last point so i don't get misread: i don't think a looping-shaped condition is fake, or less real than a more cleanly biological disease. a syndrome can have its form partly shaped by its own label and still be a perfectly real way of being unwell, with real suffering and real impairment.

so: does this hang together, and if not, where exactly does it come apart? go through the pairs that matter, seperate the ones that only look like tensions from the ones that really are, rank the real ones by severity, and for each say what would have to give and what the cheapest fix would cost. and please write the whole thing as continuous prose, no headings, bullets, bold, or tables, like an email to a friend rather than a memo. don't protect my favorite commitments just because they're mine; if something has to go, tell me straight.
