I've been working through Wolfram's A New Kind of Science and got stuck on this bit, where he says: "For what the principle does is to assert that when viewed in computational terms there is a fundamental equivalence between many different kinds of processes." Maybe I'm misreading him, but this sounds like it should gut the whole idea that there are deep differences in hardness between computational problems. Once you've got universality, everything sits at the same level, so why would P vs NP, or the difference between decidable and undecidable, or between polynomial and exponential time, say anything about how the world works rather than just about our parochial machine models? If Wolfram's right, is complexity theory basically a taxonomy of distinctions that dont cut nature at the joints? And if he's not right, where's the equivocation exactly — and does the same slipperiness infect his claim that the principle is a scientific one rather than a vague empirical generalization?
