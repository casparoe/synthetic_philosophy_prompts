I have been defending a particular view in my graduate seminar on the ethics of historical judgment, and I would like to use it as an exercise in testing a position by mapping everything that could count against it. The view is this. The 'man of his time' defence — the idea that we should withhold, soften, or qualify moral criticism of a historical figure because the conduct in question, Jefferson's slaveholding, Churchill's racial views, Columbus's treatment of the peoples he encountered, was normal or widespread among the figure's contemporaries — is almost always weaker than it is taken to be. I am not claiming that these figures should be dismissed as nothing more than their worst acts, nor that historical context has no role. My claim is narrower: in the standard cases where the defence is invoked, the prevalence of a practice or attitude does little to justify, excuse, or even mitigate the conduct, and the defence derives most of its apparent force from sliding between an explanation of how the conduct became possible and a reason for not blaming the agent for it. What would count against this position? I do not want the usual set-piece replies; I want a map of its exposure. Tell me which observations about the cases, which distinctions between justification, excuse, and mitigation, which considerations about moral knowledge and the historical availability of moral concepts, and which arguments about single acts versus whole lives should lead me to revise it — and which of them a careful defender of my position should themselves concede as counting against it. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and conclude by saying whether the position, once the needed qualifications are added, has become so insulated that it no longer risks anything, and whether that insulation is itself a reason to distrust it. Please write your answer in continuous prose, as you would write an email or a letter, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
