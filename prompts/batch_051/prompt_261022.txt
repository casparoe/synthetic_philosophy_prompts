I am a PhD student working in moral psychology, and I keep returning to an analogy that virtue ethicists lean on in their replies to the situationist critique. The situationist evidence, as it is usually presented, suggests that whether someone helps, lies, cheats, or resists pressure depends heavily on minor features of the situation — being in a hurry, finding an unexpected coin, the presence of bystanders, the immediate demands of an authority figure — and much less on the broad character traits we ordinarily attribute to people. A familiar virtue-ethical reply concedes the variability but denies that it threatens character. The reply draws on Aristotle's old analogy between virtue and a craft or skill: the honest or just person is to their virtue as a pianist is to piano-playing. A pianist's skill does not manifest when the instrument is broken, the conditions are distracting, or the repertoire is unfamiliar; it manifests in the conditions for which the pianist has trained and under which the skill can be expressed. So, the reply concludes, situationally variable behaviour is not evidence against virtue; it is exactly what the skill model predicts about the performance conditions of character.

I would like you to audit this analogy in full. Lay out the mapping explicitly: what in the domain of skill corresponds to what in the domain of virtue — acquisition through practice, the gap between knowing and doing, the role of teachers and exemplars, mastery and its decay, performance conditions, the relation between a skill and a person's other traits. Then identify where the mapping strains or breaks. In particular, I worry that the analogy imports features that may cut the other way. Skills are typically domain-specific and situation-relative rather than global; a skill can be exercised without any commitment to the value it serves, since a competent pianist can loathe music or play only for pay; and skilled performance is often carried by automaticity and local cues rather than by a stable, cross-situational sensitivity to ethically relevant features. If those are genuine parallels, the skill analogy might actually support the situationist's preference for local, situation-bound traits over global virtues, which is the opposite of the work the analogy is being asked to do. I want a verdict on which disanalogies matter for the virtue-ethical reply, which are harmless, and whether the reply survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If the analogy is beyond repair for this purpose, say what a better analogy would be and what it would need to secure.
