Suppose you are designing the initial work program for a new five-person unit inside a government audit office. The unit's job is not to produce the office's forecasts but to write internal guidance on how forecasters should combine the inside view (a detailed model built from the specific features of the program being assessed) with the outside view (a reference class of similar past programs, and the distribution of their outcomes). The unit has eighteen months, cannot run new empirical studies, and must end with a usable rule for when a base rate should override a case-specific model and when the case-specific model may override the base rate.

I am asking for a defended priority ordering of the open conceptual questions the unit should settle first, not a literature survey or reading list. Below are candidate questions; add, cut, or merge them as you see fit. A good answer might also argue that one of these is really two different questions, or that two of them collapse.

- What makes a reference class relevant enough to support an outside-view estimate, and how much of that relevance judgment is itself an inside-view judgment?
- When does having private, case-specific information justify deviating from a base rate, and when is that deviation best explained as special pleading?
- How far does decomposing the inside view improve accuracy before further decomposition becomes a way of rationalizing a preferred answer?
- Is the outside view best understood as a claim about base rates, about deference to more experienced agents, or about something else, and how does it relate to epistemic modesty?
- How should one use reference classes that are sparse, survivorship-biased, or drawn from a different context or era?
- Is there a principled stopping rule for updating a base rate after one has begun building the inside model, or should the two be combined once and then held fixed?

For each question you place near the top, give the dependency-aware reasoning: which downstream questions it controls, whether the unit's conceptual and formal skill profile can make progress on it without new data, what a good-enough answer would look like as a foundation for later work, and how the final guidance rule would differ depending on the answer. Where one question presupposes another, make that explicit. Also identify plausible-looking candidate questions you would deliberately deprioritize because their answers would not change the guidance much, and state what development during the first year would most plausibly move a question up or down your ordering.
