After I retired from the lab, I started reading philosophy in an unsystematic way — mostly because lab life taught me that the interesting problems are usually about where you draw the boundary of a system. That's probably why the extended mind thesis caught my eye: the claim that cognition doesn't stop at the skull, and that things like notebooks, phones, and written records can be genuine parts of a person's memory and thinking, not just tools attached to a brain.

I kept lab notebooks for thirty years, so here is a puzzle. Imagine two people miss the same meeting. The first stored the time in her head and forgot it. The second wrote it in a small notebook, had the notebook in her bag, and never opened it; the information was literally on her person but never got consulted. Honestly, most of us treat these differently. We call the first a memory lapse — a cognitive failure, the sort of thing she should be embarassed by, maybe an early sign of decline. The second we treat as a failure of organization or attention: annoying, but not quite a failure of memory. We probably wouldn't say the second person forgot the meeting at all, or that her memory is poor; we'd say she failed to check her notes. But if the extended mind people are right, both are failures to retrieve information from a stored record, and the fact that one record is encoded in neurons and the other in paper is a difference of substrate, not a difference in cognitive kind. So I want to know whether this asymmetry in ordinary judgment can be defended. Is there a principle that would justify treating a retrieval failure from a neural store differently from a retrieval failure from a paper store, even after we grant that the paper store does the same cognitive work? If not, which should go: the asymmetry, or the extended mind thesis? Or is the asymmetry just a psychological habit we should notice and try to correct? I'd like a careful answer in continuous prose — the way you'd write a letter — with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
