I am a novelist writing a book about a public sculpture that divides a neighbourhood, and I have been reading Hume's essay Of the Standard of Taste. As I understand him, Hume grants that taste can feel subjective but denies that all judgements of art are on a par: he claims that a sufficiently practised, widely comparative, and unprejudiced critic judges more soundly than a novice, and that the joint verdict of such critics supplies the true standard. Before inventing my fictional controversy, I want to scrutinise that doctrine against a real one: Richard Serra's Tilted Arc, a 120-foot, slightly tilted wall of rusted steel installed in Federal Plaza in Manhattan in 1981 under the General Services Administration's Art-in-Architecture programme. Workers in the surrounding federal buildings mostly detested it, saying it bisected the plaza, blocked their paths, and offered nowhere to sit; much of the New York art world defended it as a major work. At a public hearing in March 1985 more people spoke for keeping the piece than for removing it, but a jury voted four to one to remove it, and the GSA dismantled it in 1989. Serra refused relocation, insisting the work was site-specific and that removing it destroyed it.

Analyse the Tilted Arc episode through Hume's standard of taste. Begin with a disciplined mapping: who in the episode could plausibly count as a Humean qualified judge, who could not, which of the qualifying attributes each camp had or lacked, and what the theory would have said should be done. Then give me your considered verdict on where the theory is genuinely illuminating and where it is silent or strained — in particular whether Hume's standard of taste is even designed to settle disputes about public art, where a work must be lived with and navigated rather than merely contemplated. Does the episode confirm the theory, refute it, or expose a case it never anticipated? I would rather you commit to a definite reading and defend it than lay out the options and leave me to choose.
