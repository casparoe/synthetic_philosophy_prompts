In *Holy Trinity Church v. United States*, 143 U.S. 457 (1892), the Supreme Court considered a federal statute that made it unlawful to assist the importation of aliens under a contract to perform "labor or service of any kind." The Court conceded that the church's hiring of a minister from England fell within the letter of the prohibition, but Justice Brewer, writing for the Court, reasoned:

"While there is great force to this reasoning, we cannot think congress intended to denounce with penalties a transaction like that in the present case. … This is not the substitution of the will of the judge for that of the legislator; for frequently words of general meaning are used in a statute, words broad enough to include an act in question, and yet a consideration of the whole legislation, or of the circumstances surrounding its enactment, or of the absurd results which follow from giving such broad meaning to the words, makes it unreasonable to believe that the legislator intended to include the particular act."

Here is the asymmetry I want to understand. Legal practice is generally committed to the view that when a statute's words plainly apply, a court must apply them even when the outcome is harsh, unwise, or contrary to what the judge regards as good policy. But "absurd results" are treated as a special reason for departing from the literal text. What is it about absurdity, as opposed to mere bad policy or serious injustice, that justifies this exceptional treatment? Is the absurdity exception an internal and principled part of a textualist or intentionalist picture, or is it an ad hoc safety valve that undermines the discipline it is supposed to preserve?
