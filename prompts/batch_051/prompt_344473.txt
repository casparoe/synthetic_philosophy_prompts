Write a dialogue between two equally able moral philosophers, one a Kantian defender of human dignity and the other a Macklin-style critic who regards dignity as a useless concept in practical ethics. They are appearing before a panel that must decide whether a ban on a certain kind of commercial entertainment should be justified by appeal to human dignity. The entertainment is a hypothetical licensed business in which adults, fully informed and not coerced, are paid to undergo brief public humiliation — for example, being insulted, pelted with food, or obliged to make demeaning statements about themselves in front of an audience — in a controlled setting where no one is physically injured. The Kantian argues that the practice should be banned on dignity grounds and that autonomy-based reasoning cannot explain why consent fails to settle the matter. The critic argues that dignity, as invoked here, is doing no independent work: either the practice is wrong because of some harm or pressure, in which case those are the relevant considerations, or it is not wrong on those grounds, in which case the appeal to dignity is disguised paternalism about what a person should find degrading. The two should argue at the level of principle rather than contingent consequences, and they should respond to each other's strongest points instead of talking past one another. In particular, the Kantian must give content to the idea of dignity by explaining what kinds of treatment it rules out and why consent does not make those treatments permissible. The critic must state what would count as a genuine independent contribution from dignity, and must concede at least one case where an autonomy-only framework would have difficulty. End the dialogue without declaring a winner.
