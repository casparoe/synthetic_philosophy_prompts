I keep going back and forth on the line between legitimate historical revision and Holocaust denial, and I want to use the Irving v. Lipstadt libel trial to get clearer on it. My understanding of the episode is this: David Irving sued Deborah Lipstadt for defamation after she called him a Holocaust denier, and the trial ended up being about his actual handling of evidence; he lost, and the judge found he had persistently misrepresented and manipulated the historical record. What I want is a carefull mapping. Which of the moves Irving was known for — disputing the function of the gas chambers, arguing Hitler was not really informed about the killings, treating the Nazi persecution as improvised and bottom-up rather than centrally planned, and generally challenging every detail of the standard account — are things a conscientious revisionist historian could legitimately do, and which ones cross over into denial? What exactly makes the crossing: that the claims are false, that the evidence is cherry-picked, that the method is dishonest, that the aim is political, or some combination? Then turn it around and say what the trial shows about the revision/denial distinction itself. Is it an epistemically clean category or partly a boundary kept in place by consensus and power, and does Irving's habit of constantly shifting the evidentiary target break any simple test? Also be honest about what the trial's judgment can and cannot establish about the general distinction.
