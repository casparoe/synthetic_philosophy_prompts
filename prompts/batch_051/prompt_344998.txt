I've been going in circles about something and I'd like an actual philosopher of law to walk me through it carefully. The other day I was doing the paperwork to form an LLC with my brother (we're starting a small landscaping buisness), and it suddenly struck me how strange it is that the state is about to treat this stack of signed forms as a kind of person. The company will be able to own a truck, sign contracts, take out a loan, and sue or be sued in its own name. If it breaks the law, the company itself can be found guilty and fined. But the same legal system that treats it as a person for all those purposes won't let it vote, get married, serve on a jury, or be sent to prison. So it's a person when that's useful and not a person when being a person would mean having a body or a conscience.

What I want to know is what this whole practice actually presupposes about what a legal "person" is, and about what law-made things are in general. Is the law just pretending, and if so why doesn't the pretending cause contradictions? Or is the company a real thing that the law brings into existence, with only the rights the law decides to staple onto it? Reconstruct the implicit theory of legal personhood that keeps this practice from being absurd — if you think it is a coherent theory — and say what that theory quietly rules out. I also want you to be careful about overstating it: distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and point to any places where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that more than one theory would license what courts actually do. Finally, tell me what would have to change in everyday legal practice if we replaced the underlying view with the idea that corporations aren't entities at all but just shorthand for the human beings behind them (so a "contract with the company" was really a contract with a list of people).

Please act as an expert in philosophy of law, and before you settle on your answer, brainstorm two or three different ways the practice could be made sense of, then tell me which one you think is most defensible and why. Think slowly and don't dodge the weird bits.
