A private members' lending library has operated for forty years under a single borrowing rule: 'A member may have no more than three items on loan at any one time.' Until now every holding was a single physical volume, and the rule was enforced by counting books at the desk. The library has just received a boxed set of six novels in a slipcase, catalogued as one holding under a single number. A member who already has two books on loan asks to borrow the boxed set. The librarian says this would put fourteen items on his account; the member says it would put three, because the boxed set is one item in the catalogue. The drafter of the rule, still alive, says her worry was keeping enough copies in circulation for other members, and that she never thought about boxed sets.

Before anyone makes a further decision, is there a fact about the rule that determines which of these two extensions is correct, or does the rule fail to determine the case until the club chooses a reading? One member argues that the rule only means what the practice makes it mean, so the club must now settle it; another argues that 'on loan' already meant something fully determinate when the rule was passed, even though no one had then imagined boxed sets. I am interested in the general problem this raises about how a rule can govern a case its authors never contemplated, not just in the library's immediate policy. Say which considerations could bind future readers of the rule and why. Please answer in British English.
