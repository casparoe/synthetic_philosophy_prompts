Over the past decade and a half I have followed the dispute about whether the world is becoming less violent, most visibly associated with Steven Pinker, and I have come to suspect that the participants are not all answering the same question. One side counts battle deaths and homicide, usually as a share of population, and reports a steep fall from a violent pre-state baseline to the present, with homicide rates in Europe declining across the centuries and interstate war nearly disappearing. The other side replies that the pre-state baseline is built on small, contested samples; that the twentieth century was nevertheless the bloodiest in absolute numbers, and absolute numbers are what matter; that state killing, famine, and structural harms are quietly left out of the dataset; and that what the charts really show is a change in how violence is organized and who counts it, not a reduction in it. The same objections and the same charts have been traded for well over a decade with little visible movement on either side, and I doubt the impasse is merely a lack of data.

My working hypothesis is that the underlying dispute is only partly empirical. Beneath it are a definitional question about what should count as violence at all; a conventional choice between per capita and absolute measures, each with a normative judgment built into it; and, at the bottom, a disagreement about whether the question has a determinate answer once those choices are made. I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement itself—not a survey of the literature and not a verdict on whether the decline is real. Please separate the substantive empirical components from the verbal, conventional, and normative ones; say where my hypothesis is too coarse; and say what would actually count as progress given the diagnosis, or argue that progress should not be expected.
