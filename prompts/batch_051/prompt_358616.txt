I'm on the science-and-ideas beat, so I might spend Tuesday reporting on a fake surgeon who practiced for years without a license, and Wednesday sitting in on a citizens' assembly that's supposed to advise on nuclear waste siting or pandemic restrictions. At some point those two folders started talking to each other in my head. We would never let an unlicensed person rewire a house, prescribe chemo, or land a passenger plane — that's basic protection from incompetence. But when it comes to deciding whether to build a nuclear plant, run a deficit, or mandate a vaccine, we let everyone over 18 vote for the officials who decide, and we call that democracy. I find myself holding both attitudes at once and I honestly cant tell whether theres a principle separating them or whether I'd just like a voting license for people who agree with me. I don't want a voting license! But I do want to know whether the asymmetry is actually defensible.

So: what is the strongest candidate justification for treating expert practice and democratic decision-making differently, and does it survive contact with nearby cases — juries, where laypeople decide guilt; referendums, where laypeople decide law directly; driver's exams for adults; the old literacy tests for voting; compulsory school boards? Tell me whether you would keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or give it up, and if you'd keep it, say where the line really is. I'm less interested in the usual tribal shouting around this than in knowing exactly which step of the standard justifications is carrying the weight, and whether any step is load-bearing enough to survive the nearby cases.
