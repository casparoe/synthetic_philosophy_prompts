After forty years doing physical chemistry (statistical mechanics, mostly), I've fallen hard for philosophy of physics, and a university reading group nearby has asked me to present. My claim is basically this: the thermodynamic arrow of time cant tell us which direction time flows. I want to argue that the low-entropy past boundary condition — the "past hypothesis" idea — is doing all the real work, and that entropy increase just lets us label one direction as "toward the future" after we've already gotten a before/after distinction from that boundary condition. I also want to press a double-standard point: people who wouldn't tolerate goal-directed talk anywhere else in physics are strangely relaxed about one special boundary condition doing what looks like teleological work in statistical mechanics.

My argument sketch, for context: take a trajectory of a box of gas. The sequence of macrostates and their entropy values can be read in either direction; nothing about the values themselves says which reading is the true one. It's only because one end of the sequence is stipulated (by the boundary condition) to be the low-entropy end that we get to call the other direction "the future." So the arrow isn't supplied by entropy; it's supplied by the boundary condition, and entropy is just the label.

The audience: a philosophy of physics reading group, maybe ten regulars. Two of them work on the arrow of time proper — one wrote her dissertation on Boltzmann and is very attached to the past hypothesis as a lawlike posit; the other is a Boltzmann-brain skeptic who worries that without a smooth, normal initial condition entropy alone gives you nonsense about typical observers. There's a cosmologist who thinks the expansion of the universe is the more fundamental arrow and that thermodynamic considerations are provincial. The rest are sharp grad students, some agnostic, one who has read a lot of Huw Price and will want to know why I'm not just saying the arrow is a temporal double standard all the way down.

What I want is a prep sheet, not a defense of the view. Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll actually come, phrased as those two specialists and the cosmologist would phrase them, with how serious each objection is, what I should concede on the spot, what I should hold, and where each of my replies is weakest. I especially need the objection I'm secretly hoping nobody asks, because I haven't got a clean answer to it.

Use these sections in this order, roughly the word counts:

1. First objections from the room (250 words) — the fair, generic challenges and quick replies.
2. The two time-arrow specialists (450 words) — their likely objections, phrased in their voices, ordered by likelihood and damage.
3. The cosmologist's wedge (200 words) — the expansion-arrow challenge and how to keep it from splitting the room.
4. What to concede and what to hold (250 words) — the line-by-line negotiating position.
5. The question I hope nobody asks (150 words) — what it is, why it would hurt, and the least-bad thing to say back.
