I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on the concept of death, and I keep returning to a puzzle about the brain-death debate. Since the Harvard committee's 1968 report, the legal and medical consensus in many countries has shifted from circulatory-respiratory criteria to neurological ones, yet a persistent minority of clinicians and philosophers has objected for just as long, and the arguments now feel entirely entrenched. Defenders of the neurological criterion argue that the irreversible destruction of the brain ends consciousness and destroys the organism's integrative unity — though they themselves split between whole-brain and higher-brain formulations — while critics reply that brain-dead bodies remain warm, heal wounds, exhibit spinal reflexes, and have even been maintained for weeks to gestate pregnancies, and that the criterion's historical success owed more to the demand for transplantable organs than to any new biological discovery. The physiology is largely agreed; what is not agreed is which loss makes a human being dead.

My working hypothesis is that the two camps are not really arguing about the same thing under the single name 'death': one side is tracking the end of the person, another the end of biological integration, another the irreversible loss of circulatory function, and the term is being asked to serve at once as a biological description, an ethical threshold for burial and organ retrieval, and a marker of when the patient is gone. I would like a diagnosis of the structure of this impasse: is it a verbal disagreement, a normative disagreement, a hidden empirical disagreement, or a question that is simply ill-posed? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a conceptual distinction, a better-framed question, an empirical result, a change in which cases are treated as data, or no progress at all?
