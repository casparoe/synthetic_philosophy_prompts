I've been rereading Oscar Wilde's "The Decay of Lying" after a discussion on a rationalist forum about whether we can judge a work of art separately from the artist's moral life. A friend there pressed an analogy that still bothers me. He pointed to the way Wilde's Vivian treats Nature's defects—not as reasons to condemn landscape painting, but as the very conditions that make art necessary. In the dialogue, Vivian says:

What Art really reveals to us is Nature’s lack of design, her curious crudities, her extraordinary monotony, her absolutely unfinished condition. Nature has good intentions, of course, but, as Aristotle once said, she cannot carry them out. When I look at a landscape I cannot help seeing all its defects. It is fortunate for us, however, that Nature is so imperfect, as otherwise we should have no art at all. Art is our spirited protest, our gallant attempt to teach Nature her proper place.

My friend's analogy was: the immoral artist's biography is like Nature's defects; judging the painting by the artist's crimes is like judging a landscape by Wilde's complaints about damp grass and insects. The defects are external to the work; the work is a "spirited protest" against exactly such external demands. Therefore, we should fully separate the work from the artist. I'd like you to audit this analogy: what maps onto what, where the mapping breaks, what it quietly imports about the autonomy of art and about moral evaluation, and whether the conclusion—that we should ignore the artist's immorality when evaluating the work—survives once the disanalogies are on the table.
