I'm in this economics-philosophy society at sixth form and next week I've got to give a short talk arguing that our school should change how it marks big group projects. At the moment everyone in a group gets the same mark, which means the same two people end up doing all the work while everyone else freerides, and then everyone acts surprised when it goes wrong. My proposal is basically to treat the project as a little commons: keep the shared grade, but build in the kind of rules Elinor Ostrom describes for real common-pool resources — a clear boundary for who counts as in the group, monitoring done by the members themselves, graduated consequenses for slacking instead of one big punishment at the end, and letting each group vote on its own rules before the work starts rather than the teacher imposing the same ones on everyone.

Problem is the audience. It'll be the society plus some teachers sitting in. There's a loud libertarian-ish group of kids who reckon any shared grade is unfair to the people who work hard, and that free-rider problems sort themselves out if you just let people choose their own groups. Then there are a couple of teachers who think the only point of school assesment is individual accountability, and that a group mark measures basically nothing real about any one pupil. I need a prep sheet: what objections am I actually going to get, in roughly the order they'll come, how those people would actually phrase them, which ones have real force and what I should concede upfront, and which one reply has to land for the whole proposal to survive. Also tell me the question I'm secretly hoping nobody asks.

Don't turn it into a bibliography or an essay that just summarises Ostrom, and please write the answer in British English.
