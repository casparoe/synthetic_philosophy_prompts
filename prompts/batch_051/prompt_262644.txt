I'm a gameplay engineer at a studio building a squad-based tactical shooter set in a real conflict within living memory. Every sprint, I sit in a feature-review meeting where a designer proposes a mechanic — a scoring rule, a cosmetic unlock, a penalty, a cinematic, an emote — and someone asks whether it glorifies the war or makes something that should stay serious feel like a theme park. Right now we decide by group intuition, and it's inconsistent: the same idea gets approved in one meeting and vetoed in the next. These decisions ship to millions of players, including people with direct experience of the conflict, so I want to replace intuition with a reusable procedure for one specific question: does this feature glorify the war or undermine the seriousness of its subject matter, and in what way?

Please write this as a decision procedure a small team of engineers and designers with no formal philosophy background can run in under an hour, with a design director and a narrative lead signing off. For each step, give: the question in plain language; what counts as a sufficient answer; how we record it; and whether the step stops with a "no" or hands the decision to a named person because the call is about values rather than facts. Flag the places where judgment cannot be eliminated and the places where the procedure is most likely to misfire. I'd like it grounded in the best available thinking about war games, glorification, and the ethics of fun rather than studio habit, but translated into tests we can actually apply without learning a philosophical vocabulary.

Then run the procedure on three features: (a) a killstreak reward that grants a temporary mechanical bonus after three kills in quick succession; (b) a medal system that recreates real historical engagements and scores the player's performance against the actual historical outcome; and (c) a civilian-casualty rule that withholds rewards when a civilian is killed, but only in cases where the system can tell the player could have avoided it. Make sure at least one of these returns an answer we will not like, and say explicitly when that happens. End by saying what the procedure cannot do — which questions it cannot settle and what we should not pretend it settles.

Finally, because we will actually ship based on this: tell me where your reasoning is most likely to fail, and what argument or counterexample would lead you to revise the procedure — not just hard edge cases, but which step might be systematically wrong.
