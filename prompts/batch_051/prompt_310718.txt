Next month I am giving a talk at a workshop on formal epistemology, with an audience that is roughly half formal epistemologists and half statisticians who work on model selection. Almost everyone there is a Bayesian of some kind, and a vocal minority regards inference to the best explanation as at best a story we tell after the real statistical work is done. I am defending a deliberately restricted version of inference to the best explanation. My thesis is this: when two models fit the observed data exactly equally well, so that their likelihoods are identical, a model may still receive a modest evidential boost from being the better explanation, provided the sense of better explanation is fixed in advance by an explicit list of explanatory virtues — simplicity, unification, and mechanistic precision — and provided the claim is only that rational posteriors need not be equal across the two models. I deny that this commits me to saying the better explanation is true, and I deny that the boost is merely pragmatic or aesthetic.

My argument in the talk has three steps. First, there are standard model-selection situations in which likelihoods are exactly tied and the only available discriminator is explanatory virtue. Second, a Bayesian who says the virtue was already in the prior must be able to specify that prior by a method that does not simply re-describe the conclusion; otherwise the claim that it was in the prior is empty. Third, if explanatory considerations legitimately shape the prior, then they have evidential force downstream, which is all my thesis requires.

Prepare me for the question period. I want a prep sheet, not a rehearsal of the talk. Give me the six to eight objections I will actually get from this particular audience, phrased the way a formal epistemologist or statistician would phrase them, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are. For each objection, give a two-minute spoken reply and a note on where that reply is weakest. I expect at least one objection about double-counting the evidence, one about the choice of virtues being arbitrary, one involving a simpler model that later predicts worse, and one about the gap between a method being useful and its being truth-conducive. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. Also mark the two places where I should make a concession in public, and show me how to concede without giving up the thesis.
