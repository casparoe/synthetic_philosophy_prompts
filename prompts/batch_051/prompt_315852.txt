I've been working through Ole Peters, "Evaluating gambles using dynamics," and there's a passage where he says:

"We argue that this null model is a priori a bad starting point because the expectation value of wealth does not generally reflect what happens over time. We propose a different null model of human behavior that eliminates, in many cases, the need for utility theory: an individual optimizes what happens to his wealth as time passes."

A friend then walked me through an argument that seems to prove too much, and I can't see which step fails. Here it is:

(1) For an individual facing a multiplicative gamble repeated many times, the relevant performance measure is the time-average growth rate, not the ensemble-average wealth.
(2) The time-average growth rate of a multiplicative gamble is the expected log return.
(3) So a gamble can have a positive expected wealth and a negative expected log return, meaning the ensemble average rises while the individual's wealth collapses over time.
(4) But since each actual person only lives one history, not an ensemble of histories, the time-average criterion is the correct one for individuals in general, not just for repeated multiplicative gambles.
(5) Using the time-average criterion is equivalent to maximising expected log wealth.
(6) Therefore risk aversion is not a psychological fact about diminishing marginal utility; it is just what time-average reasoning demands. Expected utility theory was mistaking a mathematical point about dynamics for a psychological one.

I half suspect step (4) is doing something too quick, and maybe step (5) smuggles log utility back in despite Peters saying no utility function is needed. But I can't say exactly where it goes wrong. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the argument is actually valid, then what should I make of Peters's claim that the time-average approach eliminates the need for utility theory?
