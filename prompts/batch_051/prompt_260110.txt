I am a second-year undergraduate reading law with philosophy, and I am serving as the student member of my college’s conduct committee. At this week’s meeting I must cast the deciding vote in an appeal that has left the rest of the committee evenly split. The dispute arises from last term’s college ball. Entry was gained by swiping a university card, and tickets were sold online in advance, but an error in the booking system means the door log records only which cards were swiped and never whether the card holder had bought a ticket. The college’s accounts show that 1,000 people were admitted and that 470 tickets were sold; the remaining 530 people appear to have entered without paying. A student whose card appears on the door log has appealed against a £40 fine for non-payment. He admits attending and says he paid, and neither side can produce a record connecting him individually to a paid or an unpaid booking. The committee’s standing rules say a fine may be imposed where it is more likely than not that the student failed to pay.

One committee member argues that the case is settled: for any admitted student, the chance that he did not pay is 53 per cent, so the standard is met. Another counters that this cannot be right, since the same reasoning would justify fining every single person who swiped in, even though we know that 470 of those people did pay and that we simply cannot tell which they are. The first member replies that the committee is not being asked to fine everyone, only to decide one case, and in this one case the odds tip against the student. I am taking a module on the theory of evidence this term, and the exchange has left me unsure: I can feel that the second member is onto something, but I do not know how to state it without giving up on probabilistic reasoning altogether. How should I vote, and is there a principled line between the evidence we have here and ordinary case-specific evidence that would leave me comfortable imposing the fine?
