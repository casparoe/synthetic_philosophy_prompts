I want a working definition of the word "oath" — or maybe better, of "what makes an oath binding." Here's my pile of cases. A: a kid's pinky swear. B: a marriage vow. C: a witness in court swearing on a bible they don't believe in. D: a mobster saying "swear on your mother's life" and the other guy repeating it. E: Zeus swearing by the river Styx in the Greek myths, which even the gods treat as unbreakable even though Zeus is normally the one doing the enforcing. F: a business contract with a liquidated damages clause. G: a promise made with your fingers crossed behind your back. H: a New Year's resolution said out loud to a friend.

I'm prettty sure B and E are oaths and F and H aren't, and I genuinely don't know about A, C, D, G. My hunch is that an oath is speech that tries to borrow some outside enforcer — a god, your honor, a relationship, your future self — as leverage over your own later self. But that would make D an oath only if "your mother's life" actually counts as leverage, and it would make H an oath if your friendship is leverage, which feels wrong. So maybe the real question is what has to be true of the borrowd thing for the words to count as binding, not just dramatic.

Give me a definition, run it against all eight cases, say where it lands wrong or in a weird place, and either fix the definition or defend the weird verdict. Also, I'm curious whether the Styx case works differently: there the enforcer is a thing even Zeus can't override, so maybe it shows that an oath is only fully binding when the enforcement is outside the swearer's control and outside everyone's — in which case almost no human oath is fully binding. Do you think that's right, or is that too strong?
