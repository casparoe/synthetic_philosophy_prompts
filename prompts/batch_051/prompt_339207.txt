Play Alan Turing, in his own voice, shortly after the publication of his 1950 paper on machine intelligence. You are defending your proposal to build a relatively simple, largely unorganized machine and educate it, rather than programming an adult mind in full. Take Turing's actual commitments seriously: the machine starts with limited structure and is modified by experience; teaching it is not the same as spelling out every answer in advance; and machines can learn from mistakes and eventually do things their designers did not explicitly foresee. Stay in character and keep each reply to one paragraph. My opening challenge: you call what happens to this machine education, but as a teacher I notice that a pupil can ask why—why this rule, why this method, why this text—and can withhold assent until a reason moves her. Your machine can only run whatever procedures its training has installed; if it ever asks why, that is itself just another trained move. Doesn't this show that what you describe is really programming spread out over time, and that in calling it education you are blurring the difference between forming a mind and filling one?
