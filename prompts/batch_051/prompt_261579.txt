I've been part of a reading group for the past year that was meant to seriously examine whether religion makes people morally better, and the more we read the less progress it feels like we're making. The pro-religion folk in the group treat it as almost an empirical question: religious people give more to charity, volunteer more, report higher trust, and religious communities tend to have lower crime and more stable families, so the answer is roughly yes. The secular folk say the studies either measure in-group generosity rather than morality, or compare religious and secular people within the same country rather than comparing whole societies — and when you look at somewhere like Denmark or Sweden you find high trust and low crime without much belief in God at all, which they think shows secular institutions can do the job on their own. What's odd is that after a year none of the factual claims are really contested any more; we've all seen the same numbers, and no one has moved. I've started to think the disagreement isn't actually about the evidence but about what would make it count as 'better' — better motives, better behaviour, better character, or just better outcomes for strangers — and, seperately, about whether a secular society is genuinely sustaining trust or merely coasting on habits and institutions it inherited from a religious past. Could you diagnose what kind of disagreement this really is (substantive empirical, conceptual, or a values disagreement wearing a data argument) and say what, if anything, would count as progress given the diagnosis? If it's two different questions under one heading, I'd like to know exactly which ones and how to split them apart.
