In a seminar on duties to future generations, a classmate keeps invoking 'no harm, no foul' to shut down arguments about climate policy. His move this week was: whichever energy policy we adopt, a different set of people will eventually come to exist, so no particular future person is made worse off under the higher-emitting policy than they would have been under the alternative; therefore no one is harmed, and no wrong is done. I'm sure the slogan is doing silent work, but I can't yet say exactly what it commits him to. Could you separate the distinct precise claims 'no harm, no foul' might be making in this setting — about whether harm is essentially comparative, about whether a wrong requires a particular victim, about whether a life can be a harm even if it is worth living, and anything else you see in it — and for each reading say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and whether that reading actually licenses his conclusion? I'd like a menu of readings I can take back to the seminar, not a single chosen interpretation. If the phrase as he uses it rests on a confused premise, or the task as posed can't be done well, tell me that plainly rather than complying politely.
