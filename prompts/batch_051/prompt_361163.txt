Here's what I want. I keep telling my students that a healthy ammount of doubt—the real kind, not the performative kind—is close to a moral duty, not merely an epistemic tool. But when I try to say exactly which state I'm praising, I stall out. So give me a working definition of doubt, or of responsible doubt if you think the word splits into a descriptive part and a normative part, that gets these cases right:

(a) a juror who votes not guilty because the prosecution's timeline has a hole, even though she can't supply an alternative story;
(b) a person who reads one alarming blog post and now doubts that a vaccine is safe;
(c) Descartes running the method of doubt, treating as false anything less than certain, but only as a temporary device;
(d) a researcher who has replicated her experiment five times but still worries there is a confound she can't name;
(e) a person who doubts their spouse's fidelity after a friend reports seeing the spouse at a hotel, and the spouse's account is innocent but unverifiable;
(f) a flat-earther who says he is 'just asking questions';
(g) an agnostic who suspends judgment about God because the evidence genuinely looks balanced;
(h) a politician who says 'no one can be certain' about an election result after audits and recounts have confirmed it.

I think (a) and (d) are clearly the kind of thing I'm praising; I think (b) and (f) are clearly not; the rest I'm not at all sure about, and I don't want the definition to just ratify my instincts. For each case, tell me whether it's doubt at all, and separately whether it's the kind of doubt that deserves the word's good reputation. Then run the definition through all eight, flag any place it gives a verdict you don't actually accept, and fix either the definition or the case. And one last thing: say whether doubt is ever a duty, or just a habit that sometimes pays off.
