Me and a friend have been going around in circles about the counterfactual mugging, and I want to see what happens when the one weird part is replaced with something ordinary. The original: Omega flips a fair coin; if it lands heads, Omega asks you for $100; if tails, Omega gives you $10,000 iff you would have paid on heads. The usual verdict is that causal and evidential decision theory both refuse when asked, while updateless decision theory pays, because UDT treats the choice as picking your policy before you've learned which observation you're in. Fine.

Now vary it like this. No Omega. Instead, a researcher who knows you about as well as a close friend — say she's right about what people like you will do around 70% of the time, and you know this, but she isn't infallible — makes a prediction before the coin is tossed about whether you would pay if asked. If the coin lands tails, she pays you $10,000 iff that prediction was "would pay." If heads, she just asks you, straight up, for $100. The question: does UDT still pay, and at exactly what accuracy does it stop paying? Run the arithmetic for causal, evidential, and updateless decision theory in the heads choice, and say wether CDT and EDT still agree with each other here or wether the noise pulls them apart. Then make the predictor's correctness asymmetric: she's better at spotting people who would pay than people who wouldn't (or pick the other direction, but pick one and defend it). Does the threshold change, and does anything weird happen when one error rate is zero and the other is high?

I want committed answers, not a menu: fix specific numbers, show the calculations, and say which parameter is really doing the work. Keep it under 800 words.
