Ok, I'm a software engineer and I recently fell down a rabbit hole reading about the classic theories of humour — superiority, incongruity, relief, benign violation — and I've ended up with a set of views that all feel right to me individually, but I can't tell whether they're an actual position or just me bolting four theories together and refusing to commit. Here's where I've landed:

(1) The thing that actually does the work in a joke is incongruity plus resolution. A setup builds an expectation, the punchline violates it, and then the violation gets reinterpreted so that it makes sense after all. The laugh comes from that resolution, not from the surprise by itself.

(2) But incongruity alone obviously isnt enough, because lots of incongruities are not funny at all; if the violation is too real or too threatening, it stops being funny. So I accept the benign violation idea as a constraint: something is funny when it's a violation but still safe or playful enough.

(3) Then again, some things seem funny with no reinterpretation going on that I can see. Nervous laughter after a near-miss, the giggle fit after a jump scare, laughing at a really dark joke right after a funeral. That feels like built-up tension getting released — relief theory — and I don't see an incongruity being resolved there.

(4) And I also can't shake that a large chunk of comedy is just mean. Fail videos, roasts, someone walking into a glass door in an office: the laughter seems to depend on feeling like you're doing better than the target, even for a second. It would feel wrong to leave superiority theory out.

(5) But here's the thing. I want the final story to be one underlying mechanism with a couple of necessary conditions, not four separate gadgets wired together with a disjunction. Funny feels like one thing evolution built. If the best answer is just that different jokes are funny for different reasons and you need all four, that feels like giving up too early — though maybe that's just how it is.

So my question is: is this package consistent? Don't lecture me on the history of the theories; I want an audit. Which commitments are actually pulling against each other, which pairs only look like they conflict, how severe are the tensions, and what would the cheapest repair cost. I don't know which one I'd give up, so don't assume I'd drop one in particular.
