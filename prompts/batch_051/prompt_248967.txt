I cover science and ideas, and I keep running into a term that people use with confidence but rarely define: complexity. In ordinary speech we call both a shuffled deck of cards and a bacterium complex, but they seem complex in different ways, and a sand dune looks intricate without obviously having been built by anything. Lately I have been reading the assembly-theoretic approach, which proposes that an object is complex to the degree that many distinct joining operations are needed to build it from elementary parts, with already-constructed subassemblies counted once. I would like a working definition of what makes an object complex — not merely disordered or statistically unusual — that gets the following cases right: a quartz crystal; a sand dune; a shuffled deck of cards; an E. coli cell; a pair of scissors; a pile of the same scissors' parts, disassembled; a snowflake; a Mondrian painting; a long DNA sequence of alternating AT repeats; and the text of a novel. My provisional instincts are that the crystal, the dune, and the shuffled deck are not complex in the relevant sense; the bacterium and the scissors are; the disassembled pile should come out less complex than the assembled scissors; and I genuinely do not know where the snowflake, the painting, and the repetitive DNA sequence should fall. Propose a definition, run it through all ten cases, note where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and either revise it or defend the surprising verdicts.
