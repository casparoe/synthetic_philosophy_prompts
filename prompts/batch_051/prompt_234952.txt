My friend Dana has gotten really into effective altruism over the last year or so, and its starting to cause friction between us. Last week I mentioned I was saving up for a new laptop, and she basically said that if I buy it, I'm deciding that my having a nicer screen matters more to me than a child not dying of malaria. She wasn't being mean about it — she said she doesn't want me to feel guilty about every single purchase, but that the logic doesn't care about our feelings: the money I'd spend is enough to buy a bunch of bed nets, and if I really believed a child's death is a tragedy, I couldn't enjoy the laptop knowing what I gave up to get it. She also said the only honest response is to give a much larger chunk of my income than I do now, maybe even most of it.

I tried to push back. I said there's a difference between actively harming someone and just not helping them, and that if I followed her reasoning all the way I'd never be allowed to buy anything nice ever again, including the coffee I bought us that day. She had replies to all of it — she said the child doesn't care about the causal route, and that "never buy anything nice again" is only a problem if I'm secretly assuming my comfort is worth more than a life. I couldn't tell wether she was right or just better at arguing. So I guess my question is: is her argument actually fair? Is she making a mistake somewhere, or is the real problem in her underlying moral view, or am I just being defensive? I'd like to know what a serious answer to her would look like, not just a way to win the argument.
