For decades, philosophers of biology have circled around the propensity interpretation of fitness and the tautology objection without any obvious convergence. The objection, in its simplest form, is that if fitness is identified with actual survival and reproductive success, then natural selection says only that the organisms which survive and reproduce are the ones which survive and reproduce. Defenders of evolutionary theory reply that fitness should be understood instead as a probabilistic disposition: a propensity to leave a certain expected number of offspring across a range of possible environments and chance histories. On this view, a fitter organism can leave fewer descendants than a less fit one through bad luck, so statements about fitness have real content and can fail to match outcomes.

The standard story is that this move settles the tautology objection. It plainly has not settled the underlying dispute. The critic then answers that propensities are never observed directly and are in practice inferred from the very survival and reproductive outcomes they were introduced to explain, so the circularity has been moved back a step rather than removed. The defender answers that this is no worse than other theoretical quantities in science — temperature and mass are also inferred from their effects — and that a disposition can be measured fallibly through its manifestations without being reduced to them. At that point the exchange usually stops, with both sides claiming the other has missed the point.

I suspect this is not one disagreement but several bundled together. There seems to be a logical worry about whether selection theory has empirical content; a methodological worry about whether fitness can be measured independently of the success it is meant to explain; and a metaphysical worry about whether a propensity is a real cause of evolutionary change or only a statistical summary of outcomes. My hypothesis is that the debate stalls because participants slide among these three questions while using the single label of the tautology objection, so an answer to one is treated as if it had failed to answer another.

I would like that hypothesis tested rather than a survey of the literature. What kind of disagreement is this, at bottom — verbal, methodological, metaphysical, or irreducibly mixed — and why has it resisted resolution? Give an honest answer about whether anything could count as progress: a formal result about how selection and drift are decomposed, a measurement procedure independent of reproductive outcomes, a distinction between fitness as a model parameter and fitness as a property of organisms, or a clearer account of what biological explanation is expected to do. Or, if no resolution should be expected, say why and what that would tell us about the original question.
