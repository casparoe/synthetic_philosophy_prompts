Recently I became responsible for a collection of antique glass instruments, and decisions about packing, insurance, and restoration now turn on the word 'fragile.' I thought I knew what it meant, but several cases make the obvious definition — fragile things are things that would break if struck — look too simple. I want a working definition of 'fragile' (or, if the account requires it, of 'x is disposed to M in circumstances C,' with fragility as the test case) that gets the following cases right without ad hoc patching.

1. A hand-blown glass flask dropped onto a stone floor; it shatters. I take this to be a paradigm case of fragility.
2. A mild-steel girder struck with a comparable impact; it suffers no more than a dent. I take this to be a paradigm non-case.
3. The same kind of flask wrapped in energy-absorbing foam, then dropped the same way; it survives. I still want to say the flask is fragile and that the foam merely masks its fragility, even though the counterfactual 'if struck, it would break' is now false.
4. A fragile glass vial is connected to a perfectly reliable device that, on detecting an incoming strike a moment before contact, switches the vial into a flexible, non-breakable phase; the vial is therefore never struck while in its fragile phase. I gather this is the sort of case called a fink. Is the vial fragile while the device is armed?
5. A person with a severe allergy wears an implanted device that automatically and infallibly blocks the reaction before it becomes symptomatic. After every exposure, nothing happens. Is the person still disposed to the reaction — still, in the ordinary sense, at risk of it? This is the case that made the question personal for me; I am not asking for medical advice, only for an analysis of the concept.
6. Suppose a pane of glass is highly resistant to a broad, flat-faced blow but shatters under a sharp point impact of the same energy. Is it fragile simpliciter, or must the definition make fragility relative to a type of impact?

I am confident about cases 1 and 2, fairly confident that 3 is fragile, and unsure about 4, 5, and 6. Propose the definition, run it through all six cases, say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and then either revise it or defend the surprising verdicts. I also want a clear statement of how your definition distinguishes a fink from a mask, and which of the two — or something else — case 5 turns out to be.
