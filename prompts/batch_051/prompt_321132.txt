I'm putting together a syllabus for a small seminar on temperance and self-control, and I want to spend one session on The Picture of Dorian Gray. The problem I keep running into is that people read it as a flat morality tale—Dorian indulges, so he rots, so temperance is good—and that feels like a cop-out. I want the students to work through three different pictures of what self-control even is in the novel. The first is the virtue-theoretic one: temperance is a settled disposition to feel and want the right things to the right degree, not just restraint or white-knuckling. The second is the prudential one: self-control is just long-term self-management, hiding or delaying gratification so you don't get caught and so your future pleasures aren't ruined. The third is the repression one, in a loose Nietzsche/Freud spirit: trying to silence or quarantine a desire doesn't end it, it pushes it somewhere it can go toxic. The portrait seems to literalize that third idea—Dorian doesn't discipline his desires, he outsources their consequences and keeps his public surface clean, and the painting is where the suppressed material piles up.

Could you read the novel through those three lenses for me? I want to know where each one actually fits what happens in the text and where it strains, and especially whether the ending—Dorian stabbing the painting, the servants finding the portrait restored and an old, withered body on the floor—vindicates any one of them or undercuts all three. A colleague keeps telling me the book is basically anti-repression propaganda and that Basil's moralizing is just prudence wearing a virtue mask; I'm not convinced the novel is that neat. Also, is the picture best thought of as a fantasy of indulgence without weakness of will, a kind of technology for self-control that fails because the underlying desires are left untouched? One more instruction: please separate what is plainly in the novel from what is genuinely contested among people who study Wilde or the philosophy of self-control, and don't present a contested reading as the consensus. I'd rather have a reading that is honest about the disagreements than a tidy one.
