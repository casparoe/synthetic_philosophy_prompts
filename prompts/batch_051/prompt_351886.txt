I am a novelist at work on a book about a family in the year after the death of its matriarch. My plan is to give each of the six point-of-view characters a genuinely distinct philosophical stance toward death, not merely a different emotional temperature. I have been reading the Zhuangzi, and I want the characters to occupy different cells in the logical space that text opens up, as well as the positions it refuses. But I do not want the result to depend on anyone recognizing the source, and I want to be able to test the thing on beta readers who have never read a line of Chinese philosophy. So I need an instrument that sorts people by what they actually believe about death, not by which label they recognize or which term of art they would reach for.

Please write the instrument as a decision tree: a single starting question, something like whether a dead person has really come to an end, and then no more than ten branch nodes leading to six to eight leaves. Each node should ask about a concrete case or claim regarding death — whether grief is the fitting response at a funeral, whether a terminal diagnosis is bad news for the one who receives it, what a child should be told about where the dead go, whether death is like the change of seasons — with answer options phrased so that an untutored reader can answer without knowing which position they are walking toward. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names: settle what the questions are and which combinations of answers are internally possible, then locate the actual positions in that space, and for any combination with no occupant say whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. The leaves should include at least these four: the ordinary mourner's view that death is a real loss and grief is fitting; the transformation view that death is one change in an ongoing process and that equanimity replaces grief once this is seen; the parity view that life and death are one and the preference for life over death is itself the confusion; and the reversal view that once we stop assuming life is better, death may be better, or at least that there is no ground for the ranking. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot actually separate rather than papering the difference over.

I do not need citations, chapter titles, dates, quotations, or a bibliography; where the Zhuangzi is invoked, paraphrase the relevant ideas. The wording of the nodes and options should avoid terms of art from Chinese philosophy, since the tree must work for people who do not know them. What I need from you is the logical architecture: which distinctions have to come first because later answers presuppose them, where the four positions I named really diverge, and which apparent differences between characters would be merely cosmetic.
