I am trying to work out whether compatriots have special claims on me or on my state: whether someone's sharing my national citizenship, as such, gives me or our shared institutions stronger reason to benefit them than a similarly situated non-citizen. By "compatriot" I mean co-citizen only, not shared culture, ethnicity, or language. I am less interested in the standard labels than in the underlying principle, and I have a rough hunch I want you to test and repair or replace. The hunch is that the special claim, where it exists, tracks ongoing shared subjection to a common political system and participation in its benefits, not nationality or culture as such. But the hunch seems to overgenerate in some of the cases below and undergenerate in at least one, so I suspect it is more a pile of ingredients than a principle.

Here are my verdicts on cases. In each, take all other factors — personal relationship, need, distance, administrative cost, and capacity to help — as equal, except where the case explicitly varies them.

1. The drowning swimmers. On a lake straddling a border, two strangers are drowning equidistant from you, and you can save only one. One is your compatriot; the other is a foreign national. My verdict: nationality adds nothing; a coin flip is exactly right.

2. The two floods. Two simultaneous floods, one in my country and one in a neighboring country, leave the same number of people equally badly off. I can give one donation; both relief funds are equally effective and honest. My verdict: I have no obligation to favor my compatriots. I do feel a small pull toward them, and I would not condemn someone who gives at home, but I suspect the pull is salience and parochial bias rather than a genuine special claim.

3. The settled non-citizen and the newly arrived citizen. A municipality has one unit of social housing left. Applicant A is a citizen who moved from another part of the country last month. Applicant B is a foreign national who has lived, worked, and paid taxes in this town for twenty-five years and never naturalized. Need and all formal eligibility conditions are otherwise equal. My verdict: B has the stronger claim, and by a wide margin.

4. The new arrival and the safety net. The same town and the same scarcity as before, but now the foreign national arrived on a lawful permanent-resident track one month ago and has just begun working. My verdict: the town may prefer the citizen at this early stage, but the permissible preference should shrink quickly as the newcomer's residence and contribution accumulate.

5. The emigrant and the vote. A citizen left the country at age four, has never lived there as an adult, owns no property there, owes no tax there, and is not subject to any of the laws the election will alter. They demand a vote in a national election that will set exactly those laws. My verdict: they should not get a vote in this election, even though I continue to count them as a citizen for other purposes.

6. The embassy evacuation. An embassy can evacuate one person from a war zone in a third country. Candidate A is a citizen by descent who has never lived in the country. Candidate B is a foreign national who has lived and worked in the country for twenty years and pays taxes there. My verdict: A, the citizen by descent, gets priority. I notice that this looks like the reverse of my housing verdict, and I do not currently know which of the two is the mistake.

7. The tariff. A proposed tariff would save fifteen thousand jobs in a struggling domestic industry but, by raising prices, destroy an estimated twenty-five thousand jobs in a much poorer trading partner. My verdict: the tariff is wrong. The fact that the protected workers are compatriots does not justify imposing those costs on poorer foreigners; paying for adjustment assistance for the domestic workers would be fine, but restricting the foreign competition would not be.

The case I keep going back and forth on: A foreign national has lived here for thirty years, raised a family, worked, paid taxes, and volunteered in local institutions; they have never naturalized, deliberately, out of attachment to their original nationality, though they never visit it. A compatriot left at age two, has never since lived in the country, owns nothing there, and now returns for the first time. They compete for the last place in a one-year, state-funded program combining subsidized retraining and income support for people out of work. Which has the stronger claim under the principle you arrive at, and why?

Please work out the principle — or the small set of candidate principles — that best captures my verdicts. State each precisely enough that its results are mechanical. For each candidate, go through all seven cases and the case I cannot settle, flag which of my verdicts it captures and which it forces me to drop, and spend real time on the tension between case 3 and case 6, since those two seem to pull against each other. Recommend one account as the best repair of my hunch, and be explicit about what it still leaves unresolved or counterintuitive.
