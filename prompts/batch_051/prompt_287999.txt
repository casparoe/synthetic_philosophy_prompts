A friend described the absent-minded driver problem to me last week and I can't get it out of my head, because the argument seems to show rational choice theory breaks down in a one-person decision problem with no enemies, no uncertainty, and no other people. The setup: a driver is on a highway with two exits. Taking the first exit gives payoff 0, taking the second gives payoff 4, and continuing past the second gives payoff 1. The catch is he's absent-minded: whenever he reaches an exit he can't remember whether it's the first or the second, so any rule he can actually follow has to be the same at both exits.

Now here's the argument that bothers me, with the steps numbered like my friend laid them out:

(1) Before starting, the driver can reason that he will first pass exit 1 and then exit 2. So the optimal plan is obvious: continue at the first exit, exit at the second, for payoff 4.

(2) But when he actually reaches an exit, he cannot tell which exit he's at. So a plan of the form "continue at first, exit at second" isn't executable. All he can do is pick some probability p of exiting at whichever exit he finds himself at.

(3) Under that symmetric rule, his expected payoff is (1−p)(3p+1). The p that maximizes this is p = 1/3, giving expected payoff 4/3.

(4) So before leaving, the best available plan gives 4, but once he's at an exit, the best implementable rule only gives 4/3.

(5) Since the plan giving 4 isn't actually implementable, standard decision theory would say he should choose the best feasible strategy, expected payoff 4/3.

(6) But the same theory also says it would be irrational to knowingly accept 4/3 when 4 is available. The problem is the 4 isn't available, and yet before leaving it feels completely right to say "I'll take the second exit."

(7) So either rational choice theory is inconsistent here, or "optimal plan" is meaningless when you can't bind yourself to it, or the ex ante perspective and the interim perspective simply yield different answers in a single-person problem, which seems absurd.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I'd rather keep the standard idea that ex ante and interim optimality should agree in single-person decisions, but I can't see which step to reject or what rejecting it costs.
