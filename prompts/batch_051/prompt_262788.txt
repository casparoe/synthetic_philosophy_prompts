I've been thinking about how, in a conversation, there's this kind of running record of what everyone is treating as settled — even if nobody ever says it out loud, and even if it isn't true. And I think I've found a way to force somebody into committing themselves to a claim just by burying that claim inside a question. I can't tell whether the argument goes through or where the cheat is. Here it is:

(1) Every conversation has a scoreboard: the set of propositions that both people are treating as taken for granted, just for the purposes of that conversation.

(2) Whenever somebody makes a move in a conversation, any proposition that the move presupposes automatically gets added to the scoreboard — unless the other person immediately objects and chalenges the presupposition.

(3) The question 'have you stopped taking money from the register?' presupposes that you have been taking money from the register.

(4) If you answer 'no' — just answering on its own terms, like 'no, I haven't stopped' — then you haven't immediately challenged the presupposition; you've gone along with it.

(5) So, by (2) through (4), once you answer, the proposition that you've been taking money from the register goes onto the scoreboard, even if that proposition is false.

(6) Whatever is on the scoreboard counts as something both people are committed to, for the purposes of the conversation.

(7) Therefore, just by answering my question, you've committed yourself to the claim that you've been stealing from the register, without ever saying it or agreeing to it.

Is this valid? If not, which step fails, and what would a corrected version of that step say? If it is valid, then what does the conclusion actually establish — can you genuinely trap someone into admitting they're a thief this way, or only something much weaker? Be specific about the step and the reading of 'committed' at stake.
