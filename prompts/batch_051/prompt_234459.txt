I spent much of last night arguing with a close friend about whether death can be bad for the person who dies. He had been reading a popular introduction to ancient philosophy, and he kept returning to its one-paragraph summary of the Epicurean position, which he took to be decisive. I suspect the summary is doing more argumentative work than he realizes, but I do not have the training to say exactly where it goes wrong, so I want an expert audit before either of us changes our mind. The summary, as he copied it out for me, runs as follows: "Epicurus's central argument is that death cannot be bad for the one who dies, because nothing is bad for you unless you experience it as bad. Death is the complete destruction of the person, so there is no subject left to have any experience at all: while you exist, death has not yet occurred, and once death has occurred, you no longer exist. Thus the fear of death must rest on a confusion—we covertly picture ourselves surviving our own deaths to observe, regret, or feel our absence, but no such experience is possible. Once this confusion is removed, the wise person will be completely indifferent to death."

Please answer as a professional philosopher working on the value and metaphysics of death. Take a moment before composing your response to brainstorm the strongest version of the Epicurean argument that this summary could be gesturing at, and the strongest objections that it passes over. Then separate the summary into what is accurate, what is a defensible simplification, and what is a distortion or question-begging move. I am especially interested in whether the claim that something can be bad for you only if you experience it as bad is something Epicurus himself argued for, or a premise the summary quietly imports—and whether the argument still goes through if harm is understood as deprivation rather than occurrent suffering. End by telling me which parts of the summary my friend can safely lean on in our dispute.
