I help run a little philosophy discussion group, and we've been going around in circles on Moore's paradox — the kind of sentence where someone says it's raining and then adds that they don't believe it is, even though the sentence can be true while the act of saying it feels impossible. I want a "which diagnosis of Moore's paradox do you actually hold?" quiz for the group, the kind of sorting quiz people do for fun but actually diagnostic.

Eight to ten questions. Each question should be a short concrete case — someone says, silently thinks, writes, or overhears a Moore-ish sentence — with two or three gut-level answer options. None of this "do you think belief reports are self-descriptions?" business; nobody in my group would know what to do with that. The quiz should sort people by there reactions to cases, not by which label sounds cool.

Please weave in at least these cases: (1) the classic present-tense first-person version; (2) a first-person past-tense version; (3) a third-person version about somebody else, which most people find fine and which makes a good control; (4) the mirror-image case where someone says they believe it's raining but that it isn't raining, which should also come out fine as a control; (5) the intention-flavored version where someone says they'll do something and immediately adds that they don't believe they will; (6) a case where the person just silently thinks the Moorean thought to themselves; (7) a case where the whole sentence is written on a scrap of paper with no name attached. Add one to three more cases if they'll split the positions further.

Map answer patterns onto at least four diagnoses, written up in plain English without the academic labels. I have four leaves in mind, but add a fifth if the patterns force it: (a) a deflationist view that the sentence is merely odd or bad-mannered, not genuinely paradoxical; (b) a view on which saying I believe that p in the first person expresses or avows the belief rather than describing some inner object, so the Moorean sentence is a clash of stances and the oddity should be specifc to first-person present-tense uses; (c) the transparency view, on which the absurdity comes from the fact that I normally settle whether I believe p by settling whether p, so the two halves lean on the same grounds and undercut each other; (d) an assertion-norm or performative view, on which plainly asserting p already represents the speaker as believing p, so the second half denies a commitment the first half just made. If a distinct fifth leaf emerges — for instance a view that the speaker is straightforwardly irrational or self-blind, or a view that the sentence is flat-out contradictory — include it and say exactly which answer pattern lands someone there.

Build the scoring key out of the patterns, not out of the names. For each question, list which answer options count toward which diagnosis, then give a simple totalling rule. Call out corners of the answer space that no diagnosis occupies and, for each empty corner, say whether it is empty because it's incoherent, because no one would want to hold it, or just because nobody has explored it. If two of the leaves turn out not to be separable by any question you can write, flag that plainly rather than pretending, and tell me what extra case would pry them apart.

Structure the response in exactly these sections, in order: "The Quiz" (the 8–10 questions, about 400–500 words total); "Scoring Key" (option-to-diagnosis mapping plus the totalling rule, about 250–350 words); "The Diagnoses" (one plain-English card per leaf, about 450–600 words total); "Empty Cells and Blind Spots" (about 200–300 words). The group members are sharp but haven't read any of the literature, so keep the diagnosis cards concrete and free of jargon.
