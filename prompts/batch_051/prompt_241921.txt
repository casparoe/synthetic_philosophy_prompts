Write a dialogue between two people arguing about whether bare statistical evidence — naked statistical evidence, the blue bus kind — can ever justify a legal finding of fact, and if not, why not. Frame it around the blue bus case (eighty percent of the buses on the route belonged to one company; a pedestrian is hit) and the gatecrasher case (a thousand people in the stands, 499 paid, so a randomly picked attendee is slightly more likely a gatecrasher than not). Have them get into the details rather than repeat the standard moves from the top.

One character holds that once the probability crosses the relevant legal threshold — preponderance for civil liability, something much higher for reasonable doubt — refusing to act on the numbers is just epistemic squeemishness or a separate policy instinct, not a principled objection to the evidence itself. The other holds that a bare frequency is a different kind of evidence from individualized evidence: it may raise the probability, but it doesn't connect the probability to this particular defendant in the way a verdict seems to require.

Make it a real back-and-forth. Neither side should be a strawman. Each should have to concede at least one point and then rebuild around it. The pro-statistics character should have to face the strongest version of the something-is-missing intuition and offer their best debunking explanation of where that intuition comes from — loss-aversion, ambiguity aversion, a false picture of what individualized evidence actually does, whatever they think it is. The anti-statistics character should have to say exactly what the missing ingredient is — something about sensitivity to the defendant's conduct, about knowledge, about the reference class, about what you could honestly say to the person you just ruled against — and defend it when the pro-statistics character points out that an eyewitness who is right eighty percent of the time is no more reliable than the blue bus base rate.

Don't let them just trade speeches; each reply should pick up the last move the other made. At some point they should also ask whether the anti-statistical objection has the same force against the civil preponderance standard as against the criminal reasonable-doubt standard, or whether it's only really compelling at one of them. By the end they should have located the real point of disagreement — even if neither converts — and at least one of them should say what would change their mind.
