I am drawn to the austere revealed-preference view that utility is only a numerical index representing a consistent pattern of choice under risk: the representation theorem fixes it only up to a positive affine transformation, which makes ratios of utility differences meaningful but renders ratios of utility levels—and with them any literal "I want this twice as much as that"—meaningless. I cannot shake the conviction that this leaves something out. The movement from mildly preferring tea to desperately wanting pain relief does not feel like a mere reordering of my dispositions to accept gambles; it feels like a change in a single underlying magnitude, one whose ratios I can at least dimly report, and introspection seems to constrain my subsequent choices the way measurement constrains prediction rather than the way redescription follows it around. I do not want the standard debunking move that preferences are just what you choose. I want to know what it would take for the felt-intensity intuition to be the thing that goes—an artifact of introspection or a scope-insensitivity illusion—what it would take for the revealed-preference view to be the thing that goes or to be revised into a genuine psychophysics of desire, and whether there is a stable reading on which both survive because utility plays two roles, representation and magnitude. If such a reading exists, what pins it down, and what would show it had been merely verbal?
