I have been recording a friend's judgments about which coincidences she is willing to treat as too unlikely to be chance, and I want to predict how she will rule on a new case. Her track record: she finds it remarkable when she meets a stranger at a twenty-five-person gathering who shares her exact birthday, but not when some two people at the same gathering share a birth month. She finds it suspicious when a named acquaintance is struck by lightning twice, but not when she reads that someone, somewhere in the country, was struck twice in a decade. She finds it too coincidental when she thinks of a friend and that friend calls within five minutes, but not when, over the course of a year, a handful of her many idle thoughts about friends are followed within five minutes by a call. The new case: she reads that a named stranger she has never met has won a one-in-ten-million lottery prize twice in a single year. Predict her verdict. Identify the candidate principles that fit her track record equally well—such as base-rate neglect about specific outcomes, personal-significance weighting, or selection effects—and say what each predicts about this case. Give your best guess about the principle she is actually running, with reasons, and propose one further case that would most cleanly separate whichever principles remain live.
