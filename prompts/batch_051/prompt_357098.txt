I keep encountering arguments about whether information about a person belongs to that person, and each one seems to be built around a single hard case rather than a complete specification. Before I read any more, I want the desiderata: what any adequate theory of personal data as property—or of the alternative relation, if property is the wrong model—has to deliver, where adequacy means usable in legal and policy argument about control, access, and redress over information about identifiable people. Give me the requirements with the case or consideration behind each. At minimum, make the list force a position on: secrets and private facts; facts you disclose or perform in public; records or tokens versus the information they carry, given that information can be copied without depriving anyone of it; inferences, profiles, and risk scores built from data about you; data generated through your own use of a service or device; data generated by someone else's observation of you; data that is also about other people, such as genetic, dyadic, or group data; data about the dead; and data you could not practically prevent others from learning anyway. For each requirement, say whether it is non-negotiable or a preference of a particular camp, and flag any subset of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied. Then, briefly, which single requirement does the most work against the main contenders. I care more about the criteria and the trade-offs than about the scorecard.
