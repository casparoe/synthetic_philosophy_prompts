Here are the criteria commonly abstracted from John Locke’s discussion of original acquisition in the *Second Treatise of Government*, chapter V, ‘Of Property’: a person acquires something from the common state of nature when (a) it was not already another’s, (b) they have mixed their labour with it or joined it to something of theirs, (c) they have not allowed it to spoil wastefully, and (d) what they leave is enough and as good for others. Now consider a case: a plant breeder gathers pollen from several wild grass species growing on unenclosed common land, puts years of work into creating a perennial hybrid that fixes nitrogen, and claims ownership of the resulting seed line. The breeder does not claim the land or the original wild plants; anyone may still enter the land and gather the wild grasses as before. The claim is a right to prevent others from planting, selling, or trading the hybrid seed, including instances where the seed has spread by wind or birds onto another person’s land. Does the breeder’s claim satisfy all four criteria? Go criterion by criterion, note where the criteria are silent or need interpretation, and say whether the case shows a weakness in the labour theory or only in this particular application of it. Please give a definite answer: either the right is acquired or it is not, with reasons, rather than merely surveying possible readings. British English, please.
