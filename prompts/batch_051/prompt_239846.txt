I'm planning a unit for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on inference in archaeology, cross-listed between philosophy and anthropology. There are 18 students and I have an 80-minute session. Most of them can state the methodological point that objects do not speak for themselves, but I want them to feel it rather than be told it. I'd like a structured activity in which small teams receive a small assemblage of objects (I can supply ambiguous, unlabeled artifacts or photographs, as long as you tell me how to choose them) and a brief provenience note, and then reconstruct the economy, social organization, institutions, and at least one belief or ritual practice of the people who produced them. The design goal is that a team becomes genuinely confident in its reconstruction, then has that confidence unsettled twice: first on discovering that another team built a quite different but equally object-consistent account from the same evidence, and then on realizing that the available objects alone cannot decide between the two accounts. I don't want the resolution to come from the instructor simply announcing the correct interpretation; the rules or roles should force participants to run into the problem themselves.

Please give me runnable specifics: group size and composition, any role cards, a timeline with timings, materials and selection criteria for the objects, and instructions that say what teams may and may not do (for example, whether they may handle the objects, compare across groups, consult background readings, or request one restricted piece of new evidence). The debrief should map specific moments in the exercise to the inference problems they instantiate, such as underdetermination by the available evidence, equifinality, uniformitarian analogy, the way artifact categories themselves depend on background assumptions, and the difference between absence of evidence and evidence of absence, but only where those genuinely arise. It should also say plainly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle it, and which features of real archaeological inference the exercise cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply. Throughout, keep the methodological content honest to the discipline: separate what is widely accepted among practicing archaeologists and methodologically informed philosophers from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as if it were the consensus.
