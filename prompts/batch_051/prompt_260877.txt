Asimov's Three Laws of Robotics were originally offered as a scheme for built-in safety rules a robot could reason from: don't harm humans, obey orders, protect its own existence. But the AI systems people actually worry about now aren't hand-coded rule-followers at all—they're trained on enormous amounts of text and then shaped by human feedback, so they end up with something more like implicit values and habits than an explicit ranked list of rules. I'm trying to figure out whether the Three Laws, understood as an alignment proposal rather than just a plot device, survives that shift. Which specific premise of the Three Laws approach does the move to trained systems put pressure on? Can the proposal be repaired into something weaker but still worth keeping, or does the whole thing just collapse? I'm not after AI policy recommendations—I want to know what happens to the argument itself. For instance, do the laws quietly assume the machine can reliably identify what counts as harm or as following an order, and that it can settle conflicts between rules in a principled way? Does machine learning make those assumptions more plausible, less plausible, or just irrelevant? And plese write the answer in American English.
