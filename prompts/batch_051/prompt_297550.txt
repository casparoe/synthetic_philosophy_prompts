I am a novelist, and I have spent the past several months researching the ordinary machinery of pest control for a book set partly on an industrial farm: the spraying of orchards and row crops, the fumigation of stored grain, bait stations and pheromone traps, the routine treatment of house foundations for termites, the mosquito-abatement trucks that move through neighborhoods in summer. One thing I keep returning to is how much killing is treated as entirely unremarkable when the victims are insects, while the same society that cheerfully exterminates a termite colony maintains elaborate welfare standards for cattle, pigs, and even laboratory rodents. The practice seems to presuppose some view about moral status or the significance of suffering, but I have trouble stating exactly which view. Does a farmer who sprays an aphid infestation implicitly hold that insects cannot suffer at all, or only that their suffering matters so little next to the crop that no threshold is ever crossed, or that insects stand outside the moral community in some more fundamental way? And if the practice presupposes one of these claims, is it a claim the practitioners would actually endorse if the question were put to them directly, or is it a claim they would disown, which would make the practice less coherent than its everydayness suggests?

I am less interested in the entomology than in the philosophy. Reconstruct the implicit theory of moral considerability, welfare, and value that ordinary pest-control practice would need in order to make sense, and distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with; I would also like to know where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, since I suspect several different theories would all license a can of insecticide while disagreeing sharply about why. Then tell me what would have to change in a practice built instead on the assumption that insect welfare counts for something, even if weakly, so that an individual insect has some claim on us, or at least that its experience is a reason against destroying it unless something sufficiently weighty is at stake, and whether such a practice is thinkable without abandoning agriculture or ordinary urban life. Please write the response in American English.
