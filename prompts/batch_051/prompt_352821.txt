Exam question: Paul Feyerabend is often summarized by the slogan 'anything goes.' Explain what Feyerabend meant in saying this, using the Galileo case as your main historical example. Then critically assess whether Feyerabend's position commits him to the view that there are no rational standards for theory choice in science. Your answer should distinguish clearly between what Feyerabend claims about the history of science and what, if anything, he recommends as a matter of method or policy.

Student's answer: Feyerabend's slogan 'anything goes' is a descriptive thesis about scientific practice: every methodological rule that has ever been proposed has been violated at some point, and those violations have often been productive. His main example is Galileo, who defended Copernicanism by combining rhetoric, propaganda, and telescopic observations that were not conclusive by the standards of the time. From this, Feyerabend concludes that scientists should stop trying to follow methodological rules, since none of them work, and that there are no rational standards by which good science can be distinguished from bad. Astrology and physics are therefore equally legitimate traditions; their different fates are settled by institutional power, not by evidence. This is also why he treats science as one tradition among many and argues that the state should be separated from science the way it is separated from the church.
