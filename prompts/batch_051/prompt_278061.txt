I help run a tenants' association of around eighteen adults—no one has any formal training in political theory or economics, including me—and every few months we choose between three proposals for spending a small shared budget. The same impasse keeps coming up: whichever voting method someone proposes, somebody else finds a way to get a better result by voting against their true ranking, and then we argue about whether that counts as cheating or just as voting sensibly. I've been reading social choice theory on my own, and I've come across the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem. As I understand it, the theorem says this isn't bad luck: with at least three alternatives, any rule that takes any profile of strict individual rankings and always selects a single winner, that allows every alternative to win under some profile, and that gives no voter an incentive to misreport their ranking, necessarily makes one particular voter's top choice win no matter how everyone else votes. If I've garbled the statement, correct it before you build the activity around it.

I want to use our next ninety-minute retreat to let the group feel the force of that fact instead of having it explained at them. Design the activity. The core should be a constrained design-and-break game: split the group into small design teams, and have each team propose a concrete rule for choosing among three alternatives that satisfies three plainly worded conditions—(1) every alternative can win under some pattern of honest rankings; (2) no single person's top choice wins regardless of how everyone else votes; (3) no one can get a better winner by misreporting their ranking. The other teams then try to break the proposal by constructing a profile of honest rankings plus one strategic misreport that gives that voter a preferred winner. Iterate so they keep running into the wall themselves.

Use a real but small payoff—the rule that survives, or the last unbroken one, actually determines which of three snacks or small shared purchases the group gets at the end—so the incentive to misreport is felt, not just described. Do not reveal the theorem or its name during the game, and frame condition (2) in neutral language so the word "dictator" doesn't tip the answer.

Then give me the debrief in enough detail to run it. Map the moments where their rules failed onto the theorem's conditions; be precise about what the theorem does and does not establish—for example, why two alternatives are different, and which assumptions (strict rankings, a deterministic single winner, everyone's preferences counting as ballots) the result depends on; and be honest about which part of the theorem's force a ninety-minute game cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in words.

Take the role of someone expert in both social choice theory and the design of cooperative workshops for non-academics. Before committing, brainstorm at least three possible mechanisms for the game, say in a sentence or two each why you reject two of them, and then commit to the one you would actually run. Give me a definite, runnable design—exact rules, timing, materials, small-group size, and debrief notes—not a menu of options.
