Can I get an honest check on a homemade test I've been using to deal with the vagueness of the intended-versus-foreseen distinction at the heart of the doctrine of double effect? I call it the counterfactual intention test: an effect counts as intended, not merely foreseen, if and only if the agent would abandon or revise the plan if that effect were guaranteed not to happen. Applied to the usual bombing pair: the terror bomber flunks, because if the civilians were guaranteed to survive, the plan loses its point and he'd do something else; the tactical bomber passes, because he'd hit the same target even with the civilians safe. The appeal for me is that it stays third-person — it doesn't require the agent to introspect about what he really meant, it just looks at what he would do under a guarantee. But I can already see the worry: maybe this just relocates the closeness problem, since a bomber who'd be mildly disappointed but bombs anyway comes out as not intending the deaths, while one who builds his plan around the deaths comes out as intending them, even when their callousness is identical. And maybe it's old news under a name I don't remember. Give me the strongest reconstruction, say whether there's a nearest existing position, and lay out the three hardest objections. I mainly want to know which failure mode is lethal and which could actually be fixed.
