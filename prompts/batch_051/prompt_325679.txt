Consider the dispute between Mozi's jian'ai, often translated as universal love, and the Confucian insistence on graded care — thickest within the family and thinning outward. Suppose a moralist tries to hold both by endorsing one of the following five positions:

A. Impartial care sets the moral standard, and graded attachment is permitted only when impartial demands would erode the agent's capacity to care at all.
B. Graded family-first care is the default, with an impartial floor that activates only for severe unmet needs outside the family.
C. Impartial care governs public and institutional roles, and graded care governs private life.
D. Care is graded by reciprocity and gratitude rather than kinship: one owes most to those who have actually cared for one, whoever they are.
E. Impartial concern for persons, combined with graded investment in particular relationships: equal respect for everyone, but unequal time and intimacy.

Score each of A–E from 0 to 1 on two dimensions: (i) how much of impartial care's core demand it preserves, and (ii) how much standing motivational strain it imposes, given ordinary human partiality and scarcity of time, attention, and material resources — strain that is likely to be discharged by drifting back toward kin-first behavior. Then rank the five by the trade-off you would defend, identify the single position you regard as the most honest compromise, and defend that verdict rather than merely surveying the options. In particular, say whether your ranking supports the view that impartial care and graded love differ in kind, or only in the steepness of the motivational gradient from nearest to farthest.
