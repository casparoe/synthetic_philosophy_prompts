Quick context: I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've been working on a piece about free will and the problem of evil. Most of that debate runs through human beings, and I keep feeling like readers have already seen every move. So I'm drafting something on a version of the problem that's less familiar to a general audience: the fall of the angels. The setup I'm working with is the traditional one — angels created good, with no bodies, no evolutionary or personal history, no outside temptation, and an intellect pointed at God from the start — and yet Lucifer somehow turns away from God anyway. My editor worries it's too theological for the outlet, but I think there's a real philosophical core in it. I'm not asking for sources or citations; I just want your reaction to the argument itself.

Here's the argument I'm building the essay around. The versions of the free-will theodicy I have in mind say evil enters creation because creatures are free, and freedom requires that they could have chosen otherwise, so a good God isn't responsible for their choices. That story has some traction for humans, because humans are ignorant, pulled around by fear and hunger, shaped by their upbringing, and full of competing desires; there's plenty of machinery available to explain a bad choice without making God the author of it. But with angels, all that machinery is missing. An angel isn't ignorant, isn't driven by bodily need, and doesn't have a bad childhood. So when the first angel chooses evil, there seem to be exactly two ways the story can go. Either the choice had no explanation at all, in which case the first evil is just a brute fact and the theodicy hasn't explained anything, or the choice did have an explanation — a motive, an inclination, a defect, whatever — in which case something defective has been smuggled in before the fall, and the angel wasn't purely good to begin with. So the free-will theodicy faces a clean dilemma: either the fall is inexplicable, or the angel wasn't good. And if its right, the free-will story isn't a general answer to the problem of evil; it's an answer that only works when creatures come pre-loaded with the usual human vulnerabilities.

What I'd like from you is comments, not a rewrite. Does the dilemma actually go through, or am I conflating explanation with causation, or having a reason with being determined? Is there an obvious third option that dissolves it — and again, no citations or named sources, just the shape of the reply? Should I soften the conclusion or is the strong version more interesting? And honestly, would this hold a reader who isn't already invested in theological puzzles, or does it only work for people who walk in caring about angels? Please write your comments the way you'd email a colleague: continuous prose only, no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. American English, and don't be polite — if the argument's bad, say so.
