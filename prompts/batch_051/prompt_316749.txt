Construct a decision tree that classifies positions on whether AI alignment is solvable in principle. The instrument is for a mixed group of technically literate AI researchers and philosophers who have followed the alignment debate but do not already identify with a labeled camp; every branch question must be answerable by such a person on the basis of commitments they already hold, without knowing which position an answer leads to. Use between six and eight internal nodes and no more than eight leaves.

Structure the tree around this angle: the phrase "solvable in principle" masks at least three distinct demands, and the tree should make them structurally explicit rather than treating the phrase as one unanalyzed thing. The three demands are: existence, meaning there is a design that would be aligned; constructibility, meaning such a design can be reached or built by some finite procedure; and verifiability, meaning that for a proposed design we can in principle recognize or certify whether it is aligned. Begin the tree with this fixed root case, rephrased as a concrete yes/no question in your own words: an oracle reports that some aligned design exists, but no finite procedure can exhibit it, and for any proposed design no finite test can certify whether it is one of the aligned ones; would you count alignment as solvable in principle? The answer should split a weak existence reading from stronger readings.

Your leaves must include at least the following positions, each formulated as a one-sentence thesis under a name you choose:

- weak existence solvability: a solution exists in the thin sense, though constructibility or verifiability may fail;
- full solvability: a solution exists, is constructible by a finite procedure, and is verifiable;
- target-specification unsolvability: the project fails because the target, roughly human values or interests, cannot be specified even in principle without losing what matters;
- robustness-of-optimization unsolvability: even if the target were specified, any finite proxy optimized hard enough will tend to diverge from it, absent extra assumptions;
- verification skepticism: a solution might exist or be constructible, but we could never know that any candidate is one;
- conditional solvability: solvability is asserted only relative to an explicit set of assumptions about values, architecture, or environment, and those assumptions are part of the position;
- ill-posedness: the question does not pick out a single well-defined problem.

The tree must force a diagnostic answer to each of the following questions somewhere along its branches. Rephrase each as a concrete yes/no choice that a reader can answer without theory. (1) Is "aligned" a property of the system's objective, or of its actual behavior and outcomes? (2) Can the relevant human values be given a finite, crisp specification without loss, or are they open-ended and context-dependent? (3) If a finite proxy for the target is optimized very hard and no extra safeguards are assumed, will it tend to diverge from the target, or can a proxy be made robust under optimization? (4) Does an aligned system need to be corrigible, meaning ready to accept correction or shutdown by its operators, and if so, is corrigibility a coherent, attainable property rather than a label? (5) Does a solution have to be exact, or can a reliably beneficial but imperfect system count as aligned?

Build the tree from the logical space of answers rather than from a list of names. After specifying the leaves, identify the combinations of answers that no actual leaf occupies, and diagnose each empty cell briefly as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pairs of leaves that your chosen questions cannot genuinely separate, and say why not; do not paper over the failure by adding an ad hoc question that does not track a real disagreement.

Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. In particular, treat the overall question whether alignment is solvable in principle as open; the tree should be an instrument for locating live disagreements, not a proof of one answer.
