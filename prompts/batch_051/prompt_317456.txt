I spent thirty-five years as a physical chemist, and only after retiring did I get into the Fermi paradox / Great Filter literature. It's got me chewing on something I've been calling the gradient debt (working title), and I can't tell whether its a real idea or just thermodynamics with a science-fiction name.

Here's the claim. A biosphere is, at bottom, a chemical heat engine: it runs on whatever gradients the planet hands it — redox couples, thermal and pH differences, photochemical potential, that sort of thing. Life taps a gradient, which lets it build complexity, but every new layer of complexity carries maintenance overhead: membranes, proofreading, active transport, immune systems, nervous systems. So a biosphere accumulates a kind of debt. The more complex it gets, the more free energy it has to spend just keeping itself at that complexity, leaving less and less for the next jump. On this picture the Great Filter isn't one improbable step (RNA, eukaryotes, intelligence, whatever); it's a thermodynamic ceiling. Each biosphere climbs until its maintenance bill catches up with the rate at which the planet rebuilds gradients, and then it plateaus or crashes. Earth got past it, I suspect, because plate tectonics and the oxygen catastrophe kept opening new redox space faster than life could exhaust it. Mars, maybe, just hit the ceiling early.
