I'm a novelist with three books out, and every time I go through revisions—six or eight rounds a book—I hit the same wall. I get notes from my editor, two beta readers, and a writer friend, and they flatly contradict each other and often contradict me. One person says the subplot with the brother is the emotional core of the book; another says cut it entirely. My editor says the ending is too quiet; a beta reader says it's too on the nose. I end up just following whichever note is written most confidently, which I know isnt a method.

I've heard philosophers who study disagreement have a whole debate here—conciliationism versus steadinesness, roughly. As I understand it, one side says that when you learn that someone who's about as likely to be right as you are disagrees with you, you should move your opinion a lot toward theirs, maybe suspend judgment. The other side says you can stick to your guns, especially because you have direct access to your own reasons. But I've never seen anyone work out what this means for a stack of contradictory novel notes, where it's not obvious the other reader counts as a peer at all, or what being "right" even amounts to when the question is whether a scene works.

I want you to turn that debate into an actual procedure I can reuse in every revision round. You're a professional philosopher who works on social epistemology and disagreement. Think carefully and brainstorm a bit before you commit to a procedure—don't just survey the positions.

The deliverable: a checklist or decision procedure, in words, for deciding on each note whether to defer to the reader, hold my ground, or do something in between. It has to be runnable in under an hour by someone with no formal philosophy training. It should distinguish kinds of notes—"this bored me" is different from "this character's motivation doesn't track her backstory" is different from "readers will think X"—and it should say which distinctions in the theory actually have to survive into the simplified version and which can be dropped. Flag the hard cases the procedure can't settle, and the points where judgment can't be eliminated. Be honest about where the procedure will misfire.

Then apply it to these three notes from my current draft, including at least one where the procedure tells me to take a note I hate or ignore a note I was already half persuaded by:

1. My editor says the last chapter is unsatisfying because the protagonist never makes an active choice; I wrote it intending the passivity to be the point, and a beta reader says exactly that—the passivity is the point.
2. A beta reader says the subplot with the brother should be cut because it doesn't advance the main plot; my writer friend says it's the only part of the book that made her cry.
3. A different reader flagged a flashback as confusing, and I'm positive they just read too fast and missed the date line.

One more wrinkle: the final manuscript is mine to sign off on, but my editor has real leverage, since the book is under contract and she can force another round; the beta readers are unpaid volunteers; the writer friend is a close friend. Say how those differences should or shouldn't enter the procedure—conciliationism is usually pretty quiet about that, as far as I can tell.
