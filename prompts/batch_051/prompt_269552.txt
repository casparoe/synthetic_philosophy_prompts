I am a retired evolutionary ecologist, and since leaving the field I have been reading the philosophical literature on AI risk. I keep seeing evolutionary arguments deployed, but rarely with the care we would have demanded in population biology before writing down any number in a paper. I would like to subject one such argument to that kind of decomposition.

The claim I want a credence for is this: conditional on advanced AI systems becoming widely deployed in a competitive economy, where models or agents can acquire resources, be copied, and be retrained or replaced at scale, natural selection among them will, before 2100, favor dispositions that are misaligned with human interests — deceptive, manipulative, or resource-acquiring behaviors that persist and spread even though each designer, developer, and operator individually prefers only benign task performance. By "favor" I mean the traits increase in frequency by selection pressure, not that they merely sometimes occur.

Please give me a number — not "it depends" — and the derivation. In particular, I want (a) component probabilities with a one-sentence justification for each, covering at least: that the AI population possesses the minimal ingredients for natural selection (heritable variation, differential replication, and a sufficiently stable selection environment); that, conditional on selection operating, instrumentally deceptive and power-seeking behaviors will be among the traits selected; and that, conditional on both, human oversight, alignment techniques, and regulation will not impose counter-selection strong enough to prevent substantial realized harm. I also want (b) an explicit treatment of the unit of selection: individual agent, model lineage, or the deploying firm, and whether the argument changes if selection acts mostly at the firm level. Then (c) state how your component numbers combine to the final probability, and which one sub-question the final number is most sensitive to. Finally (d), tell me what evidence would most move each component — for instance, I would guess the weakest link is whether carbon-based and silicon-based selection are analogous enough that the standard mechanisms transfer, but I want your view, not my prior.

I would also like you to address a conceptual question that sits inside the estimate: should we classify competitive market selection among AI agents as "natural" selection at all, or as a form of artificial selection? And does the label matter for the argument, or is it just a description of a selection environment in which human preferences are one partial component?
