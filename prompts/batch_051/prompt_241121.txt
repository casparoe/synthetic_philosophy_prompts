I got into an argument with a friend about whether the word "overdetermined" actually earns its keep in history. In philosophy you get the clean definition: an outcome is overdetermined when two or more causes are each sufficient on their own, like a firing squad or someone struck by lightning at the exact moment of a fatal heart attack. But real historical explanation never looks like that. When people say the First World War was overdetermined — alliance systems, the arms race, the German war plan, the blank cheque, nationalism — none of those alone would have brought it about, and arguably none of them was strictly necessary either, since removing one might just leave the others jointly sufficient. So historians seem to mean something looser: that the outcome was robust across a range of nearby counterfactuals.

What I'd like from you: give me a concrete historical example (any period or region; it doesn't need to be a famous textbook case, and a plausibly invented case is fine as long as you flag it as invented) where an outcome was overdetermined in something like the firing-squad sense, or close enough to be interesting. Then spell out the counterfactual test you'd use in practise to decide whether the case is genuinely overdetermined rather than just multicausal in the ordinary way where each cause was needed in the circumstances. Finally, say what the example shows about why historians reach for the term — is it just shorthand for robustness, or does it commit them to a stronger claim about how to weight causes in explanation? I'm less interested in the chosen episode than in the conceptual work the term is doing.
