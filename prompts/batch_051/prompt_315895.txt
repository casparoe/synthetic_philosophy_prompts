For a course I'm putting together on moral progress and the expanding circle, I want to use Ursula Le Guin's 'The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas' — but I don't want the standard reading where the story just functions as a thought experiment against utilitarianism. The story is more specific than that: the city's happiness is built on the excluded suffering of one child, everyone learns this when they come of age, and most people come to accept it while a minority walks away. I'd like a reading through the expanding-circle / moral-progress frame. Does the story show a circle that has expanded to cover every citizen except one — and is that exclusion a bug the framework can name, or is it the enabling condition for the circle to exist at all? Where does the expanding-circle story of moral progress (roughly, progress as widening the set of beings that count) genuinely illuminate the citizens choice to stay, and where does the story resist it — for instance, because staying isn't a failure of sympathy so much as a terms-of-cooperation problem, or because walking away looks like moral progress without actually changing anything for the child? Be honest about where the lens distorts the story.

After the reading, give a brief structured summary as a JSON object with these fields: frame_illuminates (one paragraph: what the expanding-circle lens makes visible in the story), story_resists (one paragraph: where the story pushes back against that lens), story_adds (one paragraph: what the story contributes to the philosophical debate that the abstract idea alone misses), and verdict (one sentence: whether the story is best read as an illustration, a complication, or a critique of the expanding-circle view).
