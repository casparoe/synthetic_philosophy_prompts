I'm a PhD student in cognitive science and I'm helping run an undergrad module on concept learning, which is built around Bongard problems — those little puzzles where you get six boxes on the left and six on the right and you have to work out what rule separates the two sides, then apply it to new boxes. I keep having the same argument with another grad student about whether someone, or something, that gets the task right has actually induced the concept from those few examples or has just passed the test somehow. So I'd like a working definition of what it is to have actually induced the concept from a few examples, where the definition is supposed to do more than just track test-set accuracy. It should clearly count a person who, after seeing three examples, says something like the left boxes all have shapes with curved outlines, and then sorts twenty new boxes correctly. It should clearly not count a lookup table that had the twelve training answers typed into it ahead of time.

The hard cases I want the definition run through are these. Someone who sorts new boxes perfectly but can't articulate the rule and says they just see it. Someone who can state the rule correctly but then fails on new cases that require applying it — say they say the left boxes have circles, but miss a circle when it's partly hidden behind a square. A deep neural net trained on twelve examples that gets high accuracy on held-out boxes, but we discover it was mostly using a spurious cue, because every left training box was slightly darker than every right one, and its accuracy collapses when we balance the backgrounds. An LLM that, given the boxes as text and a couple of examples, says the rule and classifies new cases correctly, even though it very likely saw similar Bongard problems in pretraining. An LLM that classifies new cases correctly but gives a rule that doesn't actually fit them, so its words and its sorting come apart. A person who extracts a rule that fits all the training examples perfectly but is clearly not the rule we intended — the left boxes happen to all contain at least one curved shape, but we meant the left boxes contain exactly one circle — and then goes on sorting correctly according to their rule; do they have the intended concept, and does the definition even care? A person who is told the rule outright and never sees any examples, then classifies new boxes fine — presumably this shouldnt count as inducing it from examples, but I'm not sure it's a counterexample to the definition. And a person who picks a baroque rule that fits the training set but falls apart on new boxes, something grue-ish like: left boxes have circles if the display is shown on a Tuesday, otherwise right boxes have triangles. Does that count as a concept, just a bad one, or no concept at all?

Part of the issue is the boring old underdetermination problem: finitely many examples are compatible with indefinitely many rules, so the definition can't just be "picked a rule consistent with the examples." I'd especially like the definition to say something non-vacuous about where humans, neural nets, and LLMs are doing the same kind of thing and where they're doing different kinds of things, and to tell me which of the hard-case verdicts your least confident in.
