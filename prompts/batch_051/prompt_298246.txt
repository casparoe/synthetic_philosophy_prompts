Acting as the course grader, assign the answer a numerical score out of 20 and provide point-by-point comments that justify that score. The comments should identify which claims in the answer are correct and which are mistaken, unsupported, or conflated; do not give only a general impression.

Exam question:

Are generational categories such as Baby Boomers, Generation X, Millennials, and Generation Z real kinds of persons, or merely conventional groupings? In answering, (i) distinguish cohort effects from age effects and period effects, and (ii) state and apply an explicit criterion for what makes a social category a real kind.

Student answer:

I take a real kind to be a group whose members share a non-accidental property that supports reliable generalization. Generational categories meet this condition. Membership in a generation is fixed by birth year, and birth year places a person in a cohort that encounters the same large-scale social and economic events at the same stage of life. Millennials, for instance, came of age during the spread of the internet and the 2008 financial crisis, and these shared experiences imprinted a common set of dispositions on the cohort. Survey data confirm this: Millennials differ from Baby Boomers in homeownership rates and political attitudes, and the differences remain even after controlling for age, so they cannot be explained away as life-stage effects. I grant that some apparent generational differences are really age effects, and that period effects can move all generations at once; nevertheless, comparing cohorts across successive surveys allows a careful analyst to isolate the distinct contribution of cohort. The vagueness of generational boundaries—whether Generation Z begins in 1995 or 1997—is not a strong objection, because many real kinds have vague boundaries: species boundaries are often vague, yet species are real. A real kind need not have sharp edges; it needs to support inductive inference. Generational categories do support such inference, since knowing a person's cohort improves predictions about many attitudes and behaviors beyond chance. Finally, unlike merely nominal groupings such as fans of a particular celebrity or residents of a given postal code, a generation has stable membership that a person cannot exit. This stability alone would not establish that a group is a real kind, but it removes one reason for regarding generational categories as arbitrary. It is the common imprint of shared historical experience that ultimately makes the category real.
