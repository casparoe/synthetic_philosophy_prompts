I keep going back and forth about blackmail. The thing that gets me is how something can be made of two pieces that each look fine on their own: I'm generally free to tell people true, embarrassing things about you, and I'm generally free to ask you for money. But if I tell you, pay me five hundred bucks or this photo goes to your boss, suddenly it's a crime and everyone agrees it's wrong. People sometimes call this the paradox of blackmail. I think the two-legal-acts framing is doing to much work, though.

Please argue for the position that the paradox dissolves once we treat the threat and the request as a single conditional proposal rather than as two separable acts: the wrong-making feature is the coercive structure of the proposal itself, not anything about the revelation or the request taken by themselves. If you want a concrete thing to push on, consider whether it still looks paradoxical when the blackmailer is someone who already had an independent reason to keep the secret regardless of payment—say, a lawyer who learned it while representing you. Two related requests. First, distinguish what's actually settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this; if the paradox hasn't been cleanly dissolved among them, say so and flag where your argument is a contested take rather than the consensus. Second, lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating the question.
