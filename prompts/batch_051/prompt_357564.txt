In a 2009 paper titled “Beyond society: the evolution of organismality,” David C. Queller and Joan E. Strassmann write: “We suggest that this near-unanimous cooperation be taken as the defining trait of organisms.” They mean this as a criterion for organismality across levels: an entity counts as an organism when its parts cooperate nearly unanimously and are nearly free of conflicts, even if the entity lacks traditional features such as physical contiguity, development from a single cell, or genetic uniformity.

I have been thinking about applying this criterion to a case I intend to use in a philosophy of biology seminar, because it seems to expose where the proposal does and does not do real work. The case is the permanent sexual attachment found in certain deep-sea anglerfish: a small male fuses to the female’s body, loses independent feeding ability, and thereafter receives nutrition through a shared circulatory connection while supplying sperm. The pair is not a clone, does not develop from a single cell, and remains genetically heterogeneous, yet the male depends on the union for survival and his reproductive pathway runs through the female’s continued success.

I would like you to work through this case using Queller and Strassmann’s criterion. What exactly would have to be true of the male–female interaction for the pair to count as exhibiting “near-unanimous cooperation” in their sense? Is there a relevant residual conflict, and if so, how much does it matter? Then give a reasoned verdict: by their criterion, is the fused anglerfish pair an organism? Finally, tell me whether you think that verdict is acceptable, and if not, what the cheapest revision to their criterion would be that excludes the anglerfish pair while still counting the cases they explicitly want to include, such as certain social insect colonies or mutualistic associations.
