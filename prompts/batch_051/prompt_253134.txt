I've got a weird one. A field manual for translators working with previously unwritten languages has a section on radical interpretation that lays down a rule something like this: "When you set out to interpret a speaker whose language you do not yet know, assign meanings to their utterances in whatever way makes the largest possible proportion of their assertions come out true by your own lights about the world you and the speaker share." Everyone calls it the principle of charity. The manual was written decades before large language models existed. I've now been asked to use the same rule to interpret the outputs of an LLM — a system with no shared environment, no beliefs or intentions in any ordinary sense, just a text-prediction machine. Does the rule reach it? If you apply it, what exactly are you supposed to be maximizing — truth about the actual world, truth about the world of the prompt, truth relative to the model's training distribution? And if the rule only makes sense on the assumption that there's a believer trying to say true things about a shared world, does that show the manual was smuggling in more than it's wording says? I'm less interested in whether the rule is "correct" in general than in what each of the usual interpretive approaches gives here — the plain words, the purpose of the rule, what the manual's authors would say if asked, what a reasonable reader would take it to require — and which of those should win.
