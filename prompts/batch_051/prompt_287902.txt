Last week I had a long argument with a friend about Hardin's tragedy of the commons. He maintained that any resource held in common is bound to be degraded, because each user captures the full benefit of taking more while the cost is spread across everyone else, and he concluded that the only workable responses are privatisation or state control. I kept insisting that he was running together open access with a governed commons, and that the empirical record of long-lived commons institutions shows that communities sometimes manage shared resources well without either full private property or a Leviathan; but I could not turn my side of the argument into something I could actually apply.

This matters in my job. I am a community assets officer for a district council, and we regularly receive applications from residents' associations, angling clubs, allotment societies and similar bodies to take over and self-manage shared resources: an old gravel pit that has become an informal fishery, an allotment site, a patch of woodland used for firewood and foraging, a village car park, a community broadband cooperative. The applications come in roughly once a month; a licence typically runs for five to ten years and can be revoked only after a dispute, so the stakes are high, and I have about an afternoon to assess each proposal before it goes to the licensing committee. Please write me a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the economics of public goods and what the empirical study of long-enduring commons institutions has actually established, that I can run on every application. It should explain how to classify a resource by whether its use is rivalrous and whether exclusion is feasible, and why the classification changes the likely failure mode and the safeguards needed. It should then turn the design features that distinguish robust commons institutions from fragile ones into a checklist of questions I can answer from an application form and a short site visit: I am thinking of things like clear boundaries, rules that fit local conditions, user participation in making the rules, monitoring, graduated sanctions, accessible conflict resolution, and nesting larger resources within higher-level institutions, but I need these translated rather than recited. Please mark the places where the procedure must hand the decision to a person because it is a judgement call about values rather than a factual test, and be explicit about what the procedure cannot do, such as protect people who are not in the room or not yet born.

Then run the procedure on two cases: an angling club that wants to manage the gravel pit through a permit system, stocking and bag limits, and a committee that includes neighbouring landowners; and a well-meaning proposal to open the allotment site to any resident at any time, with no plot tenancy, on the grounds that the land belongs to everyone. Make sure at least one worked example produces a decision the applicants will not like, and explain where my friend's privatise-or-centralise dilemma is and is not a real constraint.
