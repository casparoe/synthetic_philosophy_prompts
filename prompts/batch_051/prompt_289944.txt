I've been going back and forth with a friend about whether statistical evidence should ever be enough on its own to convict someone or find liability, and we keep getting stuck on the usual thought experiments — the blue bus company and the rodeo gatecrasher. The trouble is those cases are made up, and I suspect the intuitions they pump are a bit to clean to settle anything.

I'd rather run the same question through an actual case: People v. Collins, the California Supreme Court case from 1968. In case you don't remember the facts: an elderly woman was robbed in an alley in Los Angeles; the prosecution leaned on a witness's description of a black man with a beard and mustache and a white woman with a blonde ponytail, driving off in a partly yellow car. A math instructor then testified that if you multiply rough base rates for those features — interracial couple in a car, beard, mustache, ponytail, blonde hair, yellow car — you get a combined probability around one in twelve million, and the jury convicted. The court overturned it, mostly becuase of the way the probabilities were built and presented.

What I want is an analysis of Collins through the lens of the naked-statistical-evidence debate: the idea that a bare statistic — four out of five buses are blue, most people in the stands didn't pay — isn't enough to ground a verdict even when it makes the defendant more likely than not to be responsible. Walk me through the mapping carefully. Is the Collins case best described as naked statistical evidence, as individualized evidence that got contaminated by sloppy multiplication, or as some third thing? Where exactly does the blue-bus/gatecrasher worry show up in the court's reasoning, and where is the court worried about something else entirely? Does the real case support the philosophical claim that naked statistics shouldn't satisfy proof beyond reasonable doubt, or does it cut against it, or just show the distinction is messier than the tidy hypotheticals suggest? Try not to force the theory onto the case where it doesn't speak — I'm more interested in an honest mapping than in a neat conclusion.
