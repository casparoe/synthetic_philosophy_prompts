I've been going back and forth on this and I keep losing track of where the trick is. Here's an argument I want you to check:

(1) Two vector potentials A and A' = A + ∇χ, related by a gauge transformation, produce exactly the same E and B fields everywhere.
(2) All the observable effects of electromagnetism are effects of E and B; the potentials are just a bookkeeping device.
(3) If two descriptions differ only by a change that alters no observable effect, then the difference between them corresponds to nothing in the world.
(4) So the gauge-dependent part of the vector potential is surplus structure: there's no physical fact about which gauge we're "really" in.
(5) But in the Aharonov–Bohm effect, an electron passing around a solenoid picks up an observable phase shift even though E and B are zero along its path, and that shift is calculated from A.
(6) So A must have direct physical significance after all, and the argument must fail somewhere.

I half-suspect (5) is too fast and that the phase shift is actually gauge invarant, but then I can't say what the original argument gets wrong, because (1)–(4) looks valid and (6) seems to follow from (5). Is the argument valid? If not, name the exact step that fails and the reading under which it fails; if it is valid, tell me how to restate (5) so it stops sounding like a counterexample.
