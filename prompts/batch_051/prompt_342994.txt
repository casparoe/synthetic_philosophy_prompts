I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and last month I sat in on an interdisciplinary workshop on machine learning and epistemology. After the last panel I lingered near the coffee urns and ended up listening to two people—Mara, a computer scientist, and Eli, a philosopher of science—argue about weather the no-free-lunch theorems in machine learning amount to a formal proof of Hume's problem of induction. I couldn't referee it then, and I still can't. I wrote the exchange down as best I could remember it.

Mara said: (1) "Take binary sequence prediction. Whatever rule you use to predict the next bit, if you average over all possible binary sequences—each continuation equally likely—the rule gets the next bit right exactly half the time. Deep network, fancy Bayesian updater, 'always guess 1': all the same. The only way any rule beats another is if it already treats some continuations as more likely than others. But that weighting is exactly the assumption induction was supposed to justify. So the theorem is Hume with equations: the data earn you nothing, and the prior is unearned."

Eli said: (2) "That's a theorem about a distribution nobody believes in. Under that uniform measure, almost every sequence is patternless—no compression, no regularity—so of course pattern-finding does no better than guessing. The actual world is not drawn uniformly from the space of all possible sequences. It's the one world we have, and it's shot through with stable structure. Induction doesn't need to work in all possible worlds, only in this one. The no-free-lunch theorem doesn't formalize Hume; it formalizes the tautology that in a patternless world, pattern-hunting doesn't work."

Mara: (3) "You just made my point. Saying 'this world has stable structure' is itself an induction—you learned that structure from past experience. The theorem shows why that can't help: any inductive rule only wins inside a small region of sequence space, and the only reason to think you're in that region is a prior that the data themselves can't supply. You can't bootstrap yourself from the data to the bias without already having the bias. So the theorem isolates the justificatory gap."

Eli: (4) "Only if 'justified' means 'performs well in every possible world,' which was never our standard. Induction is justified the way any instrument is justified: by its track record in the actual world. Our inductive practices deliver planes that fly and drugs that cure. You can't make that disappear by averaging over goblin worlds where induction fails. No-free-lunch says lunch isn't free in all possible worlds; fine—we pay for our lunch with assumptions that have so far paid rent."

Mara: (5) "The track record move is the circle Hume already spotted. 'Induction has worked in the past' is a fact about the past, and you're using induction to project it forward. No-free-lunch makes the circle visible: the track record is just one tiny patch of sequence space, and it only looks like evidence for your rule if you've already weighted that patch heavily. So the prior isn't confirmed by the track record—it's reconstructing the track record as evidence in its own favor."

Eli: (6) "Then I'll own the circle. Any self-correcting method has this shape: you trust your corrections because they're yours. The alternative isn't a clean skeptical neutrality, it's paralysis. There's no position outside all priors from which to weigh priors. So no-free-lunch doesn't refute induction or even embarrass it; it just tells us what we already knew, that empirical success is success relative to what we posit."

Please act as an expert epistemologist who is also comfortable with the mathematics of no-free-lunch theorems. Think carefully. Start by stating, in your own words, exactly what the theorem shows and which of its assumptions matter for this dispute. Then go through moves (1) through (6) one by one and rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification. Finally, say who is more right overall, and what, if anything, both of them are missing or using ambiguously.
