Compare two ways of understanding utility in economics, and ask what the comparison shows about whether a utility function tells us anything about a person's good. On the first understanding, utility is a colorless device for representing choice. Provided an agent's choices satisfy the usual internal-consistency conditions, there exists a utility function such that the agent behaves as though she were maximizing it; the function does no more than assign higher numbers to the alternatives she chooses and thereby recover her preference ordering. On the second understanding, utility is meant to be a measure of welfare: how well off the agent is, how much satisfaction or desire-satisfaction an option would give her. The difficulty arises because the first, austere understanding is routinely used to underwrite conclusions expressed in the vocabulary of the second—as when an agent's having chosen some option is cited as evidence that the option made her better off.

Trace exactly what must be added, at each step, to move from observed choices to a statement about someone's good. In doing so, separate the claim that choice reveals preference from the further claim that preference reveals welfare, and say what would have to be true concerning the agent's information, the origins of the preferences, and the choice set for the inference to be warranted. Then address this question: when the revealed-preference framework recovers an agent's utility function, is that function better described as a discovery about the agent or as a construction of the theorist? Does the welfare reading of utility silently presuppose psychological or normative doctrines that the choice-based reading was designed to avoid, and, if it does, are those doctrines defensible? Conclude by saying whether revealed preference theory clarifies the meaning of utility or changes the subject.
