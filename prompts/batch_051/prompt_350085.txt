I'm a retired pharmacologist and I've landed, slightly to my own surprise, as the token scientist on our university hospital's research ethics board. We meet Thursday to review a small healthy-volunteer pilot from the neurosurgery and neuroengineering group, and I've been asked to write the short opinion because the chair knows I've been reading philosophy in retirement. I need you to draft it for me.

The study would implant a temporary electrode array over visual cortex (awake subjects, low currents, the array comes out the same day, no permanent implant) and stimulate in a pattern that the investigators claim should produce a forbidden colour: a single hue that is experienced as both reddish and greenish, which the standard opponent-process account says is impossible for vision through normal eyes. Their argument, as far as I understand it, is that direct cortical stimulation can bypass the retinal wiring that makes red and green mutually exclusive, so a subject could have an experience whose qualitative character is genuinely new — 'novel qualia', as the principal investigator keeps saying, not my phrase. Afterwards the subjects try to describe the colour and match it against samples.

What's keeping me up isn't the surgery, which is ordinary risk-wise. It's the consent. In a normal study I can tell a volunteer what their signing up for: a headache, a metallic taste, a brief flicker in the left visual field. Here the entire point is an experience the volunteer has never had and that can't be described in advance except with the very phrase I just used, which none of them can cash out from their own experience. I've been reading around the qualia debate in retirement, Mary's room and the like, and this feels like prying the thought experiment open and asking a real person to sign inside it. Does informed consent require that someone be able to grasp beforehand what the experience will be like, or is it enough to tell them the physics and the accepted physiological risks? If we approve, what conditions would make it approvable — starting with a less weird colour first, a stop word the moment the colour appears, a clinician in the room? If we deny, are we denying because the experience might be distressing, or because consent to an unknown experience is impossible in principle? I need the memo to land on an actual recommendation, not end with 'the board should deliberate further'.

So please draft the written opinion I'll circulate Wednesday night. Give a clear recommendation — approve, deny, or approve with specific conditions — and argue fairly against the strongest version of the other side rather than a strawman. Keep it short enough to be read aloud in five minutes, and please no footnotes or reading list; this isn't a seminar. One last thing: add a short closing paragraph on what Thursday's meeting can settle and what properly belongs to the investigators, or, I admit, to the philosophers, so we don't sit there until nine.
