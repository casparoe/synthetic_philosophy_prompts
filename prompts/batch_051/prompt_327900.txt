I've been trying to figure out whether "avoid side effects" can actually be defined without a full specification of value, and the position I want pressure-tested is the option-preservation one. Roughly: an action's impact is the amount by which it shrinks the agent's ability to achieve goals in general. You fix a state space and a notion of what can be done from each state; you fix a distribution over a huge number of possible reward functions (not as a claim about which goals are real, just as a measure of which futures are still reachable in principle); then you compare, for each goal, how well the agent could do from the current state versus from the state the action would lead to, and average the shortfall. Low impact means the action leaves this reachable-options value more or less unchanged. The selling point is supposed to be neutrality: you never say which goals matter, you just conserve the ability to pursue whatever goals turn out to matter.

I know the obvious worries: the answer is relative to a state representation and a distribution over goals, and it probably over-rewards doing nothing. But I want a straight-up map of it's exposure, not the usual objections. What would have to be true for this whole approach to be wrong, rather than merely hard to operationalize? Which assumptions are load-bearing, and which failures are live rather than just logically possible? I'd personally find it most damning if you could show that almost any action can be made to look low-impact by picking the distribution cleverly, or that the measure systematically prefers preserving the status quo to fixing things that are already broken. But I don't want just my two worries — if a defender of option-preservation would themselves concede some third thing as a real defeater, I want that too, and I want to know whether the position is well insulated and whether that insulation is itself the problem.
