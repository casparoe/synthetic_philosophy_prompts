I'm taking a seminar this term on the ethics of influence, and we have been circling around the question of what makes an influence manipulative rather than legitimately persuasive. The view that keeps surfacing in discussion is a mechanistic one: persuasion engages the target's capacity to assess reasons, while manipulation bypasses, short-circuits, or subverts that capacity, for instance through pressure, framing effects, emotional triggers, social comparison, or the exploitation of biases. There is something intuitively right about this, but I suspect it runs together two things. An influence can circumvent a person's conscious, effortful deliberation without subverting their rational capacities at all; a carefully chosen default might track what the person would choose after reflection, and a vivid analogy might move someone by way of affect while still supplying something to reason about. Conversely, an influence can look exactly like reasoned persuasion while still operating manipulatively: an argument that exploits motivated reasoning or presents selectively favorable evidence works through the machinery of reasoning, but it seems to use that machinery against the person rather than on their behalf.

I would like your help in figuring out whether such a mechanistic criterion is empirically examinable at all. Suppose the psychology department would let me run a study on how people classify influence attempts. Design the strongest test you can of the claim that what leads people to classify an influence as manipulation, rather than legitimate persuasion, is whether it engages or bypasses or subverts their rational capacities. Be concrete about the setup: what kinds of influence would the conditions compare, what would you measure as evidence of rational engagement (such as the generation of counterarguments, consistency with the person's reflective judgments, or the role of presented reasons in their decision), and how would you elicit the manipulation/persuasion classification? Then present what pattern of results would count in favor of the mechanism criterion, what pattern would instead suggest that reason-engagement and non-manipulativeness come apart, and, just as important, what no version of this study could settle even in principle, such as whether engaging a person's rational capacities is what makes persuasion legitimate or merely a modifiable signal of it.
