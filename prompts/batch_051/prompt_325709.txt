You are a professional philosopher who specializes in applied animal ethics and in turning abstract moral principles into decision procedures that non-specialists can actually run. I am the head of dining procurement at a mid-sized university serving roughly 40,000 meals per week. We have just adopted an animal-impact purchasing policy whose core commitment is this: the unit of moral concern is the individual sentient animal's mental states — pain, fear, hunger, stress, and deprivation — whether the animal is farmed or wild — and comparable suffering must count equally across species, size, human attachment, and whether the harm is an intended production input or an unintended side effect of growing feed. I need you to write a reusable decision procedure, not a one-off recommendation.

The policy has already exposed a hard case that my team does not know how to think about. Our beef supplier's cattle eat large quantities of corn and soy, and growing those crops kills wild animals — field rodents, birds, and insects during ploughing, harvesting, and pest control. Our chicken supplier kills vastly larger numbers of animals directly, and those birds live in conditions considerably worse than the cattle. One option kills fewer farmed animals but more wild animals indirectly; the other kills far more farmed animals directly but may involve less crop cultivation per calorie. Staff cannot tell which choice the policy actually favors, and the same uncertainty arises whenever we compare animal products, or compare an animal product with a plant-based alternative.

The deliverable is a written procedure my procurement team could run in under 30 minutes per product decision, once a month, without ethical training. It should take the form of an ordered checklist of questions. For each question, state what a sufficient answer looks like, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it must escalate to our sustainability director because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. The procedure must be faithful to the policy's core commitments — expected impact on all affected animals, equal consideration of comparable suffering, and attention to both farmed and wild animals — but it must translate them into concrete steps a non-philosopher can execute. Do not write this as a literature review, and do not rest the worked applications on precise empirical findings you cannot verify; where the procedure needs an empirical input, say exactly what input is needed and provide a clearly labeled placeholder the team could replace with vendor or published data.

Before you write the final procedure, spend several paragraphs brainstorming the main difficulties: how to compare many short-lived chickens or field mice with a few long-lived cows; how to attribute crop-cultivation deaths to a particular product when feed is sold through pooled commodity markets; how to treat insects, whose sentience is uncertain; how to avoid double counting the same animal impact; and how to distinguish factual uncertainty from value trade-offs. Then set out the procedure itself.

After presenting the procedure, run it on three cases: (1) choosing between beef patties and chicken patties for the dining halls; (2) replacing battery-cage eggs with tofu in a breakfast scramble; and (3) a proposal to replace a conventional beef patty with a plant-based lentil patty in which the lentils are mechanically harvested in a region with dense small-mammal populations. For the worked applications you may use rough illustrative numbers from your own knowledge, but flag every such figure as an estimate that the university would need to check before relying on it. Make sure at least one worked application yields a result the procurement team will resist because it cuts against ordinary intuitions about eating less beef or assuming plant-based options are automatically cleaner. End by stating plainly what the procedure cannot do: which comparisons it leaves to the sustainability director, where it will be least reliable, and why no checklist can eliminate the underlying moral judgment.
