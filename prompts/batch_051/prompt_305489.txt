I've been arguing with a buddy who helps run a small retro-software archive, and every time we disagree about whether we can host an old game or utility, the argument bottoms out on what counts as "abandoned." It's a word I use all the time until someone makes me define it, and then it slips around. I don't mean the legal sense — I honestly don't know whether copyright even has a clean notion of abandonment, and I want to bracket the law for now. I mean the everyday practical sense in which a piece of software or some other work has simply been left behind by whoever made or owned it.

Here are the cases I keep going back to. Give me a working definition of "abandoned" — in that practical, reuse-relevant sense — that gets these right:
(a) a 1998 game whose publisher went bankrupt in 2003, with no obvious successor holding the rights;
(b) an out-of-print novel by an author who died in 1971 and has no known heirs;
(c) a GitHub library untouched for six years, whose author's email bounces;
(d) a GitHub library untouched for nine years, whose author replies within a day saying they still use it and just haven't needed to change it;
(e) a once-popular app whose new corporate owner keeps it listed and still collects ad revenue but hasn't updated it in years;
(f) a fan translation from 2002 whose translator vanished, for a game the original publisher still sells in Japan and otherwise ignores;
(g) a photo on a dead forum with no metadata and no way to identify the photographer;
(h) a Windows driver for hardware the manufacturer stopped making, where the company still exists but no longer hosts the file;
(i) a Nintendo ROM for a game Nintendo has never re-released but actively takes down from archives.

My gut says a, b, c, and g are clearly abandoned, d is clearly not, and e is the one I flip-flop on: its monetized but not maintained, and I can't tell which of those matters. I also can't decide whether "orphaned" (you can't find anyone to ask) and "abandoned" (nobody is tending it) should turn out to be one definition or two.

So: propose the definition, run it through all nine in order, and tell me where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, then either revise it or defend the surprising verdicts. Lead with the definition in the first sentence — that's your bottom line — then walk through the cases, with no preamble and no restating the question. At the end, say how confident you are in the definition overall and single out the one case or point you're least sure about.
