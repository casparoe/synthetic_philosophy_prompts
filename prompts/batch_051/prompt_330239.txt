I volunteer in a hospice, and over the years I have watched families fight about advance care plans: the capable person who signed the form, the changed person it now applies to, and everyone else claiming to speak for one or the other. I have no formal background in philosophy, but I have been turning these cases over on my own, and I would like a satirical piece for our staff newsletter. Please write a parody of an advance care planning document, in the deadpan bureaucratic style of a standard health-service form, around 300 words. It should consist of exactly five numbered clauses and nothing else: no title, no preamble, no signature block, no explanatory notes. Begin at clause one and stop at clause five, and keep each clause to one or two sentences.

The satire should target two habits of thought at once: the assumption that the person who signs today is entitled to bind a later self simply because the signatory came first, and the opposite assumption that a later self may brush the signatory's wishes away as the demands of a stranger. The comedy must come from real features of the underlying problem as a non-specialist would run into them: whether values remain stable over decades, whether a wish survives losing the memories that made it intelligible, whether disagreeing with the signed form gets treated as loss of capacity or as the illness talking, and the fact that someone still has to decide which self counts as the real one. Keep the irony entirely deadpan, with no winking at the reader. A colleague with no background in philosophy should find it legible and funny, and a specialist in the ethics of advance directives should find the satire fair rather than a caricature. British English throughout, please.
