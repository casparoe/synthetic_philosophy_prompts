My philosophy of language professor has spent the last two weeks on externalism about word meaning, and Thursday we get a new thought experiment she hasn't commented on. I'm trying to figure out what she'll say. Here's the track record she's given us:

- Twin Earth: Oscar on Earth and Toscar on Twin Earth are molecule-for-molecule identical, and both call the clear drinkable liquid in their lakes 'water'. Earth's is H2O, Twin Earth's is XYZ, and neither can tell them apart chemically. She says they're in the same psychological state, but the word means different things, so meaning isn't in the head.

- Arthritis: Bert says "I have arthritis in my thigh." She says he doesn't have a true belief about some new disease; he has a false belief about arthritis, because he defers too the doctors and the word keeps its communal meaning rather than whatever he happens to have in mind.

- Elm/beech: Ned can't tell an elm from a beech and thinks of both as "big trees." He points at a beech and calls it an elm. She says his word 'elm' still means elm, so he said something false; his cluelessness doesn't make 'elm' mean "big tree" in his mouth, because he intends to use the English word with its standard meaning.

- 1750 water: a speaker before molecular chemistry points at the stuff in a river and calls it 'water,' with an incomplete and partly wrong theory of what it is. She says the word still referred to H2O back then; the speaker's descriptions don't fix the reference.

- First baptism: in 1800 a colonist points at the liquid in a lake and says "I'll call this water." She says the word got its reference from that original sample and then passed down the historical chain, even though later speakers couldn't have described the stuff.

The new case: On a planet, in 1800, colonists point at the only liquid in the great lake and call it 'brin.' The lake is H2O. In 1900, an earthquake re-plumbs the lake so it fills with XYZ; it looks, tastes, and behaves like the old liquid and nobody notices. By 2000 their descendants use 'brin' for the stuff now in the lake and in the rain, and they believe it's the same liquid their ancestors named. The original samples from 1800 are lost. What does 'brin' mean in their mouths now—H2O or XYZ?

I can see at least two rules that fit her old verdicts. A Kripke-ish one says the original baptism fixes the reference and it stays H2O, so the descendants are just wrong about their own lake. A Putnam-ish one says reference tracks the local paradigms, so 'brin' now means XYZ and the word changed meaning without anyone noticing. Which does her track record point toward, and which earlier case is the closest analog? And if her verdicts genuinely underdetermine it, what follow-up case would settle which rule she's actually running?
