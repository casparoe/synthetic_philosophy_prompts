I'm the director of operations at a small cargo airline. Before every trip our pilots fill out a fitness-for-duty form—the usual IMSAFE self-check, illness, medication, stress, alcohol, fatigue, emotion—and initial a line saying they are fit to fly. In the last year we've had two incidents where a pilot signed off as fit and then, by pushback or mid-flight, it was obvious to everyone else that he was impaired: one had been up most of the night with a sick kid, and one had started a new medication that listed drowsiness as a side effect but said he felt fine. After the second incident I got into it with my brother-in-law, who teaches philosophy, and he kept insisting that a form that asks "are you currently impaired?" is asking the impossible. His claim was that there is no mental condition C such that just being in C puts you in a position to know you are in C; you can slide from clearly fine to clearly not-fine through small steps, and at no particular step can you reliably notice that the switch happened. He called this the anti-luminosity argument, and he tied it to the KK principle by saying that piling on higher-order checks—am I sure, am I sure I'm sure—doesn't fix the problem, since each further check can fail in the same gradual way. I obviously can't tell our CEO that epistemology says pilots can't self-certify, but I can rewrite the fitness-for-duty procedure.

Please write a reusable decision procedure, grounded in that anti-luminosity idea, that my operations team and pilots can actually run at 4 a.m. before a cargo leg. It should say when self-report alone is enough and when it isn't; specify the objective markers or external checks that have to be run at each stage; give the question order and what counts as a sufficient answer to each; say where the procedure stops with a "no" and where it gets handed to a human (me or the chief pilot) because the question is one of judgment or values rather than checkable fact; and be honest about the false-positive problem—we're a small airline, and if we ground to many pilots we can't cover the schedule, so I need to know what the procedure can't do. Then run the procedure on these three cases: (1) a pilot reports feeling fine but has been awake 19 hours and is scheduled for a two-hour leg; (2) a pilot with a mild head cold started a new medication whose package insert lists drowsiness as a possible side effect, but he reports feeling totally sharp; (3) a pilot writes "not sure" next to the fatigue line. One of these should come out in a way I won't like—please flag that one, and say exactly where the procedure still leaves room for second-guessing.
