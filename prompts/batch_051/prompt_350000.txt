As a longtime reader of rationality blogs and forums, I tend to treat knowledge claims as calibrated bets, which is why the following asymmetry in ordinary judgment bothers me. Suppose I hold one ticket in a fair lottery with ten million tickets. Before the draw, I will not say that I know my ticket will lose; the tiny chance of winning seems enough to block knowledge. Yet I will say, without hesitation, that I know I will not be able to afford a new car next year, on the basis of my salary, savings, and fixed expenses—even if I put the chance that this financial picture is misleading at one in a thousand, through an unnoticed accounting error, an unannounced layoff, or a lost inheritance letter. I am therefore comfortable claiming knowledge on evidence that is 99.9% reliable, and unwilling to claim knowledge on evidence that is 99.99999% reliable. Is there a defensible epistemic principle that draws the line where ordinary judgment draws it? The candidate justifications I can think of—a sensitivity or tracking condition, a rule excluding merely statistical evidence, a relevance or stakes requirement, a contextualist account—each look plausible until the cases are varied. Before I conclude that the asymmetry is simply an inconsistency we should dissolve one way or the other, I want the strongest case for keeping it and a verdict on whether it should be kept, reversed, or given up. Test the case against at least these variants: a ten-ticket lottery, a deterministic draw whose outcome is already fixed but unknown, and a financial claim based purely on statistical data.
