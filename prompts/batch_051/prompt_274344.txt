In my gov class we spent a whole week on whether we owe more to fellow citizens than to people in other countries, and I came up with a list of what I thought made someone count as a compatriot — like someone I definately have special obligations to:

1. They share my citizenship and are subject to the same laws as me.
2. They pay into and get stuff from the same system (taxes, schools, roads, whatever).
3. They actually live in the same place / community, so we can affect each other's lives.
4. They think of themselves as part of the same country.

The case I can't settle: my cousin has a U.S. passport but moved abroad when she was one, has never lived here since, doesn't pay U.S. taxes, doesn't vote, and says she doesn't really feel American. My classmate has lived in this town since kindergarten, is in band with me, volunteers at the same place I do, and basically knows my whole social world — but he's undocumented, so he's not a citizen and can't vote.

By my own list, my cousin seems to come out as a compatriot and my classmate doesn't, but that feels backwards. Like, do I really have more reason to care about my cousin's problems than his? Maybe my gut is wrong, but work through it. Go through my criteria honestly: does each person count, where do the criteria underdetermine it, and if the list gives a weird answer, which criterion is doing the bad work — or is citizenship itself just a stand-in for something else I should actually care about? Don't just give me a yes or no, I want to know if my criteria need fixing and which one is doing the real philosophical work.
