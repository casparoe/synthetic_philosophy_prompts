I've spent the last year or so reading about whether death is actually bad for the person who dies — the Epicurean line that once you're gone there's no you left to be harmed, the Lucretius symetry point that we don't mind the time before we were born so why mind the time after, and on the other side the deprivation account where death is bad because it takes away the life you would have had. I've noticed I've developed a habit with these arguments that I'm not sure is honest. When an argument says death is not bad, I automaticly go hunting for the flaw and I hold it to a very high bar. But when an argument says death is bad — say, because it cuts off projects you cared about — I just nod and don't check it nearly as hard. The situation where this actually operates: a close friend of mine lost her mother a while ago and kept telling me 'it's okay, it's nothing to her now,' and I couldn't push back without feeling like I was making her grief worse, so I said nothing and went off and read more. Since then my policy has been that death-is-no-loss claims have to win by a mile before I'll count them, while death-is-a-real-loss claims get a pass. Is this a defensible way to assign the burden of proof, or is it my own motivated reasoning pointing the other direction? And how would I tell the difference from the inside?
