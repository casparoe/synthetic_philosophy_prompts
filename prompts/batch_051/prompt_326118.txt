I am a PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science, and I am writing a dissertation chapter on when intentional vocabulary is legitimate in comparative cognition. The chapter is organized around Dennett's distinction among the physical stance, the design stance, and the intentional stance, together with his proposal that the patterns such stances pick out can be real patterns—roughly, projectible regularities whose recognition buys predictive leverage and compression, even when they are not visible from a lower level of description. At a recent lab meeting, a speaker described a corvid's food-caching and re-caching behavior by saying that the bird knows it was watched, then immediately added that, of course, the bird merely behaves as if it knows. No one in the room pressed what work that qualification was doing.

Please describe the most important implications of Dennett's real-patterns view, and of the three stances more generally, for how I should reason about cases of this kind. Consider a stipulated example: a corvid caches food while a conspecific is present, and later re-caches it more often when that observer is absent, in ways sensitive to whether the observer could have seen the original cache. What would follow if I treated the intentional description—for instance, that the bird acts so as to prevent the observer from pilfering the cache—as tracking a real pattern in the bird's behavior rather than as a merely instrumental predictive shortcut? I am especially interested in what the view implies about how determinate the content of such attributions should be, about when two intentional descriptions of the same behavior should be treated as competing accounts of the animal's actual state rather than as interchangeable glosses, and about what behavioral evidence would show that a given intentional description picks out no real pattern at all.
