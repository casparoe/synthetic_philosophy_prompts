I've been arguing with colleagues about whether AI development is really an arms race, and the discussion keeps going nowhere because everyone uses 'race' to mean something different. I want to stop going model-first and instead pin down what any decent theory of an AI race would actually have to explain. Take Fearon's rationalist account of war as the base — the idea that even rational, war-averse actors can still fight rather than strike a deal everyone would prefer, because of private information plus incentives to misrepresent, credible-commitment problems, and issue indivisibilities — and use it as a launching pad for a theory of AI races: competitions between labs or states where most parties would honestly prefer a slower, safer trajectory, but no one can credibly opt out. Before I read more specific models, write me the desiderata. What must a theory of AI races get right? Which cases must it handle — for instance, a race where both sides publicly say they'd prefer a pause and still keep accelerating? one with a clear leader and a catch-up rival? one where the finish line keeps receding because capabilities are open-ended? one where neither side can observe the other's true progress and both have reason to bluff? one that looks like mutual fear (a security dilemma) rather than greed for commercial dominance, and the theory has to tell them apart? Which distinctions can't be collapsed — a race versus ordinary market competition, an arms race versus a war, fear-driven versus greed-driven acceleration, a race you can unilaterally exit versus one you can't? And which results from the rationalist war literature does a good account have to recover as special cases — and which of Fearon's three mechanisms genuinely carry over to AI, rather than just being name-dropped?

I especially want you to sort the requirements into non-negotiable and merely parochial preferences, to say whether any two of them can't be jointly satisfied (thats the result I most want), and to identify which single requirement does the most work in ruling out the glib 'AI race' takes. Then, briefly, run the usual candidate frameworks against your list — expected-utility/bargaining models, old Richardson-style arms-race equations, multi-agent RL pictures, and security-dilemma accounts — but keep the weight on the criteria themselves, since scoring looks easy once the criteria are fixed. Oh, and write it in British English, spellings and all.
