I have been going in circles on an argument I constructed while arguing with a friend about fast versus slow AI takeoff. It seems to show that once you grant a human-level system the ability to modify its own source code, a hard takeoff is inevitable within days, but the same argument also seems to show, falsely, that ordinary human institutions should already have entered a finite-time singularity. I want to know exactly where the argument slides.

The argument runs as follows. (1) Suppose a system S is at human-level AI and can modify its own source code. (2) S's ability to find further improvements to S is a monotonically increasing function of S's problem-solving ability. (3) Every successful improvement raises S's problem-solving ability by some positive amount. (4) Therefore S's rate of improvement increases with each improvement. (5) In a digital system, the latency between an improvement and its deployment is bounded only by compute time; S can replace its own code in seconds rather than waiting for a new training generation. (6) Therefore, absent a binding external constraint, S undergoes a finite-time singularity in capability. (7) The candidate binding constraints are compute, data, and the difficulty of finding further algorithmic improvements. (8) Compute and data can be acquired by S's increasing ability to earn money, write code, and build datacenters; and algorithmic difficulty is itself a problem-solving task, so it cannot bind unless it grows faster than S's ability to solve problems. (9) So no candidate constraint binds, and hard takeoff follows.

The trouble is that the same argument, with only vocabulary changed, seems to prove that any self-improving institution—a corporation's R&D division, a scientific community, a software ecosystem—should undergo a finite-time singularity in capability, since their rate of discovering improvements is also a monotonic function of accumulated capability, and their deployment latency is small relative to history. Yet scientific and economic growth have been roughly exponential, not singular. So either the argument contains a subtle equivocation or invalid step, or it is valid and one of the premises is far less innocent than it looks. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I had to choose, I would most like to keep (1) through (3) and reject something around (6) or (8), but I cannot name the exact slide without losing the intuitive pressure behind recursive self-improvement. I am less interested in your yes-or-no verdict on hard takeoff than in the diagnosis: is the flaw an equivocation, a scope shift, an unjustified extrapolation from monotonicity to accelerating growth, or a false claim about the candidate constraints?
