For next week's session on whether we can explain why some nations are rich, I'd like you to write a short dialogue between a growth economist and an economic historian who disagree about what historical natural experiments actually show. The economist thinks the two Koreas and the settler-mortality strategy—roughly, using how deadly a colony was for European settlers as an instrument for the institutions they then built—give real causal evidence that institutions, not geography or culture, do the heavy lifting. The historian keeps objecting that history isnt a controlled experiment: colonial disease environments carried institutions, human-capital patterns, and crop regimes all at once, and countries aren't isolated cases, since their borders and economies were made by the same forces and constantly spill into each other. Make both characters smart and make sure they respond to each other's actual points rather than talking past one another. In particular, have the economist defend the assumption that settler mortality affects modern wealth only through institutions, and have the historian say whether spillovers between countries are a fixable statistical nuisance or a deeper problem that undermines the whole causal project.
