Please describe the most important implications of the Bayesian convergence theorems — the results that underpin the familiar claim that priors wash out as shared evidence accumulates — for how a retired experimental scientist should think about the following situation. Two research communities have examined the same body of published evidence for decades, yet they continue to assign sharply different posterior probabilities to a single, well-defined hypothesis. I would be grateful for a careful separation of the claim that the posterior opinions of two agents with different priors will eventually merge from the claim that a single agent's posterior will eventually concentrate on the truth, and for a judgement about which of these claims bears on the persistent disagreement I have described — and under what conditions the optimism encouraged by that claim is misplaced. My interest is in the epistemic lessons rather than in the mathematics.
