I'm a novelist, and I've been kicking around a thing I call 'tense discounting.' The idea is: when people value a pleasure now more than the same pleasure a year from now, that's usually chalked up to impatience, or uncertainty, or the fact that you might not be around to enjoy it. But I suspect a lot of what gets called pure time preference is really a point-of-view effect. Right now I experience myself the way a first-person narrator experiences a protagonist: full interiority, every twinge and hesitation. Future-me is more like a character in the back half of a book I haven't drafted yet. I know some facts about him, but he's thin, he doesn't breathe on the page. So of course I care about his pleasures less; it's not that they're genuinely less important, it's that I've narrated him badly.

I'd predict that the more vividly you can render your future self—like actually imagining an ordinary Tuesday in ten years, the texture of it, what you'd notice and dread and look forward to—the lower your discount rate should fall. I know there's real work on time preference and on mental simulation, but I've never seen anyone say the discount rate is a narrative variable, an artifact of how rendered the future self is. Is this actually a distinct theory, or just vividness/empathy with extra steps and a fancy name? And where does it break? What would it wrongly predict about someone who's great at imagery but still hugely impatient, or someone who barely imagines the future at all yet saves like a monk?
