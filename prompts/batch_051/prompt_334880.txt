I teach a seminar on Kantian ethics, and while rereading the Second Section of the Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals I got stuck on a colleague’s objection. Kant there considers a man who, pressed by need, borrows money and promises to repay it, though he knows that he never will. The maxim is something like: whenever I need money, I will borrow it and promise to repay, even though I know the repayment will not happen. Kant says that this maxim, if made a universal law, would make promising itself impossible, since the assurances one offers would be worthless if everyone knew they were empty. My colleague says this looks like a special case of a general principle, namely that any maxim which, if universalized, destroys a practice on which the agent is relying must fail. But that general principle seems to prove too much. His argument is:

(1) If a maxim fails because its universalization would destroy the practice it depends on, then the maxim that I will never make a promise must also fail, since a universal law of never making promises would destroy the practice of promising no less thoroughly than the false-promise maxim does.
(2) It cannot be right that one is morally forbidden simply from refraining from promises; refusing to promise is not a violation of any duty.
(3) Therefore Kant’s test, interpreted through that general principle, cannot be doing the work he thinks it does in the false-promise case.

I want to resist his conclusion, but I am not sure where to push. Where exactly does the argument go wrong, and what does the correct diagnosis reveal about the difference between a maxim that contradicts itself when universalized and a maxim whose universalization merely makes an institution unavailable? If I have to choose, I would rather say that a consistent universal law of non-promising is conceivable and the false promisor’s is not; but I want to know what principle explains that difference without simply stipulating it.
