I've been trying to pin down what it would mean for the alignment problem to be solvable "in principle," and my reactions to particular technical results keep pulling me in different directions. I want you to reconstruct the principle—or a small menu of candidate principles—that best systematizes my verdicts below, test the candidate(s) against a further case, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts have to go.

Here are my verdicts on seven concrete scenarios.

1. Someone proves that a natural formal planning problem—a partially observable finite environment in which the agent must achieve a goal while never entering any forbidden state—is PSPACE-hard to solve exactly. I take this to be a result about feasibility, not a threat to solvability in principle. The algorithm exists; it just might take an astronomical amount of time, and "in principle" should abstract away from time.

2. A no-free-lunch-style theorem says that, averaged over all possible environments, every learning algorithm has the same expected performance. I take this to be irrelevant to whether alignment is solvable in principle. We do not need a learner that works on all possible worlds; we need one that works here, or at least in realistic environments. Universal quantification over all possible environments strikes me as the wrong benchmark.

3. Someone defines an agent that is optimal in the limit on all computable environments, using a mixture over all possible programs as its prior. But the mixture is uncomputable, so no finite program implements the agent. I refuse to count this as a solution in principle, even though a mathematically well-defined "optimal policy exists." A solution must be something a concrete agent could, in principle, follow.

4. A nonconstructive proof establishes that for any finite set of safety constraints, some finite program satisfies those constraints together with a self-consistency condition; the proof gives no recipe for finding any such program. My instinct is that this is still not enough to establish solvability in principle—if no one can ever exhibit the solution, what has actually been shown? But I see the tension with (1), where I treated the mere existence of a slow algorithm as enough. Is this difference principled, or is one of these verdicts simply wrong?

5. A computable reinforcement learner is guaranteed to eventually stop making a certain class of catastrophic errors in every computable environment, but only in the limit: there is no finite time at which the guarantee kicks in, and no certificate of safety at any finite time. I take this not to count as a solution in principle, because "eventually safe" is not "safe." But I worry this is just the temporal analogue of the issue in (1) and (4).

6. A theorem shows that no algorithm can satisfy a particular formal safety property requiring the agent to assign exactly zero probability to any action a human would judge harmful, across all computable environments. Since any finite agent will assign positive probability to something under some environment, the theorem is near-trivial. I take it not to show unsolvability in principle; it shows the formal property was badly chosen. But then I owe an account of when an impossibility theorem bites because the formalization is faithful, and when it is only an artifact.

7. Here is a further case I have not settled. Suppose a diagonalization result shows that no fixed evaluator program can correctly sort deceptive from non-deceptive agents across all possible agent source codes: for every evaluator, there is an agent that fools it but that some other evaluator would catch. Under the principle you extract, does this establish that verification-based approaches to alignment are unsolvable in principle? Or is it an artifact of the bad benchmark in (2) or (6)? Or is it only a limitation of one particular method, leaving the in-principle question open? Please state your principle precisely enough that I can tell which of those three conclusions follows.

Can any one account of "solvable in principle" capture all of my verdicts (1) through (6)? If not, which verdicts should be overturned, and what is the simplest revision that makes the set coherent? Please also say what the resulting account predicts for case (7), identify which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and note the point where your account is most exposed to a counterexample of the same kind.

Please end your answer with a final line beginning "Summary:" that states in one sentence the principle you endorse, or the main reason no single principle is adequate.
