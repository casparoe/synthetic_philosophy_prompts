Alright, here's what I need. I teach an upper-level undergrad philosophy of computation course—about 15 students, one 80-minute meeting. I want an in-class game that makes students feel computational irreducibility in their bones, not just hear me define it. The point I want them to come away seeing is this: for some simple, fully deterministic systems, there is no shortcut from the rules to the state at time t+n. Any way of figuring out that state has to run through (or effectively simulate) the intermediate steps, so the only available "explanation" of a later state can sometimes be the whole history of updates that produced it.

Here's the rough shape I'm imagining, but improve it where you can. Split the class into two groups. Group A plays the automaton: each student is one cell in a one-dimensional cellular automaton, holding a card (black/white), and everyone updates each round by looking at their own card and their two neighbors' cards and applying a tiny lookup table. Use the elementary rule where next = left XOR (center OR right)—the usual poster child for complex-looking behavior—or another rule that actually produces messy output. Don't use XOR alone, since that one turns out to be additively predictable and would teach the wrong lesson (though it might make a nice decoy). Group B plays the oracles: they can inspect the initial row, they know the rule, and they can do any hand calculation they like, but they can't just simulate the whole row step by step. Their job is to announce the configuration at time 1, then time 2, then maybe time 5, each before Group A reaches it by actually updating. Award fake money based on how far ahead a correct prediction is relative to how long Group A took to just run the thing. The kicker should be that Group A's dumb mechanical updating wins, and the oracles get slower the farther ahead they try to see.

Then a debrief that connects what happened to the formal idea: when did prediction turn into re-running? Why can some systems be "solved" with a closed form (maybe demo one as a control), while in this one the only explanation of the fiftieth state is the genealogy of every step before it? Map specific moments in the game onto the parts of the idea, be honest about what the game can't reproduce—cards and students aren't cells, and nobody is disputing that the rule is simple—and say what the facilitator has to just tell them outright. Also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it: teams simulate under the table, one student stalls on a step where the row settles into something boring and insists the whole system is predictable, students don't keep there row in order, that sort of thing—and how to handle each.
