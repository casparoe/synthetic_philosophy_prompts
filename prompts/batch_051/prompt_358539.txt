I work in a policy unit that has been asked to draft a recommendation on whether the state should issue a formal apology for an episode of sixteenth-century violence committed by a chartered trading company under a now-extinct crown. Our unit is expected to follow the pattern of the state's own Commission on Historical Responsibility, and I would rather anchor the recommendation in that record than in the minister's personal instincts. The Commission's published rulings, abbreviated: it apologized, with compensation, for the wartime internment of a resident minority in the 1940s, stressing that the same state had acted and that survivors were alive; it apologized for the state's role in a nineteenth-century colonial land seizure, citing the dispossessed people's continuing landlessness; it declined to apologize for a fourteenth-century massacre in a long-defunct principality, holding that the present state is not that principality's successor in any relevant sense and that no property or institution passed from the massacre to the present state; it declined to apologize for a seventeenth-century execution carried out under a legal code and regime since superseded, where no descendants could be identified and no lasting effect traced; it apologized for the abuse of children in state-run residential facilities in the 1960s, on the ground that survivors were alive and the state's responsibility was plain; it declined to apologize for a massacre by soldiers who had acted against orders and were court-martialled at the time, saying the state had already repudiated the act; and it apologized for a 1920s forced-sterilization program even though most of its primary victims were dead, because some victims and direct descendants were still alive and the program's administrative consequences persisted.

The case before us is different in structure. In the mid-sixteenth century, a chartered trading company created by the crown of a kingdom later abolished carried out a massacre in a distant territory to secure a monopoly over that territory's main export. The company was dissolved after scandal, and its port and war college were subsequently transferred by sale and treaty to the state we now serve, which has no institutional descent from either the company or the chartering crown. The present state still owns those assets, and the briefing papers prepared for the minister note that the monopoly secured by the massacre was a nontrivial source of the capital behind early industrialization. No descendants of the massacre's victims have come forward or organized, and the victim community no longer exists under the name it bore at the time. Given this record, should we recommend an apology or none? I would like you to state the decision that best coheres with the Commission's precedents; identify the single closest prior ruling and say why a plausible rival reading of the record pulls toward the opposite outcome; spell out the principle the Commission would be adopting if it apologizes and the principle it would be adopting if it declines; and, if the record genuinely underdetermines the case, specify one further hypothetical ruling that would most cleanly disclose which principle the Commission has actually been running.
