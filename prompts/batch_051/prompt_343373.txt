I keep noticing a move in arguments about scientific credit, and I can't tell if it's a named pattern or just something people do. It usually starts with Newton and Leibniz both ending up with calculus, or Darwin and Wallace both getting to natural selection at basically the same time. Somebody then says: see, the idea was just in the air, so if neither of them had existed someone else would have found it within a few years anyway, which means it doesn't really matter who gets the credit or who gets remebered. I'm suspicious, and I want to know if this thing has a name and whether it actually holds up. The first step, from two people finding it at once to it being inevitable, seems to ignore that the cases people bring up are selected precisely because they're the ones where simultaneity happened; nobody keeps a list of all the discoveries that sat there for a century or got made exactly once. And the second step, from it being inevitable to credit not mattering, seems even stranger: if it would have taken the field another five years without the actual discoverer, then the actual discoverer did make a difference, didn't they? Is there an established concept for this whole inference, and how would you separate the ordinary fact that two people hit on the same thing roughly together from the shakier claim that the discovery was bound to happen anyway, and from the even shakier claim that credit is a wash? If there's no exact name, what would you call the two steps, and is the conclusion ever actually warranted by simultaneity alone?
