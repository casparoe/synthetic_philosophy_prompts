Consider Pascal's mugging in the following form. A mugger asks you for $5 and offers, in exchange, to bring about an outcome worth 10^200 utils with probability 10^-100, and otherwise nothing. An expected-utility maximizer with an unbounded utility function pays, since the expected value is 10^100 utils. The standard replies are: (i) bound utility above by some finite U; (ii) discount the claimed probability, on the grounds that the hypothesis that the mugger can deliver the reward is far less probable than the bare claim suggests; and (iii) refuse to act on probabilities below some minimum threshold ε. Now vary the case. The mugger offers a menu: for each integer n ≥ 1, you may pay $1 now and, with probability 10^-n, he will deliver an outcome worth 10^(2n) utils; otherwise nothing. Treat the $1 cost as 1 util, and assume the deals are independent. You may accept any finite set of deals, but you must choose the set in advance, before any uncertainty is resolved. Each individual deal has an expected value of 10^n utils. Work through what each of the three replies says about this menu. For (i): given U, which deals have positive expected value, and how does the accepted set depend on U — does the reply solve the original problem or only move the point at which you stop accepting? For (ii): use a simplified discounting rule that replaces any claimed probability p with p^2 before taking expectations; which deals are accepted, and is the policy stable if the mugger expands the menu? For (iii): given ε, which deals are accepted, and what happens as ε is lowered? Finally, address whether any finite policy is maximal: for any finite set of accepted deals, does adding one more deal always improve expected value, and what does that imply about whether the standard replies have genuinely tamed the mugging?
