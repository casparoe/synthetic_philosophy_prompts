I've been chewing on an argument for a study group and it's doing my head in, because every step looks right but the conclusion says I'm morally required to doubt the beliefs that make me act like a decent person. Can you tell me where it goes wrong?

(1) If holding one of my beliefs could change how I treat someone, I'm morally on the hook to check that it's true before I act on it.
(2) Practically every belief I actually lean on — about people's characters, about who's to blame, about what's fair, about who's lying — could change how I treat someone.
(3) So I'm on the hook to check practically every belief I lean on.
(4) Checking a belief just is doubting it: actively looking for counterevidence, trying out the possibility that I'm wrong, listening seriously to the other side.
(5) So I'm on the hook to doubt practically every belief I lean on.
(6) This includes my moral beliefs themselves. "Cruelty is wrong" is a belief I lean on, and if it were false I'd be wronging the people I blame or try to stop.
(7) So I'm on the hook to doubt that cruelty is wrong.
(8) But someone under a duty to doubt that cruelty is wrong has to hold the question open, and someone who holds it open wont reliably stand up to cruelty. A morality that requires you to hesitate about whether cruelty is wrong has undercut itself.
(9) Since that conclusion is absurd, at least one premise has to go. I'd like to keep (1) and (2) — they're what got me into this — but I can't tell which later step is doing the damage. Which step would you reject, and what does rejecting it actually cost?
