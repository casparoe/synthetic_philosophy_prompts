I am giving a colloquium talk next week defending a specific reading of the debate between Schopenhauer and Nietzsche over pity. My thesis is this: the standard opposition between Schopenhauer as pity's defender and Nietzsche as pity's critic obscures a large descriptive agreement between them. Each treats pity not as a clean, selfless orientation toward the other's good, but as a state in which one undergoes the other's suffering by imaginatively taking it up as one's own; pity therefore always carries an element of self-feeling, even self-enlargement through the other. Their real disagreement, I will argue, concerns not what pity is but whether its valorization strengthens or corrupts a life. The audience is a philosophy department colloquium: several moral sentimentalists who work on compassion and will suspect that any Nietzschean descriptive claim about pity is a covert attack on the moral standing of compassion; two Schopenhauer specialists who will suspect I am reducing his ethics to psychology; and one Nietzsche specialist who will suspect I am domesticating his polemic. Prepare my question period: the six to eight objections I will actually receive from this room, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased in the vocabulary these people would use, each with a reply I can give in about two minutes, a note on where that reply is weakest, and an indication of what I should concede on the spot rather than defend. Include the question I should most hope nobody asks.
