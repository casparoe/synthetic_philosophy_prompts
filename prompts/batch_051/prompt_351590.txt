I've been reporting a magazine feature on whether the real numbers are discovered or defined, and somewhere around interview twelve I stopped being confused and started being, possibly, overconfident. I've been calling my position the API view. The idea is that the continuum is discovered at the level of a specification and defined at the level of an implementation. The specification is the job that needs doing: a complete ordered field, no gaps on the line, an arithmetic rich enough to keep analysis running, uniqueness up to isomorphism. I want to say that specification is discovered, in the way you can discover that a certain job exists before anyone signs up to do it. The implementation is the object we actually hand in to meet the spec: Dedekind cuts, Cauchy sequences, that sort of thing. Those are defined, and there is more than one workable implementation, which is why the two constructions don't agree set-for-set but still give you the same line. So the original question is badly posed, or at least missing a layer: it's like asking whether a standard is discovered or a piece of software is written.

Before this goes in print I want it attacked. Is the API view coherent, or is it just Platonism with the metaphysics left unpaid? Does it already exist under a better name, and if it does, is my version adding anything? What does it get right and where does it actually break? My own worry list is this: the fact that the computable reals are only countable, so almost every real is noncomputable (what is the implementation when no finite rule picks the number out?); the independence of the continuum hypothesis (does the specification settle CH, or is it genuinely open?); and the suspicion that a specification is not a thing in the world at all, just a description we've invented. Give me your three strongest objections and say whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable. And can you answer in British English—my editor get's twitchy otherwise.
