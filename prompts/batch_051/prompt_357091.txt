In my jurisprudence course we have been contrasting Burkean conservatism with the principle behind Chesterton's fence — that one should not tear down a fence until one understands why it was erected. This has made me think about how far the common law already embodies that principle. A court facing an established precedent normally treats the prior decision as having a default claim to be followed: it may keep applying a decision it believes was wrongly reached unless something further, such as changed circumstances or reliance, justifies overruling it. By contrast, a court deciding a question of first impression encounters no inherited rule and simply decides it on the merits. What view of the value of inherited legal decisions does this asymmetric burden of justification presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely coheres with, and say how the doctrine of precedent would have to change if courts were required to justify every decision solely by the best present reasons. Please write the answer in British English.
