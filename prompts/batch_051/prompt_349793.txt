I have been collecting my own settled and unsettled reactions to sentences in the family of Moore's paradox, and I want you to do reflective equilibrium on them. Each case below is a sentence uttered by a single speaker, about the present moment, addressed to me. These are my actual verdicts, not the received view, and some of them already strike me as hard to reconcile. I want you to construct a precise principle—or a small set of principles—that gets as close as possible to my verdicts, test it on the extra cases I append, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which I should revise. I would rather you commit to one account than lay out a menu of options.

The cases.

Sentence (1): *It's raining, but I don't believe it.*

My verdict: I find this unassertable. The sentence could be true—someone can be oblivious to the weather—but the speaker seems to pull the rug out from under the very act of telling me about the rain. I would distrust the utterance, and not only the rain report.

Sentence (2): *It's raining, but I don't know it.*

My verdict: bad in the same family, and maybe worse. The speaker gives me the weather while disclaiming the very backing that such a claim is normally expected to carry. I am less sure about the worse part, though.

Sentence (3): *I believe it's raining, but it isn't.*

My verdict: perfectly fine. Rueful, self-deprecating, informative: an ordinary way to confess that one's belief was wrong.

Sentence (4): *It's raining, but John doesn't believe it.*

My verdict: fine. It says something about John, and I could say it directly to John without the oddness I feel in (1).

Sentence (5): *It was raining, but I didn't believe it at the time.*

My verdict: fine. A past failure of belief is perfectly reportable.

Sentence (6): *It may be raining, but I don't believe it is.*

My verdict: fine. I can hold a possibility open without embracing it.

Sentence (7): *I don't believe it's raining, but it is raining.*

My verdict: this is where I keep flipping. On paper it seems indistinguishable from (1) in content—rain, plus my failure to believe it—but I can hear it as a coherent self-correction: first I confess where I stand, then I state how things are. Is that felt difference real, or just an artifact of the order of clauses?

Sentence (8): *It's raining, but I suspect it isn't.*

My verdict: as bad as (1); maybe worse, since the speaker does not merely lack belief but leans the other way. But I cannot identify the principle that would rank degrees of badness here.

My working principle is roughly this: by asserting a proposition, I publicly represent myself as being in the states an asserter is normally expected to be in—centrally, believing that proposition and knowing it. So an utterance that first asserts p and then explicitly disavows one of those represented states is self-defeating; an utterance that merely reports a cognitive state, even a mistaken one, is not. The rule cleanly rejects (1), (2), and (8), and admits (3) through (6). But it seems forced to reject (7) too, just as much as (1), since the disavowed state and the content are the same and only the order differs. I cannot tell whether the rule should correct my feeling about (7), or whether it is missing something about the difference between starting with the world and starting with the mind.

Here are three further cases I want the principle to adjudicate before I accept it:

(a) *It's raining, but I have no evidence that it is.*

(b) *I know it's raining, but it isn't.*

(c) *It's raining, but I wouldn't say so.*

One caution on (b): if knowledge requires truth, then (b) cannot be true; but I want to know whether it goes wrong in the same self-defeating way as (2), or just in the ordinary way that any flat-footed contradiction goes wrong.

Please give me a committed account, not a menu of options. State the best principle precisely, clause by clause, each clause tied to the case that forces it; say exactly how it handles (7) and the three added cases; and identify which of my stated verdicts should be overturned or sharpened. If saving every one of my verdicts would force the principle into epicycles, say so and tell me which verdict I should drop instead.
