Okay so I've been chewing on this idea I call the Prior Half-Life, and I want someone to tell me if it's real or if I've just reinvented something badly.

The background: everyone says priors wash out. With enough shared data, two Bayesians with different priors end up agreeing because the likelihood swamps the prior. But the convergence results I've seen are asymptotic—they tell you they agree in the limit, not how much data you need before the disagreement stops mattering.

My idea is to make that finite-time. Define the half-life of a prior as the number of observations it takes, on expectation, for the expected KL divergence between two Bayesians' predictive distributions to fall below some epsilon, where the two start from a fixed gap in prior odds and see the same data. My claim is that this behaves like a radioactive half-life in a specific sense: doubling the prior disagreement in log odds costs you only a constant amount of extra data, because each observation multiplies the prior odds by the likelihood ratio, so the log prior odds get washed out linearly. And there's a diagnostic corollary: if two priors differ on a hypothesis the data can never distinguish (two unfalsifiable cosmological models, say), the prior half-life is infinite, which tells you the disagreement is rationally unresolvable and not just stubborn.

The part I'm unsure about: I know the half-life depends on the divergance measure and on epsilon, and maybe that makes it arbitrary rather than principled. Also I might be smuggling in a bad assumption that the data are i.i.d. and the model is correctly specified.

Hit me with the three strongest objections to the Prior Half-Life idea, and tell me whether any of them is fatal or whether it's just a clumsier version of something people already use.
