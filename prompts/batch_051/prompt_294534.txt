I've been arguing with a friend about whether annihilationism can hold the middle ground between eternal conscious torment and universal salvation. She thinks annihilationism is the position that takes proportionality most seriously: finite sins merit a finite response, so the unrepentant are not tortured forever but are simply removed from existence. Someone in our group replied with the following argument, which feels too quick to me, but I cannot locate exactly where it goes wrong.

(1) A punishment is proportionate only if the total harm it inflicts does not exceed the gravity of the offence.
(2) A sin committed by a finite creature has finite gravity.
(3) Therefore, a proportionate punishment for any such sin must inflict only finite total harm.
(4) Eternal conscious torment inflicts infinite total harm, since a finite harm extended without end has no finite sum.
(5) But annihilation inflicts infinite total harm too: it deprives the person of an endless future of goods they would otherwise have enjoyed.
(6) So annihilationism faces exactly the same proportionality objection as eternal torment.
(7) Therefore, someone who rejects eternal torment on proportionality grounds cannot consistently stop at annihilationism; consistency pushes them all the way to universalism.

I suspect that "total harm" does different work in (4) and (5), and that (5) is smuggling in a baseline the annihilationist is not committed to — namely, that the person would otherwise have an endless good future. But I am not sure whether those are real defects or just my unease. Please assess the argument: is it valid? If not, at which step does it first fail, and on what reading of the relevant term? If it is valid, which premise is doing the controversial work, and why accept it? Lead with your bottom line — either "valid" or "invalid, and the first bad step is N" — and keep the entire answer under 450 words.
