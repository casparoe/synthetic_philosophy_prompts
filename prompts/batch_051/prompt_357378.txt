I've been reading about reflective equlibrium — the idea that you justify a view by working back and forth between general principles and particular judgments, revising whichever side needs it until the whole thing hangs together — and I kept thinking about Sidney Lumet's 12 Angry Men. The jury room looks like an almost too-perfect case study. The men walk in with a near-unanimous verdict and a stock of rough rules of thumb (about those people, about how a defendant ought to behave under questioning, about what a credible witness sounds like). Then one juror starts poking at the details, and over the afternoon some of the specific judgments and some of the general rules get revised in something like the way the method describes.

What I'd like is a reading of the film through that lens that doesn't just announce 'the deliberation is reflective equilibrium' and stop. Does the jury actually reach something like reflective equilibrium, or are they doing a narrower thing — looking for the most coherent story about the evidence, which isn't quite the same as justifying a normative view the way Rawls had in mind?
