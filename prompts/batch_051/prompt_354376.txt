Ursula K. Le Guin's story *The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas* is usually taught as a parable about utilitarianism: the city's happiness is purchased by the permanent, deliberate misery of one child. I would like a reading of the story through a less frequently paired lens, Jeremy Bentham's attack on natural rights. Bentham held that rights are not things persons possess prior to law and society; they are created by legal arrangements and justified only insofar as they serve utility. On his view, natural and imprescriptible rights are not so much facts to be discovered as elevated rhetoric, and the demand that they be respected regardless of consequences amounts to a license for anyone who dislikes a law to claim an exemption from it. On that view, the child in the cellar has no natural right not to be there; the only serious question is whether the institution of Omelas makes for more happiness, all things considered, than its feasible alternatives.

Write an essay reading Le Guin's story through Bentham's position and using the story to pressure-test that position. Address the following. First, how would a Benthamite assess Omelas, given that the story seems deliberately engineered to rule out the usual empirical doubts—the arrangement is stable, the child cannot be helped, and the only available protest is to walk away? Second, does the story's moral force depend on exactly the kind of pre-legal right Bentham denies, or can a Benthamite account for the judgment that something is wrong even when the bargain pays off? Third, what are the ones who walk away actually doing—claiming a right, making a utilitarian refusal grounded in their own integrity, or merely opting out in a gesture that leaves the child no better off?

Reach and defend a clear conclusion. Is the story better read as a refutation of Bentham's skepticism about natural rights, or as an illustration of how little the language of rights adds once the underlying costs, benefits, and alternatives have all been specified? Do not slip into a balanced-sides ending; take the side your argument supports and be explicit about the strongest consideration against you.
