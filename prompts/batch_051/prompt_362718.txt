I'm a journalist covering sleep and consciousness, and I keep tripping over a debate that sounds like stoner philosophy but has real experimental teeth. Norman Malcolm's line is that the question "what did you experience last night while you were asleep?" is badly formed: being asleep just is being without experience, so when you wake up and tell me a dream you're confabulating a story and then mistaking it for a memory of an experience. Dennett's line is different—he thinks dreaming is something like a cassette inserted into memory at the moment of waking, so you come to have a story, but it was never "played" during sleep. The difference matters for how I report on all the EEG and fMRI dream-decoding studies: are they decoding an experience, or the construction of a report?

I want you to help me design the cleanest test you can of the claim that dreams are experiences during sleep rather than after-the-fact constructions. I'm not asking for a literature review or citations—just the design. What would you measure (serial awakenings in different sleep stages, lucid dreamers signaling with eye movements, time-stamped dream reports, neural decoding of report content, whatever you'd pick), what manipulation would you run, and what result would you take as evidence for "experience during sleep" versus "story built at waking"? Then do the uncomfortable part: suppose the study comes out looking strongly like there is a real experience. What would that still leave open about whether it was conscious in the way waking experience is, or about whether the self that seems to undergo the dream exists before waking? And if the study came out the other way, what would that refute versus merely fail to detect? I'm trying to figure out wether the debate is empirically settleable at all, or if the two sides are just disagreeing about what "a dream is an experience" means.
