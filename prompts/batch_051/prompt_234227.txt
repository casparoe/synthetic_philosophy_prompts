and here's what the student wrote:

Noether's theorem says that for any symmetry of a physical system there is an associated conserved quantity. The standard cases are time translation symmetry giving energy, spatial translations giving momentum, and rotations giving angular momentum, but the theorem is completely general and also covers discrete symmetries like parity, which gives a conserved parity charge. The usual way to understand the explanation is that the laws don't single out any particular moment, and that is what explains energy conservation: since the system behaves the same at every time, the total energy has to stay the same. Energy conservation is just the statement that the system's own motion is unchanged when you shift it in time. There is a subtlety about the direction of explanation though. Noether's theorem is a biconditional in the relevant cases, so symmetry and conservation are mathematically equivalent, and from that I'd say neither one really explains the other; saying symmetries explain conservation is just a loose way of talking. In practice we still say energy is conserved because our best theories are time-translation invariant, and that's all the theorem is really telling us.
