In my seminar on the epistemology of disagreement I have been defending the following view, and I want it tested rigorously. I hold that the common prior assumption — the requirement that rational agents with common knowledge of one another's rationality and of the structure of the situation can differ in their posterior beliefs only because they have observed different signals — is not an empirical hypothesis about how actual degrees of belief are formed, but a constraint of coherent Bayesian modeling. Once the state space has been specified richly enough, including the private information each agent received and the process that generated it, there is nothing left for a difference in priors to be; what looks like prior heterogeneity is always a diagnostic that the model has omitted part of the information structure. On my view, the correct response to apparent disagreement between ideally rational agents is never to posit divergent priors but to enrich the state space until the disagreement reappears as a difference of information.

Tell me what would count against this way of thinking. I want the strongest considerations — formal results, experimental findings, philosophical objections, possible cases — that a committed defender of my position should acknowledge as counting against it, even if we ultimately answer them. Distinguish what would be fatal, meaning the position is incoherent or collapses into the rival view that priors are genuinely agent-relative and disagreement can be brute, from what would be merely awkward, meaning the position survives but needs qualifications. I would also like an honest verdict on whether the position as I have stated it has been insulated so thoroughly that little could count against it, and whether that is a problem for a view of this kind.

Give me exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, ranked from most to least threatening to the position, with no introduction or conclusion.
