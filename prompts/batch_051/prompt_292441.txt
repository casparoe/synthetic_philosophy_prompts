Okay here's one I've been chewing on. Psychiatrists diagnose mental disorders using a manual whose criteria are almost entirely about symptoms, self-report, behavior, and how the person is functioning — not about brains. Two people can both end up with a diagnosis of major depressive disorder while their brain scans look completely different, and for plenty of diagnoses there is no known neural marker at all; the manual itself is pretty explicit that it is not classifying by cause or mechanism. But the wider system treats the diagnostic label as a real kind: insurers reimburse by diagnosis, hospitals admit on the strength of it, and drugs get approved to treat depression as if the category picks out one stable thing. I'm trying to work out what theory of mind, if any, this practice presupposes. Does it commit the system to a functionalist picture, on which mental kinds are defined by what they do and can be multiply realized in different underlying stuff? Or could a type-identity theorist sign off on the whole thing by treating the label as a pragmatic billing category rather than a natural kind? And what about someone who thinks mental states are perfectly real but these diagnostic categories are gerrymandered? Reconstruct the implicit theory as best you can, then seperate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and point out whether different parts of the system — the manual, the insurance codes, the drug approvals — presuppose the same thing or pull in different directions. At the end, tell me how confident you are in the reconstruction and what single point you're least sure of.
