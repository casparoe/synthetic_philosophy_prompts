On the LessWrong-adjacent forum I still read, someone was arguing that uploaded or forked minds shouldn't worry about whether the same self survives, and their whole case leaned on Dennett's idea that the self is a center of narrative gravity — a useful abstraction over the story an organism tells about itself, real the way a center of gravity is real, but not a thing in the brain you could preserve or copy. The conclusion they wanted to license was big: if there was never a unitary thing there in the first place, then continuity through teleportation, forking, gradual neural replacement, and the like is arguably a category mistake, and these cases just reduce to questions about which narrative we decide to extend.

I've read enough Dennett to get the point, but I keep suspecting that analogy is carrying more weight then it's rated for. You're a professional philosopher of mind who also knows the rationalist/AI-alignment literature; think carefully, and maybe start by brainstorming correspondences before you commit. Give the explicit mapping: in the self, what plays the role of the mass distribution, the resulting field, the center point itself, its uniqueness, its causal inertness, the fact that an outsider can compute it, and the fact that it moves when the distribution moves? Then push on where the mapping breaks, and separate the disanalogies that actually matter for the upload/fork conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic. In particular I want to know whether the analogy quietly imports that the self is unique, causally inert, objective from the outside, and fairly stable, and which of those imported features does the real argumentative work. Does the conclusion survive once they are on the table? And if the center-of-gravity picture fails under load, is there a better analogy for what a self is? American English, please.
