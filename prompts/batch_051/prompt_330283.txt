The debate over what the experience of existential emptiness means has persisted for decades across clinical, existentialist, and contemplative traditions, and the strange thing is that all three seem to begin from nearly the same first-person report while drawing incompatible conclusions. One tradition treats the felt void as a deficit: a signal that affect, meaning, or self-structure has somehow collapsed and should be repaired through treatment, relationship, or the restoration of ordinary activity. A second treats the same felt void as a disclosure: in the felt absence of any external source of significance, one supposedly encounters the truth that one is radically free, and the effort to fill or cure the void is itself a form of evasion. A third treats the void as neither symptom nor revelation but as an unstable condition that can be refined through sustained practice into a settled spaciousness, cultivated rather than eliminated or heroized. The first-person descriptions across these traditions are strikingly similar: a hollow or missing centre, a fading of the felt importance of projects, a sense of standing outside the concerns that once organized daily life. Yet each side acknowledges the others' reports without moving. I am not asking you to adjudicate which tradition is right. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not a factual disagreement about what causes or follows from the experience, but a disagreement about the epistemic authority of first-person feeling—about whether such a feeling can disclose anything beyond itself, and about which features of the experience are data and which are interpretation. Is that diagnosis correct? If not, is the real disagreement verbal, empirical, normative, or a difference in unarguable starting intuitions? And given the diagnosis you defend, would anything actually count as progress here, or should we expect the rival interpretations to persist because the underlying question is ill-posed?
