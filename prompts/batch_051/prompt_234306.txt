I have been trying to settle whether any of the three moral cultures—honor, dignity, victimhood—is morally superior, and I keep hitting the same wall: almost every defense picks a failure mode of the rival culture and then declares victory without saying what the defended culture is for in non-ideal conditions. So I built a small theory I would like you to assess. I call it the bail theory of moral order.

The idea is to treat each culture type as a different strategy for posting collateral against wrongdoing. In an honor culture, each person is his own collateral: your reputation for retaliation is what makes you too expensive to wrong, and the cost of a wrong is borne largely by the victim, who must enforce or lose standing. A dignity culture pools collateral in an institution: the state or a company holds the collective capacity to sanction, and the tradeoff is that everyone must accept the pool's payout decisions, even when the pool wrongly refuses or overpays. A victimhood culture, as I understand it, is a private reinsurance market for the collateral that institutions decline to post: a wronged party appeals to an audience of potential supporters, and supporters underwrite the claim with their own attention and moral credit, competing partly to be seen as the most committed underwriter. The variable I use across all three is not the content of the moral rules but what I call settlement latency: the number of intermediaries a claim must pass through, and the time it takes, before a violator pays.

My central claim is that the question "which is morally superior?" is underdetermined until you specify what I call the environment's bail capacity: the supply of credible third parties, the reach and stability of audiences, and the persistence of reputations over time. Honor has low settlement latency and works where bail capacity is low but social memory is high; its distinctive failure is escalation spirals, because the collateral is loaded and can discharge too early. Dignity has higher latency and works where institutional bail capacity is high and trusted; its distinctive failure is procedural laundering, where an institution's valid process validates the wrong outcome, and the loser has no legitimate private recourse. Victimhood has variable latency and works where institutions have high capacity but low trust; its distinctive failure is overbidding, where the most legible story of vulnerability outcompetes the strongest claim, and the market rewards the wrong underwriters.

I suspect I have not really invented anything, but I also suspect the standard "honor is violent, dignity is rights-respecting, victimhood is fragile" framing is too thin. Tell me honestly: is the bail/latency apparatus a real concept or just an economic metaphor? What are the three strongest objections to the strong claim that none is intrinsically superior, and are any fatal? What does the theory wrongly count as a failure? Also: is there a fourth implicit option I'm missing because my three-way taxonomy does not exhaust the possible collateral arrangements?
