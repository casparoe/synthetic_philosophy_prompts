I'm not a lawyer or a philosopher, just someone who has been turning an idea over for a while, and I'd like you to think through how it would actually operate if a country tried it. My starting thought is that a child has an interest in reaching adulthood with their life options still open — the ability to choose or leave a religion, pick a kind of work, get more education, form relationships, and move between communities. Parents can close off those options long before the child is old enough to object, and not only through outright abuse or neglect, which is already illegal. They can do it quietly: pulling a child out of school early, refusing standard medical care, isolating a child from anyone unlike the family, or raising a child inside a closed community so that they cannot function outside it.

Here is the scheme I want you to analyze. Suppose a country gives every child a legally enforceable right to an open future. At birth, each child is assigned a state-employed advocate whose job is to stand in for the child's future self. The advocate has a large caseload of several hundred children; she is promoted partly on how many of her petitions succeed in court and partly on how the children she watched score on well-being surveys when they turn eighteen. Whenever she believes a parental decision will foreclose a basic life option — literacy and numeracy, basic health, the practical ability to leave one's community later, meaningful exposure to information about other ways of life, or the chance to form close relationships outside the family — she may petition a family court. The court cannot remove the child or jail the parents. Its remedies are narrower: an order to enroll the child in a school or recognized equivalent, a vaccination or medical-examination order, an order guaranteeing the child regular access to a public library or the internet, or an order requiring the child to attend some activities outside the family's community. Parents who ignore these orders can be fined or lose tax benefits attached to the child, but they retain custody.

I am not asking only whether the scheme is good or bad. I want you to work out how it would behave once it was running. What incentives would it create for parents, for the advocates, for the courts, and for the agencies involved? How would parents game it or route around it, and how would the people inside the system game it from within? Where is it most likely to fail or to produce the opposite of what it intends, and which of those failures could be fixed without turning the scheme into something entirely different?
