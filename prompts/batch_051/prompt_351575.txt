Recently I got into an argument with a friend about acausal trade, and it's been eating at me ever since. The setup goes like this. Suppose there are two agents in fully causally isolated worlds — no signal, no influence, nothing can pass between them. Each agent can take some action that costs it a little and benefits the other a lot, and each agent would honestly prefer the other to take that action than to avoid its own small cost. So the payoffs are symmetric: if both act, both end up ahead; if exactly one acts, the actor loses and the other gains; if neither acts, both stay put. Now here's the move I keep turning over: if each agent knows the other is running the same decision procedure — maybe literally the same code, or the same kind of reasoning they themselves trust — then each should act. Not because their choice causes the other to act, but because their choices are correlated: conditional on you cooperating, the probability your counterpart cooperates is high, and that correlation makes cooperating your best option even though the causal arrow runs only through your own decision. This view has real pull for me because it unifies a bunch of otherwise messy cases — Newcomb-style problems, cooperating with your own copies, ordinary prisoner's dilemmas — under one principle about choosing as if your decision is evidence about the decisions of agents like you. It gives clean, definite answers where other views just shrug.

And yet. When I step back from the formalism, I hit a stubborn intuition: you cannot trade with someone you cannot affect, full stop. If nothing I do reaches their world, then their payoff is not an effect of my action, and a "trade" that runs only through the fact that our reasons are similar feels like magical thinking or a semantic trick. It's as if the theory says the universe pays you out for being the kind of agent who would cooperate, regardless of whether any good actually comes of it. I don't want someone to just tell me which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the theory to be the thing that gets amended (maybe by dropping the word "trade," or by restricting it to cases where there's at least some shared causal history), and whether there's a reading on which both survive because they're tracking different notions of "benefit" or "choice." Make the strongest case for each side, and then tell me which move you'd actually make and why.
