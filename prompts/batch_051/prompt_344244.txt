For most of my adult life, first as a software developer and now as an engineering manager, I have relied on a specific rule of thumb for predicting and explaining human behavior: when a person gives a principled or values-based explanation for a choice, I set that stated reason aside as an after-the-fact justification and look instead at the actual incentives, monitoring, and penalties in play. When a colleague tells me she refactored a module because she cares about code quality, I privately assume the refactor happened because it raised her status, lightened her own future workload, or protected her from blame. When a politician explains a vote in terms of principle, I assume the operative factors are donors, their electoral base, and re-election risk. I even apply the rule to myself when I catch myself explaining why I broke a habit or changed my mind.

I have read just enough about Han Feizi to notice that my rule sounds Legalist: I understand him as arguing that a ruler cannot reliably govern through people's virtue or through moral example, and should instead rely on fixed laws, calibrated rewards, and punishments that target self-interest. I am not a careful student of the text, and I am not asking for exegesis. I only want to know whether my everyday version of that stance is defensible.

As an engineer, my daily use of the rule looks like this: in a meeting where someone invokes team cohesion or code quality, I translate each stated reason into a prediction about what people will do when no one is watching, or when the reward structure changes. I have been right often enough that the rule now feels like a reliable instrument rather than a theory I would defend.

I want from you a committed assessment, not a survey of possible views. Is this policy a defensible epistemic shortcut, or is it a bias dressed up as a principle? If it is partly defensible, what more precise restatement would keep its predictive power without its possible blind spots? If it is dangerous, where is it most likely to fail? Most of all: under what conditions does virtue-, norm-, or idea-driven behavior do real causal work that a purely incentive-based model would systematically miss, and how should an ordinary manager decide, in advance, whether a given situation is one of those?
