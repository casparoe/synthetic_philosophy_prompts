I've had this policy for years and a friend recently called it a crude "science says X, so the folk category is fake" reflex, which made me want to test whether I can actually defend it. The rule is: when an everyday classification and a scientific one come apart, I treat the scientific one as the real one and the everyday one as a useful fiction at best, a mistake at worst. Tomatoes are fruits, whales aren't fish, koalas aren't bears, and there's no biological kind "vegetable" — that's just a kitchen category. I catch myself doing this with social categories too: if personality psychology hasn't validated some type as a reliable construct, I try to talk about it as if it doesn't exist, not as if it's "real in another sense."

What bothers me is that I don't run the same rule across the board. When physicists says a table is mostly empty space, I don't conclude that tables are unreal or that "solid" is false — I say physics is describing a lower level and the table is still there at the everyday level. But when biology says whales are mammals rather than fish, I do conclude that calling a whale a fish is just wrong, full stop. So I'm drawing a line between scientific redescriptions that overrule a folk category and scientific redescriptions that are just true at a different level, and I can't state the principle that puts something on one side or the other. Is there a defensible version of my habit, or am I basically deferring to whichever science sounds more fundamental at the time? If there is a defensible version, what does it actually commit me to — would I have to give up "fish" but keep "table," and is there any way to tell in advance which ordinary categories get to survive?
