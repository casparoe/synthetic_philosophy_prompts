I've been reading a long argument on a forum about how historians of science and mathematics should talk about older work, and the dispute keeps stalling on an undefined word: anachronism. One side says that any description that uses concepts, notation, or categories the original author did not possess is anachronistic, and that terms such as "anticipated," "precursor," and "implicitly proved" should be retired. The other side replies that a rule that strict would either leave the past trapped in its own vocabulary or make it impossible to explain to a modern reader what an old argument was actually doing; by that standard, almost any useful exposition of Archimedes would count as anachronism. I want a working definition of "anachronistic reading" in the historiography of science and mathematics—not a survey of the positions people have taken, but a criterion I can actually apply, which classifies the cases below correctly or tells me exactly where it has to be amended.

Here are the cases I want it to get right. (1) A textbook says Newton and Leibniz invented the calculus, even though Newton wrote in terms of fluxions and ultimate ratios, Leibniz in terms of differentials, and neither used limits in anything like the modern sense. (2) A historian says the Babylonians knew the Pythagorean theorem because a surviving tablet lists Pythagorean triples. (3) A researcher says Archimedes' quadrature of a parabolic segment is early integral calculus, or at least that he was working with a limiting idea. (4) A commentary says Aristotle's physics lacks the concept of inertia and therefore gives the wrong account of projectile motion. (5) A teacher rewrites a Euclidean proof in modern algebraic notation while explicitly telling students that Euclid had no algebra. (6) A popular history says Democritus anticipated Dalton's atomic theory. (7) A philosopher says Ptolemy's epicycles just fit a finite trigonometric series to the observed planetary positions—mathematically true, but unintelligible to Ptolemy himself. (8) A survey says medieval alchemy was chemistry without the concept of a chemical element.

My own unstable view is that (5) is clearly not anachronistic and (6) clearly is, and that the difference should have something to do with using a later framework as an instrument of explanation rather than attributing its content to an author as a belief. But (7) seems to be an instrument too, and I am reluctant to call it innocent. Please take a definite position. State the definition, run it through all eight cases in order, say which verdict it forces you to bite a bullet on, and defend those surprising verdicts rather than hedging. If the right answer distinguishes two or three species of anachronism, that is fine, as long as the final criterion is stated plainly.
