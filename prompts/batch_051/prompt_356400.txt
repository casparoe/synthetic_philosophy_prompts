I run a small local-election monitoring group, and after we nearly published a fake "candidate confession" video last month, I'm the one stuck writing our policy on which recordings to trust. The asymmetry I keep noticing: if a volunteer says he heard the mayor say something ugly, we treat it as heresay and sit on it. If he has a phone video of the mayor saying the same thing, nobody argues; it's on tape, end of story. We know the video can now be fabricated with no more effort than it takes to coach a human source, but the default trust in recordings has barely moved. The cleanest statement I can find of the principle that trust in reports comes from experience is in Hume's An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, Section X, "Of Miracles":

"To apply these principles to a particular instance; we may observe, that there is no species of reasoning more common, more useful, and even necessary to human life, than that which is derived from the testimony of men, and the reports of eye-witnesses and spectators. This species of reasoning, perhaps, one may deny to be founded on the relation of cause and effect. I shall not dispute about a word. It will be sufficient to observe, that our assurance in any argument of this kind is derived from no other principle than our observation of the veracity of human testimony, and of the usual conformity of facts to the reports of witnesses."

Hume's writing before cameras, so the question is whether that picture extends. If it does, isn't the special authority video gets over human testimony just a stale empirical habit now? Or is there still a principle—something about the causal chain in a recording, how fakes surface, or the difference between a person lying and an artifact—that keeps the asymmetry justified after deepfakes? I'm looking for the strongest version of that principle and an honest verdict on whether it actually survives.
