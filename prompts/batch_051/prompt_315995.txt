I am a retired physicist who has recently been reading philosophy, and I have been turning over Parfit's hitchhiker case and the idea of rational irrationality. I would like to understand what happens to the case under a modification that I have not seen stated explicitly.

In the familiar version, a traveller is stranded in a desert and will die unless a passing driver takes him to safety. The driver will take him only if he promises to pay a substantial sum on arrival. The promise is not legally enforceable, the two will never meet again, and non-payment carries no further penalty; the driver is, however, a good enough judge of character not to accept promises that will not actually be kept. The traveller knows that, once safe, he will have no self-interested reason to pay; ex hypothesi he is a rational egoist, so he would not pay. A bare promise therefore cannot be believed. The standard conclusion is that the traveller needs to bind himself in some way that will make his future self keep the promise even though doing so is then contrary to his interests. The case is meant to show that it can be rational to bring about a future state in which one acts irrationally.

Now vary the case. Suppose there is no device that can guarantee later compliance. Instead, for each probability p between 0 and 1, the traveller can take, before the ride, a pill that works as follows: at the moment of payment, with probability p it induces the disposition to keep the promise even though keeping it is then against the traveller's interests, and with probability 1-p it has no effect, so the future self remains the same rational egoist and does not pay. Neither the traveller nor the driver can know in advance which outcome will occur in this particular case; both know only p. The driver can verify which p-pill has been taken, and will give the ride just in case p is at least some threshold t, with t strictly between 0 and 1.

Let the utility of being delivered to safety be R, the disutility of paying be C, with 0 < C < R, and the utility of being left in the desert be 0. On the standard expected-utility analysis, which pill should the traveller take, if any? Does this modified case still involve rational irrationality, or has it been reduced to a gamble over one's own future dispositions? And what becomes of the original lesson once the driver can be satisfied by a sufficiently high probability of compliance rather than by certainty? I am less interested in the arithmetic than in knowing whether the philosophical point survives the change.
