I'm a retired physicist — did statistical mechanics for thirty years, now spending my time in philosophy of mind — and I've got a talk coming up at a reading group that's mostly machine learning people at the local university. My thesis, roughly, is that the usual stochastic-parrot / just-a-fancy-interpolator objections settle much less than people think, because once you try to make "novelty beyond the training distribution" precise, most of the criteria on offer either over-fire (they'd make a lot of ordinary human theorizing count as non-novel) or under-fire (they'd make trivial LLM outputs count as creative leaps). I want to end by proposing that the only serious remaining candidate for a genuinely new intuitive leap has to do not with where a network's output sits relative to its training data in high-dimensional space, but with whether the system can deliberately change the problem representation it's optimizing over — the way a physicist moves from particles to fields, or re-writes a problem in terms of an effective variable. I'll concede that current LLMs show little sign of doing that, while humans plainly do it. I think that's a better frame for the whole "can machines be creative" argument, but I know it's not watertight.

The audience: mostly ML researchers, some of whom do NLP and have read the scaling and emergence literature, and they're already convinced recent LLMs are fancy interpolators; two cogntive scientists who think the "it just interpolates" framing is sloppy and will be with me on the negative part of my talk but not on my positive proposal; and an old colleague of mine, a philosopher of science, who will jump on any definition I leave wobbling. They'll be polite, but they will go for the weak joints.
