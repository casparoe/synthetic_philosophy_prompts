I'm giving a paper next month at a workshop on long-run economic history, and I want to be ready for the question period. My thesis is that Malthus's principle of population was not falsified by the nineteenth-century escape from the Malthusian trap, and should instead be read as a conditional mechanism: where technology and the productivity of land are stagnant, rising real incomes are pulled back toward subsistence through the interaction of fertility and mortality (the positive and preventive checks), so that average living standards are held near a subsistence ceiling. On this reading, the industrial era did not refute Malthus; it altered the conditions under which the mechanism operates, above all through sustained productivity growth that may itself have been partly a response to population pressure and to changing institutions. I call the position "right but early": the mechanism is genuine, but its domain is historically bounded, and marking that boundary is a legitimate correction of the theory's scope rather than an ad hoc rescue. The audience is mostly economic historians and historical demographers who work on real-wage series, the European marriage pattern, and the timing of the fertility transition, plus two philosophers of science who work on idealization and model-based explanation; several of the historians are openly hostile to grand theory that survives every apparent counterexample.
