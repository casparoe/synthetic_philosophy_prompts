I'm giving a work-in-progress talk next month to a seminar run jointly by our statistics department and the philosophy of science group. The thesis I'm defending is that the reference class problem should be treated as a permanent feature of single-case probability, not as a defect that better methodology will eventually remove. My running example is a patient at a tumor board: the same clinical history yields a five-year survival estimate that swings by roughly thirty percentage points depending on whether we place the patient in the class of patients with this histology, patients of this age and comorbidity profile, patients treated at this institution, or patients enrolled in recent trials. I argue that after all the data is in, choosing among these classes still requires a judgment about which similarities matter to us, and that this judgment is normative and interest-relative rather than settled by the facts. I therefore suggest we stop speaking of the probability for the individual case as if it were simply out there to be discovered, and instead present ranges tied to explicitly defended reference classes.

The audience is not unsympathetic, but it is the wrong audience for a purely deflationary message. It will include several statisticians who work on causal inference and risk prediction and who are disposed to say the problem dissolves once one conditions on the right set of covariates; two formal epistemologists who regard single-case chance as a mind-independent physical quantity that rational credence should track; and one philosopher of biology who thinks natural-kind classifications mark the privileged reference classes. They will also care a great deal about the downstream cost of my view for decision procedures, clinical guidelines, and legal standards of proof. They are polite, but they will not let me get away with treating the reference class problem as a reason to stop asking for precision.

Please prepare a Q&A prep sheet for me: the objections I am most likely to hear from this particular room, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way these people would actually phrase them, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what I should concede up front, what I should hold even under pressure, and which question I should be most afraid of — the one I'm hoping nobody in the room thinks to ask.
