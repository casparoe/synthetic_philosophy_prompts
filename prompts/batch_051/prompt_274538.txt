In a social choice seminar we went through Sen's liberal paradox with the usual Lady Chatterley's Lover setup. There are three alternatives: x = only Prude reads, y = only Lewd reads, z = no one reads. Prude ranks z > x > y; Lewd ranks x > y > z. The "liberal" rights are these: Prude is decisive over (x,z), since that pair concerns whether he himself reads, and Lewd is decisive over (y,z); being decisive over a pair means the social ranking of that pair follows your preference. So Pareto gives x > y, Lewd's right gives y > z, Prude's right gives z > x, and the result is a cycle with no best social state.

Now the variation. Keep the same two people and the same preferences but swap the rights: Prude gets to decide whether Lewd reads, so he is decisive over (y,z), and Lewd gets to decide whether Prude reads, so he is decisive over (x,z). When I run this version I get something transitive — Pareto gives x > y, Prude's right gives z > y, Lewd's right gives x > z — so the social order is x > z > y and the contradiction seems to vanish. Does the paradox really disappear under cross-assigned rights, or does it just relocate? I tried to construct a different preference profile under the swapped rights that brings a cycle back (I think one exists, but I'm not sure I've charactized the boundary correctly). Can you run both versions carefully, state the general condition on the rights assignment plus preference profile that generates the Sen cycle, and say what the swapped version reveals about whether the paradox is ultimately about "meddlesome" preferences or about something more structural?
