I've been trying to get clear on what I actually think about famines, and I'd like you to force me into reflective equilibrium. My gut says most major famines are political crimes rather than natural disasters, but I keep hitting cases where that feels too harsh. Here are my verdicts. Bengal, 1943: the harvest wasn't catastrophically short, but wartime inflation and hoarding pushed rice beyond what poor rural people could pay, and the British administration barely acted; I read that as a political failure with a body count, not an act of nature. Ireland, 1845–52: the potato blight was natural, but grain and livestock continued to be shipped out to Britain through the worst years, and the relief schemes were deliberately stingy so as not to interfere with the grain trade; I call that culpable. China, the Great Leap Forward famine of 1959–61: weather made some difference, but the collectivization drive, absurdly high procurement quotas, local cadres reporting phantom harvests, and a state that kept exporting grain rather than admit failure did the real killing; clearly a political crime. Now take a poor but genuinely democratic and landlocked country, with elections and a free press. A once-in-a-century drought destroys the harvest. The government appeals for foreign aid, struggles to move food because roads wash out, and aid arrives too late. People die. I want to call that a tragedy, not a crime—though I can't point to exactly what the government did right either. And another case: a state at war, a drought creates a shortage in a rebel province, and the government blocks humanitarian aid on the grounds that rebels would tax it, while preventing civilians from leaving. I count that as a crime even though the state didn't cause the initial scarcity.

My tentative principle is something like this: a famine is a political crime when the state either actively takes food from people, or has both the food and the administrative capacity to save them but chooses not to; it's a natural disaster when no state, however well-intentioned, could have prevented the deaths. I'm also drawn, seperately, to the thought that democracy is doing the real work—Bengal was colonial, Ireland was governed from London with its interests treated as subordinate to England's, China was authoritarian, the aid-blocking state is despotic. But the drought-in-a-democracy case doesn't fit the democracy story, and my capacity principle fits it so neatly that I worry I've rigged it.

Is there a coherent view behind all this? Test the tentative principle against the cases, repair or replace it, and say which of my verdicts I'd have to abandon under the best version. Then apply that best version to one further case I can't settle: world grain prices suddenly triple, causing a famine in a poor but democratic country with no reserves and no way to buy food on the world market. State the principle precisely, not as a slogan, and keep the whole answer under 900 words.
