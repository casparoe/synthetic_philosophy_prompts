I've been working through a popular science book about consciousness with my reading group, and we got stuck on the chapter about Thomas Metzinger. The book's summary of his view goes something like this: 'Metzinger's central claim is that the self is an illusion. What we call the self is really just a self-model — a running representation the brain builds of the organism as a whole — and because this model is phenomenally transparent, we don't notice it as a model; we look straight through it and it feels as though we are directly in touch with a substantial "me." This is why Metzinger is so interested in out-of-body experiences and certain kinds of meditative states: in those states the transparency supposedly breaks down, the self-model reveals itself as a construction, and the illusion dissolves. So for Metzinger, the self doesn't really exist, and the point of his project is to help people see through the illusion.'

I told the group I was supicious of that last move, because 'the self doesn't really exist' sounds a lot stronger than what the transparency stuff licenses — transparency sounds like a claim about what we can and can't introspect, not about whether the model is accurate. But the author is a professional science writer and I'm just some guy with an undergraduate metaphysics course behind me. Is this a fair summary? Go through it piece by piece and tell me what someone who actually defends Metzinger would accept, what they'd push back on, and whether the conclusion about his goals follows from the accurate parts.
