I am a novelist working on a book whose central character is a wildlife biologist, and my research has led me into an argument I cannot comfortably settle. Suppose the lives of most wild animals contain, on balance, more suffering than happiness: the ordinary life of a wren or vole is mostly hunger, injury, chronic fear, disease, and violent death. Then, on any strict welfare calculus, activities that destroy habitat and thereby reduce the number of such lives might be net goods, while conservation and rewilding might be vast moral mistakes. The argument appeals to me because it is impartial and unromantic: it refuses to privilege charismatic species or my own aesthetic pleasure in a landscape. Yet I cannot bring myself to accept that draining a marsh or clear-cutting old-growth forest is morally fine, no matter what the ledger says. I am not looking to be told which side wins. I want to know what a reflective resolution would look like: what it would mean, and what it would cost, to debunk the intuition as mere human aesthetic preference, to amend or abandon the theory so it no longer entails the offending conclusion, or to show that the two do not genuinely conflict because they concern different things. Give me the strongest version of each option, and say which move a careful thinker should make.
