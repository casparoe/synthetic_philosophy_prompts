I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I want to work through a proposal that has been circulating among philosophers of language, lexicographers and science-policy researchers. The proposal is presented by its authors as a partial answer to the implementation problem in conceptual engineering: the difficulty, once someone has argued that a concept should be improved, of actually getting anyone to use the improved version, and the prior difficulty of deciding who, if anyone, has the standing to improve a shared concept at all.

Under the proposal, a standing Concept Commission would select a small number of contested everyday and semi-technical concepts every five years — its founding list includes planet, fish, vegetable, sandwich, weed, memory and addiction — and would issue a revised 'stewardship definition' for each. A revision would have to pass two separate tests. A continuity panel would check, by means of pre-registered surveys of ordinary speakers, that the revised definition still classifies cases in line with at least ninety per cent of clear current usage. An amelioration panel would separately certify that the revision serves the legitimate ends for which the term is in fact used — legal drafting, scientific classification, dietary labelling, horticultural regulation, psychiatric practice, and so on — better than the status quo does. Approved definitions would be entered in a public Concept Register. Courts, regulators, scientific bodies and publishers would be invited, but not compelled, to adopt register definitions as defaults in their own rules and style guides. Bodies that deliberately kept an older sense could apply for a renewable 'legacy usage' notice on the register, valid for up to ten years. Courts and regulators could cite the register as a default, though never as a trump card, and a group of, say, five thousand registered members of the public could petition after three years to have a definition reopened, which would restart the process.

I would like you to work through this as a mechanism rather than to endorse or condemn the ideal behind it. What would it incentivise among lexicographers, courts, scientific societies, interest groups and ordinary speakers? How would it be gamed? Which concepts would attract the first revision attempts, and which definitions would be hardest to shift? Where would the first breakdowns occur? Are the failure modes fixable by adjusting the continuity threshold, the panel composition, the opt-out rules or the petition procedure? Please work through those questions in turn, and in giving your overall verdict tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would make you substantially more or less sanguine about the proposal, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
