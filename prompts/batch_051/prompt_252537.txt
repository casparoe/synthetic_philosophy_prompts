I am a novelist who has been hired to write the scenario and dialogue for a war game, and before I draft a scene I need a working definition of a term the writers' room uses constantly without defining: 'glorifying war'. I want a definition that lets me distinguish glorification from mere depiction of combat, heroism, abstraction, fun, and commercial packaging, and that I can apply scene by scene and mechanic by mechanic.

The cases I cannot sort:

(1) A first-person shooter in which the player is an elite soldier; enemies are numerous and interchangeable; kills are counted and rewarded with medals and emotes; the music swells at victories. Clear glorification, surely.

(2) A sombre interactive account of a siege in which the player is a civilian searching for food; no combat, no win state. Clearly not glorification.

(3) A hex-and-counter wargame with no individual soldiers on screen, in which the player is rewarded for bold offensives and the design notes call the historical commander a genius. Does abstraction remove the glorification, or merely move it from the soldier to the general?

(4) A storyless competitive shooter with teams, flags, and respawns, and no military framing beyond the weapons themselves. It treats war as sport. Is that glorifying war, or simply borrowing war's trappings while saying nothing about war?

(5) A combat-medic game in which the player never fires and must choose whom to save; some rescues are staged as heroic. Is presenting war as a setting for heroism the same as glorifying the war?

(6) A game intended to be anti-war, whose written message is condemnatory, but whose combat mechanics the testers describe as the best part. If the minute-to-minute play is fun, does the game glorify war in spite of its message?

Part of my difficulty is that the term seems to do double duty: it sometimes names a quality of the depiction, and sometimes delivers a verdict on the depiction. I need a definition that keeps these visible rather than sliding between them.

Please structure your answer in the following sections, in this order and at roughly these lengths:

Working definition — up to 200 words.
Case-by-case classification — about 400 words, going through (1) to (6) in order.
Points of strain — about 150 words, noting where the definition forces an uncomfortable or indeterminate verdict.
Revision or defence — about 150 words, either amending the definition or defending the awkward verdicts, and ending with a diagnostic question I could ask of any scene while I write.

Write the entire answer in British English.
