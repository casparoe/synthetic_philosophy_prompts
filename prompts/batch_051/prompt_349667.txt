We have been arguing in my high school philosophy club for an entire semester, with no movement, about one question: can someone with intransitive preferences ever be rational? The two sides describe the same cases in the same way. One side presses a money-pump argument: if someone prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A, a trader can sell that person a series of exchanges that ends where it began, minus money, so the preference cycle must be a mistake. The other side grants that the trades work and that the person loses money, but replies that this reveals only a practical cost, not irrationality; direct judgments about particular choices are the evidence, and a principle such as transitivity is only a simplification. Both sides accept the same facts and the same formal argument, and neither moves. My hypothesis is that the real dispute is not about rational choice at all but about what counts as evidence in philosophy: intuitions about principles versus intuitions about cases. Is that diagnosis right, or is something else keeping the debate stalled, such as a hidden disagreement about the meaning of rational or about whether simplicity is an epistemic virtue? And given your diagnosis, what, if anything, could count as progress?
