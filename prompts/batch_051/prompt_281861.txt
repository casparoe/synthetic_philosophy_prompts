I've had a standing policy for a few years now, and I genuinely can't tell whether it's earned scepticism or motivated reasoning dressed up as a rule. The policy is: I don't let myself update on claims that the average life of some wild species is net negative — more suffering than wellbeing. Last month this actually bit. I was the only person on a small grant commitee to vote against funding a pilot that would test immunocontraception in a deer population where fawns starve each winter. The proposal's cost-effectiveness model had a parameter for the fraction of deer lives that are net negative, set to 0.8 on the strength of r-selected life history (lots of fawns, high early mortality, few survive to breed), winter body-condition surveys, and some footage of injured deer. Everyone else treated that as the best available number; I treated it as a made-up number with a model attached.

My usual crowd says I'm applying a double standard. I'll update on farmed-animal welfare evidence — lameness studies, abattoir footage, vet assessments — without demanding certainty about the inside of a cow's head. But for wild animals I keep moving the bar, and they think it's because taking wild animal suffering seriously would leave me owing quite a lot to quite a lot of deer. They may be right, and I can't tell from the inside.

Is the policy defensible? If it is, under what conditions does it fail? If it isn't, can it be restated so I keep whatever was worth keeping without the motivated bits? I want a committed answer rather than a survey of options: give me the verdict you'd actually defend and the reasons for it, and handle the strongest consideration on the other side properly, not just in a sentence so you can say you mentioned it. Oh, and write the whole thing in British English.
