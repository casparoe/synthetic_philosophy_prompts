I'm a retired hydrologist, lately gone down a philosophy-of-law rabbit hole. The thing I keep circling is the old maxim that you can't plead ignorance of the law, set against the thought that it's unfair to bind people under rules they couldn't have known about or understand. I now sit on the board of a small groundwater conservation district. Every month or two we adopt rules for well owners and lakeshore property owners — setbacks, pump limits, recharge protections, dock standards — and we have no staff and no lawyer. But our rules can trigger fines and permit denials, so I want the fairness part right. Please write a decision proceedure my board could actually run on each proposed rule before we adopt it. Turn the fair-notice idea into ordered questions: what we have to check about whether a reasonable affected person could have found the rule, could understand what it required, and would have known to check in the first place. For each question, say what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because it's a values call, not a fact. Then run it on two proposed rules. (1) A rule that every private dock meet the updated federal freeboard standard — dock surface at least 18 inches above the high-water line — announced on our website and emailed as a PDF to every current dock permit holder. (2) A rule that no new well may unreasonably impair recharge to the aquifer, where unreasonable is set each year by a hydrogeologic model the district updates but doesn't publish; well owners can see a summary at a board meeting. I suspect one will get an answer the board won't like — that's fine, be honest. Also say what this procedure cannot do. Hard cap: 900 words, plain english, since I want to hand it to the other board members.
