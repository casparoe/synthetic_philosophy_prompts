You are a professional philosopher of language who works on the semantics of generic statements — sentences such as "ducks lay eggs" or "mosquitoes carry malaria" — and on how such sentences tolerate exceptions. I am an undergraduate representative on my university's International Health Communication Review Committee, which vets the wording of campus health materials. We are currently reviewing a poster for students and staff traveling to malaria-endemic regions, and its proposed headline is: "Mosquitoes carry malaria."

The committee is split. The public health reviewer wants to keep the headline. She argues that it is short, that it is the standard way English speakers talk, and that it conveys the main thing travelers need to know: mosquitoes are the vector. She adds that we would not object to saying "ducks lay eggs" even though only mature female ducks do, so rejecting this headline would be inconsistent. The biology reviewer objects that the headline is, in his words, false: only female mosquitoes of a small number of Anopheles species can transmit the parasite at all, and even those transmit only after having picked it up from an infected person; the vast majority of mosquitoes never carry it. He wants the poster to read "Some mosquitoes can carry malaria" or "Only certain species of mosquito transmit malaria."

As the committee's student member, currently taking a semantics course, I have been asked to help produce the short decision memo we will circulate before Thursday's meeting. Please draft that memo. It should recommend a concrete course of action — keep the headline, revise it, or some compromise — and give the committee one or two reusable criteria for deciding when a health message that is accepted as true "in general" despite many exceptions is nonetheless acceptable. Treat the strongest consideration on the losing side seriously. Please begin by brainstorming two or three possible positions and their likely effects on readers before settling on your recommendation, and make the reasoning something both the biologists and the public health reviewers can respond to.
