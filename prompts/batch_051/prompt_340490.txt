I serve on the standing conduct committee of a professional licensing body for civil engineers. Three weeks ago we had to decide whether to revoke the license of a member who falsified two safety-inspection reports under severe financial pressure; the actual structural harm turned out to be negligible, but the committee split cleanly between those who asked what he deserved and those who asked what penalty would protect future clients. I have not been able to think the matter back together since.

In Thucydides' History of the Peloponnesian War, Diodotus tells the Athenian assembly:

However, I have not come forward either to oppose or to accuse in the matter of Mitylene; indeed, the question before us as sensible men is not their guilt, but our interests. Though I prove them ever so guilty, I shall not, therefore, advise their death, unless it be expedient; nor though they should have claims to indulgence, shall I recommend it, unless it be clearly for the good of the country. I consider that we are deliberating for the future more than for the present; and where Cleon is so positive as to the useful deterrent effects that will follow from making rebellion capital, I who consider the interests of the future quite as much as he, as positively maintain the contrary. And I require you not to reject my useful considerations for his specious ones: his speech may have the attraction of seeming the more just in your present temper against Mitylene; but we are not in a court of justice, but in a political assembly; and the question is not justice, but how to make the Mitylenians useful to Athens.

I want to test whether that distinction can be turned into a workable institutional rule rather than left as a rhetorical move. Suppose our licensing body adopts the following procedure for every disciplinary case.

Each case is heard by two separate panels, drawn from the same pool and given the same evidence. The first panel, called the court panel, answers a single question: what penalty does this conduct, considered in itself, merit? It may choose from the full statutory range and must publish written reasons. The second panel, called the assembly panel, answers a different question: what penalty, if any, would best protect the public and the profession in expectation, taking into account deterrence, trust in the licensing system, rehabilitation, and enforcement costs? It too may choose from the full range and must publish written reasons. The final penalty is whatever the assembly panel chooses, with one constraint: if the assembly panel selects a penalty harsher than the court panel's, the court panel's penalty is imposed as a ceiling. The court panel's verdict is published alongside the assembly panel's ruling but has no other legal effect.

Analyze how this institution would actually behave over its first several years. What does it incentivize panelists, defense lawyers, expert witnesses, and the licensing body itself to do? How would advocates game the boundary between "desert" and "interest," especially once the two panels' published rulings become a dataset that later panels and lawyers study? Where does the ceiling provision create unintended leverage? What failure modes does the design produce, and can any be fixed without collapsing the two panels back into one? Finally, does Diodotus's distinction survive being re-described as two panels, or does the institution merely move the original quarrel one level up? Give a concrete account of the likely second-order effects, not an abstract verdict.
