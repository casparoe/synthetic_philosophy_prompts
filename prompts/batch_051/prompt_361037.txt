I'm currently taking a course on Romance historical linguistics, and we've been circling a question the scholarship tends to treat as settled by stipulation rather than by evidence: whether there is a fact of the matter about when Latin ends and Italian begins. The standard view is that the boundary is a convention — that late Latin shaded continuously into the early Italo-Romance vernaculars, and that labels like "Latin" and "Italian" are retrojections imposed long after the fact. I'm no longer sure that view is a conclusion; it might just be a refusal to operationalize the question.

I'd like to see whether the contrary claim can be tested rather than merely asserted. Suppose a classmate claims that, continuity notwithstanding, there is a real and non-arbitrary transition: a fairly narrow window in which a cluster of grammatical properties changed together, rapidly enough that it makes sense to say the ancestor of Italian had come into being while what preceded it was still Latin. How would one test that claim? I want a concrete design: the corpus or set of texts one would sample; the linguistic variables one would score — for instance, the dissolution of the nominal case system, the rise of definite articles from demonstratives, the replacement of the synthetic future by infinitive plus habere, the restructuring of complementation, or any other variables you judge more diagnostic; and how one would measure whether those changes cluster in time rather than drift independently. Specify the result that would favor the "real transition" reading and the result that would favor the "pure convention" reading.

Then be precise about what the test would not settle even if it came out strongly one way. I'm particularly interested in two limits: whether a clustered-change result would show that the Latin-to-Italian boundary is real, or merely that language change sometimes proceeds in bursts; and whether a no-clustering result would show that the boundary is a convention, or merely that the variables chosen were too coarse. I'd like you to commit to a definite position on the underlying question as well: is the claim testable at all, in your view, and what is the strongest version of the test? Please defend your choices rather than laying out options and leaving me to pick.
