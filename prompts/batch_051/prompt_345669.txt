I have a standing policy for evaluating claims about twentieth-century mass atrocities. When someone challenges the established account of the Holocaust, the Armenian genocide, or the Nanjing Massacre — disputing, for instance, that the killing was systematic, centrally directed, or on the accepted scale — I do not examine the evidence they offer. I classify the claim as denialism and refuse to engage with it. I am aware that historians themselves revise many details about these events (the numbers, the mechanisms, the distribution of responsibility), so the policy cannot be justified simply by saying the record is settled. My question is whether this policy is a defensible epistemic rule or a bias dressed up as a principle. Under what conditions, if any, is it rational to preclassify a challenge to a well-established historical account as outside the bounds of serious inquiry? Can the rule be restated so that it lets legitimate revisionism through while still excluding denialism, and what features of a claim would the restated rule have to track — source, method, responsiveness to evidence, direction of the conclusion, or something else?
