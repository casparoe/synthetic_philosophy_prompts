A few weeks ago I listened to a long argument between two friends of mine about what we should call it when a large language model produces a plausible but false answer — the thing the companies call a hallucination. I have been turning it over since and genuinely cannot settle it, so I would like an adjudication.

Maya took the position that hallucination is, most of the time, a kind of lying. Her argument was that the model states false things in exactly the same tone of confidence it uses for true things, in a context where users are invited to take its outputs as answers. She admitted the model itself may have no inner intention to deceive, but she said the deception is carried by the whole arrangement: the companies know the systems routinely invent facts and still package them as assistants, so the utterance, taken in context, functions as a lie regardless of what is going on inside the weights. She compared it to a spokesperson reading from a script prepared by an organization that knows the script is false.

Devon disagreed and said the right label is bullshit, in roughly the sense Harry Frankfurt gave the term. On his telling, Frankfurt's point is that a liar is still oriented toward the truth — a liar knows or suspects the truth and tries to steer others away from it — whereas a bullshitter is indifferent to whether what he says is true or false, because he is only trying to make an impression or fill a role. Devon said a language model is an almost pure case of this: it does not consult reality and then misreport it; it produces statistically plausible text, and whether the output corresponds to the facts is simply not part of the operation. Hallucination is not lying, he said, because lying needs someone responsive to truth; it is bullshit, because it is speech produced without that responsiveness.

Maya came back with two points. First, she said Devon's account lets the model off on a technicality. Even if the model is indifferent to truth, the people who deploy it are not; they present it as a reliable assistant and profit from users' trust, so any particular false output is still issued under a standing claim of reliability. The lie, she said, does not have to be in the token generation — it can be in the deployment. Second, she pushed on the word bullshit itself: Frankfurt's bullshitter, as she understood it, does something with his speech — he wants to seem knowledgeable, to win an argument, to carry off a moment. A model has no such project. If bullshit requires at least a motive, then calling the model a bullshitter smuggles in an agent where there is none, which is exactly the thing Devon accused her of doing with lying.

Devon answered that motive is not required once you look at the output rather than the agent. Bullshit, he said, is a property of utterances that are offered as if truth mattered when in fact it did not guide their production; the model's outputs have that property even if the model has no mind at all. He added that error is the only alternative left, and error is the one label that clearly does not fit: a machine that adds 2 and 2 and prints 5 is making an error because arithmetic is the very thing it is supposed to get right, but a language model is not attempting facts and slipping; the machinery contains no stage at which a truth claim could be checked and failed.

Then a third friend, Priya, who had been quiet, said she thought both were overcomplicating it. She allowed that individual outputs can be false, but a hallucination is not a speech act at all — it is a failure mode of a statistical process, like a shadow that looks like a person. We do not say the landscape lies to us or bullshits us; we just misread it. The only correct description, she said, is that the model generated a high-probability continuation that happens to be false, and the normative vocabulary belongs only where there are truth-tracking practices — in the user's responsibility to check, and in the company's responsibility to warn.

There the argument stalled, because each was partly talking about a different thing: the token sequence, the system that produced it, the company that deployed it, or the user's practice.

Could you go through this exchange and say what each person gets right and wrong at each step? I am not mainly asking who wins overall, though if you have a view I would like it stated at the end. What I most want is a point-by-point adjudication of the claims as they were actually made, including whether it is coherent to say that a model's output is bullshit but the model is not a bullshitter.
