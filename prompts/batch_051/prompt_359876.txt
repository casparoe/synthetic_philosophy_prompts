Right, this one came out of a pub conversation with a friend of mine who does couples counselling in Manchester. She says she's basically making it up on the hoof: sometimes she treats a client's list of reasons for loving their partner as a sign the relationship is well-grounded, and sometimes she treats a long list as a warning, because if you love someone for their good qualities, then when the qualities go — or when someone with more of them appears — the love should go too. She'd like something she can reuse, instead of re-deciding her whole theory once a week.

The setup: she sees about six couples a week, the first appointment is fifty minutes, and she says she can usually get a person's 'why I love them' story out of them within three sessions. She has no philosophy background, so whatever you give her has to be translatable into plain questions. The stakes are fairly high — she's helping people decide whether to stay married or split up, and she's worried her off-the-cuff intuitions are really just rationalising whatever decision she formed in the first ten minutes.

Here's the theory I'd like translated. There's a live three-way row in the philosophy of love (the names Frankfurt, Velleman and Kolodny are the ones attached to it, but don't send her to the library — and don't put citations in the procedure). One camp says love is appraisal: you love someone because you see them as having valuable qualities — kindness, wit, whatever — and your reasons track those qualities, so the therapeutic question is whether the client sees the partner accurately, and what they'd be left with if the qualities disappeared. A second camp says love is bestowal: it creates value rather than responding to it, so demanding a list of reasons is close to a category mistake — the love is what gives everything else its importance, not something a list justifies. And a third camp says love is justified neither by qualities nor by a free act of will but by the relationship itself — shared history, joint projects, the second-personal stuff — so the relevant reasons, if any, have a 'because of us' shape rather than a 'because of you' shape.

I want a decision proceedure she can run in those three sessions. It should sort what a client says about why they love their partner into: appraisal-type reasons (qualities of the partner), relation-type reasons (history, shared identity, joint projects), bestowal-type talk (love as a choice or commitment that comes before reasons), and a fourth bin for talk that fits none of the above and should make her clinically curious. The procedure should be a sequence of questions to ask in order, with what counts as an acceptable answer before moving on, where it stops with a no, and — importantly — where it hands over to her because the theory can't settle it; it's a sorting machine, not a verdict on whether they should stay together. Flag which distinctions have to survive the simplification (the difference between 'I love her because she's kind' and 'I love her because of what we've built' has to survive, or the whole exercise collapses) and which distinctions can be dropped.

Then run the procedure on three clients:

1. A gives a tidy, almost lawyerly list: she's kind, she's organised, she's a good mother, they share values about money. Asked what he'd do if she had a brain injury tomorrow and lost most of those qualities, he says he'd stay, but can't say why without reusing the qualities he just listed, and it visibly bothers him.

2. B can't name a single quality. 'I just do, she's my person.' Pressed, she says questions like that make the love feel smaller, like pinning a butterfly.

3. C is a widower in a new relationship. He says he loves his new partner because the two of them grieved bad marriages together and now have a private language of near-misses; the qualities he can list are all true of several close friends.

For each, say which bin the procedure would put them in and what it should tell her to probe next.

Last instruction: make sure the procedure includes at least one case where it gives her an answer she won't like — a class of client where the machine says 'none of the above' when she'd rather offer reassurance, or where it says 'this is just appraisal' about a marriage she thinks is dead. And be honest about what the procedure cannot do: some of what clients say is post-hoc rationalisation, and no checklist can tell earnestness from performance, so mark the point where she has to use her own judgement.
