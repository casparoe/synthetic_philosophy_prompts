Consider a simultaneous one-shot prisoner's dilemma with the standard symmetric payoff values T = 5, R = 3, P = 1, and S = 0, where T is the temptation payoff for defecting against cooperation, R the reward for mutual cooperation, P the punishment for mutual defection, and S the sucker payoff for cooperating against defection. The textbook analysis holds that defection strictly dominates cooperation for each player, so (Defect, Defect) is the unique Nash equilibrium even though mutual cooperation would leave both better off.

Change one assumption. Rather than being matched at random, individuals interact assortatively: in an infinite population, each individual interacts with a player of the same behavioral type with probability r, where 0 ≤ r ≤ 1, and with a randomly drawn member of the population with probability 1 − r. The two pure behavioral types are Always Cooperate (ALLC) and Always Defect (ALLD), and their frequencies change according to replicator dynamics, so a type increases when its expected payoff exceeds the population average expected payoff.

Let x be the frequency of ALLC, and work through the following.

(1) Baseline. With r = 0, confirm the standard result: defection is strictly dominant in the stage game, (Defect, Defect) is its unique Nash equilibrium, and under replicator dynamics ALLD is the unique evolutionarily stable outcome, while the frequency of ALLC converges to zero.

(2) Fitnesses. For general r, write the expected payoffs V_C(x) and V_D(x) of the two types as functions of x.

(3) Invasion thresholds. Determine when a rare ALLC type can invade an ALLD population, meaning V_C(x) exceeds V_D(x) as x tends to zero, and when ALLD can invade an ALLC population, meaning V_D(x) exceeds V_C(x) as x tends to one. Give each threshold first in terms of T, R, P, and S, and then evaluate it for the numerical values above.

(4) Interior equilibrium. If the two thresholds allow a range of r in which the types coexist, solve for the interior equilibrium x* satisfying V_C(x*) = V_D(x*), express it in terms of r, and determine its stability.

(5) Survival report. Which standard claims about the one-shot prisoner's dilemma survive this variation, and which fail? Address specifically: Is defection still strictly dominant for each player in the stage game, and is (Defect, Defect) still its unique Nash equilibrium? Does the further claim that cooperation cannot evolve in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma survive at the population level? Where a standard conclusion fails, locate the exact step of the original argument that breaks; your analysis should make clear that the break lies not in the payoff ordering but in the random-matching assumption, under which an individual's own type carries no information about the opponent's type.

(6) Takeaway. End with a short paragraph connecting your result to the contrast between assortment as a mechanism for the evolution of cooperation—kin recognition, tag-based assortment, or homophily, all summarized by r—and direct reciprocity in repeated games.

Write your answer in American English.
