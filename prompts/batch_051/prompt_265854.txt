I am trying to work out what it means for a physical system to genuinely compute, because I want to know whether the thesis that the brain is a computer — that the brain's cognitive processes are computations — is a substantive empirical claim or an empty one. Let "X computes" mean that X's own operation is computational, not merely that someone can describe, simulate, or interpret X on a computer.

Here are my gut verdicts on cases.

- A laptop running a chess engine computes.
- A boulder rolling down a hillside does not compute, even though its trajectory is computable.
- A thermostat switching on a furnace when the temperature falls below its set point does not compute; it is just a switch, though I cannot say exactly why that disqualifies it.
- A bacterium that modulates its swimming so that it moves up a sugar gradient computes.
- A plant bending toward a window does not compute. I am uneasy here: this looks much like the bacterium case, and I cannot state the difference.
- A person doing long division on paper computes.
- A neural network trained to recognize faces in photographs computes.
- A brick wall whose water stains, under a sufficiently contrived mapping, can be paired with the moves of a chess game does not compute; the computation is being projected onto the wall, not performed by it.

Is there a principle that captures all of these verdicts, or nearly all of them? State the principle precisely, and say which feature of each case each clause of the principle is meant to handle. If no reasonably simple principle captures all of them, say which verdict it overturns and why you think that is the right place to revise. Then apply the principle to the case I actually care about: a human brain recognizing a face. What does this cleaned-up pattern of verdicts commit me to about computationalism, and does the resulting principle avoid the standard worry that if any physical process can be interpreted as computing, then computationalism is trivial?

End your reply with a final line beginning "Summary:" and giving, in one sentence, the principle you settle on and its verdict on the face-recognition case.
