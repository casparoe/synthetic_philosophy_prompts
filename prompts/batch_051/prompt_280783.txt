I am caught between a theory I find compelling and an intuition I cannot disown, and I want a negotiated verdict rather than a side. The theory is something like Mozi's impartial care: the only non-arbitrary answer to why my child's welfare should count more than a stranger's is that it should not; equal welfare counts equally, and any partiality coefficient looks like special pleading once I consider choosing the rule from behind a veil where I do not know which child is mine. It also has the systematic virtues I normally prize — a single norm, a clean decision procedure for scarce time and resources (act where marginal welfare is greatest), and a debunking story for my impulse to favor my own: that impulse is the familial analogue of ingroup bias, and I would not let ingroup bias write my moral theory. The intuition is the Confucian view of graded love: a parent who paused to consult an impartial ledger before saving their own child in an emergency would not be admirably impartial but defective as a parent, and someone whose love for their own child ran no warmer than their love for a stranger would have a failed relationship, not a corrected one. On that view parental partiality is not a bias to be debunked but a constitutive demand of the role, and role-relative reasons need not be derived from any impartial rule. I do not want the cheap compatibilism that says impartial principles permit partiality, unless it can explain why the permission does not quietly reinstate the very partiality the theory denied. Which should yield — the theory or the intuition — and what would justify each move: debunking the partiality as bias, amending or abandoning impartial care, or showing they operate at different levels? How would I tell which resolution I have actually adopted rather than merely narrated?
