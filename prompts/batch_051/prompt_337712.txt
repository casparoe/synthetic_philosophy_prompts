So I'm teaching a course that has a unit on the philosophy of emotion, and I've been trying to find a movie to assign alongside the readings, something that isn't just a lecture with a plot attached. I keep coming back to Pixar's Inside Out, partly because half the students can recite it and partly because it clearly has a position on what emotions are, even if I can't quite pin down what position. The setup alone already looks like basic-emotion theory — five discrete little characters in a control room, each with a name, a color, a job — which is strange given that we spend most of the unit on the three views I actually care about: emotions as feelings, emotions as judgments, and emotions as perceptions of value. My question is whether the film can be read through each of those lenses anyway, and where the film pushes back. The James-Lange-ish feeling view, for instance, would want the bodily symptoms to be the emotion or at least its raw material, but in the movie the tears and the slumped posture come after the console decision; the body is downstream. The judgment view does better with Sadness, who doesn't just feel bad but seems to register that something valuable has been lost, especially in the memory-tinting scenes where she turns a happy core memory blue. The perception-of-value view maybe fits the way the characters literally monitor Riley's world and appraise it in real time. But then there's the homunculus problem, which I usually teach as a reductio of folk pyschology — and here the film just leans into it. Could you give me a scene-by-scene reading of the film along these three theories, and then take a stand on what the movie is actually committed to, whether it knows it or not? I especially want the end, where Joy lets Sadness take the console, and the moment after Riley runs away when the console goes gray and she kind of shuts down: does the film think emotions can be absent, or just that they can be dysregulated? And don't force it: I want to know where the movie genuinely resists the reading, because I don't want to assign something that ends up teaching my students a bad theory.
