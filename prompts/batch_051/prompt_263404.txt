I am a high school junior doing a semester-long community service placement at my county historical society. The society is run by a small board of older volunteers, and its 1973 constitution includes the following clause: The Society shall hold and interpret the history of this county for its people; in any public exhibit, publication, or program, it shall give first regard to the recollections of the county's longtime families, it being the purpose of this Society to keep alive the memory handed down among our people and not to substitute for that memory the speculations of professional historians or other outsiders.

A question has come up that the board clearly never imagined in 1973. A descendant of the town's founder recently donated a box of papers that had been sitting in her grandfather's barn: among them are 1880s court records in which the founder — the same man the town celebrates every year in our Founder's Day pageant as a virtuous homesteader who built the first school with his own hands — is shown to have been convicted of embezzling money from a neighboring town's railroad fund. The pageant is built entirely on the version of him preserved in local family memory. I have been asked to draft a short recommendation to the board about whether our Founder's Day exhibit may or must remain based on the recollections, or whether the newly found documents require changing it.

I care less about the practical outcome than about the interpretive question: what does the clause actually require or permit here, and how do the main approaches to interpretation — the plain text, the clause's evident purpose, what the drafters would have said if asked about this case, and what a reasonable member of the public would take the words to mean — each handle the conflict? Please give me a reasoned comparison, and end with what you think is the most defensible reading, in American English.
