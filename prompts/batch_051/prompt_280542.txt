Take the four-colour theorem as a case study for the idea that a proof should be surveyable: something one competent mathematician can in principle check end to end. The original 1976 Appel–Haken proof, and the cleaned-up computer proofs after it, let a machine work through thousands of cases, and no one has checked all of them by hand — probably no one could. I know the rough history, so just skip the retelling. What I want is the careful mapping: whether the not-really-a-proof reaction was about verification, explanation, or something else; what the computer-proof side had to assume for the theorem to count as established knowledge; and what the episode actually does to the surveyability demand. Does it refute it, force a weakened version, or show it was never doing the work people thought?
