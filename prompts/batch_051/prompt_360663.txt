I'm on the orientation-policy committee at a university with a big incoming cohort of international students. We're revising the handbook section on everyday local customs, and last fall's complaints forced the issue: one new student from a non-tipping country refused to tip at a sit-down restaurant, saying tipping is morally objectionable (complicity in underpaying workers, and insulting back home). The server complained to the manager, who wrote to us; the same week we got separate complaints about students bunching at a bus stop instead of forming a queue, and jaywalking across the busy road near campus.

Half the committee thinks the handbook should say that by choosing to live here you take on a moral obligation to follow local conventions like tipping, queueing, and crossing rules, whatever you think of them. The other half says that's fine as purely practical advice for avoiding dirty looks, but it isn't a moral obligation—at most you're obliged not to harm or exploit anyone, and a lot of local conventions are morally arbitrary. Write the decision memo choosing what the handbook should actually say about whether visitors are morally required to follow these conventions; recommend one of the two positions, or a third if you think the split is badly posed, and be sure to give the strongest argument on the losing side before you reject it.

Format: exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Make the fifth point the actual handbook wording we could print.
