Write the joint statement that a commited explanationist and a committed Bayesian could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration about inference to the best explanation. For concreteness, let the explanationist be the kind who distinguishes an explanation's loveliness (unification, simplicity, consilience, mechanism) from its likeliness, and who thinks loveliness is an epistemically basic guide to truth rather than just a compute-saving shortcut. Let the Bayesian be the kind who thinks priors and likelihoods are the only rational currency, and explanatory virtues earn their keep only insofar as they track those; where they don't, they're bias. I want a document that seperates the parts of this fight that are real from the parts that are mostly verbal, and that sets up the actual cruxes. Phrase each side's view so that a card-carrying member of that side would say "yes, that's my view, not a strawman." The deliverable isn't a verdict on who's right; it's the document itself, with the residual disagreement made exact.

Sections, in this order:

1. Agreed claims (numbered list, ~400–600 words). What both sides can sign without wincing: what inference to the best explanation is, where Bayesian and explanationist machinery overlap, what would count as evidence against each view, and the difference between the pragmatic and the epistemic role of explanation.

2. Apparent disagreements that dissolved (bullets, ~100–150 words). Places where the two camps were mostly talking past each other or using "explanation" and "probability" differently.

3. Disagreements table (~800–1,000 words). Columns: Question; Explanationist formulation; Bayesian formulation; Crux; Test/case both sides accept as bearing on it; Explanationist's predicted outcome; Bayesian's predicted outcome. Rows should cover at least: the status of loveliness vs likeliness, simplicity as a prior, the curve-fitting problem, the bad lot objection (the worry that the true hypothesis may not be among the candidates we generated), and consilience (e.g., a Newton-style argument where one hypothesis explains several apparently independent phenomena).
