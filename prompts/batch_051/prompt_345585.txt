I am a PhD student working on long-run prediction in the history of economics, and I have been trying to make precise a distinction that feels intuitive but keeps slipping away: the difference between a theory that was right but early and one that was simply falsified and then kept alive by retroactive rescue. My own verdicts about a small set of cases are pulling me in different directions, and I would like to know whether a single principle can capture them.

Here are the cases.

1. Malthus's Essay on the Principle of Population (first edition). As I read it, the argument is that population tends to grow geometrically while the means of subsistence can grow only arithmetically, so any rise in living standards is temporary: it is held down by positive checks such as famine and disease unless preventive checks such as delayed marriage and moral restraint operate. My verdict: this is a plausible case of right-but-early. The mechanism genuinely describes most of agrarian history, and it still operates in societies before the demographic transition. The post-1800 escape came from sustained agricultural technical change and a fertility decline that accompanied rising incomes, developments Malthus had little reason to expect in 1798. So I want to say the theory captures a real tendency for the regime he studied but failed as an unconditional forecast for industrial economies, because a background condition changed, not because the mechanism was never operative.

2. Paul Ehrlich's The Population Bomb. Ehrlich predicted widespread famine in the 1970s and 1980s, with hundreds of millions of deaths, and argued that India could not be expected to feed itself in the coming decades. My verdict: falsified, not right-but-early. The mechanisms on which the prediction rested—fixed land constraints, negligible substitution, and population momentum swamping agricultural gains—were tested within the stated horizon and failed. The reasons it failed, the Green Revolution and the response of prices and incentives, were exactly the adjustments the original treatment treated as negligible. Calling it right-but-early now because environmental limits later reappeared in other forms strikes me as rescue: the specific event predicted did not occur, and the later mechanism was not part of the original prediction.

3. Hubbert-style peak oil. Hubbert's approach predicted that United States oil production would peak around 1970, which it did. Global peak-oil claims in the early 2000s predicted an imminent peak and long-run irreversible decline in conventional oil. My verdict: I want to split this case. The regional prediction was right-but-early, or at least locally right; the global prediction was falsified because it treated technology and substitution as fixed, when rising prices made shale oil and other unconventional sources economic. I seem to be saying that depletion models are sound when they track a well-defined stock under a stated technology, and become unsound when they project boundedness to the whole system while treating technology and substitution as exogenous. That principle sounds clean here, but I am not sure it handles Malthus or Ehrlich consistently.

4. Alvin Hansen's secular stagnation thesis. In the late 1930s Hansen argued that the American economy faced chronic demand weakness because population growth was slowing, the frontier had closed, and saving would tend to run ahead of investment. The postwar boom appeared to bury the thesis. I want to say Hansen was right-but-early: the mechanism was real but was masked for decades by war spending, the postwar investment boom, and favorable demographics, and it re-emerged after the 2008 crisis under conditions Hansen would have recognized. But this verdict makes me uneasy, because structurally it resembles the Ehrlich case—a failed near-term prediction whose mechanism later gets re-invoked once circumstances change. I cannot articulate why I treat Hansen as early and Ehrlich as false.

What I would like is a principle, or a small family of candidate principles, that distinguishes right-but-early from falsified-and-rescued. Please state it precisely, show how it handles each of the four cases, and say honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and whether you would overturn that verdict. I would also like to know whether the distinction between a tendency and an unconditional forecast can do the work here, or whether I am just using that distinction opportunistically to protect the mechanisms I antecedently like. Finally, apply your principle to a further long-run prediction of your choosing, something I have not listed, and say what it predicts.
