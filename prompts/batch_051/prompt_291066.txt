Bernard Williams argued that an endless life is not something we should wish for, because the desires and projects that make a particular life worth living—what he called categorical desires—are finite and belong to a particular character. On his view, an immortal faces a dilemma. Either she eventually exhausts or endlessly repeats the same projects and attachments, lapsing into a boredom that no mere variety of pleasant experiences can cure, or she replaces those desires and projects so thoroughly that the person who continues is no longer her in any sense that matters for survival. Eternal life is therefore either intolerably tedious or only a disguised succession of different lives.

Now suppose a specific technological development: reliable, voluntary motivational rewiring. An immortal can periodically edit her memories, desires, and values—erasing a completed or stalled project and installing a new one, or letting an old source of delight be forgotten so that it can be encountered again as if for the first time—while gradual neural intervention preserves her psychological continuity, reflective self-awareness, and ability to narrate her own life as a single story. Carried out carefully, the rewiring is compatible with the standard psychological-continuity account of personal identity: she counts as the same person throughout, with overlapping memories and a continuous self-model.

Does Williams's argument survive this development? Or does the availability of continuous re-engineering merely relocate the problem, turning what would have been boredom into a technological version of the second horn of his dilemma, in which the original person does not really survive her own immortality? Identify the precise premise or premises of Williams's argument that this technology bears on, say what the argument needs to be true in order to retain its force, and assess whether that requirement still holds in this setting. If the argument can be repaired, say how; if it cannot, say exactly where it gives way.
