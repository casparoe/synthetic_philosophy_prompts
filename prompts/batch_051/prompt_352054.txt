I am trying to pin down what it is to understand a particular proof, as distinct from merely being able to follow it when it is written out or to perform the associated computations. To that end I have been compiling a list of candidate operational tests: things a person could do with the proof that would, if done successfully, count as evidence of understanding it. My list so far runs as follows.

1. Unprompted reconstruction: they can produce the proof on their own, without notes, filling in the routine intermediate steps as they go.
2. Logical architecture: they can say what is being assumed and what is to be shown, identify the method of the proof (for example, contraposition, induction, or a least-element contradiction), and keep the assumption-goal structure clear throughout.
3. Isolation of the load-bearing moves: they can state in a sentence or two the central idea of the proof and pick out the one or two non-obvious moves on which the argument turns.
4. Transfer of the technique: they can recognize when the same strategy applies, perhaps in a disguised form, to a different theorem, and can carry the technique over to that new setting.
5. Flaw detection: given a subtly corrupted version of the proof, they can say that it is wrong and locate the step at which the argument breaks.
6. Hypothesis sensitivity: for each substantive hypothesis, they can produce or sketch an example in which the theorem fails when that hypothesis is dropped, or can explain how the argument would break at the point where the hypothesis is used.
7. Modification and extension: they can strengthen or weaken the theorem, or extend it to a wider setting, and explain how the proof would need to change.
8. Teaching: they can explain the proof to someone without prior familiarity, in a way that leaves the listener able to say why the theorem is true rather than merely able to repeat the steps.

I suspect this list is still incomplete. Give me one further candidate test, one that is not a restatement, special case, or combination of the eight above. Then commit to a definite judgment about it. State plainly which candidate you would add and why it is genuinely distinct from each of the eight. Defend a position on whether it is any good as a test of understanding: does it track understanding itself rather than something nearby such as memory, ingenuity, or facility with the surrounding subject? And say where it belongs relative to the existing list — more basic than all of them, a capstone that presupposes several of them, or something else — and whether adding it suggests that any of my eight should be merged or demoted. Please do not lay out a menu of possibilities: give one answer and argue for it.
