I was a physical oceanographer for thirty-some years and only recently started reading legal philosophy, so I may be mangling this. The account of the rule of law I keep coming back to is something like this: a legal system should operate through general, public rules laid down in advance; the rules should be clear enough that a competent person can comply; they should not demand the impossible or contradict one another; they should be reasonably stable; and the officials who enforce them should be bound by the same rules rather than exercising bare discretion. I gather there is a running fight about whether this ideal is ultimately grounded in dignity, liberty, or just predictability, but the criteria themselves seem right to me.

Here is the case I cannot settle. A friend who works in criminal-justice data science described a jurisdiction that keeps ordinary criminal statutes intact but replaces sentencing with a continuously retrained machine-learning model. The model's objective is to minimize five-year reoffending, subject to public constraints: no protected characteristics as inputs, no sentence outside the statutory range, and total prison-bed and cost caps. Judges must follow the model's recommended sentence except within a narrow band, and when they depart they must give written reasons. The model is retrained every week on new outcome data, and its recommendations drift as the training set grows. No one, including the people who built it, can state a stable rule that distinguishes the burglars who get six months from the ones who get eighteen, beyond saying that the model returns that output under the published objective and constraints.

Does this system satisfy the rule of law? My first instinct is no, but when I go through the criteria I am not sure which one it actualy violates. The statutes are public, prospective, general, and possible to obey. The sentencing model is, formally, a public decision rule that applies to everyone in the same input classes. The stability criterion seems strained, but the criminal law itself changes slowly, and maybe a system of guided judicial discretion changed just as fast. Prospectivity bothers me too, but I cannot turn that worry into a criterion, and I already know judges sometimes develop new sentencing practices without warning. I would like an answer that works through the criteria one by one and then says whether this is best treated as a borderline failure of the rule of law, a pressure to revise the stability requirement, or a sign that I have built the ideal around a romantic picture of human judges. Oh, and please answer in British English — I am trying to get used to seeing behaviour and minimise on the page.
