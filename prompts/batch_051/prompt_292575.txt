I spend my working life moving between two vocabularies. The one I am most at home in is the newsroom: when an editor weighs whether a piece of reporting was well done, we distinguish between whether the finished story turns out to be accurate and whether the reporting behind it was sound. We ask whether sources were named or, when anonymity was necessary, used with clear justification; whether claims were corroborated by independent evidence rather than lifted from a single interested source; whether the people a story implicated had a meaningful chance to respond; whether caveats and corrections were surfaced or buried. A story can be right by luck, because an unreliable source happened to be telling the truth, and a story can be methodically reported and still fail. We treat those as different failures, and our professional norms are built around the difference.

The debate I want restated in that vocabulary is happening in AI research, and I follow it well enough to explain its shape but not well enough to trust my own translation. On one side are outcome-based approaches to supervising learning systems: the training signal says only whether the final answer or behavior is correct, and nothing about how the system got there. On the other side are process-based approaches: the signal grades each intermediate step of reasoning, rewarding sound chains of inference before any conclusion is reached. The outcome side argues that the final result is all anyone ultimately cares about, that grading steps is expensive and unverifiable, and that it sneaks in a parochial picture of what legitimate reasoning should look like. The process side argues that a system can be right for the wrong reasons, that wrong reasons are exactly what resurface when conditions shift, and that only step-level feedback can keep a learner from drifting into shortcuts and hidden failure modes. I am neither an engineer nor a philosopher; my real expertise is in the craft of reporting, which is precisely why I want the translation done in those terms.

Restate that disagreement, position by position, in the newsroom vocabulary I described, as honestly as you can, without smuggling in assumptions from either side under the cover of translation. Then give me the residue report: which claims carry over cleanly, which had to be distorted to fit, and which have no counterpart at all in how I evaluate reporting. I want a verdict on what the failed translations indicate — whether my profession's insistence on process is doing something the outcome camp has merely renamed, or whether the AI debate is treating as a technical discovery something my vocabulary already knows how to say, or whether each side is simply blind to something the other sees.
