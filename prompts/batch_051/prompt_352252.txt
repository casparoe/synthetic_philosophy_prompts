Okay so I got into a long argument with a friend last night about conscription versus professional armies, and I ended up defending a position I hadn't thought through before, and now I want to see if it survives contact with someone who actually knows the subject. The angle is armed neutrality — Switzerland's militia system, Finland's huge reserve, Sweden flip-flopping between conscription and an all-volunteer force. The thesis I'm kicking around is that for a state trying to stay out of military alliances, or at least keep some strategic independence, conscription isn't mainly about manpower or civic virtue; it's an institutional brake on expeditionary drift. If your army is made up of people who chose to be there, sending them abroad is politically cheap for everyone else, and the officer corps has career reasons to go looking for missions. A conscript force built for territorial defense is clumsy at expeditionary war and makes any deployment a problem for the whole public, which locks in the defensive posture that armed neutrality needs to be credible. My friend's main objections were, one, this is just the civic-republican fairness argument with fancier language, two, actual neutral states keep conscription for path-dependent reasons — history, geography, labor markets — not because somebody reasoned through the incentives, and three, conscription hasnt stopped plenty of states from being aggressors, so the mechanism can't be doing that much work.

I want to write this up as a short essay. Could you give me comments on the idea itself? What's the weakest evidential link in the claim that a volunteer force makes expeditionary policy more likely for a neutral state? Should I bracket the gender objection or face it head-on? And am I overromanticizing the militia as a brake on intervention when a professionalized officer corps can still dominate a conscript force in practice?
