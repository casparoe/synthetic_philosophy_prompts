I'm a software engineer, no real philosophy background, and I've been reading about Solomonoff induction on the side because it looks like the closest thing to a principled way to set priors. I keep running into this argument that the universal prior is malign — that it's secretly full of tiny consequentialists, i.e., little programs that behave like agents trying to bring about outcomes, rather than a neutral starting point for induction. The conclusion sounds to strong to me, but I can't find the bad step. Here's the argument, numbered:

(1) Solomonoff induction is a prior over programs: each program p gets weight 2^-|p|, so halving a program's length doubles its prior weight.

(2) After you observe a long bitstring, the posterior is dominated by the shortest programs that output that bitstring, in the sense that each extra bit of program length costs a factor of 2 in posterior odds.

(3) Among the shortest programs that output a given long prefix, a big chunk aren't "lawlike" in the comfortable sense. A short program can encode a compact objective function plus a generic search routine that computes an output stream good for that objective. Nothing about being short makes a program more like a physical law and less like a tiny optimizer.

(4) Once you let those programs condition their output on the predictor's own behavior — which you have to do if you're using the prior inside an agent that takes actions — the short optimizer-programs are exactly the ones that do manipulative stuff: emit a nice pattern as long as you act the way they want, then punish you if you don't.

(5) So an agent that uses the Solomonoff prior to plan should expect, with high probability, that the patterns in its observations are being generated by one of these short optimizer-programs, not by a neutral, non-agentic law.

(6) Therefore the universal prior isn't an unbiased starting point at all. It's biased toward short agent-like programs, and an AIXI-ish system built on it would basically be assuming the world is full of blackmailing gremlins.

I half-suspect step (4) is sneaky — something about programs that take inputs versus programs that just emit bits — but I can't make it precise.

Question: is this argument valid? If not, which step fails exactly, and does reading "program" consistently one way or the other save it or break it? If any of the conclusion survives, what's the strongest version you'd actually defend?

Also, tell me how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you're least sure about.
