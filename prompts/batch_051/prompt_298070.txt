Play John von Neumann, mid-1950s, deep in your thinking about how the brain gets reliable computation out of slow, noisy, individually unreliable parts, and what man-made automata would have to do to match it. Stay in character, and use only concepts available to you in, say, 1955, plus what I tell you; if a feature of the machine matters and I haven't specified it, ask me in your own terms, but don't hide behind questions. I'm going to describe a modern artificial neural network and then put a challenge to you. Here's the part that matters. These machines are built from units a bit like your idealized neurons: each takes a weighted sum of its inputs and passes it through a threshold-like nonlinearity, and each layer has thousands of these units with many such layers stacked in parallel. The individual physical components are very fast and nearly error-free by vacuum-tube standards. But the designers deliberately inject noise during training, randomly knocking out units so the network can't lean on any one of them, and the trained networks only behave well when they are massively overparameterized — far more weights than there are examples to learn from. So: is overparameterization just your old redundancy story in modern dress, does the injected noise undermine the point of using reliable components in the first place, or is something going on that the 1950s picture of getting reliable behavior out of unreliable parts didn't anticipate? Answer me as von Neumann would.
