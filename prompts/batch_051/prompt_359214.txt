I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, working on skill and know-how, and I'm trying to get the lay of the land in the knowing-how/knowing-that debate. A draft survey chapter I was sent contains the following characterization of the debate (I paraphrase, but I've tried to preserve the claims and the order of argument):

Ryle's distinction between knowing how and knowing that is really the distinction between practical ability and factual information. To know how to ride a bicycle is just to have the ability to ride it, while to know that Paris is the capital of France is to hold a piece of factual knowledge. Ryle's central argument against intellectualism — the doctrine that knowing how is a species of knowing that — is a regress argument: if an action counts as intelligent only when it is preceded by an act of contemplating a proposition, then the act of contemplating that proposition would itself need to be intelligent, and so would require a further contemplated proposition, and so on forever. Since intelligent action does begin, intellectualism must be false; knowing how is therefore a non-propositional disposition to act rather than a matter of representing the world as being a certain way.

The survey goes on to say that contemporary intellectualists, most prominently Stanley and Williamson, have attempted to revive intellectualism by holding that knowing how to do something is knowing the answer to a question — so knowing how collapses into knowing that. But, it says, this revival is now widely regarded as unsuccessful. First, expert cyclists, pianists, and native speakers often cannot state the rules that guide what they do, which is supposed to show that their know-how is not propositional. Second, someone could in principle memorize every true proposition about swimming and still drown, which is supposed to show that no amount of knowing that adds up to knowing how. The moral the survey draws is that Ryle was fundamentally right: know-how is a practical capacity that cannot be captured in factual terms.

I don't trust my own sense of where this goes wrong, and I don't want to internalize a caricature. Could you audit this passage for me the way someone working directly on the knowing-how debate would? Specifically: separate what is accurate from what is a simplification from what a careful intellectualist (or a careful Rylean, for that matter) would reject; say where the summary conflates claims the relevant parties keep apart; and indicate whether the two arguments against the revival survive as stated. If the whole framing — or my request — rests on a confused understanding of the debate, I would rather be told that directly than receive a tidy answer that politely goes along with it.
