I'm a decision theory PhD student and I’ve been stuck on a paragraph in Daniel Bernoulli’s 1738 paper Specimen Theoriae Novae de Mensura Sortis, usually translated as Exposition of a New Theory on the Measurement of Risk. He sets up the coin-toss game where payouts double each time and shows the expected money is infinite, then moves to a passage where he says, roughly — I don’t have the translation in front of me so this is paraphrase not a quote — that the usual rule for judging risky prospects by expected payoff is defective, because a ducat is not the same value to everyone: a thousand ducats means more to a pauper than to a rich man, and the value of a gain depends on the holder’s circumstances. I read this as claiming the paradox isn’t really about infinity or probability; it’s about confusing price with utility. My view is the full dissolution: rational utility must be bounded, and once it is, the game has a finite expected utility and nobody is irrational for priceing it modestly.

What would actually count against that? Not just the standard tu quoque that concave utility is ad hoc or that you can re-run the game with utils and get the same infinity. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward. What would the world have to look like for bounded expected utility to be the wrong diagnosis? What would real choice behavior show if my view is true, versus if something like rank-dependent utility, cumulative prospect theory, or an ergodicity-based resolution is doing the real work? Are there formal results that would force me to give up boundedness while keeping expected utility, or give up expected utility entirely? And has the bounded-utility resolution been qualified into unfalsifiability? I realize I might be making Bernoulli say more than he did, but I’m less interested in the exegesis and more in where the view actually breaks.
