I've been trying to work out what I actually think about path dependence, and my gut verdicts on cases keep pulling in different directions. For each case below, I have a verdict both on whether the outcome is locked in and on whether a replay of history with small perturbations would probably have produced the same outcome.

- The QWERTY typewriter keyboard. I think of it as the paradigm of lock-in: a layout fixed by early mechanical limits and then entrenched by retraining costs and network effects, long after those mechanical constraints stopped binding, despite alternatives like Dvorak being available. Yet I've read that the evidence for Dvorak's actual superiority is weaker than the standard story claims, which leaves me unsure whether QWERTY is a case of a worse standard winning or merely an arbitrary standard persisting.

- VHS versus Betamax. I've always thought Betamax had the better picture quality, but VHS won because of longer recording time, more permissive licensing, and the bandwagon in video-rental stock. That feels like lock-in, but with an uncomfortably large role for VHS being better at what consumers then cared about.

- Alternating current versus direct current. I think AC won on the technical merits: transformers allowed voltages to be stepped up for transmission and down for use. I don't think this is lock-in, and I think a replay of history would give us AC again.

- Metric versus imperial units. I don't think either is functionally superior for ordinary use, so the continued use of imperial units in the United States feels like coordination on an arbitrary standard rather than lock-in, even though switching costs are real.

- Gasoline cars versus early electric cars, around the start of the twentieth century. I think gasoline won on the merits as they appeared then — energy density, range, and refueling speed — but that victory then built a filling-station and service infrastructure that made the later re-entry of electric cars much harder. So the outcome seems merit-based first and locked-in afterward.

- The case I keep going back and forth on: QWERTY on smartphone touchscreens. There the mechanical constraints and retraining costs are gone, yet the layout persists anyway. Does my principle say this is harmless convention, or does it cast doubt on whether the typewriter-QWERTY story was ever really lock-in?

Is there a single principle — stated in terms of increasing returns, network effects, switching costs, and counterfactual robustness — that captures all of these verdicts, or close to all of them? Please state the most defensible principle or small set of principles, say for each clause which case motivates it, say which of my verdicts it forces me to revise, and apply it to the smartphone case.
