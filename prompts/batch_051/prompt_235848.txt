I keep returning to Hofstadter's "Person Paper" and finding that I cannot state, in a defensible form, the convention it is satirizing. Give me a working definition of male-as-default language — the practice of letting masculine forms or male examples serve as the unmarked way of referring to people in general — that sorts the following cases correctly.

I regard the following as clearly male-as-default: the old prescriptive "generic he" in "If a student wants to leave early, he should ask permission"; "man" used for the species, as in "Man is a tool-using animal"; and "chairman" used of a woman who holds the post.

I regard the following as clearly not male-as-default: singular "they" for an unknown person; a textbook that deliberately alternates "he" and "she" in its examples; and a document that uses "she" throughout for an unspecified person as an explicit, visible choice.

I cannot settle these: "mankind" in ordinary speech; "guys" addressed to a mixed-gender group, including by one woman to another; "male nurse" and "female doctor" as expressions that mark a deviation from an assumed default for the profession; the Spanish convention under which a group of women and one man, and sometimes any mixed group, is referred to with the masculine plural; and replacing "fireman" with "firefighter" in an institution where no woman has ever held the job.

I suspect the label "male-as-default" names at least three separable things — a syntactic default (which form the grammar treats as neutral), a semantic default (a masculine word acquiring a widened, supposedly generic sense), and a social default (which sort of person is the normal case in examples and imagery) — and that many arguments about sexist language slide between them. If you agree, give me the separate definitions, apply each to every case, and say which of my contested cases migrate between categories. Then tell me which of your classifications is least secure, and whether anything in Hofstadter's satire depends on keeping the three defaults fused.
