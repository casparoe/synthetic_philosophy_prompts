I am working on a piece about the assumptions embedded in how AI developers describe the reasoning of large language models, and a routine practice has come to seem philosophically loaded in a way I cannot yet articulate. When such a model answers a difficult question, its developers typically instruct it to produce a step-by-step chain of thought, and evaluators then inspect that chain to judge whether the model reasoned soundly or merely happened upon the right answer. Yet the same developers increasingly withhold the raw intermediate text from the public, releasing instead a condensed summary of the reasoning, which they still present as a faithful account of how the model arrived at its answer. On its face, the practice seems to presuppose that a language model can accurately report its own cognitive process: that the steps it verbalizes stand in a transparent enough relation to whatever computation actually produced the answer to serve as evidence for it.

I want to know what exactly that practice presupposes. Reconstruct the implicit theory of reasoning, transparency, and self-report that would make the practice coherent, and distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely assumes as a fallible expedient. In particular, does it commit its practitioners to thinking that the model has some sort of access to why it answered as it did, or only to the weaker claim that its verbalized reasoning is correlated with its underlying processing closely enough to count as evidence? Where is the practice underdetermined, and what would have to change in how labs describe, evaluate, and certify model reasoning if the stronger view turned out to be false?
