Write a dialogue between two people who disagree about whether their friend Callie is doing something wrong—epistemically or morally—by being a vegetarian on the say-so of animal ethicists. Callie says factory farming is wrong, but when you push her on why she can't reconstruct the argument; she says she trusts the people she's read summaries of, and doesn't see why she needs to be able to derive it herself any more then she needs to understand how her vaccine works. One of them think there's a specifically moral failure here: moral belief isn't like belief about vaccines or geology, because you don't fully possess a moral view unless you can at least roughly answer the "why" for yourself. The other thinks that's a double standard dressed up as principle: we all run on a division of epistemic labor, and requiring everyone to work through the argument from marginal cases before they're allowed to say factory farming is wrong is elitist and practically unworkable. They fall into calling themselves the pessimist and the optimist about moral testimony.

Have them actually engage with each other. The optimist should force the pessimist to say what makes moral testimony different from deferring to a doctor or an engineer. The pessimist should force the optimist to explain how someone without prior moral understanding can pick a trustworthy moral source in the first place without already exercising the very judgment she's supposedly outsourcing. Both should be sharp: the pessimist shouldn't just repeat "morality requires thinking for yourself," and the optimist shouldn't just repeat "everyone defers all the time." Each should concede at least one point under pressure, and they should respond to each other's actual moves rather than talking past each other. They don't have to end in agreement, but by the end the reader should see exactly what's still separating them—say, whether the issue is about belief, understanding, justification, or worthiness to act. Don't resolve it for them.
