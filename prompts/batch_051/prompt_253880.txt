I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science working on the doomsday argument, and I've been struck by how stationary the debate has become. The basic moves have been on the table for decades: Carter and Leslie argued that, conditional on treating your birth rank as typical among all humans who will ever live, you should raise your credence that the species ends soon; Gott proposed a non-anthropic, Copernican version in terms of random sampling from a phenomenon's total duration; critics replied that the argument illicitly treats an indexical fact as ordinary evidence, and that a self-indication assumption — that your very existence should favor hypotheses on which observers are more numerous — cancels or reverses the update; and a further camp holds that the whole inference is hostage to an arbitrary choice of reference class, such as humans, sentient beings, or observer-moments. The formal tools have since been refined considerably, but people's all-things-considered views do not seem to have moved: those who find self-sampling intuitive are unmoved by the self-indication response, and conversely, even when both sides accept the same conditional probabilities under each assumption. My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not really about probability at all, but about what self-locating probability is supposed to be — in particular, whether the fact that I am the Nth human should count as evidence about the world in the same way a non-indexical observation does, and whether the prior over total population is a genuine credence about the world or merely a rule for distributing credence among self-locations in worlds one already recognizes. Is that the right diagnosis? If not, where does the disagreement actually live: a hidden empirical premise, a conflict of priors, a disagreement about which facts count as admissible data, or two questions run together under the heading of anthropic reasoning? And on the diagnosis you favor, what would actually count as progress — a formal constraint on reference classes, a sharper distinction, an empirical constraint, or should we accept that the two sides differ only in bedrock intuitions and expect no convergence? I want the structure of the disagreement, not a verdict in favor of one side.
