I keep running into a confusion in arguments about how much weight public policy should give to benefits and harms that arrive far in the future, and I suspect the disagreement is partly a failure to keep three things apart. Two instances make the structure vivid. In one debate about a flood-protection project whose main beneficiaries would be people living a century out, one side said a 3 percent rate was the standard basis for evaluating government investments, while the other side said 3 percent effectively treats a flood death a century from now as worth almost nothing. In a second case, involving how a public pension fund should value liabilities falling due in several decades, the two sides again quoted numbers that sounded incompatible, yet when I pressed them they mostly agreed about the likely growth of wages and about the actuarial risk that the payments would not need to be made.

On examination, the number being argued over appears to bundle at least three different operations. The first is the thought that people in the future will probably be richer than we are, so a dollar or a year of healthy life in their hands should count for less than the same unit in ours — the way a benefit going to a well-off person is often weighted less than the same benefit going to a poor one. The second is the thought that the future might be cut short, the program might fail, or the intended beneficiaries might never come to exist, so the expected value of the future benefit is smaller than its nominal value. The third is the thought that even after wealth, risk, and the identity of the beneficiary are all held fixed, a benefit now simply counts for more than the very same benefit later, because the later one is later. In the debates I have seen, both sides largely accept that the first two considerations should lower the weight placed on future benefits; the real fight is only over the third. Yet the third is the hardest of the three to defend, because without it one can still weight future benefits less if the future will be richer, and one can still weight them less if the future is uncertain; the third component adds nothing but a pure penalty for location later in the calendar, applied even to a different person who will exist at that later time.

I would like to carve this apart precisely. Does the third component have a standard name of its own, and what marks it as the site of the genuine normative disagreement while the first two are treated as largely technical? If the closest established concepts fit the thing I am describing only imperfectly, I want to know how the standard distinctions differ from it and which, if any, captures it exactly; a bare label will not settle the matter for me.
