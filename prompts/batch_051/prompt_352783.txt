So a collegue of mine and I have been going back and forth for weeks about how to attach probabilities to a genuinely vauge decision at work — roughly, whether a big product bet has at least a 40% chance of paying off, where the only "data" is a handful of non-comparable historical cases. He's a former physicist, very into decision theory, and last week he sent me an article he said cleared up his confusions on the whole topic. His summary of the article goes like this: ordinary Bayesianism tells you to represent your uncertainty with a single sharp probability, but when the evidence is weak or ambiguous, any single number you settle on is pure invention, and pretending otherwise is a kind of epistemic dishonesty. The imprecise probability view says you should instead keep all the probability distributions that are compatible with your evidence, and for each claim you report a range running from the most optimistic distribution to the most pessimistic one. It also says decision-making under imprecision is handled by a conservative rule: for each option, compute the worst-case expected value across all your compatible distributions, then pick the option with the best worst case. The article's clincher is the Ellsberg paradox — people prefer betting on a red ball drawn from a 50/50 urn over betting on a red ball from an urn with an unknown mix, and according to the article they're right to prefer it, which in his telling shows that anyone who assigns a single probability to the unknown urn is making a mistake.

He now wants us to switch our whole forecasting workflow to that: report ranges instead of point estimates, and always go with the most conservative option. I have a gut sense the summary is mostly fair, and the Ellsberg point feels compelling, but I can't shake the feeling that there's a slide between "a single number isn't forced by the evidence" and "a single number is a mistake." I also doubt the conservative decision rule is as settled as the article implies — as described, it sounds like it would have us never take any bet if the worst case across some vaguely specified set of priors looks bad, which feels like it could paralyze us. I don't need a bibliography; I'd like an actual accuracy audit. Go through the summary piece by piece, say what a careful defender of imprecise probability would nod along with and what they'd object to, and tell me whether the practical prescriptions (ranges plus always choosing the action with the best worst case) really follow from the parts that are correct. And one more thing: is it fair to present imprecise probabilities as a rival that shows Bayesianism is wrong, or is it better understood as a generalization of Bayesianism that just refuses to collapse your uncertainty?
