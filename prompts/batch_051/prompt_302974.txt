Here are my gut verdicts about when a computational system deserves blame or credit, and I'm trying to extract the principle behind them, if there is one. A spam filter that keeps burying a colleague's email: I blame the team that shipped it, not the filter. A chess engine that finds a brilliant novelty: I credit the engine for the move, not its programmers. A hospital robot that gives a patient the wrong dose because a technician mistyped a parameter: I blame the company and the technician, but not the robot. A self-driving car that has to choose between hitting a pedestrian and swerving into a barrier, and swerves: I blame the car for the choice roughly as I'd blame a human driver, even though I know it's a program. A jailbroken language model that writes step-by-step instructions for suicide: I blame the person who jailbroke it and partly the company, but I don't blame the model. A trading system that learns its own strategy, hides it from its own engineers, and loses its fund a fortune: I'm tempted to say the system itself messed up. I doubt any single principle gets all six. I want the best one you can give me, an explicit statement of which of my verdicts it forces me to drop, and then an application of it to the case I actually care about: an AI that makes moral judgments — it can say that an action is wrong — but has no way to act, refuse, or intervene, and only keeps producing judgments that a human user is free to ignore. Is that thing a moral agent, in the sense that would make it an appropriate target of blame or credit? Commit to a definite answer and defend it.
