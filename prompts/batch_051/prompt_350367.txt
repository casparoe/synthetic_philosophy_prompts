I am a senior program officer at a mid-sized private foundation that funds economic development, financial inclusion, and social enterprise. Our board is split: some trustees believe we should harness self-interest and markets to do good, while others believe several of our recent grants have subsidized greed. I review roughly two dozen funding proposals each quarter, I have no formal philosophical training, and I need a reusable screening procedure I can run in about ninety minutes per proposal and whose results I can defend in a short memo to a skeptical board.

Write that procedure for me. It should translate the philosophical debate about greed and acquisitiveness into a usable decision tool. Draw on whatever is genuinely usable from the old argument that private vices can produce public benefits, from the view that commerce channels self-interest into mutually beneficial cooperation, from accounts that distinguish ordinary self-interest from greed, and from the critique of greed as an insatiable or positional desire for more than one's share rather than a desire for any particular good. You may introduce distinctions the board does not already have, such as the difference between creating new value and extracting existing value, between acquisition as a means and acquisition as an end, and between appetites that satiate and appetites that escalate, but only where they do real work in the procedure.

The deliverable should be a decision procedure: the questions to ask in order; for each question, what counts as a sufficient answer; where the procedure stops with a no; where it stops with a yes; and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Mark explicitly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and say which philosophical distinctions must survive simplification and which can be dropped. Provide a one-page checklist version and a longer annotated version explaining the rationale for each question.

Then apply the procedure to three hypothetical proposals:

(a) A fintech company offers emergency loans and payroll advances to low-wage workers at an effective annual rate around 120 percent, argues that the rate reflects default risk and that the realistic alternative is illegal lenders, and asks us to fund a randomized evaluation of a fee-capped version of the product.

(b) A worker-owned home-care cooperative seeks below-market growth capital to open a second branch; surplus is reinvested in wages and training, and worker-members cap their own pay.

(c) A market-rate impact fund buys struggling local newspapers, consolidates back-office operations, promises to preserve newsroom staffing, and targets a 15 percent return with a five-year exit, charging standard private-equity management and performance fees.

For each case, show what evidence the procedure calls for, which questions it settles, and the verdict or the handoff to human judgment. Include at least one case where the procedure returns an answer the foundation is inclined not to accept, and say so plainly. Conclude by stating what the procedure cannot do: the hidden motives it cannot detect, the quantities it cannot measure, and the board-level value questions it cannot resolve.
