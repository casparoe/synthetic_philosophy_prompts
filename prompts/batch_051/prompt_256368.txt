Novelist here. I teach one fiction workshop a semester for the health insurance, which means I sit through curriculum committee meetings. I keep noticing a particular move when people defend a field that looks, from the outside, completely useless. The defender doesn't argue the field is secretly useful. They argue that uselessness is exactly the point, because useless fields are where future usefulness comes from. The move usually goes: take two or three cases where an apparently pointless line of inquiry became infrastructure decades later — the abstract algebra that supposedly became the language of computers, the conic-sections fiddling that supposedly became orbits, the formal logic that supposedly ended up inside programming — and treat those stories as if they licensed a general rule: current obscurity predicts future payoff. I'm attatched to this move myself, because I live off one of those fields. But it feels like it's cheating and I can't say why. Does this exact move have a name? It isn't plain survivorship bias — the examples are real successes, not just the visible winners of a lottery — and it seems a little different from the general slogan that apparently useless knowledge is what drives discovery. I don't just want a label. I want the carving: what the move actually is, what it isn't, how it differs from survivorship bias and from the general slogan, and what a borderline case would look like. If there's no established name for the exact thing, coin one and define its edges.
