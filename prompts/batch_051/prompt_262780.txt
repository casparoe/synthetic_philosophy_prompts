I keep getting stuck on a thing about betting, and I want to know whether there's a real principle underneath it or if it's just a feeling people have. Take two even-money bets on a coin flip. In the first, I've flipped the coin a hundred times and it's come up heads almost exactly half, so I'll happily take either side. In the second, the coin was just pulled from a jar of coins with all kinds of unknown biases and hasn't been flipped at all, and I find I don't want to take either side; I'd probably even pay a couple bucks to swap to the first coin. Most people I've asked feel the same way. But before either coin is flipped, heads and tails are perfectly symmetric in both cases — for the jar coin I've got exactly as much reason to think heads as tails, just like with the fair coin. So if I'm willing to bet on the fair coin, it seems like I should be willing to bet on the jar coin too, and my refusal is just nerves. The obvious comeback is that with the fair coin I know the odds and with the jar coin I don't. But when do I ever literally know the odds? The 'fair' coin could be a tiny bit bent, or I could have miscounted. If unknown odds were a good reason to refuse a bet, that reason would seem to apply to every real bet, since no real coin comes with a guarantee. And the other obvious move — that symmetry forces 50/50 on the jar coin too, so passing up an even-money bet is just leaving money on the table — doesn't match how the bet actually feels either. So what is the actual difference between the two coins? Is it a difference in kind, or just a difference in how much uncertainty I have, and if its just amount, where's the line? I want the strongest principle you can find that makes one coin bettable and the other not, and then a straight verdict on whether the ordinary reaction here is something we should keep or train ourselves out of.
