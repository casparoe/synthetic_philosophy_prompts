I have just agreed to lead a one-evening session for an adult-education course, and the topic I've been assigned is the dogmatism paradox. The students are attentive but have no formal background in philosophy, and I have eighty minutes. I want to teach it as a live puzzle about someone who knows something and then encounters apparent counterevidence, with the payoff being that the paradox exposes a tension between two attractive norms — trusting the evidence one already has and staying answerable to future evidence — rather than a skeptical conclusion. Plan how I should run the session rather than explaining the paradox to me: what must come first and what depends on what, one concrete carrying example that recurs throughout the session and why it suits the material rather than an arithmetic or mathematical one, and the misconceptions I should expect — I suspect at least the thoughts that knowing means being certain, that the solution is simply that one should hear out the new evidence, and that the puzzle is trivial because the counterevidence is stipulated to be misleading — and exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off. Spend most of the plan on the move from the thought that, if the proposition known is true, then any evidence against it is misleading, to the claim that the subject is in a position to know that any such evidence is misleading: I want the students to see that this step is carrying the whole weight of the paradox without formalizing it for them. End with a single question I can ask to tell whether they have grasped the distinction the paradox turns on, and say what I should deliberately leave out at this level and why.
