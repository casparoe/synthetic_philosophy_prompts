A friend and I went around in circles last night over a single wager, and I would like a referee for the unresolved core of the argument. Consider a fair coin that increases your wealth by fifty percent on heads and decreases it by forty percent on tails. A single flip of that coin has a positive arithmetic expectation of five percent per unit staked, and many people who consider themselves expected-value reasoners would take such a bet, at least at modest stakes, and would look askance at someone who refused. Now leave the coin untouched and change only the horizon: the offer becomes an indefinitely repeated series in which the entire current wealth must be staked on every flip. The arithmetic expectation of terminal wealth grows without bound, but the typical trajectory collapses, because one head and one tail together multiply wealth by 0.9; the geometric growth rate is negative, and over many flips wealth tends to zero almost surely. Suddenly the same favorable coin looks like a path to ruin, and nearly everyone reverses the earlier verdict.

That reversal is the asymmetry I cannot get past. In the one-shot case the arithmetic mean is treated as the criterion of a good bet; in the repeated case the geometric mean is treated as the criterion, even though every individual flip is identical and has the same favorable expectation. My friend insisted this is rational and principled; I am not sure it is anything more than changing one's theory of rationality with the time horizon. What I would like is the strongest single principle that yields both verdicts without that ad hoc switch. Candidates might include expected utility with the right utility function, the Kelly criterion or growth-optimal betting, time-average rather than ensemble-average reasoning, or something more basic. Then test the principle against nearby cases: fractional betting, where staking a fixed small share of wealth each round turns the same long series into a favorable strategy; one-shot bets of very different sizes; insurance, which has negative expectation but is widely bought; and correlated risks that lack the clean independence of the coin flips. Where the principle breaks, say so. The question I actually need answered is whether the asymmetry should be kept as it stands, dissolved, or revised.
