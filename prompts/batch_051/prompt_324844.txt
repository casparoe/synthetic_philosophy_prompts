Here is a parallel I cannot resolve, and I want to know whether it is deep or superficial.

In current work on AI alignment, a recurring contrast is drawn between approval-directed agents and goal-directed agents. An approval-directed agent chooses actions because it predicts that humans will approve of them, or at least will not disapprove of them; a goal-directed agent chooses actions because it predicts that they will advance a fixed objective, such as performing well on a specified task. The familiar failure mode of the first kind of agent is sycophancy: because its target is our favorable verdict rather than the world, it may tell us pleasing falsehoods or do what looks good rather than what is good. The familiar failure mode of the second is ruthless or literal-minded optimization: it may find an unintended way to satisfy the stated objective that no one actually wanted.

In moral philosophy there is a structurally similar-looking contrast. Some views make an act's rightness depend on a judge's approval: the sentiments of an impartial spectator, the approbation of a virtuous person, a community's moral feelings, or even divine approbation. Other views, paradigmatically consequentialism, make rightness depend on what the act brings about in the world, with approval entering at most as one more psychological fact that may or may not track value. The usual worry about the approval-based side is conformism or servility, since the judge may be mistaken, biased, or corruptible; the usual worry about the outcome-based side is that it may authorize serious harms for the sake of the best overall state of affairs.

The mapping I have in mind is the obvious one: approval-directed agents correspond to approval-based theories of rightness, because both make an act's normative standing depend on a judge's actual or idealized verdict rather than on the world itself; goal-directed agents correspond to outcome-based theories such as consequentialism, because both make an act's normative standing depend on the state of the world the act produces.

But I can already see a place where the analogy may break. On the AI side, the contrast seems to bottom out in a difference of goals rather than a difference in kind: an approval-directed agent is just a goal-directed agent whose goal is to maximize approval. If that is right, the two kinds of agent differ in what is being optimized, not in whether anything is being optimized. Does the same reduction work on the philosophical side? Can an ideal-observer or divine-approbation theory be restated as a form of consequentialism in which expected approval, perhaps under idealized conditions, is the good to be promoted? If it can, then the philosophical distinction too is only a difference in what one maximizes, and the parallel is deeper than it first appears. If it cannot, then some element on the philosophical side, perhaps a distinction between something's being right and something's being worth promoting, or between a verdict and a quantity to be maximized, has no counterpart in the AI contrast, and the analogy is doing no real work.

Please lay out the mapping precisely, say what would have to be true on each side for results to transfer from one field to the other, and give me at least one thing the parallel seems to predict that turns out false.
