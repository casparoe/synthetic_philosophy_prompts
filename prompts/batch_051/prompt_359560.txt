After our political philosophy seminar, two friends of mine, Alan and Beth, got into a long argument about Sen's liberal paradox, and I could not follow the formal points well enough to judge who was right at each step. As our instructor stated it, the paradox is that no social decision procedure can satisfy all three of: unrestricted domain, so that every profile of individual preferences over social alternatives is admissible; the weak Pareto condition, so that if every individual strictly prefers one alternative to another the social ranking does too; and minimal liberalism, so that at least two individuals each get to be decisive over some pair of alternatives falling within their own protected sphere, in the sense that the social ranking follows that individual's preference over the pair. I am reporting their exchange as faithfully as I can.

Alan said the paradox is a genuine discovery: you cannot combine even a minimal sphere of personal liberty with the Pareto condition, because with a protected private pair for each of two people, some profile of preferences — the standard prude-and-lewd case, where each person cares which of the two ends up reading the book — forces a social cycle. Beth replied that she did not deny the theorem follows from its conditions; her objection was to the modeling. She said Sen represents a right as dictating society's preference over a pair of social states, but a right is not a society-level preference at all; it is a rule about which choices are assigned to whom, a constraint on the scope of collective choice rather than another input to a social welfare function. On her view, the cycle is an artifact of forcing rights into the language of social preferences, and it dissolves if you model rights properly, as assignments of strategies to individuals or as constraints on what may be collectively decided.

Alan answered that Beth's repair merely relocates the problem and quietly abandons unrestricted domain. In the prude-and-lewd case, he said, the two people genuinely care about the same outcome, so there is a real collision between one person's protected choice and the other person's preferences; if Beth's game form avoids the cycle only by declaring that the disapproving person's preference may not enter the aggregation, she is drawing the boundary of the protected sphere on the basis of the very values whose conflict she claims to have dissolved, which is a substantive moral choice dressed up as a formal repair. Beth answered that Alan was smuggling the misdescribed alternatives back in. Unrestricted domain, she said, means freedom over profiles of preferences defined on the alternatives that society actually chooses among, and it does not obligate us to treat someone's private act as a social state merely because someone else has an opinion about it. A person's disapproval of another person's private reading choice no more creates a social choice problem, she said, than a preference about someone else's hairstyle does, and the conflict Alan keeps pointing to exists only inside a description that already labels private actions as social states.
