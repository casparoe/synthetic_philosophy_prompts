Write a sonnet about the poverty of the stimulus argument. The first eight lines should render the child's actual linguistic input accurately: finite, fragmentary, full of false starts, hesitations, slips, and utterances that go uncorrected. The turn should land on the mismatch between that input and what the child comes to know — for instance, that yes–no question formation in English is sensitive to hierarchical structure rather than to the linear order of words. Use the closing couplet to take a definite, argued line about what explains the gap: not a vague gesture at innate knowledge, but a stated reading such as an inference to the best explanation, a claim about what can in principle be learned from such data, or a constraint on the space of possible grammars. The poem should be accurate enough that a linguist or philosopher of language would recognise the argument, and not mistake it for the claim that children are born knowing English. Then add a short prose note of three or four sentences defending the reading taken in the couplet and noting one place where the poem leans on a contested aspect of the argument. Please write in British English.
