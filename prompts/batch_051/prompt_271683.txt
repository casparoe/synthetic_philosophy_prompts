My 15th-century ancestor's will left the family a farm on the condition that the household chronicle be kept up, and the clause says the chronicle must be continued 'in the Latin tongue, as the preceding volumes are written.' The volumes still run unbroken. Each generation clearly believed it was writing Latin, but the entries drift little by little: by the mid-1500s the spellings and word order are doing things no Roman would of accepted, and by the 1800s the entries read to me like Italian with Latin endings glued on. Today I keep it in what is honestly just Italian, and the family argues about whether we've already broken the condition. When, if ever, did the writing stop being Latin? Is 'the same language as the preceding volume' enough to keep the condition satisfied even though the chain starts at Latin and ends at Italian? Try it from the words, from what the condition was for, and from what my ancestor probably thought he was requiring, and tell me which approach should decide it.
