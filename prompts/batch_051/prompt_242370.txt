Suppose a country passes a law requiring every published work of history — academic and trade, plus school textbooks — to begin each chapter with a short "ex ante report" on the turning points it covers: what a well-informed observer at the time could reasonably have expected to happen, with rough probabilities, and which outcomes would have counted as surprises at the time. The author has to draft these reports before writing the chapter itself, and a review board spot-checks to make sure the probabilities aren't just backfilled from what actualy happened. Predict how this country's historical writing and public historical memory look after forty years. I want the chain of reasoning, not just a story: do readers actually become less hindsight-biased, or do they read the ex ante reports as a formality? Do historians start gravitating toward episodes where the ex ante view flatters their interpretation, or where surprise is easy to dramatize? What happens to words like "inevitable" and "overdetermined" in serious history? Which predictions are you most confident in, and which depend on assumptions about how the review board works or how people actually read? I'm less interested in whether the law "works" overall than in the mechanism-level argument.
