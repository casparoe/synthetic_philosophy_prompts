I'm a journalist covering decision theory and the psychology of promising, and I'm trying to get a handle on Parfit's hitchhiker. For those who don't have it in front of them: you're stranded in the desert, a driver offers to take you to town iff you'll pay him $100 once there, and you know that, once you're safe in town, you'll have no self-interested reason to pay. The driver can tell if you're lying, so you die unless you can honestly convince him you'll pay. The puzzle is usually framed as a clash between causal decision theory, which says you won't pay and therefore die, and the idea that it might be rational to make yourself irrational—to cultivate a disposition to keep promises even when keeping them has no payoff. I've collected four proposed solution for my article: (1) causal decision theory bites the bullet; (2) functional/timeless decision theory says you pay because your earlier self's model of your decision procedure is what the driver reads; (3) self-binding or precommitment devices, if available, force the payment; (4) a rule-utilitarian or virtue-theoretic answer that says cultivate promissory dispositions because they pay globally. I'm looking for a fifth proposed solution to Parfit's hitchhiker that isn't on this list and isn't a restatement or special case of any of them. It should genuinely change how the problem is set up or what rationality amounts to in the story. Then tell me whether you think the solution is any good, and what its most serious flaw is.
