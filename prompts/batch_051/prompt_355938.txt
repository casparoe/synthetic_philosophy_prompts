I'm editing the humor section of a small philosophy zine whose next issue is on the philosophy of love, and I would like you to write the satirical centerpiece. The issue is organized around the contrast between appraisal and bestowal views of love. As our contributors use the terms, appraisal views hold that love is a response to the antecedent value of the beloved—to their qualities, excellences, or some standing feature in virtue of which they are worth loving—while bestowal views hold that love is not a response to value that is already there but something that confers value, so that the beloved becomes valuable to the lover through the lover's concern and caring. The two families have characteristic embarrassments. Appraisal views get the "trading up" question: if love is keyed to a set of valuable properties, why isn't a rational lover obliged to transfer love to whoever has more of them, and how can love survive the loss of the very properties that grounded it? Bestowal views get the arbitrariness question: if love confers value regardless of what the beloved is like, what distinguishes loving a particular person from loving a stone or a stranger, and why should the beloved be flattered by a love that no feature of theirs earned?

Please write the piece as a terms-of-service agreement and companion FAQ for a fictional company—say, Meridian Relationship Appraisals—that has built an entire business on the appraisal view, offering prospective and current partners "evidence-based sustainable affection" and periodic "portfolio reviews" of what a partner contributes. The form should do the work: sections of agreement (eligibility, representations and warranties, covenants to maintain valued properties, events of default, reassignment, arbitration) plus a customer FAQ. Aim for roughly 700 words, deadpan throughout, in the voice of a boutique professional-services firm: polite, quietly monstrous, legalese only where it adds to the joke. The satire should target the appraisal view and the broader practice of treating love as a fully evidence-managed, justification-managed engagement, not a cartoon of shallowness. A reader who knows the debate should recognize that the jokes rest on its real structure: the trading-up problem, the constancy problem when properties change, and the appraisal theorist's standard retreat to a footing that is not an ordinary property, such as personhood or the bare particularity of the beloved. Handle that retreat fairly rather than ignoring it—for instance, Meridian might explain, with terrible patience, that "being a person" is a universally held property and therefore cannot distinguish one partner from another, and that the firm remains agnostic about the particularity of human attachment. Include one straight-faced quote from the company's Chief Valuations Officer responding to a bestowal-theorist's complaint that being loved under this system would feel arbitrary and unflattering. Close with a FAQ item whose calm answer lets the reductio land, without the piece winking or announcing its own thesis. Please write in American English.
