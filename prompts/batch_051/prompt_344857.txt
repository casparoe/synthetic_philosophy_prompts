I spent the last year working in São Paulo, and the conversation that keeps coming back with Brazilian colleagues is whether the 2016 removal of Dilma Rousseff was a constitutional check on an executive who had lost the ability to govern, or a parliamentary no-confidence vote smuggled into a system that has no no-confidence mechanism. That argument has pulled me into the comparative literature on presidential versus parliamentary government, and I want to think through the episode carefully rather than trade slogans about coup versus rule of law.

You are a comparative constitutional scholar who works on executive-legislative relations and regime design. Analyze the 2016 impeachment of Dilma Rousseff through the lens of the Linzian critique of presidentialism and its later critics. Take the following as background facts: Rousseff was re-elected in 2014 and governed through a fragmented multi-party coalition; in 2016, amid a deep recession and the Lava Jato corruption investigations, the Chamber of Deputies and the Senate removed her for budgetary accounting maneuvers (the pedaladas fiscais) that many regarded as a pretext; her vice president, Michel Temer, served out the term, and no fresh election was held. The theoretical landscape I want you to work with runs from Linz's argument that presidentialism's fixed terms and dual democratic legitimacy make executive-legislative deadlock rigid and dangerous, through the revisionist reply that the real culprit is party-system fragmentation rather than presidentialism as such, to the more recent idea that many presidential systems have in effect invented a substitute for the parliamentary vote of no confidence by stretching impeachment into an openly political removal device.

Please proceed in three structured stages. First, map the episode onto the theory's terms: specify who the relevant players were, what their constitutional options actually were, what the fixed term and dual-legitimacy facts would have led the theory to predict, and what routes out of the impasse each side faced. Second, say what the theory explains well about the 2016 crisis and what it misdescribes or leaves silent, especially the roles of corruption, ideology, and coalition maintenance, which do not reduce neatly to a president-versus-legislature confrontation. Third, turn the lens back on the theory: does this episode confirm the perils-of-presidentialism diagnosis, support the party-system revision, or expose something the debate never anticipated—for instance, that a presidential system's substitutes for parliamentary flexibility change the regime's character rather than repair it? Think step by step, begin by stating the distinctions you are using, and resist forcing the episode into a framework where it does not fit.
