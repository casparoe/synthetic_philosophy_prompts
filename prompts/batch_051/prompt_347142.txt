I'm in a reading group on trinitarian theology — mostly analytic folks, so we keep sliding from "persons" to "centers of consciousness" — and one member set out what he claims is a clean version of the old social-trinitarianism-is-really-tritheism objection. My notes are messy and I might have garbled a step, but this is close to what he said:

(1) On the social account, the Father, the Son, and the Spirit are three distinct selves: each has a first-person perspective, a will of its own, and proper actions (one sends, one is sent, one proceeds, etc.).
(2) If there are three distinct selves and each of them is fully God — as the doctrine says each person is fully divine — then there are three divine selves.
(3) A divine self is just a being with the full set of God-making properties: necessary existence, omnipotence, omniscience, perfect goodness, and whatever else belongs on the list.
(4) If there are three beings that each have the full God-making properties, then there are three beings that each deserve the title god — in other words, three gods.
(5) So the social account entails tritheism.
(6) Since orthodoxy rules out tritheism, the social account is false, and the only alternatives are that the persons are one self appearing three ways (modalism, which the tradition also rejects) or that "person" doesn't mean anything like a distinct self.

I can't tell whether the argument is valid and the problem is all in the premises, or whether one of the inferences is doing real work. Is it valid as stated? If not, which step exactly fails? If it is valid, which premise should a social trinitarian deny, and what does that denial commit them to? More specifically: is there a stable fourth option where the three persons are distinct selves but still count as one God for some reason other than sharing a common universal nature, and what would that option have to say about identity — numerically one god, numerically distinct selves, and is that combination coherent?
