I am a high school student, and the argument in my philosophy club this month is whether people can cause things by doing nothing. I have been keeping a list of my own honest verdicts on omission cases, and the list embarrasses me because it does not cohere in any obvious way. I want either a principle that captures the pattern or a clear statement of which verdicts I should regret. If my neighbor promised to water our plants while we were away and simply forgot, and the plants died, I call the forgetting a cause of the death, though I blame him less than if he had seen the plants wilting and chosen not to water them. If my sister sees the plants wilting and deliberately does nothing, I call her not watering a cause more confidently, though I cannot say whether the cause is the same as in the forgetting case. If a stranger at a lake watches a child drown and does not throw the life ring lying beside him, I call his failure to throw it a cause of the death, and I blame him. But if I pass a famine-relief collection and give nothing, even though a small donation would very likely save a child's life, I do not call my not giving a cause of anyone's death, and I feel considerably less blame. If a doctor fails to order a standard test that would have caught a curable illness, and the patient dies, I call that omission a cause of the death. If a classmate assigned to my group project does none of the work and the project fails, I call his inaction a cause of the failure; but a student in another period who was never assigned to the group is obviously not a cause. If a friend notices that my shirt is stained before an interview and says nothing, and I do not get the job, I do not call his silence a cause of my not getting the job.

My working theory is that an omission is a cause when it violates a norm or expectation tied to a promise, role, or relationship. That handles the neighbor, the doctor, the group project, and the shirt; but it breaks on the pair I keep returning to, because the stranger at the lake has no special relationship and I still call his inaction a cause, while the famine-relief case involves a general moral norm of helping and I still resist the causal claim. It also says nothing about why the forgetful and the deliberate omission feel like different causal verdicts even though the outcome is identical. I would like a precise principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that best captures these verdicts, and I want to know which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and whether the cause/blame distinction is doing hidden work in my list. I also want what the best principle says about a case I keep going back and forth on: a teacher watches a student cheat on a final exam and says nothing, and the cheater then wins a scholarship that the honest student would have received — is the teacher's silence a cause of the honest student's losing the scholarship? Please answer as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion; within those five points, include the principle and the case each clause is answerable to, the verdicts I must abandon, and your ruling on the teacher case.
