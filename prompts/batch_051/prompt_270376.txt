I am preparing a seminar that pairs Rousseau's *Letter to d'Alembert* with Molière's *The Misanthrope*, and I would like a reading of the play through Rousseau's philosophy of theater that takes both seriously. Rousseau treats the play as the decisive test of his claim that theater cannot improve morals: he argues that Molière stages the one character who insists on speaking the truth as a figure of ridicule, so that the audience is trained to prefer the supple hypocrisy of Philinte and Célimène's circle to honesty whenever honesty is socially costly. His deeper charges — that theatrical spectatorship isolates us, flatters amour-propre, and lets a brief moment of pity substitute for action — seem almost tailored to what the play does with Alceste. I want to know where this reading genuinely tracks the comedy and where it overreaches. Work through the concrete scenes: Alceste's refusal to flatter Oronte's sonnet, the quarrel with Philinte over whether one should tell a friend his verses are bad, Célimène's exposure, and Alceste's final retreat. Is Molière's Alceste a portrait of virtue made ridiculous, or of something narrower, such as a self-regarding honesty that refuses every distinction between courtesy and lying? Does the fact that the play also exposes the coquette and the flatterers blunt Rousseau's charge, or does it sharpen it, since the audience is still left laughing at the very demand for sincerity? End by assessing what the disagreement reveals about the moral psychology of spectatorship — whether and how laughter at a staged character trains real-world attitudes, and whether the theater Rousseau attacks is the same institution Molière practiced.
