I teach a philosophy of law course and want to use the trial scene in Shakespeare's The Merchant of Venice as a way into the textualism-versus-purposivism dispute. I don't want the familiar lecture version, where Shylock simply stands for cold literalism and Portia for mercy or equity; I want a reading that treats the scene as genuinely testing both approaches to interpretation.

Read the trial scene through that lens and give me a critical analysis that does the following. First, reconstruct the dispute precisely enough to classify the interpretive moves: where does a character argue like a textualist, looking to the words of the bond rather than to what the parties presumably intended, and where does a character argue like a purposivist or an equity-minded interpreter, looking to the point of the arrangement or to what a reasonable lawgiver would have meant? Pay particular attention to the fact that Portia defeats Shylock partly by out-literalizing him — the bond grants flesh but says nothing about blood, and nothing about taking more or less than the exact weight — while at other moments she appeals to considerations that go beyond the instrument's wording.

Second, say whether the play vindicates either position, or whether it shows something less comfortable: that both letter and spirit were available as tools, and that what determines the outcome is less a correct method of interpretation than who has the power to choose the interpretive frame. Third, address the honest critic's objection: the bond is, in substance, an arrangement authorizing the taking of a life, so its literal enforcement was always going to look monstrous, which may mean the scene stacks the deck against textualism rather than refuting it. Does the scene survive that objection, and if so, what exactly does it demonstrate about the limits of literal interpretation?

Finally, tell me what the play adds to the philosophical debate that abstract argument about statutes and legislative intent does not. I want a reading that is faithful in both directions — willing to say where the play distorts the issue or loads the case, not just where it illustrates the distinction.
