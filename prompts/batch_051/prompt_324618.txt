I need help settling an argument I overheard between two friends, because each of them made points I couldn't answer and I have no philosophical training to fall back on. The argument started with my brother's financial decision but quickly became about something more general.

My brother recently inherited a large sum of money and is trying to decide whether to give most of it to groups that prevent malaria deaths in poor countries. He is genuinely torn: he thinks there is roughly an 80 percent chance that giving at that scale is admirable but not required, and roughly a 20 percent chance that he is actually obligated to give away nearly all of it, because the suffering prevented is so much larger than the sacrifice involved.

Elena said the 20 percent should settle the question. If there is a real chance that a serious obligation exists, caution demands acting as if it does, because the downside of wrongly ignoring the obligation is enormous. She compared it to a doctor who gives a drug while aware of a one-in-five chance that it will kill the patient. No competent doctor would ignore that risk merely because the most likely diagnosis says the drug is safe.

Marcus said the comparison fails. In the medical case, everyone agrees about how bad the bad outcome is; the only dispute is about the odds. Between rival moral views, by contrast, there is no shared scale for measuring how bad the bad outcomes are. A 20 percent chance of killing a patient joins a shared probability to a shared valuation, but a 20 percent chance of holding the wrong moral theory joins it to nothing comparable: the other moral view does not say the harm matters less, it says there is no such obligation at all, or that the harm matters in a different way. He said the only defensible policy is to act on the view you find most plausible, and, where real uncertainty remains, to prefer actions that would be acceptable under every view you take seriously.

Elena answered that this lets people off the hook too easily. Suppose someone is 60 percent confident that a certain tax is fair and 40 percent confident that the tax is a serious injustice. On Marcus's policy, the majority view wins outright and the 40 percent is simply set aside, even though the act would be deeply wrong if that minority view is the right one. She said ignoring a substantial risk of wrongdoing is no better than ignoring a substantial risk of harm; the absence of a shared scale does not make the risk disappear, it just makes it harder to weigh.

Marcus replied that saying "err on the side of caution" presumes there is a side to err on. From the 80 percent view, there is no obligation and no moral catastrophe in keeping the money; calling the missed obligation a catastrophe is already a verdict delivered from inside the 20 percent view. You cannot import that verdict into the decision while claiming to stand neutrally between the views.

At that point they asked me to referee. I could see some force in both positions, but I could not identify what they were really arguing about. Please go through the exchange point by point: which claims is each one right about, which analogies hold and which fail, what both of them are missing, and which side is on stronger ground overall.
