Right, here's the working definition I've been using when I call something a carbon tax:

1. **Price on emissions**: it makes emitting more carbon worse for your bank balance than emitting less, by attaching a set amount of money to each tonne (or gram) of CO2-equivalent.
2. **Public and compulsory**: the charge is laid down in law, and a public authority administers and enforces it; it is not a voluntary offset or a private contract between firms.
3. **Uniformity**: two people releasing the same amount of CO2 in the same covered activity owe the same amount. The charge is a function of emissions alone, not of a benchmark, category, or which side of a line the source falls on.
4. **Price, not quantity**: it works by changing relative prices; it does not cap total emissions, and it leaves the size and location of emissions reductions to whoever faces the price.

Now consider this scheme. A small country brings in a "clean car levy" on all newly registered cars. For every model, the transport ministry sets a benchmark emissions figure, and the benchmark varies by the car's class and footprint, so a big estate car gets a looser benchmark than a city hatchback. If the model's rated CO2 is above its benchmark, the buyer pays the ministry £40 for every gram above it. If the model is below its benchmark, the ministry pays the buyer £40 for every gram below it. The scheme is meant to be revenue-neutral over the long run, and the fees and rebates run through a single public fund. The government calls it a price on carbon with rebates for clean choices; the opposition calls it a subsidy dressed up as a tax.
