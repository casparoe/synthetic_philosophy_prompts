Something that keeps bugging me: a liberal state is supposed to be scrupulously neutral about religion, but nobody expects it to be neutral about culture or language. France enforces a version of laïcité strict enough to ban consipicuous religious symbols in public schools, yet it bankrolls a national language academy, subsidizes its film industry, and treats the French language as a national project. Meanwhile the same Anglosphere liberals who cheer bilingual education and publicly funded ethnic festivals tend to treat direct state funding of religious worship, or recognizing religious family law, as a line the state shouldn't cross. Both camps seem to be running the same assumption: religion is the special category the state must keep its hands off, but culture is fair game for promotion, subsidy, even coercion. Is there a principled distinction between religion and culture that can bear that weight, or is the line just inherited from a history of church–state battles? Give me the best defense of the asymmetry you can, then stress-test it on hard cases: public money for explicitly religious art, a language-revival program for a liturgical language, an established church treated as national heritage. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then reason—no preamble and don't restate the question.
