Act as a philosopher of science who specializes in the foundations of complexity, measurement, and how one can infer causal history from present structure. I teach a seminar called "What Makes an Object Complex?" and this year we are working through proposals that try to measure complexity as the amount of constructive work that went into producing a particular object — the lineage that includes assembly theory but also older ideas about hierarchical construction, modularity, and the recursive reuse of parts, contrasted with entropy- and information-based approaches. Before I let the class loose on the proposals themselves, I want a specification of what such a theory or measure would have to get right. Please think through the desiderata carefully, and begin by brainstorming a list of canonical objects and construction histories that any measure should handle; then present your final list cleanly, with the motivating case or consideration behind each requirement.

Write the list of requirements that an adequate measure of "assembled complexity" — how much construction an object required, for the purpose of distinguishing genuinely constructed or selected objects in scientific settings such as prebiotic chemistry, artifact analysis, and biosignature detection — must meet. At a minimum, address these phenomena, but add any others you think matter: (i) a perfect crystal versus a random polymer of the same length versus a folded functional protein, which ordinary scientific practice treats as differently complex, and not in the order that entropy or Shannon information would give; (ii) an object produced by assembling modules and reusing them, such as a wooden chair built from repeated strut-and-joint units, versus an object with the same final structure produced in one continuous, non-modular process; (iii) an object that acquired many "parts" by fragmentation or weathering rather than construction, which presumably should not count as highly assembled; (iv) two physically identical objects with different construction histories — say, a molecule made by an organism and the same molecule synthesized stepwise by a chemist — and whether the measure should be intrinsic to the object or historical; (v) the granularity problem, since any such measure depends on what is counted as an elementary building block, so a proposal must specify its units or say how sensitive it is to that choice; (vi) the random-sequence problem, where, if every new combination counts as a new assembly step, a maximally disordered arrangement can look maximally "constructed" even though nothing selected or preserved it, which suggests the measure may need to be paired with something like abundance or copy number rather than used alone; and (vii) tractability, since the measure should be effectively computable in realistic cases, unlike something such as Kolmogorov complexity.

For each requirement, say whether it is non-negotiable for any theory in this area or a preference of one camp — for example, the requirement that the measure be intrinsic rather than historical, or that it be single-scale rather than multi-scale. Then address the two issues I most want the class to see: (a) which subsets of these requirements are in tension, such as resolution-independence versus computability, or capturing actual construction history versus using only the object's present structure, and whether those tensions can be resolved by adding a second axis like abundance; and (b) which single requirement does the most work in separating the serious proposals from the popular-but-inadequate ones. End with a brief scorecard comparing the main contenders against these criteria, but keep it short and subordinate to the criteria: I am after the specification itself, not a ranking contest.
