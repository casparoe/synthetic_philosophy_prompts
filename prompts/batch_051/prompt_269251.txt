After a career in experimental physical chemistry, I have lately been reading decision theory, and I now convene a small seminar for upper-level undergraduates and first-year MA students; we have just finished expected utility theory and have started on puzzles in which a rational agent's own past and future selves seem to pull apart. I would like to set a closed-book, 60-minute exercise on the absent-minded driver, built around the following parameterization. A driver on a long highway will pass two exits that are indistinguishable to him; exiting at the first leads to payoff 0, exiting at the second leads to payoff 4, and continuing past both leads to payoff 1. He knows all of this, knows he is absent-minded, and must settle on a single probability p of exiting at whichever exit he finds himself at. The exercise should be fully self-contained and should not ask for any bibliography, citations, or named positions.

Please draft your response in three sections, in this order. Section 1, "Question" (about 200 words): an exam question with clearly labelled parts. It should require the candidate (a) to derive the ex ante optimal p and the associated expected payoff; (b) to justify the conditional probabilities of being at the first or second exit upon finding himself at an exit, expressed in terms of p; (c) to show that maximizing expected payoff conditional on being at an exit leads to a different randomization from the ex ante optimum; and (d) to diagnose in a paragraph why the conflict arises, given that a single play can pass through the decision point twice. For your own checking: the ex ante optimum is p = 1/3 with value 4/3; the odds of being at the first versus the second exit are 1 : (1 − p), giving probabilities 1/(2 − p) and (1 − p)/(2 − p); and the conditional-expected-payoff maximizer is 2 − √21/3, approximately 0.47, not 1/3. Section 2, "Model answer" (about 1,000 words): a complete answer with the algebra for both optima set out step by step, and a concluding paragraph on imperfect recall and the status of precommitment. Section 3, "Marking scheme" (about 400 words, 20 marks total): a list of the must-hit points, each with the marks it is worth and a one-line note on the specific error candidates typically make, such as treating the probability of being at an exit as 1, or assuming that the denominator 2 − p is an ordinary normalizing constant rather than an expected number of visits. The question should separate students who can apply expected utility to an unfamiliar model from those who have merely memorized the puzzle's label.
