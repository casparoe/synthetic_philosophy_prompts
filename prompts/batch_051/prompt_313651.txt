I'm helping a small policy institute set up a democracy-monitoring project, and we keep hitting the same problem: when does a government stop being "the side we disagree with" and start being a case of democratic backsliding? The label gets applied to governments doing very different things, and our partners on the left and right reach for it in sharply different directions, which has made me wonder whether the term has any shared content at all, or is just a partisan label.

I'd like a working definition of democratic backsliding that satisfies three tests. It should clearly count: Hungary under Orbán, where an elected supermajority has rewritten electoral rules and captured the judiciary and much of the media over two decades; and Turkey, where Erdoğan has imprisoned political opponents and hollowed out parliament. It should clearly not count: a routine, contested welfare-state reform in a consolidated democracy (e.g., a government raising the retirement age after campaigning on it), or a temporary emergency measure with sunset clauses and genuine judicial review in a country with strong courts. And it should give a definite, defensible verdict on these six hard cases:
1. Poland under PiS, where the executive captured the constitutional tribunal and public media within a few years, but free elections continued and the incumbent coalition eventually lost one;
2. The United States around 2016–2021: a president pressuring state election officials to change results and then challenging the transfer of power, while most state and local election administration held;
3. A state-level voting law in the US that cuts early-voting days and tightens ID requirements in a state where one party controls the legislature;
4. An executive making a serious attempt to pack the supreme court in a democracy with a written constitution (the US in 1937 is the standard example);
5. A democratically elected government that keeps winning free elections but uses its majority to redraw districts and entrench itself;
6. Hungary in, say, 2011: after the 2010 supermajority but before the later consolidated system you count as a clear case.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence: the definition, and which of the six cases it counts as backsliding. Then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. It would also help if the definition says whether backsliding is a property of a trajectory or of a condition at a time, since the boundary cases seem to turn on that.
