I am a philosopher of mind, and for some years I have found myself drawn to a particular version of panpsychism — constitutive micropsychism — according to which the consciousness of a human being is not an emergent novelty but is literally constituted by, or grounded in, the experiential states of the organism's most fundamental physical parts. The theory's appeal, as I see it, is systematic: it refuses to permit consciousness to pop into existence at some arbitrary threshold of complexity; it avoids both a nonphysical soul and a brute psychophysical bridge law; and it keeps the ontology lean, since the only stuff there is experiential stuff, merely organized at different scales. When I try to take the view literally, however, I collide with an intuition I cannot shake. My experience is had by exactly one subject. However many micro-subjects one packs into a brain, their experiences co-occurring looks to me like at most a chorus of tiny minds — never the single, unified thing it is like to be me. The difficulty is not merely that my sensations might combine in some surprising way; it is that subjects seem to be the wrong sort of entity to be summed at all. That, in my view, is the combination problem in its sharpest subject-summing form, and it makes me suspect that my attraction to the theory may be little more than a taste for parsimony and gradualism dressed up as metaphysics.

I want you to help me decide what should yield here: the theory's promise of a lean, non-emergent ontology of consciousness, or the firm intuition that a composite of many subjects cannot be one subject. Please respond in exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction, no conclusion, and no headings apart from the numerals. The five points should, in order, do the following. (1) State the strongest case for thinking my anti-composition intuition is a product of imagination or of conflating the whole with its parts, and therefore a datum I should discount rather than let refute the theory. (2) State the strongest case for thinking the intuition tracks a genuine and nonnegotiable feature of subjects that any adequate account of consciousness must preserve. (3) Identify what I would lose, philosophically, if I gave up constitutive micropsychism and replaced it with a strongly emergentist account of macro-level consciousness. (4) Describe the amendment or alternative within panpsychism that the intuition most naturally pushes toward — for instance, a cosmopsychist view that proceeds from one cosmic subject down to my finite experience, or the introduction of a primitive phenomenal-bonding relation — and say what that move buys and what it costs. (5) Offer your own negotiated verdict: which of these moves is best justified, and whether the move described in (4) is a genuine resolution or merely a relabeling of the very problem it claims to solve.
