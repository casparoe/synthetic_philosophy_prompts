I keep noticing a pattern in how people argue about rights, and I don't have a word for it. Someone insists a right is absolute, full stop — you can't violate it no matter what the consequences are. But push them on a concrete case where violating it prevents something enormous (torturing a suspect who knows where a bomb is, to stop it killing hundreds) and they'll say "well, obviously you can do it then," without apparently feeling that this concession damages their earlier claim. The right works like a trump card in ordinary cases, then stops being a trump once the stakes get high enough — except nobody states in advance where the line is. I've seen the same move over property: taking a family's land without consent is treated as unthinkable, except when its for a flood-control project that would save a whole town, and then the same people shrug and say its not really a rights question anymore.

It isn't plain consequentialism, because in everyday cases these same people refuse to trade the right for modest benefits — they'd never allow slapping one person to make two others happy. And it seems different from the "rights as side constraints" idea, since a side constraint, as I understand it, is supposed to hold regardless of the consequences. So what is this position called? Is there an established name for rights that are absolute until some catastrophe threshold, then give way without anyone calling it a violation? And if there's no standard name, what would you call it? I'm less after the label than the machinery: how the view is supposed to work, how it sets the threshold, and whether it can still call itself deontological once it admits the threshold. I'd also like to know whether I'm running together two different things — a right you're permitted to break in extreme cases, and a right that just gets outweighed by the stakes.
