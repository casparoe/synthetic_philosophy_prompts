I'm in a philosophy of mind reading group and I've cought myself using a policy I want somebody to stress-test. Here's the situation. We were talking about the color phi experiment: a red dot flashes on the left, a green dot flashes on the right a moment later, and with the right timing people report seeing one dot slide across and change from red to green roughly in the middle, which is weird because the second flash hasnt happened yet at the point where they say the color changed. One way to make sense of it is that the brain holds the whole thing back for a beat and only then "presents" you with a tidy, already-edited sequence (call it the Stalinesque story). Another is that you consciously see whatever you see in real time, but then your memory gets revised so fast and so completely that all you can ever report is the tidy sequence (call it the Orwellian story). The two stories make identical predictions about what you'll say, how startled you'll be, where your eyes go, everything we can measure, at least so far.

My policy is this: when two stories about when an experience happens differ only in the chronology they posit inside the head but agree on everything observable, I treat them as the same story for purposes of belief. I'll say there's no factual question about which is right, or I'll just refuse to have a view. My friend in the group says that's a dodge: I'm smuggling in verificationism, or I'm too lazy to think about what genuinely separates the two stories -- one says the edited version was never conscious, the other says it was conscious and then got erased from memory. She thinks my rule is fine for choosing between rival weather models but not for questions about consciousness, where the whole point is to ask which internal story is true.

Is my policy defensible, specifically for cases like color phi? When would it go wrong? Could it be restated so it keeps whatever is good about it without me just refusing to look at the hard part? I'm not running experiments, just reading the papers, so maybe I'm allowed to be this indifferent, but I honestly can't tell whether I'm being disciplined or evasive.
