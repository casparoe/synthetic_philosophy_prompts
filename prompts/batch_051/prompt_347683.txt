I am trying to work out what I actually think about when small causes matter in history — the for-want-of-a-nail problem — and my case-by-case verdicts point in different directions. I want to know whether there is one principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures the pattern, and which of my verdicts would have to be dropped or revised.

Here are the cases and what I find myself wanting to say about each.

1. The thrown shoe. A courier's horse throws a shoe on the morning of a battle; the orders arrive an hour late; the attack goes in late and uphill; the army is destroyed; the kingdom is annexed within a year. I want to say the thrown shoe was a genuine cause of the defeat and the annexation, but only because the campaign had no slack. I would not conclude that horseshoes generally decide dynasties. The lesson I draw is that a trivial failure becomes load-bearing when a whole chain is tightly coupled and nothing in it is redundant.

2. The wrong turn. A visiting heir apparent survives a first assassination attempt; later that day his driver takes a wrong turn and stops the car directly in front of the same would-be assassin, who kills him; within weeks the great powers are at war through a cascade of ultimatums and mobilizations. I want to say the wrong turn determined the date and the particular trigger of the war, but not whether such a war occurred. The system contained several live sparks, and a war of roughly that shape was probable within a few years. The wrong turn changed when and how, not whether.

3. The storm. An invasion fleet is scattered by an unseasonable storm; the invasion fails; the cause of the dynasty it was meant to restore collapses; thirty years later the rival regime falls to revolution. I want to say the storm was decisive for the failed landing and perhaps for the timing of the dynasty's demise, but not for the revolution that ended the rival regime. That fall was overdetermined by debt, military overreach, and domestic unrest. The storm belongs in the story of the campaign, not in the fundamental explanation of the regime's collapse.

4. The deciding vote. A funding panel splits four to four. The chair, tired and eager to end the meeting, switches from no to yes; the project is funded, succeeds, and opens a new field. I want to say the chair's tired flip was a genuine cause of the discovery. No other group was doing the work, the proposal would otherwise have been dropped, and there was no redundant path to the result. A procedural micro-decision can be fully load-bearing.

5. The false alarm. During a standoff, a faulty early-warning sensor registers an incoming attack. The duty officer, acting against protocol, holds the report back and waits for corroboration. No war follows. Years later it emerges that the system produced many such false alarms, and that peace was supported by several independent checks. I want to say the officer's judgment mattered enormously that night, and that the counterfactual in which he escalates is genuinely frightening. But I resist saying the glitch, or the officer's decision, caused the peace. The system generated near-miss after near-miss; a single glitch seems the wrong unit of explanation, even though the night-by-night counterfactual appears dramatic. I notice this may be in tension with the thrown shoe, where I was willing to say exactly that a single trivial event was load-bearing because the system was tightly coupled.

6. The garbled schedule. A scheduler misreads a voicemail and books a candidate's flight two hours too late; the candidate, exhausted, gives a poor debate performance; the candidate loses the election by fewer votes than the rally in the missed district could reasonably have moved; the incoming government pursues a policy with consequences that persist for decades. On one side, this looks like the deciding vote: no redundancy, a clear counterfactual chain, and durable downstream effects. On the other side, it looks like the false alarm: an election is a densely coupled process full of near-misses, and treating one scheduling error as the cause of the new policy feels like the kind of explanation I rejected in the storm case. This is the verdict I cannot settle.

Is there a coherent account of what makes a small event load-bearing, or a cause at the right level of explanation, that captures all or most of these verdicts? Please state the principle precisely, defending each clause by the case that motivates it. Then apply it to the garbled-schedule case. Finally, tell me which of my verdicts the account forces me to drop or amend, or, if no single principle gets them all, lay out the candidate principles and the trade-off between saving every verdict with extra machinery and accepting a simpler principle that overturns one or two.
