I've been trying to steelman the European position on reproductive markets, and I keep hitting an asymmetry I don't know how to dissolve.

In much of Western Europe you can't pay a woman to carry a pregnancy; the contract is void or the whole arrangement is banned. But a bunch of those same legal systems are fine with compensating a woman for an egg cycle: two weeks of self-injected hormones, a minor surgical retrieval, a small but real chance of ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome, and a few thousand euros framed as compensation for discomfort. The medical burden is a lot smaller than pregnancy, but it isn't trivial, and nobody reaches for body-renting vocabulary in that context. Meanwhile in the US both are legal commerce, and egg banks will pay noticeably more to a donor with high test scores and a degree from a selective college.

So the line isn't being drawn by how invasive the procedure is, how much money changes hands, or whether the woman is the genetic mother—egg donors always are, gestational carriers often aren't. What's left looks like the pregnancy itself: months of entanglement, labor, a child she has carried. I want you to tell me whether there's a principle that genuinely justifies treating paid gestation differently from paid egg retrieval. If you were drafting the rules from scratch, would you keep the European split, adopt the American one, or build a third thing? Weigh the strongest candidates—exploitation, the specialness of pregnancy, the wrong of treating bodies as fungible, differences in consent and exit—and then defend a verdict. Don't end by saying both sides have a point. If your best principle has a hard case it can't handle, say what the case is and whether that should sink the principle or just mark it's limits.
