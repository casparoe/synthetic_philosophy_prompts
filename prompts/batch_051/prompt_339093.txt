Hey — I run a monthly discussion group (14ish people, 90 minutes, a mix of students and working adults, not much philosophy background), and we're doing a session on Moloch, by which I mean multipolar traps and races to the bottom, not the poem or the god. I want the centerpiece to be a game rather than a lecture: I want people to feel themselves getting pulled into the trap, seeing the bad outcome coming, not wanting it, and still choosing into it because each local move looks like the reasonable one. I've run the standard shared-pool sim before (fish quotas, a pot with a multiplier) and it flops: people coordinate verbally in the first minute and the lesson evaporates. I think what was missing is the multipolar part — the bad equilibrium shouldn't be one dramatic act of defection, but dozens of small, individually sensible choices accumulating across players, where no single player's restraint seems to change anything, so even the people who see exactly what's happening keep making the small choice.

Could you design a runnable game that captures that? Constraints: it has to work in a room with at most index cards, a whiteboard, and some tokens; 90 minutes including debrief; no phones or laptops. I'd also love a final round where someone's restraint would only have mattered if a threshold had been crossed earlier, so the last-minute refuser feels the futility of having held out — but only if you can set the payoffs so early restraint isn't obviously correct and the game doesn't collapse into "everyone should just obviously restrain." If that mechanic doesn't work, leave it out. Give me the full rules, payoffs, a worked example or two of scores, a timeline, debrief questions that map specific game moments onto the actual idea, a prediction of what will go wrong when it's run and how to handle it, and an honest note on which feature of real multipolar traps the activity can't reproduce and the facilitator will have to supply.
