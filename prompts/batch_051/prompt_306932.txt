In his post Meditations on Moloch, Scott Alexander writes:

"Bostrom makes an offhanded reference of the possibility of a dictatorless dystopia, one that every single citizen including the leadership hates but which nevertheless endures unconquered. It’s easy enough to imagine such a state. Imagine a country with two rules: first, every person must spend eight hours a day giving themselves strong electric shocks. Second, if anyone fails to follow a rule (including this one), or speaks out against it, or fails to enforce it, all citizens must unite to kill that person. Suppose these rules were well-enough established by tradition that everyone expected them to be enforced."

Okay, so take that exact setup and vary it like this: suppose one person in ten thousand is a ‘martyr’ whose utility function just doesn’t include staying alive—they would rather die ending the shock system than live as an enforcer. Everyone knows that such martyrs exist and roughly how many, but not who they are until one of them acts. A martyr can publicly announce a date to skip shocks, which would get them killed under the rules, but the announcement might serve as a coordination point for the other citizens who all hate the system. Run the equilibrium analysis. Does a tiny martyr minority make the all-enforce equilibrium unstable? Does it matter whether martyrs can commit to a date beforehand, or can only spontaneously defect? What if the martyrs are known to be strategically rational but suicidal, so they will only defect when they expect it to work—does that make them useless as a coordination device? Work through the thresholds for when the trap unravels, and say whether adding martyrs changes the standard multipolar-trap diagnosis or just relocates the coordination problem.
