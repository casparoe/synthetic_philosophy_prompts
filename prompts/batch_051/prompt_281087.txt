Exam question: Explain Clifford's evidentialist principle and James's response to it. Then identify the strongest thing James has to concede to Clifford, and explain why James thinks that concession still leaves a space for faith.

Student answer:

Clifford thinks it is never acceptable, for anyone, in any circumstances, to hold a belief on insufficient evidence. His ship-owner case—the man who convinces himself his ship is seaworthy, sends it out, and it sinks—is meant to show that beliefs can't be kept private, because they shape what we do. So even a belief that feels harmless becomes objectionable once it guides action. James responds by saying intellectual duties aren't the whole story. He argues that some decisions cannot wait for sufficient evidence, because refusing to decide is already a decision. The key notion is the genuine option: a choice between two hypotheses that is living, forced, and momentous. Religious faith is his example: it is forced because you either commit or you don't, and momentous because it shapes an entire life, and the evidence doesn't settle it. In cases like that, James says our passional nature may decide, and we have a right to adopt a belief even though the evidence is not decisive. So his view is that whenever the evidence is insufficient and the matter is important to us, it is permissible to believe the thing we most want to be true. The strongest thing James concedes to Clifford is that this exception only applies when the belief cannot harm other people; when a belief would put others at risk, Clifford's strict rule still holds. That's why the ship-owner remains wrong even on James's picture.
