For a class project in my school's economics and society seminar, I have to facilitate an adversarial collaboration between two classmates who keep talking past each other about Goodhart's law. One of them, whom I will call the inevitabilist, treats Goodhart's law as a structural regularity rather than a mere empirical tendency: in his view, whenever a proxy is optimized as if it were the target by a sufficiently capable optimizer — a student gaming a rubric, a firm gaming a reported metric, or a reinforcement-learning system gaming a reward function — the proxy and the underlying goal will decouple, and the only reliable response is to avoid maximizing fixed proxies at all. The other, whom I will call the mitigationist, treats Goodhart's law as a description of naive proxy design rather than an inevitability: in her view, carefully selected proxies, combined with multiple relatively independent measures, human audits, scheduled revision, and limits on optimization pressure, can keep a measure aligned with its target for practical purposes, and specification gaming is a signal that a measure needs repair rather than proof that measurement itself is hopeless.

I would like you to produce the document such a collaboration would yield, written so that both classmates could sign it. It should contain: (1) a numbered list of agreed claims that are specific and non-vacuous, covering at least the distinction between a proxy and the target it is meant to track, what optimization pressure does to a proxy's reliability, and the role of intention in gaming; (2) a disagreements table whose rows state the residual disagreements, with each position formulated in wording its own side would accept, and the crux on which each row turns; and (3) for each crux, the observation, experiment, or case history that both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. If you judge that some portion of their dispute is verbal rather than substantive, identify that portion and explain why.
