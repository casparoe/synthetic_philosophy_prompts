In our reading group the dispute about the procreation asymmetry has become a perfect stalemate, and I want to understand the stalemate itself rather than win the argument. By the asymmetry I mean the claim that the absence of pain is good even when no one exists to enjoy its absence, while the absence of pleasure is not bad even when no one exists to be deprived of it: we have reason to avoid bringing a miserable child into existence, but no parallel reason to bring a happy child into existence merely because the life would be happy. The same deadlock reappears in adjacent cases, such as whether a couple choosing between two possible children has a pro tanto reason to select the one who would not carry a mild congenital condition rather than the one who would, even though on person-affecting views neither child is worse off in the outcome where it is never born. For months the loop has been identical. Critics treat the asymmetry as manufactured: a shadow cast by status quo bias, by the psychological difference between gains and losses, by an unnoticed slide between person-affecting and impersonal comparisons, or by the usual two-by-two table of presence and absence doing the intuitive work rather than recording it. Defenders reply that any real difference in what we owe merely possible people would have to be generated by some corresponding difference in the value of presence and absence for the person, and that the critics' debunking strategies would apply equally to our reasons against creating miserable lives — a conclusion the critics themselves are unwilling to accept. Neither side moves.

I am not asking you to adjudicate the asymmetry itself. What kind of disagreement is this, actually? My hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that the two sides disagree about the direction of explanation: defenders treat the asymmetry as a fixed intuitive datum that an account of procreative reasons must explain, while critics treat it as a construct that must earn its keep against other commitments. Rival diagnoses could also fit: perhaps the dispute is verbal, turning on an ambiguity in phrases like a benefit or a cost to no one; perhaps it is a clash of bedrock intuitions about whether existence and nonexistence can be compared as states at all; perhaps a hidden formal question about how reasons aggregate when there is no subject to receive the good or bad is doing the work. Diagnose the structure of the disagreement as precisely as you can, and given that diagnosis, say what would count as progress: a disambiguated question, a formal constraint, a new class of cases, a change in what the question is taken to be, or a reasoned case that convergence should not be expected.
