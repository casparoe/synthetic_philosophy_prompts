I am putting together the research agenda for a new three-year programme on decision-making under complete ignorance—the setting in which an agent knows the possible states of the world and the payoff of each act in each state, but has no probability distribution over the states, so that maximin, minimax regret, and similar non-probabilistic rules are the live candidates. The programme has room for roughly four postdoctoral researchers, so I need to decide which conceptual questions to put at the centre, and in what order, rather than spread effort across everything.

The questions I am currently weighing include, though you should feel free to add to the list or argue that some of these should be dropped:

(1) How the state space should be individuated, given that maximin, minimax regret, and the other standard rules are not invariant under redescription of states. The same underlying situation can be partitioned into states in different ways, and the rules then issue different verdicts; it is therefore not clear that any of these rules is well-defined until this is settled.

(2) Whether maximin and minimax regret are answers to different questions—whether the agent should minimise the worst absolute outcome or the worst shortfall relative to what could have been had—and whether either notion of loss is non-arbitrary.

(3) Whether the worst-case concern behind maximin is better captured as a family of cautious expected-utility rules over sets of priors, and whether that recasting dissolves, relocates, or merely sharpens the disagreement between Rawls's use of maximin in the original position and Harsanyi's impartial-observer derivation of average utilitarianism.

(4) Whether the original position is best modelled as decision under complete ignorance at all, or whether modelling choices about the veil of ignorance and about the principles of justice are doing the real work before the decision rule is ever invoked.

(5) Whether complete ignorance is a genuine epistemic situation or only an idealised limit of severe uncertainty, and what downstream work turns on the answer.

Prioritise these questions, along with any other open question you think belongs in the top five, by three criteria: how much other work in the area depends on settling it first; how tractable it is for philosophers with training in decision theory and formal epistemology rather than for economists; and how much downstream philosophical work would change if it were settled one way rather than another. I want a defended ordering, not a survey: for each placement, say what makes the question more pressing than the one below it, and identify any question you would leave out of the programme because settling it would change surprisingly little. Please also flag any question that looks foundational but is in fact a trap for a small programme with a three-year horizon.

Finally, I want the answer to show its working about its own ranking. Say which argument or consideration would most change the ordering you have given, and where your dependency claims are least robust—that is, where further work might reverse your judgement about what is upstream of what.
