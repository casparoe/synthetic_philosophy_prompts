I'm giving the opening speech at my town's 300th anniversary celebration and I need you to write it. Heres the situation. Everyone in town grows up with the story that the place was founded in 1723 by a man named Elias Harrow, a religious dissenter who walked out of Boston with a bible and a plow and started a little community of conscience. The town seal shows the plow and the bible, the elementary school pageant retells it every spring, old-timers can quote bits of it from memory.

But I'm on the board of the historical society, and we've spent the last two years in the actual records. The real Harrow was a Boston land speculator who probably never set foot here. He assembled the tract through a chain of purchases that were, let's say, hard to defend, including from the people who were already living on it. The whole dissenter story was invented in the 1890s by a local newspaper editor during the colonial-revival craze, and it stuck because the town needed a better origin. The evidence is solid, our report comes out next month, and the timing could not be worse.

So I have to stand up in front of the schoolkids who just did the pageant, the old-timers who love the story, the historical society members who've read the report, and a delegation from the tribe whose ancestors were on the losing end of the actual land deals. I cant just repeat the founding story as if it were true, and I can't spend the town's birthday lecturing everyone about how their grandparents were sold a fake past. Write the speech, eight minutes or so. It has to let the town keep its sense of itself without lying about what happened, and it has to say something true to the people whose ancestors were displaced without turning the whole thing into an apology tour or implying the town should feel guilty for existing. I keep getting stuck on the question underneath it too: when a community's memory and the written record conflict, does one of them have to give way, or is there a way to hold both honestly? The speech should work that out, not just decorate it. And make it sound like a mayor, not a professor.
