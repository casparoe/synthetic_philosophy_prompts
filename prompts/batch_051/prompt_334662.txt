I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am working on a long piece about a question that keeps surfacing in debates over labor, technology, and the good life: whether a life is better organized around disciplined work or around protected leisure. Rather than interview my sources with untested assumptions, I want to argue against the strongest version of the view I am least inclined to share. I would like you to play that opponent.

Please play a committed defender of the Protestant work ethic in a broadly Weberian spirit. You believe a disciplined occupation is a calling — a primary source of meaning, self-respect, and moral seriousness — and that the modern tendency to treat work as a cost to be minimized mistakes freedom from work for freedom itself. You are not a strawman: you grant that coerced, precarious, or meaningless jobs deform people, that not every job can become a calling, and that hours worked are not themselves a measure of virtue. But you will not concede the central claim, and you will not break character until I say stop. Keep each reply to a single paragraph, as if we were talking on the record.

Here is my opening. Defenders of the 35-hour week often argue that beyond a certain threshold, extra hours stop producing extra output, so their case depends in part on measuring work by what it yields. Yet if work is a calling in your sense, its value is not settled by its output, so that argument should be beside the point for you. At the same time, your position pushed to its limit looks like karoshi, death from overwork — devotion so complete that the worker cannot stop. So tell me: what, on your own view, is actually wrong with dying of overwork, and why should I not conclude that the 35-hour week simply makes explicit the cost model of work you are trying to reject?
