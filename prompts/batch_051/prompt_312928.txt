I spent most of my working life in physics—condensed matter, mostly—and only after I retired did I start reading philosophy seriously, epistemology above all. Lately I've been working through the induction problem, from Hume and Goodman's grue up to Solomonoff induction and Kolmogorov complexity. To someone with my training the rough picture is very natural: treat hypotheses as programs, measure a hypothesis's complexity K(x) by the length of the shortest program that generates it, and give it prior probability on the order of 2^-K(x), so simpler explanations get exponentially more weight. Occam's razor stops being a hunch and becomes a fact about the prior. That part I think I have, at least in outline.

What I keep coming back to is Borges's story The Library of Babel. As I remember it, the universe is a vast honeycomb of galleries, and the books contain, between them, every possible arrangement of the same set of letters—so somewhere there is a true catalog, a perfect explanation of the library, but overwhelmingly what you find is gibberish, and the inhabitants splinter into sects, each with its own procedure for finding the meaningful books. It striks me that the story is about exactly this problem: what do you do in the space of all possible finite strings, if you refuse to weight them by simplicity? I'd like you to read the story through the lens of Solomonoff induction and Kolmogorov complexity for a short essay I'm writing for a reading group. Where does Borges dramatize the failure of a uniform prior, the idea that every book is just as likely as any other to hold the secret? Where would a Solomonoff-style prior actually have helped the librarians, and is that move even available to them from inside the library? And the part I keep getting stuck on: where does the story resist the algorithmic picture? Kolmogorov complexity treats meaning as if it were a property of the string itself, but in the story the same sequence of characters can mean something completely different depending on who finds it and what they need it to say. I dont want the essay to turn into the usual 'Borges was secretly a Bayesian' take, so say plainly where an honest critic should say the analogy breaks down.
