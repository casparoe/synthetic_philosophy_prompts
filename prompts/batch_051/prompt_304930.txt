You are an expert in the philosophy of geology and stratigraphic classification, and you have been asked to referee a technical disagreement between two specialists. Please think carefully: before reaching a verdict, reconstruct the strongest possible version of each side, and then evaluate each numbered move on its own merits rather than judging the dispute as a whole first.

At a recent department lunch, two colleagues of mine—Marta, a stratigrapher, and Daniel, a historian of geology—spent the whole hour arguing over whether the Anthropocene should be recognized as a formal unit of the geological time scale. Neither convinced the other, and when they turned to me I found I could not referee them. Here is the dispute as it unfolded.

(1) Marta began: "There is now a distinctive, globally detectable Anthropocene signal in the accumulating rock record: plutonium-239 from mid-twentieth-century nuclear tests, fly ash and black carbon, plastics, concrete, and a carbon-isotope excursion from fossil-fuel burning. Stratigraphic units are defined by real, preservable, mappable material. So a boundary placed in the early 1950s, anchored in a specific reference section, would be as legitimate as most boundaries already in the time scale."

(2) Daniel replied: "A chronostratigraphic boundary should have two features this one lacks: geological clarity and global synchrony. Human impacts spread gradually and unevenly—regional agriculture across millennia, industrialization in a few countries in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, then genuinely global changes only in the twentieth. The markers are diachronous rather than a single crisp horizon, and choosing 1952 is a narrative or political choice, not the rocks deciding. We should at least wait to see how this episode is preserved."

(3) Marta answered: "No boundary in the time scale is perfectly synchronous or purely geological rather than chosen. The Holocene boundary is an abrupt climate shift roughly 11,700 years ago, but it still has to be pinned to one dated reference point. And the 'not enough time has passed' objection begs the question. The late Holocene already has formal subdivisions only a few thousand years long, so there is no minimum-duration principle at work. A mid-twentieth-century boundary is no more gerrymandered than those—except that we happen to be alive for the vote."

(4) Daniel came back: "But that reveals the deeper issue. The geological time scale is a classification maintained by a professional institution, not a natural-kind structure read directly off the rocks. It works for deep-time units such as the Cambrian or the Pleistocene because those packages serve geological mapping and correlation. The Anthropocene is doing different work—legal, ethical, and historical work about human responsibility. Calling it a geological epoch is an attempt to make the rocks issue a verdict they cannot issue. We can recognize the signal without granting it chronostratigraphic rank."

Please adjudicate the dispute move by move. For each of the four moves, rate it as clearly mistaken, weak but defensible, sound, or clearly right, and briefly justify each rating. Then say what both parties are missing—especially concerning the "who decides" question and whether a unit named for human activity still belongs to natural history. Finally, say which of the two is more right overall, and why.
