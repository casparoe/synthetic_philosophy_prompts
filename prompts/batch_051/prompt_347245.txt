Early in Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind Sellars runs through the things philosophers have said are given: sense contents, material objects, universals, propositions, real connections, first principles, even givenness itself. I want to use that list as a test suit for what an account of observational knowledge is supposed to accomplish once you reject the given. So here is what I would like. Write the desiderata for a theory of perceptual justification that can accept Sellars's negative point without collapsing into mere coherentism. For each desideratum, give the motivating case or consideration and say whether it is non-negotiable or just a preference of one camp. Make sure you cover at least these questions: What distinguishes a sensation from a seeing-that? What must a theory say about the difference between being a reliable differential response and being able to offer a reason? What cases must it get right, such as the chicken sexer who can sort but not explain, the novice looking at a cloud chamber, the child learning color words, the person hallucinating? Which distinctions cannot be collapsed, for example between sentience and sapience, between justification and causation, between the space of reasons and the space of laws? And tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude the usual suspects: classical empiricist foundationalism, direct realism about justifiers, and maybe McDowell-style conceptualism if it ends up re-introducing a given. Do not give me a scorecard for those views past a sentence or two; the criteria are what I want. And please write it as continuous prose, like an email, with no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables.
