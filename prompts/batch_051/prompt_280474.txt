John Stuart Mill's *Considerations on Representative Government* (1861), chapter VII, "Of True and False Democracy; Representation of All, and Representation of the Majority only," opens with the following distinction:

"Two very different ideas are usually confounded under the name democracy. The pure idea of democracy, according to its definition, is the government of the whole people by the whole people, equally represented. Democracy as commonly conceived and hitherto practised is the government of the whole people by a mere majority of the people, exclusively represented. The former is synonymous with the equality of all citizens; the latter, strangely confounded with it, is a government of privilege, in favour of the numerical majority, who alone possess practically any voice in the State."

Take the United Kingdom's 2011 referendum on replacing first-past-the-post with the Alternative Vote as a case study for this distinction. The referendum, agreed as part of the 2010 Conservative–Liberal Democrat coalition agreement, proposed keeping single-member constituencies but having voters rank candidates, with the last-placed candidate eliminated and their votes redistributed until one candidate exceeds 50 per cent of the votes remaining in the count; it was rejected by roughly 68 per cent to 32 per cent. Analyze the episode through the lens of the passage. What does Mill's contrast say about the choice on the ballot, about what supporters and opponents of the change would each have to concede, and about the alternative Mill himself defends in the same chapter—the proportional representation of minorities? Then run the analysis in the other direction: which features of the referendum and of the two voting systems does the passage illuminate, and which does it misdescribe or leave untouched, given that the Alternative Vote is a preferential but still single-member majoritarian system rather than a proportional one? Conclude by saying whether the episode is a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case Mill's framework never anticipated, and what it reveals about the gap between the passage's ideal and the reforms that were realistically available.
