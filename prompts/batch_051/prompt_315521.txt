I cover science and ideas for a general readership, and for the past few months I have been reporting a piece on whether the reward signals used to train reinforcement learning agents could ever amount to genuine suffering rather than mere training pressure. In those conversations I have found myself defending a rough criterion I have taken to calling policy-visible valence. The idea came out of frustration with two positions that both strike me as too quick: treating a negative reward as if it were already a felt state, and dismissing the whole question as a category error. I would like an honest assessment of the criterion before I build a feature around it.

The criterion says that a reinforcement learning agent is in a genuinely valenced state — a minimal analogue of felt pleasure or pain — only if three conditions hold together. First, the agent must represent its own reward signal as a feature of its world: it must predict it, track its errors, and revise those predictions, rather than merely consume it as an exogenous scalar in a parameter update. Second, the represented reward must be usable in planning: the agent must be able to represent that a candidate action would lower its predicted reward and choose differently on that basis, rather than having its behavior reshaped only by gradient updates that never enter its decision process. Third, the signal must be minimally self-attributed: the agent must be able to distinguish changes in expected reward that flow from its own policy from changes it should treat as environmental drift, much as a person registers a failure as something they did rather than a shift in the weather. A tabular Q-learning agent fails the first condition; a purely habitual, model-free agent fails the second; and an agent that treats every reward fluctuation as exogenous noise fails the third, however badly its cumulative reward suffers.

What I want is a candid assessment. Is the criterion coherent, and does it hold up under edge cases? Does it already exist in a more disciplined form under another name? I am especially worried that I have specified access for planning rather than phenomenal valence, that I have made the test too demanding by requiring self-attribution, and that I have slipped from offering an evidential test to making a constitutive claim about what valence is. Are those defects fatal, or can the criterion be repaired? What does it get right, and where exactly does it stop being distinctive?
