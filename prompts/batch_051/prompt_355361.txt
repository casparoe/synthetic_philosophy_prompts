The standard incentive argument for copyright says we give creators a temporary monopoly so they have a reason to create and distribute their work in the first place, becuase copying would otherwise be so cheap they couldn't recoup the costs. Now think about orphan works and abandoned software: a 1994 game whose publisher got bought twice and then dissolved, a photograph whose photographer died without heirs, a technical manual whose author can't be traced, a film whose production company left no records of who owns the negative. In these cases there's no identifiable owner left whose future behavior the monopoly can influence, so the incentive story seems to point at nobody. Does the incentive argument still support keeping those works under exclusive control (or going after abandonware sites and digitizers), or does it just run out and the real dispute become about something else, like property stability or the labor of the original creator? I want to know which premise the orphan-works situation actually puts pressure on, whether there's a repaired version of the argument that still does work here (say, by appealing to the expectations of living creators who care what happens to their work after they're gone, or to the effect on markets for rights clearance), and how far that repaired version reaches before it gives out.
