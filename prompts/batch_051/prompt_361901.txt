I'm taking a philosophy of economics class and we just did Friedman's F-twist, the idea that the realism of a model's assumptions doesn't matter, only its predictive success. I've basicly adopted this as a personal policy: whenever someone criticizes a model for having unrealistic assumptions (rational agents, perfect information, no transaction costs, whatever), I dismiss the criticism unless they can show the model makes bad predictions. I even catch myself doing this for models way outside economics, like in psychology or epidemiology. Is this defensible as a general epistemic rule, or am I using Friedman as a license to ignore relevant information? Is there a version of the rule that keeps the good part without the bad part, and how would I tell from the inside whether I'm applying it well rather than just protecting models I like?
