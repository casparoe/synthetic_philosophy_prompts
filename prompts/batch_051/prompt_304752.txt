I'm sketching an essay on Turing's discussion of extrasensory perception in Computing Machinery and Intelligence, and I want comments on whether my line is defensible or just me being too generous to him.

The draft idea is this: everyone treats the ESP section as a weird footnote or evidence that Turing was gullible, but I want to argue it's actually a useful stress test for his whole test. His imitation game works by restricting the interrogator to observable linguistic behavior. If telepathy were real, the interrogator could be receiving information through a channel that has nothing to do with intelligent behavior, so the game would need a telepathy-proof room to keep the comparison fair. On my reading, Turing isn't endorsing telepathy as part of intelligence or saying it's the next frontier; he's specifying the boundary conditions under which the test remains a test of the imitation rather than a test of hidden information channels.

The awkward bit is that he also says the statistical evidence for at least telepathy is overwhelming. That's empirically almost certainly false, and it's the main reason the passage gets written off. But I want to ask whether the argumentative structure survives even if his assessment of the evidence was wrong. The deeper point would be that any behavioral test of intelligence has to assume a list of channels that don't count, and those assumptions can be revised if empirical anomalies show up. Today the analogues might be sensors, transcripts hidden in training data, or unintentional cues, and the same logic of ruling out confounds applies. So Turing's ESP discussion can be read as a methodological point about fair tests, not a paranormal claim.

My worry is that I might be laundering a false empirical claim into a respectable abstraction. Maybe he was just credulous about ESP and then used the fact that his test would break to argue we need to take ESP seriously, which is a different and worse argument.

Tell me: is this a historically plausible reading, does it confuse intelligence with indistinguishability under test conditions, and is the methodological point actually interesting enough to carry an essay? Also tell me whether the empirical wrongness about ESP should matter for the argument. Start with your strongest criticism or suggestion, then explain, with no preamble and no restating of my notes.
