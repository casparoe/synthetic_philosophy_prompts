I am a novelist drafting a book about a community that quietly depends on the protracted sacrifice of a group it claims to cherish, and about the people who administer that sacrifice while never quite killing anyone. The book keeps forcing me to think through the doing/allowing distinction: the difference between bringing a harm about and merely failing to prevent it, between killing and letting die. I would like to think through it by way of a novel that already lives inside this problem, Kazuo Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go.

Here is the philosophical tension I am trying to handle. One tradition treats the killing/letting die line as morally important: actively causing death is categorically forbidden in a way that failing to save is not, in part because the harm would have happened anyway. A rival view treats the line as often morally inert: a person who drowns a cousin for an inheritance and a person who stands by while the cousin drowns for the same inheritance differ in method but not obviously in culpability. A third thought, the one that haunts me as a novelist, is that the distinction matters less as a rule for assigning fault to individuals and more as a social arrangement: a society can organize itself so that almost nobody kills and almost everybody lets die, and the moral character of that society is the real object of judgment even though no single person's hands are red.

Read Never Let Me Go through that lens. Who in the novel kills, and who lets die? I am thinking of the surgeons, the guardians, the donors themselves, and the wider world that benefits. Does the novel treat the guardians' kindness and decency as a genuine moral difference or as a refinement of the mechanism? Does Ishiguro's choice of Kathy H., a carer who neither runs the system nor fights it, as the narrator amount to a position on the doing/allowing distinction, and what is it? As a novelist, I especially want to know which narrative devices carry the book's philosophical weight: point of view, the withholding of direct confrontation, the gap between what is said and what is done. I also want to know whether the distinction ultimately clarifies the novel, or the novel clarifies the distinction, or both.

Please structure your response in these sections, in this order:

1. The distinction, stated for fiction (roughly 150 words): state the doing/allowing and killing/letting die contrast in the terms a novelist would need, including what the line is supposed to mark and why one might think it either decisive or beside the point.

2. The reading (roughly 600 words): map the novel onto the distinction. Identify which acts and omissions in the world of the novel count as doing and which as allowing, and argue for the reading the text most strongly supports.

3. Where the novel resists (roughly 300 words): identify the moments, characters, or narrative structures that pull against that reading, and say honestly where the distinction cannot accommodate the novel.

4. What the novel's craft adds (roughly 250 words): explain what the novel shows about the doing/allowing distinction that a bare argument, or a pair of invented cases, does not.

5. What I should take from it as a novelist (roughly 150 words): draw out the concrete lesson for my own book, without reducing the work to a moral.

Please write in American English and assume I know the novel; do not summarize the plot except where the argument requires a detail.
