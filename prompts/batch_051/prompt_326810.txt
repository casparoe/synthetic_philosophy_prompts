I'm in Philosophy of Language and we just did slurs. A friend summarized the lecture for me like this:

"Slurs have the same descriptive or truth-conditional content as their neutral counterparts — a racial slur for a group just means 'member of that group,' for instance. What the slur adds is a derogatory attitude (something like contempt), and that attitude is conventionally attached to the word itself. That's why you can't cancel the offensiveness by using a friendly tone or by reporting someone else's use: the contempt is part of the word's literal meaning. The live debate is between semanticists, who say the derogatory attitude contributes to truth conditions (so a sentence like 'Sam is [slur]' is true only if Sam is contemptible because of his race), and pragmatists, who say the attitude is more like an implicature or a matter of speaker intent, not part of what's literally said. Since the offensiveness obviously can't be canceled, the semantic side is almost certainly right."

I get the gist but that last step feels too fast to me, and I can't tell if he's drawing the semanticist/pragmatist split the way it's actually drawn in the literature. Could you go through this piece by piece and tell me what a careful philosopher of language would accept, what they'd push back on, and whether the non-cancellability point really settles the debate?
