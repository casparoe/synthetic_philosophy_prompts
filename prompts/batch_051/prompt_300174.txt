I have been reading about Frank Jackson's Mary's room, and I want to work through a variant of the case. In the original, Mary has spent her entire life in a black-and-white room but has somehow learned all the physical facts about color vision; when she leaves the room and sees a ripe tomato, she seems to learn something new — what it is like to see red — and Jackson takes this to show that not all facts are physical facts.

Here is the variant. The setup is exactly as before, except that the first chromatic experience Mary receives after leaving the room is not an ordinary color. Instead, she is connected to a direct neural interface that produces a forbidden color: a genuinely reddish-green, a single hue that is experienced as red and green at the same time, not a mixture that looks like an ordinary third color. Mary had complete physical knowledge of the interface, of opponent-process color coding, and of the exact neural state the interface would produce, and she knew in advance that she would see reddish-green. When the interface is switched on, she is startled and reports that she has learned something — what reddish-green is like.

Work through what this variant does to the knowledge argument. First, does Mary learn anything in this version, and if she does, is what she gains better described as a new fact, a new ability, a new acquaintance with an old fact, or a new phenomenal concept? Second, take three standard replies to the original case — the ability hypothesis, the phenomenal concept strategy, and the old-fact view — and say for each whether replacing ordinary red with a forbidden color makes the reply stronger, weaker, or about equally plausible, and why. Third, consider this objection: since opponent-process theory says a true reddish-green should be impossible under normal viewing, the physicalist might claim that no amount of physical knowledge could have let Mary predict or imagine the experience in advance. Does this concession help the physicalist, or does it make physicalism's situation worse? Conclude by saying whether the forbidden-color Mary case strengthens, weakens, or leaves unchanged the original conclusion that Mary gains non-physical knowledge when she first sees color.
