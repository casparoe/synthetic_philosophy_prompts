In my decision theory seminar we discussed the absent-minded driver problem, and two of my classmates ended up in a dispute about it that they never resolved. I can reconstruct their exchange, but I have not settled on a view and would like it adjudicated move by move before I commit.

The scenario we used is this. A driver is on a highway with two identical exits, one after the other. He is absent-minded: whenever he reaches an exit, he cannot tell whether it is the first or the second. He remembers the payoffs and whatever policy he has adopted, but he has no memory of how many exits he has passed and no cue, external or internal, that would let him distinguish the two exits. At each exit he must either exit or continue. If he exits at the first exit, he receives 0. If he continues past the first and exits at the second, he receives 4. If he continues past both, he receives 1. Because the exits are indistinguishable, any policy must treat the two exits in the same way, but the driver may randomize. Let p be the probability with which he exits at each exit.

Their exchange went roughly as follows.

Priya: "Before the trip, the only thing the driver can choose is a rule, and since the exits are indistinguishable the rule must use the same p at each exit. The expected payoff of such a rule is (1 − p)(1 + 3p), which is maximized at p = 1/3, with a value of 4/3. That settles the problem: the driver should adopt that rule and not re-open the question at the exit. Any reconsideration is just an occasion for the driver to talk himself into a worse plan than the one he optimized ex ante."

Jonah: "You are optimizing only from the start. Decision theory applies at the moment of choice as well. At an exit, the driver learns something: he is at an exit. If his rule is p, then, conditional on that information, the probability that this exit is the first is 1/(2 − p), and the probability that it is the second is (1 − p)/(2 − p). Exiting now is therefore worth 4(1 − p)/(2 − p), while continuing now—assuming he follows the rule p at any later exit—is worth 2(1 + p)/(2 − p). These are equal exactly when p = 1/3. So the intra-trip calculation reproduces your answer rather than undermining it. Reconsideration is not a temptation away from the plan; it is what justifies the plan from the driver's own standpoint."

Priya: "That argument makes things worse for you, not better. At p = 1/3 the driver is exactly indifferent. Expected-utility theory gives no instruction to an indifferent agent; any way of breaking the tie is as permitted as any other. So your calculation does not tell the driver to exit with probability 1/3 rather than some other probability. And if he breaks the tie by exiting a little more often, the posterior you used depends on p and the apparent equality collapses. Ex ante maximization gave a determinate rule; your moment-of-choice approach gives only a knife-edge of indifference. Without some commitment device, the target policy is not something the driver is bound to carry out."

Jonah: "The indifference is not a knife-edge in that sense. If p is slightly above 1/3, continuing has higher expected value than exiting; if p is slightly below 1/3, exiting has higher expected value than continuing. The direction of the rational response pushes any deviation back toward p = 1/3. That is exactly what makes the mixed policy self-correcting from the driver's own point of view, rather than imposed from outside. Ex ante optimization and action-by-action optimization converge on p = 1/3; they do not collapse into whim."

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each of the four moves, say where it falls on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and justify the rating briefly. Then say who has the better overall position, and what, if anything, both are missing. Finally, tell me what would change your adjudication: which argument or consideration, if its merits turned out to be other than you currently take them to be, would lead you to reverse the overall verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail—for instance, whether your treatment of the tie-breaking objection depends on a contested reading of what it is to follow a policy under imperfect recall.
