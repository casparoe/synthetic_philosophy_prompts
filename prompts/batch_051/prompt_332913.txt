I teach an upper-division seminar in philosophy of physics to about eighteen undergraduates, most of whom have never taken a formal quantum mechanics course; sessions run seventy-five minutes. When we reach Bell's theorem, I want the students to collide with the problem themselves before I attach any labels to it. Please design a classroom game around the following correlation task, which I understand to be a version of the CHSH game. Students work in pairs, with a third student acting as referee. The referee privately hands each player one card, red or blue, and each player must answer yes or no without seeing the other's card. A round is won exactly when both answers match if at least one card is red, and the answers differ if both cards are blue. Partners may coordinate as much as they like before the scoring rounds, including any pre-agreed instruction set or any use of shared coins, but they may not communicate once the cards are dealt. What I want the activity to surface is that no pre-agreed strategy of this sort can win more than three rounds in four, whereas quantum mechanics allows correlations between suitably prepared entangled particles to win at a rate above that ceiling — roughly 85 percent in the standard analysis. I hope that discovery can do the pedagogical work a lecture on hidden variables usually does poorly.

Please specify the activity end to end: the materials, a scoring scheme that does not require real money, the number and timing of rounds, and how to sequence it so students converge on the 75 percent ceiling themselves before any theory is introduced. Include both a fifty-minute and a seventy-five-minute version. Then add a second phase in which I demonstrate — with a printed table of precomputed outcomes from an entangled-pair simulation plus the measurement settings that generate them — that a strategy apparently satisfying the same no-communication constraint beats their best score. The debrief should map each element of the game onto the physics: pre-agreed strategies onto local hidden variables, the 75 percent wall onto a Bell inequality, the demonstration onto quantum correlations, and the referee's card draws onto the assumption that measurement choices are free and independent. Be explicit about which feature a classroom cannot literally reproduce and what I must supply by stipulation, and note two or three predictable failure modes — at minimum, a pair sneaking in a signaling scheme, and the misconception that the moral is that quantum mechanics permits faster-than-light messages from Alice to Bob. End with one discussion question that pushes students to trade off locality, realism, and measurement independence rather than settling for "quantum mechanics is just weird." Please write the activity in American English.
