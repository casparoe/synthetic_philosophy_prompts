I've been going back and forth with a friend about Pascal's mugging for a couple weeks now, and I've started writing down the standard ways people try to wriggle out of it. The setup, for anyone who hasnt internalized it: a stranger asks for your wallet and in exchange promises, on no evidence, to produce an astronomically large reward later — large enough that any nonzero probability you assign to his being sincere, no matter how tiny, would make handing over the wallet the expected-utility-maximizing act. The replies I already have in my notes are these: (1) utility has to be bounded, since a finite mind has some ceiling on how much good it can actually contain, so beyond a point bigger promised rewards shouldn't move you; (2) the probability of his story should drop at least as fast as the payoff grows, for instance because a prior that penalizes complicated, precisely-specified claims assigns an absolutely tiny probability to the exact story he told, so the product never gets large; (3) choose the policy, not the act — an agent with a known disposition to pay off strangers making wild promises will get mugged over and over, and the expected cost of that policy across a lifetime swamps the one shot at the jackpot; (4) the speech itself is no real evidence, since you could have dreamed up the same claim on your own, so being told it shouldn't raise your conditional probability of the payoff by enough to matter; and (5) there should be some lower bound on the probabilities you're willing to act on, because once you let astronomically tiny probabilities into the calculation, expected utility starts asking for a kind of precision your probability judgments can't deliver.

I'm fairly sure those are the standard moves, so I'm not asking you to rephrase them or pick among them. What I want is one more response to the mugging that is genuinely different from all five — not a restatement, a special case, or a combination. It's fine if it attacks a premise of the setup rather than solving it from the inside, as long as it's new. Then tell me honestly whether the new response works, or whether all it really does is force you to say which premise you were always going to abandon.
