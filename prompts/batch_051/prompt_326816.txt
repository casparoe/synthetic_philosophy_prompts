I keep coming back to the raven paradox because it feels like the sort of argument that should of been settled as soon as Bayesians got their hands on it, but somehow it hasn't. The setup: 'all ravens are black' is logically equivalent to 'all non-black things are non-ravens,' so a green apple is a positive instance of the second generalization, and if you accept the usual confirmation rules — a generalization is confirmed by its positive instances, and whatever confirms a sentence confirms anything logically equivalent to it — you seem forced to say the apple confirms the first one. Hempel's move was to bite the bullet and say fine, it confirms, just by a negligible amount; the standard Bayesian gloss says the posterior on 'all ravens are black' moves a tiny bit in the right direction. But people keep arguing about whether the apple is really evidence, so I get the sense the 'solution' didn't dissolve whatever was actually bothering them. What I want is a diagnosis of the leftover disagreement, not another attempt to solve the paradox. Is it a substantive dispute about how confirmation works, a verbal fight over the word 'evidence' (probability-raising vs. decision-relevant vs. explanation-improving), or two different projects under one heading — one about the formal logic of induction, one about the psychology of when an observation feels relevant? Is one side treating the intuition that a green apple can't tell you about ravens as a datum to be honored, while the other side treats it as a bias to be explained away? And what would actually count as progress here, if anything, short of everyone agreeing to mutter 'confirms, but only by an epsilon' and walking away?
