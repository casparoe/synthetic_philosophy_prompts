I'm a PhD student working on animal minds and decision theory, and I've been trying to get clear on what's actually doing the work in what people call the scale argument for taking insect sentience seriously. I keep seeing it gestured at in talks and blog posts — the sheer number of insects as if that settles things — but rarely laid out as an actual inference, so I tried to reconstruct it myself. Here's what I've got:

(1) There are something like 10^18 insects alive at any one time. (The exact figure doesn't matter much; pick whatever estimate you like, it's vast.)
(2) Even if you assign a very low probability to each insect being sentient — let's say 0.1% — the expected number of sentient insects alive right now is still around 10^15, since 10^18 × 0.001 = 10^15.
(3) If those insects are sentient, a decent fraction of their lives plausibly involve pain, e.g. from predation, parasitism, starvation, disease, or pesticides.
(4) So the expected amount of insect suffering in the world right now is staggeringly large: a small probability of a vast amount of suffering is still a very large expected quantity of suffering.
(5) When you're deciding under uncertainty — including uncertainty about sentience — and one option has massively greater expected disvalue than another, you ought, at least pro tanto, to prioritise the first, even if the probabilities are rough and not fully grounded.
(6) Most ordinary altruistic causes, and especially interventions aimed at fairly small numbers of humans or animals, are very unlikely to have expected welfare effects within orders of magnitude of 10^15 sentient lives' worth.
(7) So, if you've accepted the steps so far, a surprisingly large share of altruistic resources should go to insect welfare work, or at least to figuring out whether insects are sentient.

My problem is I can't tell where a reasonable person is supposed to stop. The arithmetic in (2) looks fine, and (3) seems empirically plausible, so I keep landing on the conclusion even though it feels like I've been mugged by an expected-value arguemnt. Is this valid? Is it sound? If not, tell me exactly which premise fails, and under what reading. Maybe (5) is doing the dodgy work, but I also can't see how to run decisions under uncertainty without something like it.
