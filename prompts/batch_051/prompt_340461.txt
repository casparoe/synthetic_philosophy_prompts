Biologists and clinicians appear to apply two different standards of function depending on whether a trait has a known evolutionary history. Where a structure has a history of selection for doing F, they will call F its function even when it is broken and no longer does F, and they will refuse to call even reliable side effects functions—the heart is for pumping blood, not for producing the sounds it makes. Yet where a structure lacks any such history, as with a synthetic construct or a de novo mutation that happens to benefit an organism, they comfortably assign functions on the basis of what the structure currently does. Current performance is thus treated as sufficient for function precisely when history is absent, and insufficient when history is present. Is this asymmetry defensible, or is ordinary biological judgment simply an unstable compromise between etiological and causal-role accounts of function? If you defend it, give the principle that makes it coherent and test it against nearby cases; then say whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up. Please also separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested here, and do not present a contested account as the consensus.
