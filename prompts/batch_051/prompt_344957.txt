You are a philosopher of mind who specializes in personal identity and the metaphysics of selfhood. Think through the problem carefully; start by brainstorming the strongest formulations of each position before you evaluate them.

I find myself drawn to a deflationary view of the self: the self is not a substance, nor a persisting thing located somewhere in the mind or brain, but an abstraction — a center of narrative gravity, rather like the way an object's center of gravity is a real explanatory posit but not a material part of the object. The view attracts me because it dissolves otherwise intractable questions about what the self is made of, where it is, and how it survives change. On this picture, the self is something we generate by telling and retelling a story of a life, not something we discover by introspection.

But I also have a firm intuition that this cannot be the whole truth. My experiences do not merely occur; they are had. There is a first-person subject who undergoes them, deliberates, and acts, and it seems to me plainly false — not merely revisionary — that this subject is a useful fiction. I can accept that much of my self-conception is confabulated and narratively constructed, but the one who is doing the confabulating does not feel like another construction.
