Last night two people in my old philosophy reading group, Jon and Lena, got stuck on whether the bare fact that I exist should push me toward cosmological theories on which there are many more observers. What started as a general question ended up as an argument about the difference between the self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption (at least, as I understand those labels), and I'm the one who has to referee, and I honestly cannot tell who is getting the better of it. Jon began with two theories, A and B, that make all the same predictions about everything anyone has ever measured, except A says the universe will eventually contain about a trillion times as many observers as B, mostly far away or far in the future so nobody has detected them yet. Jon said A is overwhelmingly better supported: if A is true, there are a trillion times as many observer-slots that could have turned out to be me, so my existence is more to be expected under A than under B. Lena shot back that this is basically the presumptuous-philosopher move: you could take any theory and make it a trillion times more probable just by tacking on the claim that there are a trillion times more unobservable observers, and that cannot be right, or at least not without an argument that extra observationally idle observers should count.

Jon then tried an urn case. God flips a fair coin; heads means he creates one person, tails means he creates a hundred. You wake up as one of them, with no clue which. Jon said tails is the obvious bet: a hundred people exist on tails and only one on heads, so your existence is a hundred times more likely on tails. Lena said no, the probability goes the other way: on heads you had to be this one person, whereas on tails you could of been any of a hundred, so heads is a hundred times more likely, not tails. Jon replied that Lena is quietly helping herself to a reference class: she has to decide which observer-slots count as possible versions of me, and any choice there is driving her answer. He also said her view seems to reward hypotheses with fewer observers, which is just as unearned as his rewarding hypotheses with more. Lena said it is not a matter of rewarding small universes; it is a matter of refusing to reward large universes when the extra observers do not change the probability of any actual observation. Jon ended up saying the total number of observers is just an unmeasured fact about the world, like an unmeasured parameter, so it is fine for it to get a probabilistic boost. Lena answered that if that were true, a theory with a trillion-trillion observers in causally closed other universes would dominate every ordinary scientific theory no matter what anyone ever observed, which she called a reductio. Then it dissolved into them re-litigating the urn case.

Could you go through the exchange move by move, rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and say who is right about what, and what both of them are missing? Some of it seems like mathematical fact, some of it seems like a disputed judgment call, and I can't tell which is which. So it would help if you could flag which parts are actually settled and which are still genuinely contested among people who think about this stuff, rather than presenting one side's contested reading as the consensus.
