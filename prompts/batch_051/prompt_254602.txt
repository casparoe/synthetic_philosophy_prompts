I'm a novelist, and my next book's protagonist is an astrophysicist who comes to believe the galaxy is a dark forest and has to defend that to a roomful of skeptics. So far I've got him four arguments: (1) the chain-of-suspicion point — no amount of friendly messaging can establish trust, because a signal is cheap, intentions can change on interstellar timescales, and you can never rule out that the other side is tricking you; (2) the technological-explosion point — because technology can jump unpredictably fast, a civilization you notice now might outstrip you before you can finish even one exchange of messages, so the only safe response to spotting someone is to hit them first; (3) the selection point — if civilizations that broadcast get destroyed, then the ones left to be observed are exactly the ones that stay silent, so a quiet universe is just what the dark forest predicts; (4) the convergence point — survival-first plus finite resources pushes every long-lived civilization toward the same hide-and-shoot logic, so its a stable equilibrium, not a local pathology. Is there a fifth argument for the dark forest I'm missing? It has to be genuinely distinct from these four, not a restatement or a special case or a combination of them, and ideally something that would work as dialogue in the novel rather than as a journal article. If you can, tell me whether the new argument is actually any good or just sounds clever.
