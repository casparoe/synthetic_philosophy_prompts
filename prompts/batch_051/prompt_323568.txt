A couple of mates of mine got into an argument at the pub recently about Kavka's toxin puzzle, and I'd like you to referee it. Quick setup, in case you've not come across it: a billionaire offers you a million pounds tomorrow morning if, at midnight tonight, you genuinely intend to drink a toxin tomorrow afternoon. The toxin makes you ill for a day but has no lasting effects. The money is paid before you would have to drink, and no one checks whether you actually drink it; all that matters is your state of mind at midnight.

Jo said the puzzle has a boring answer: you can't intend to drink the toxin, because you know for certain that once the money's in your account you'll have no reason to drink and every reason not to. Intending isn't merely saying 'I intend to'; it involves some commitment, and you can't be committed to an act you know you won't perform.

Ben replied that people form intentions they later abandon all the time. Someone intends to quit smoking tomorrow even if they suspect they'll fail; someone intends to run a marathon and then doesn't. Weakness of will doesn't show the earlier intention wasn't genuine, so why is this case different?

Jo said the difference is certainty. With quitting smoking you don't know in advance that you'll fail; you might succeed. Here you know with absolute certainty that at drinking time the money is already yours and drinking is pure cost, so you will not drink. An intention you know for certain will be abandoned isn't an intention at all; it's a performance.

Ben came back: then the only route to the money is to alter your future self's reasons. You could hire someone to force the drink down at noon, or sign a contract that forfeits more money if you don't drink, or take a pill that makes you forget the money is already secured. In each of those cases you could genuinely intend at midnight. Doesn't that show the puzzle isn't about intending but about how much of your future agency you're willing to modify?

Jo said those moves change the game by removing the option not to drink, or by changing what you believe; in the original puzzle the choice at noon is still open. If the only way to intend is to rig your future self, that just confirms you can't intend in the original case. At that point Ben said the puzzle makes rationality look like a disease: the clear-eyed, self-knowing agent gets nothing, while the self-deceiving or self-binding agent gets a million. If a theory of intention or rationality implies that the person who best understands herself is the only one who doesn't get paid, that's an embarrassment for the theory.
