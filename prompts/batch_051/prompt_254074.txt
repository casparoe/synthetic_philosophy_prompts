I have been helping run a small tenants' union for the past two years, and I have begun using a term that I want checked before it hardens into a habit. I call it "inquiry debt." I mean by this the distance between a decision a group actually makes and the inquiry that, by the group's own lights, would have been needed to make that decision well. The idea comes out of Dewey's experimentalism about democracy and inquiry: democratic publics are supposed to function as communities that frame shared problems, test proposed solutions in experience, and revise in light of observed consequences. A vote or a policy that is settled before the relevant experimental work has been done—before a pilot, before the affected public has even been identified, before anyone has specified what observable outcomes would count as failure—leaves the group with a standing liability, and the interest on that liability is paid later as crisis, distrust, and reversal. I borrow the metaphor from software engineering, where "technical debt" names the future cost of shipping code before it has been made maintainable; inquiry debt would name the future cost of settling a course of action before it has been made into a warranted judgment. A concrete case: our city adopted a new rent-stabilization enforcement rule on the strength of one consultant's model and an hour of public comment, and nobody in the room could have said what evidence would show the rule was backfiring. I want to call that high inquiry debt rather than merely a bad decision.

My questions are these. Is "inquiry debt" a real concept, or just a vivid restatement of decision-making under uncertainty or of the familiar idea of a deliberative deficit? Has something like it already been worked out under another name, in Dewey himself or in the literature on democratic deliberation? And where exactly does it break? My main worry is that Dewey treats inquiry as continuous and its conclusions as always provisional, so perhaps every democratic decision carries some inquiry debt, and the concept either collapses into triviality or requires thresholds that I do not know how to justify. I would like the three or four strongest objections to the concept, and a candid judgment on whether it is worth keeping rather than quietly abandoning.
