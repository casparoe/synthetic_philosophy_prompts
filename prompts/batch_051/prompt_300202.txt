I've been turning over a puzzle about how to revise your beliefs when the only new information you receive is conditional in form, and I can't decide which step of the reasoning to abandon.

Setup. A soldier is dropped at night onto a region divided into four quadrants of equal area: Red Headquarters, Red Second Company, Blue Headquarters, and Blue Second Company. Red territory is the two Red quadrants taken together, and Blue territory the two Blue ones. She has no further information, so her prior is uniform: each quadrant has probability 1/4. Then she receives exactly one radio message: "We don't know where you are. If you are in Red territory, the odds are 3 to 1 that you are at a Headquarters post rather than a Second Company post."

Two chains of reasoning then pull in opposite directions.

Chain A — minimal revision.

(1) When new evidence arrives, you should adopt the probability assignment that respects the evidence while staying as close as possible to your old assignment; the standard way to make "as close as possible" precise is relative entropy.

(2) The unique assignment satisfying the message's constraint, P(HQ | Red) = 3/4, while minimizing relative entropy from the uniform prior, is proportional to (3^(1/4), 3^(-3/4), 1, 1) in the order listed — approximately (0.3505, 0.1168, 0.2663, 0.2663).

(3) In that posterior, P(Red) ≈ 0.467, down from the prior value of 0.500.

(4) So if the minimal-revision rule is correct, a message that is purely conditional on being in Red territory should make you less confident that you are in Red territory.

Chain B — the antecedent is irrelevant.

(5) The content of the radio message is entirely conditional: it says how likely Headquarters is relative to Second Company on the supposition that she is in Red, and it says nothing about whether she is in Red.

(6) If evidence is silent about a hypothesis, rationally updating on that evidence should leave that hypothesis's probability unchanged.

(7) Therefore P(Red) after the message should remain exactly 0.500, with Headquarters and Second Company within Red in the ratio 3:1 — that is, the posterior (3/8, 1/8, 1/4, 1/4).

Steps (4) and (7) conflict, and this is not an arithmetic mistake: the posterior in (7) really does satisfy the constraint, but by the same relative-entropy measure it is slightly farther from the uniform prior than the posterior in (2) — relative entropy about 0.0654 versus about 0.0633. So the disagreement is a collision of principles, not a calculation error.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If I give up (1), what general rule is supposed to select (7)'s posterior, rather than some other distribution that also keeps P(Red) fixed, and why is "stay close to the prior" no longer a requirement? If I give up (6), I seem to accept that purely conditional information can shift the probability of the condition itself, which feels like losing the distinction between evidence about "if" and evidence about "whether." I would most like to keep both (1) and (6), but I cannot see how to do so.
