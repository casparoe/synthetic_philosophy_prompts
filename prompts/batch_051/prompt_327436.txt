I'm a software engineer without formal philosophy training, but my job involves probabilistic forecasting: I estimate the likelihood that a release ships on time, that a latency spike has a particular root cause, that a feature change moves a metric. A colleague with an economics background frequently tells me that many of my stated degrees of belief are, in his words, incoherent—that taken together they cannot be represented by any single probability function, and that this leaves me vulnerable to a Dutch book, meaning a finite set of bets, each of which looks fair at my own stated odds, that would guarantee a loss no matter what happens.

The habit I've adopted is this: I do not treat an abstract appeal to coherence as a reason to revisit my estimates unless someone is willing to actually produce the Dutch book. If you cannot show me the specific combination of bets at my own odds and the resulting sure loss, I treat the incoherence as a bookkeeping curiosity and deprioritize it. I understand that there are theorems connecting incoherence to the existence of such a book, but I think of it like an exploit in a system I maintain: a vulnerability that no one has actually demonstrated against my live environment ranks below the concrete problems in front of me. For example, after an incident I once put 60% on the database being at fault and later said 50% on the fault being database or network; my colleague called this incoherent, but since he did not show me how to drain my wallet over it, I left my estimates alone. Is my rule rationally defensible, or is it just a way of shielding sloppy numbers? And if it is wrong as stated, is there an amended version that keeps what is useful in it?
