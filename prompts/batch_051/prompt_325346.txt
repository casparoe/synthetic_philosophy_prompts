I've been trying to pin down something about how historians and archaeologists draw the line with pseudohistory, and I'm fairly sure there's an implicit epistemology doing a lot of work that nobody states out loud. The same profession that treats Troy as a real archaeological site — and treats the search for a historical city behind an old legend as normal, respectable work — mostly refuses to treat Atlantis, ancient aliens, or Fomenko's new chronology (the claim that the standard ancient-and-medieval timeline is largely a later fabrication full of duplicated events) as rival hypotheses at all. When those claims get attention, it's as objects of study, for explaining why people believe them, not as candidates for excavation or chronology-building. And it's not simply that the evidence is worse: the treatment is categorically differnt. A boring new Bronze Age settlement gets ordinary evidential standards and a fair shot; a claim to have found Atlantis would have to clear wildly higher ones, and most archaeologists would still not feel any obligation to refute it. What theory of historical evidence — or demarcation between real and pseudohistory — does that professional practice presuppose? I want a committed answer rather than a menu. Reconstruct the implicit criterion that is actually doing the work, say which parts of the practice genuinely presuppose it and which parts would be compatible with several different epistemologies, and tell me what would have to change in how the profession handles fringe claims if the underlying view were swapped for a straightforward probabilistic one where every hypothesis just has a prior and gets updated on evidence. In particular, I want to know whether the practice needs something like an unthinkability norm, or whether it's all just an extremely strong prior.
