I run root-cause investigations at a company that builds automated lab instruments. Every time a machine gives a wrong result or damages a sample—roughly once a week—my team writes up a cause and a corrective action, and the write-up goes to customers and sometimes regulators. Right now our method is basically "find the broken part or the person who messed up," and I want to replace it with a reusable checklist built from Aristotle's four causes: material, formal, efficient, final. Translate those into plain questions an engineer with no philosophy background can actually ask, and put them in the order we should ask them. For each, say what counts as a good-enough answer before we move on, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to escalate to a human because the question is a value judgment rather than a fact. Be especially honest about the final cause: where a "what is this machine supposed to be for?" question makes an investigation better, and where is just turns into storytelling or blame. Then run the procedure on two cases: (1) a pipetting robot that started dropping plates after a firmware update changed its acceleration profile, and (2) a blood analyzer that reported normal potassium results on grossly hemolyzed samples because we'd marketed it as not needing sample inspection. I'd like at least one case where the procedure gives an answer my leadership won't like, and at the end say plainly what this procedure cannot catch.
