In engineering circles I keep hearing a claim that a proper name works the way a pointer does. On this view, a name is bound to its bearer at an original act of naming—the analogy's version of assignment—and from then on it simply points at that bearer. Later speakers acquire the name the way one function receives a reference from another, and the descriptive beliefs they attach to the bearer are like the mutable fields of the object at the other end: they can change or be entirely false without disturbing the pointer. This is used to argue that a name's reference is not fixed by the descriptions speakers associate with it but by a historical chain of transmission, and that someone could successfully refer to Aristotle even if every description the person believed about Aristotle were false.

I want you to audit this analogy. Give the full correspondence table first: what plays the role of the reference, the object in memory, assignment, parameter passing, fields on the object, string equality versus reference equality, null, and a dangling reference? Then go through the points where the mapping breaks. Which of those disanalogies matter for the intended conclusion—that reference comes apart from description—and which are harmless? In particular, what does the programming picture quietly import that natural-language naming does not have: a well-defined allocation event, a runtime that resolves every reference, uniqueness, reference integrity, an object that persists however anyone thinks of it?

Then stress-test the analogy against naming conventions it was not built for. A given name looks like a one-time assignment at a ceremony; a family name is inherited along a line and seems to group people into a lineage rather than designate a single individual; the same given name is reused across millions of people; some names never had a bearer at all; and some names have been passed on to a new bearer through a chain of mistaken beliefs about the old one. How does the pointer model handle each, and does it stretch into something that no longer supports the conclusion it was meant to carry?

Finally, give me your verdict: how much of the claim that reference runs through a historical chain rather than through descriptions survives the audit, and what would be a better analogy if any. Say explicitly what consideration would change your verdict, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.

Format: lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble and no restating my question.
