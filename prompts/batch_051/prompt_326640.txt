I spent my working life as a chemist, and since retiring I've been reading around in epistemology. Theres a pattern I keep noticing that I cant find a clean name for, and I'd be grateful if you'd help me carve it up rather than just hand me a label.

Case one. A fair lottery sells a million tickets and exactly one will be drawn. For each particular ticket, the rational thing is to be very confident it will lose, and in fact to accept that it will lose. And yet if I ask whether all the tickets will lose, the rational answer is no: one of them has to win. So I seem to end up accepting, for each ticket, that it will lose, while also accepting that at least one of those accepted claims is wrong.

Case two. I write a long book in which I make eight hundred factual claims. I check each one carefully, and for each one I would happily vouch for it. But I also know from long experience that any manuscript that long contains at least one mistake, so I vouch for that as well: at least one of the eight hundred claims is false, though I have no idea which.

The two cases feel as if they share a single shape: high justification for each member of a large set, plus high justification for the claim that at least one member fails. But they also feel different, and I can't quite say how. In the lottery case the second belief is forced by statistics and by the set-up of the game; in the book case it is a kind of induction from my own track record as a fallible instrument.

What I want to know is whether this general configuration has an established name, whether the two cases are treated as the same problem or as two distinct problems that merely rhyme, and where exactly the line between them runs. I'd also like to know whether the standard ways of responding to one case carry over cleanly to the other, or whether they change their meaning when moved across. I'm not after a bare label here; I want the carving.

Oh, and could you answer in British English, please.
