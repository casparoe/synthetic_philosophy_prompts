I'm teaching a small upper-level seminar on self-sufficiency in the spring, and I've relaized I don't have a clear worked-out sense of what I'd even count as a good theory of it. I don't mean self-sufficiency in the narrow pantry-and-generator sense; I mean autarkeia as an ideal — the thought that a good life, and maybe a good polity, ought to be in some important way complete and standing on its own, not at the mercy of fortune or of other people's choices. The label gets pulled in a lot of directions: the old eudaimonist idea that happiness must be self-sufficient, Stoic independence from externals, economic autarky for nations, contemporary off-grid and self-reliance movements, and the feminist/disability critique that the ideal is covertly ableist and that no one is actually self-sufficient.

Before I assign anything or commit to my own line, I'd like you to write me the desiderata. What should any adequate theory of self-sufficiency as an ideal — for persons, for nations, or both — have to deliver? I want to know which cases it has to get right, which distinctions it has to respect (self-sufficiency vs. isolation, freedom from need vs. freedom from dependence, sufficiency for what, etc.), which objections it has to absorb, and which demands people typically make of it are really just one camp's prejudice and should be dropped. Flag any pair of desiderata that you suspect can't be jointly satisfied — say, whether a theory can value self-sufficiency without thereby denigrating dependence and care. And tell me whether you think the person-level ideal and the nation-level ideal are really one thing or two, since a lot of work seems to just assume the analogy. Then, briefly, sketch how two or three broad approaches (a virtue-ethical one, a political/economic autarky one, a relational or care-ethics critique) would score against your list — but keep the weight on the criteria and their motivation, not on the scoring.
