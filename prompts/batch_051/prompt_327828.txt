Set up two baseline games, then consider a modification.

In the Platonia dilemma introduced by Douglas Hofstadter, twenty people are invited, with no possibility of cooperating, to decide individually whether to send a telegram. If exactly one telegram arrives by the stated deadline, its sender receives a billion dollars; if none arrives or more than one arrives, no one receives anything. The superrational prescription for the symmetric case is for each person to send the telegram with probability one in twenty.

In the Luring Lottery, a contest actually run in the pages of Scientific American in the 1980s, any reader could enter by sending a postcard marked with a positive integer, which counted as that number of entries. The prize was one million dollars divided by the total number of entries received, awarded to the submitter of an entry drawn uniformly at random. Sending more entries raises one's chance of winning but dilutes the per-entry value of the prize. Against a fixed total number of entries submitted by everyone else, the expected-money-maximizing response is to submit exactly that same number; the superrational prescription for a symmetric readership was to roll an imaginary die with one side for each expected entrant and submit one entry only on a roll of one.

Now modify the Luring Lottery as follows. There are exactly twenty contestants, all risk-neutral expected-money maximizers, and this is common knowledge; once the rules are announced, they cannot communicate or make agreements. Each contestant secretly chooses a nonnegative integer number of entries. A winner is drawn uniformly at random from among all entries. Unlike the original contest, the winner receives the full one million dollars, with no dilution by the total entry count — but only if the total number of entries submitted is at most some fixed, commonly known cap K. If the total exceeds K, the prize is voided and nobody receives anything; if the total is zero, there is no draw and nobody receives anything. Take K to be 60.

Analyze the modified game. (i) For a contestant who knows the total number of entries submitted by the other nineteen, derive the best response as a function of that total, taking care at the points where the cap makes the payoff discontinuous. (ii) Describe the pure-strategy Nash equilibria of the game, and explain how they depend on the cap. (iii) Give a superrationality argument, in the spirit of the two baseline cases, for what the twenty symmetric contestants should do here, and compare its result to the Nash equilibria you found. (iv) Say precisely which features of the modified game are inherited from the Platonia dilemma and which from the Luring Lottery, and explain what changes if K is made very large or the cap is removed altogether.
