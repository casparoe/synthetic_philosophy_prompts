At a reading group someone kept reaching for the classic rope-snake example to explain māyā: you see a coil of rope in dim light, panic thinking it's a snake, then see it's just rope, and the snake is "unreal" in the way Advaita says the world is unreal — you're just misperceiving Brahman. I've heard this enough times that I want to actually audit it. First walk me through what maps onto what — the rope, the snake, the dim light, the fear, the moment of recognition — and what each is doing in the theory. Then tell me where the analogy breaks. A few suspicious spots: the snake never existed at all, while the world is usually said to have some kind of provisional or transactional reality; the rope is a distinct object you can point at, but Brahman isn't one thing alongside the world; and once the light comes on the snake is simply gone, whereas after liberation the world is still supposed to appear. Which of these disanalogies actually matter for the point being made, which don't, and does the conclusion the example is used to support — the world is māyā, only Brahman is real — survive once they're on the table? If there's a better analogy, I'd want that too.
