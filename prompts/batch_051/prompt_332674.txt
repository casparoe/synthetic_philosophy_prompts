I am a high school student writing a term paper for an independent study on how long-run predictions age, and I keep using a phrase I cannot actually defend: that Malthus was right but only early. What I mean is the familiar claim that his core mechanism — that population tends to outrun the food supply and is held back by famine, disease, war, and deliberate restraint — was never really falsified by the industrial and agricultural revolutions; those developments merely postponed a mechanism that still shows up wherever the postponing conditions are removed. My advisor says this sounds like a claim that survives anything: every failed prediction gets absorbed as a delay, and no observation could ever count against it. She asked me what evidence would tell a genuinely early theory apart from one that keeps being rescued until it is empty, and I do not know how to answer her.

I would like help designing the strongest honest test of the claim. My rough intuition is that the test should compare populations or periods that differ mainly in the factors that allegedly postpone the mechanism — sustained technological improvement, institutions, the demographic transition — and check whether Malthusian checks reappear once those factors are absent; that would be the pattern favoring early-but-right, while their failure to reappear would favor a deflationary reading. But I suspect that comparison is too simple, because the line between part of the theory and an outside rescue is exactly what is in dispute. So I am asking for a concrete test a student could construct from widely known economic and demographic history, the pattern of results that would favor each reading, and an honest account of what the test would and would not settle — in particular, whether the phrase describes one claim about a mechanism or a flexible label for a research program that quietly swaps its auxiliary assumptions every time a prediction fails. I am less interested in a final verdict on Malthus himself than in learning what kind of evidence could make the difference between premature and unfalsifiable a real one.
