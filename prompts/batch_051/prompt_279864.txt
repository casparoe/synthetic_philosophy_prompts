I'm on the program committee for my institute's annual "Governing the Future" lecture (I mostly handle logistics, so I'm still getting my head around the political theory side). This year's nomination has split the room down the middle. The proposal is to invite a senior fellow at a government-linked Singaporean think tank to give a lecture arguing that "performance legitimacy" — the idea that a state can be legitimate when it reliably delivers prosperity, public order, and basic security even without much liberal-democratic contestation — accounts for Singapore's stability and, with more caveats, for parts of China's record since the late 1970s. A donor has already agreed to underwrite the event, so money isn't the issue.

Half the committee thinks this is exactly the difficult idea a public lecture series should be willing to air; the other half says a named lecture with our branding and a drinks reception amounts to laundrying an apology for one-party rule, preventive detention, and censorship. The chair asked me to draft the committee's decision memo: invite, decline, or invite with conditions (a formal respondent, a reframed title, a panel instead of a single voice?). I want the memo to take a definate position and defend it rather than laying out options and stopping. It should also be honest about the strongest consideration on the losing side — not a strawman. And please resist the easy "free speech means invite everyone" line: treat it as a real question about whether an institution should signal that prosperity and order can ground legitimacy.
