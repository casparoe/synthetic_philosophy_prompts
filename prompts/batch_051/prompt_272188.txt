I am putting together a short handout for a reading group of postgraduate public-health students who have started reading behavioural genetics, and I want one section to be a catalogue of the recurring ways heritability estimates get misread in popular and even professional discussion. The topic became personal for me after a colleague dismissed a proposed nutritional intervention by citing a high heritability figure for a growth measure, and I found I could not explain quickly enough why that inference should not be made.

The standard confusions I have already written up are these:

1. the individual-level fallacy: taking a heritability estimate, which is a ratio of variances in a population, as a statement about how much of a particular person's trait is caused by genes;
2. the immutability fallacy: taking a high estimate to imply that the average trait value cannot be moved much by environmental change, when heritability is compatible with large population-level responses to intervention;
3. the invariance fallacy: treating an estimate as a fixed property of the trait itself, when it is specific to a particular population, range of environments, measurement protocol, and time;
4. the between-groups inference: using a within-population estimate to explain observed average differences between groups, such as sexes, birth cohorts, social classes, or geographical populations, as partly genetic;
5. the inverse of the immutability fallacy: taking a low estimate to show that genes are not importantly involved in producing the trait, rather than that there happens to be little heritable variation in that population;
6. the design confusion: treating heritability as a measure of how genetically encoded or natural a trait is, when a trait can be developmentally dependent on genes yet show near-zero heritable variation.

I believe that list already contains the standard items, so I am not looking for any of them in different clothing. Please give me one further, distinct way in which people misread heritability estimates when interpreting them, that is, a misinterpretation or erroneous inference about what an estimate means, not a criticism of how estimates are produced. It should not be a rewording, special case, or combination of any of the six above. Then explain how it differs from the nearest two or three items on my list, state how often you think it actually arises, and give an honest judgement of how serious the error is.

Return your answer as a JSON object with the fields "label" (a short name for the mistake, under eight words), "description" (two or three sentences stating the mistaken inference and why it is wrong), "distinctness" (why it is not a variant of the listed items), "prevalence" (e.g., rare, occasional, common, with one sentence of justification), and "seriousness" (one or two sentences on how much practical or theoretical damage it does). Write in British English.
