I've been chewing on something for an essay and want a philosopher's read on a film. Longtime lesswrong lurker here, so I've marinated in the idea that good agents plan: precommitment, expected value estimates, contingency trees, all of it. But I've started to suspect planning is overrated in a certain class of situations, and that the improvisers — or even the fatalists — sometimes do better. No Country for Old Men (the movie, or McCarthy's novel) seems like a natural test case. Moss does all the planning a person can: he stashes the money, rents motel rooms, tries to stay a few moves ahead. His one unplanned act, going back with water for the dying man, is arguably what undoes him. Chigurh, by contrast, barely plans the way a rationalist would: he follows the transponder, kills whoever's next, and lets a coin toss decide wether someone lives. Yet Chigurh keeps coming through walls. So: does the film actually support the view that planning is overrated, or would a smart planner just say Moss made identifiable mistakes? What does the movie think the difference between planning and improvisation amounts to in a world with this much chance and arbitrary violence? And where does the film resist the easy 'planning is pointless' reading? I want to avoid the bog-standard 'fate beats effort' take, so push me past that.
