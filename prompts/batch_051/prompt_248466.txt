You are a political theorist and institutional designer who works on both Machiavelli's account of fortune and virtù and the incentive structures of real legislatures; think carefully, and begin by brainstorming how each class of actor—legislators, commissioners, interest groups, and voters—would plausibly respond before you commit to a verdict.

I am evaluating a proposed constitutional rule, offered as an attempt to turn the following passage from Machiavelli's The Prince, chapter XXV (trans. W. K. Marriott) into a standing institution:

"I believe also that he will be successful who directs his actions according to the spirit of the times, and that he whose actions do not accord with the times will not be successful. Because men are seen, in affairs that lead to the end which every man has before him, namely, glory and riches, to get there by various methods; one with caution, another with haste; one by force, another by skill; one by patience, another by its opposite; and each one succeeds in reaching the goal by a different method. One can also see of two cautious men the one attain his end, the other fail; and similarly, two men by different observances are equally successful, the one being cautious, the other impetuous; all this arises from nothing else than whether or not they conform in their methods to the spirit of the times."

The proposed rule: every standing law and treaty automatically lapses fifteen years after enactment. In a measure's final year, the assembly may re-enact it only by a simple majority and only after a Conjuncture Hearing. A nine-person Conjuncture Commission, chosen by lot from citizens over forty-five who have never held elective office and serving a single four-year term, first presents a written Report on the Times; the Commission is forbidden to recommend any policy and may describe only how the state's circumstances have changed since the measure was last enacted. Proponents and opponents of the lapsed measure then each have one hour to argue that it does or does not conform its method to the spirit of the times. The assembly must then vote the measure up or down without amendment; a measure that fails is dead and cannot be reintroduced for two years.

Analyze this as a mechanism, not as a moral proposal, and take a definite position on what it would actually do: what it incentivizes in legislators, in the Commission, and in organized interests; where it is gamed first; its most damaging failure mode and its most important second-order effect; and the single fix you would make, if any, to keep it from becoming ritualized sunset review. Use the Machiavelli passage to say whether the rule genuinely institutionalizes the capacity to change one's method with fortune, or only simulates it.

Deliver your analysis as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. In each item, state one definite consequence or verdict and your reason for it; do not hedge by listing alternatives.
