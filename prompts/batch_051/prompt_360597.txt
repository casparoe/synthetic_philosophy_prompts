I'm applying for a six-month internal grant at a research nonprofit, and I need you to turn my half-formed idea into a one-page proposal I can submit as-is, modulo citations, which I'll add myself. The idea concerns the so-called DNA–Gödel isomorphism: the claim, familiar from Hofstadter's typogenetics and developed in and around Gödel, Escher, Bach, that biological self-replication and Gödelian self-reference share a single deep structure, with a strand of DNA playing something like the role of a sentence that refers to its own encoding. As a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, I've watched this analogy get invoked as profound and also dismissed as a vivid metaphor; what I haven't seen is anyone pry apart the different things it could mean before making either move.

My suspicion is that the analogy quietly bundles three separable claims: (a) that replication is an instance of self-reference; (b) that a self-replicating strand is a fixed point of a copying function, the way a Gödel sentence is a fixed point of a provability predicate; and (c) that what matters in both cases is that the relevant object is described rather than directly duplicated. I suspect these come apart, and that which one is load-bearing changes what the analogy licenses. In particular, I want to know whether the load-bearing reading is more permissive than the usual story suggests — for example, whether error-prone replication and mutation are not disanalogies but exactly what a fixed-point-style copying relation would predict.

Write the proposal. It must contain: (1) a precise statement of the question, framed so that it has a determinate answer rather than an occasion for vague profundity; (2) why the existing positions — the deep-isomorphism camp and the just-a-metaphor camp — leave the question open rather than settling it in one direction; (3) the method, meaning what I would actually do month by month, and why that method rather than the two obvious alternatives of building a toy formal system to exhibit the analogy or doing intellectual history of Hofstadter's argument; (4) what a deliverable would look like for a reader who wants to know whether the analogy can carry weight in reasoning about natural or artificial self-replicating systems; and (5) an honest statement of what would count as the project failing. Set the scope realistically for six months. Assume the reader already knows Gödel, Escher, Bach, Gödel numbering, and the diagonal lemma, so don't re-explain them; don't include citations or a bibliography. Dense, polished, no throat-clearing.
