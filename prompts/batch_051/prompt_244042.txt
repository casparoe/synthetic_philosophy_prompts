Write a deadpan parody of a layered privacy notice — the format in which a friendly, bolded "short version" sits above the long legal text — for a fictional company that sells an always-on home assistant, published on the company's website. The notice should read at first as though it were written to reassure users, while the fine print quietly does the opposite. Its satirical target should be the notice-and-consent model of privacy and the control theory that underwrites it: the view that privacy simply is control over information about oneself, so that whatever a user clicks through counts as a free and privacy-preserving choice. The short version should state this view confidently; the long version should then follow the view to absurd conclusions, with each absurdity tracking a real philosophical objection. Include, for instance, a definition of "choice" under which a customer who cannot opt out without losing essential services has still chosen; a rewriting of the limited-access rival — the view that privacy is others having less access to you — so that the only access restricted is the user's access to their own data; and a contextual-integrity clause (contextual integrity being the view that privacy norms are context-relative rules about how information may flow) that lets the company move data across contexts because it judges each move "integral" to the service. End with something like a definition of total surveillance as "continuous consent," so that the control theory has been stretched to the point of protecting its own opposite. The humor should come from getting the philosophy right, so a reader who knows the privacy-as-control and contextual-integrity debates laughs rather than winces, while a reader new to them can still tell exactly what is being ironized. Keep it deadpan throughout, around 600 words, and do not name any real company or person.
