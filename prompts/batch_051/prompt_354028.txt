Yesterday evening I had a long argument with a friend about Arrow's impossibility theorem — specifically about whether every known response to it can be filed under a small number of headings. For context, I have in mind the standard theorem: at least three alternatives, unrestricted domain, weak Pareto, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and the requirement that the social preference relation be a complete and transitive ordering. By the end of the evening I had assembled what I took to be a complete catalogue of the available responses. There is (1) restricting the domain of admissible preference profiles, as in the move to single-peaked preferences, where majority rule again yields a transitive ordering. There is (2) weakening the collective rationality condition, requiring only quasi-transitivity or acyclicity of the social relation — a move that tends to replace the dictator with an oligarchy or a set of vetoers rather than eliminate the difficulty. There is (3) dropping or weakening independence of irrelevant alternatives, so that the social ranking of two alternatives may depend on more than their pairwise comparison, in the manner of Borda-type rules. There is (4) leaving Arrow's ordinal, interpersonally non-comparable informational base and allowing cardinal utility or interpersonal comparisons, along the lines of a Bergson–Samuelson social welfare function or straightforward utilitarianism. There is (5) denying that the object Arrow axiomatized — a single, complete social-preference ordering — is what a democratic decision procedure needs, and retreating to deliberation, agenda setting, or pairwise voting whose outcome may be path-dependent. And there is (6) refusing the deterministic setting altogether and adopting a probabilistic social choice rule, such as a lottery over alternatives or over individuals.

My friend's contention is that this list is exhaustive: any further proposal, he says, will turn out to be a restatement, a special case, or a combination of these six. I am looking for a response to Arrow's impossibility theorem that is genuinely not on that list. It should not be a rewording of one of the six, not an instance of one of them, and not a package that merely stacks two or three of them together. If you can supply one, explain what it does that the six listed moves do not; then assess it honestly — does it survive my friend's accusation, or does it, on inspection, reduce to something already in the list?
