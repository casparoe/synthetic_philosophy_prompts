First-order representationalism says a mental state is conscious just in case it is a first-order representation with the right kind of content, and that content is poised/available to the right downstream systems for rational control of thought and action. Higher-order thought theory says a state is conscious only if the subject also has a suitable higher-order thought to the effect that they are in that state, where the higher-order thought is itself not conscious (unless there's yet another thought about it). I want a case were these two come apart in a specific direction: the first-order theory counts the state as conscious, and the HOT theory counts it as not conscious.

So build me one ordinary-life scenario—adult human, no blindsight, no subliminal perception, no anesthesia, no split-brain, no meditation—where a particular perceptual state (not a pain and not a mood) is conscious by the first-order theory but not by the HOT theory. The case should make it plausible both that the state is genuinely poised for rational control of thought and action and that the subject has no higher-order thought about it; don't just write in "and they have no higher-order thought." It's for a slide, so keep the scenario itself tight, a paragraph or less. Then do the check: say exactly which mental state is at issue, what it represents, which consuming systems it's poised for, and why each theory gives the verdict it gives. Finish by saying how a HOT theorist would most plausibly attack the case, and identify the single feature of the scenario that does the real work, including what would happen if you removed it. I've found that when my students build these cases they often just stipulate the verdicts instead of letting them fall out of the details, so be honest about whether yours actually generates the verdicts.
