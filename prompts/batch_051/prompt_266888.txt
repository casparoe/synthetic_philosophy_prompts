So I've been noticing a weird asymmetry and I can't quite figure out what theory of the good life it rests on. Whenever people actually plan or evaluate futures—government foresight reports, national wellbeing frameworks, a city's strategic plan, the stuff foundations fund—the future's goodness gets cashed out in terms of health, income, longevity, education, safety, clean air, and sometimes self-reported life satisfaction or happiness. Fun almost never shows up as its own thing. Even the 'beyond GDP' people who ask about thriving usually mean feeling happy, calm, or satisfied with your life, not whether your week had any genuinely absorbing play, novelty, challenge, or plain comic absurdity in it. The implicit picture seems to be: get people healthy, secure, and reasonably satisfied, and a good future just follows; fun is either a private hobby or a byproduct, not something a serious evaluation has to name.

I'd like you to reconstruct the account of the good life this practice presupposes, and then pick at it. Does this pattern strictly presuppose hedonism, or a desire-satisfaction view, or is it actually compatible with objective-list views too—just with a bad list? Where exactly does the exclusion of fun get encoded: in what's measured, in how tradeoffs are made, or in what's allowed to count as a 'serious' reason in a planning document? Is there a coherent view under which fun is genuinely not a public good but only a private matter, or is that just a cop-out? And then tell me concretely what a practice would have to do differently if it treated fun as a first-class ingredient of a future worth living in—what would its indicators, its planning questions, and its version of cost-benefit look like? If part of the problem is that fun resists measurement, say whether that's a fact about fun or a fact about the measures, and whether hard-to-measure things are ever legitimately left out of an account of a good future.
