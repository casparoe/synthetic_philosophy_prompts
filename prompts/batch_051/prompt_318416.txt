"Frankfurt argues that bullshit is a greater enemy of truth than lying is. Using his essay On Bullshit, explain how he distinguishes lying from bullshitting, and why he thinks the liar is in a certain way on the side of truth. Then apply the framework to LLM hallucinations: is a hallucinated answer best classified as a lie, bullshit, or honest error? Defend your classification and say why each of the other two classifications is initially tempting."

And here's the student's answer, word for word:

"Frankfurt's distinction is basically about the speaker's relation to the facts. The liar has to be tracking the truth, because to lie you have to know (or at least think you know) what is true and then say the opposite, with the intent to decieve your audience about that fact. So lying, however bad, still treats the truth as authoritative: the liar is on the other side of the line, but he knows where the line is. The bullshitter, by contrast, doesn't care whether what he says tracks the facts at all. He isn't trying to get the world right; he's trying to sound right, or to get through the occasion, and he's indifferent to how things actually are. Frankfurt thinks this is what makes bullshit worse than lying as a cultural matter: the liar chips away at particular truths, but the bullshitter corrupts the whole practice of caring about truth, since he teaches us that it doesn't matter whether anyone is actually connected to reality.

Applied to LLMs: hallucination obviously isn't lying, because the model doesn't know the truth and isn't trying to deceive you — there's no goal of leading the listener away from what it takes to be true. It's also not exactly an honest error, because an error requires the speaker to be trying to get things right and just failing; the LLM isn't aiming at truth in that way, it's just sampling from a distribution. So I'd classify hallucination as Frankfurt-style bullshit: the system pays no attention to whether its outputs correspond to how things are, and its speech is produced without regard to the truth. Lying looks tempting because a fake citation or a hallucinated fact can have the same downstream effect on a reader as a deliberate lie; error looks tempting because the model gets things wrong without any motive, like a student who misremembers. But neither fits, since both lying and erring presuppose the speaker has a relation to the truth that the model simply lacks. One residual wrinkle: Frankfurt says the bullshitter misrepresents what he is up to, not just the facts, and an LLM is not up to anything. So maybe the model can't literally bullshit, and the bullshit-like character belongs to the output or the people who deploy it. Still, as a classification of the utterance itself, Frankfurt's label is the closest of the three."
