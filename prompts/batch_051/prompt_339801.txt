I have no formal background in mathematics or game theory, but I have been reading about perfect play in board games in my spare time and turning the arguments over on my own. One passage from a popular introduction keeps nagging at me, because its conclusion seems stronger than the reasoning can support. The passage runs roughly as follows.

Consider any finite two-player game with perfect information and no luck, in which the players move alternately and the rules treat the two sides symmetrically. If the second player had a winning strategy, the first player could open with some throwaway move and then copy that winning strategy for the rest of the game, pretending to be the second player. The only snag is that the copied strategy may at some point instruct the first player to play the move already used as the throwaway; in that case she simply makes another throwaway move. Since in a genuinely symmetric game an extra move or extra piece can never make a position worse, the first player ends up at least as well off as the second player would have been under the supposed winning strategy. This contradiction shows that the second player cannot have a winning strategy, and because the game cannot end in a draw, the first player must have one. The book says this is the standard proof that the first player wins at Hex, and that the same reasoning shows the first player wins at chess and at Go once drawn games are ruled out.

I find the Hex proof plausible, because placing an extra stone in a connection game seems as though it cannot hurt. But I have played enough chess to know that sometimes a player is worse off precisely because it is their turn, and that makes me suspicious of the throwaway-move step and of the leap to chess. I am not confident, though, because the book says drawn games are excluded, and it may be that this restriction removes the troublesome positions.

Is this a fair summary of the strategy-stealing argument? Please separate what the argument actually establishes, what it must assume, where the passage quietly overreaches, and what would need to be corrected before the general conclusion and the remarks about chess and Go follow. I am interested in the reasoning itself rather than in references or further reading.
