Look, here's an argument I keep half-having at work and I want to actually get clear on it. I sit near an LLM lab — not a researcher, I'm on the product/ops side — which means I watch how we actually try to make models 'aligned' on a day-to-day basis. The stack is roughly: pretrain on internet text, supervised fine-tuning on hand-written examples, RLHF (or DPO, same idea) using human preference data where the model is trained to prefer responses that raters liked, then red-teaming and a battery of eval benchmarks, and finally a system prompt telling the model to be helpful, harmless, and honest. The thing that nags at me is this: everyone on the team talks as if we're installing values in the model — it should want to be helpful, care about not deceiving people, etc. — but everything we actually measure and optimize is behavior. Which answer got a thumbs-up from a rater, did it refuse the jailbreak, what score did it get on the benchmark. Even when the model says I care about being honest, nobody in the room treats that as anything but a token sequence that scored well.

Treat the whole alignment stack as a practice, the way you'd treat a court system or a medical ethics board, and tell me what commitments about mind and value it presupposes. In particular: does the practice commit itself to some kind of operationalism or behaviorism about values — aligned means does aligned-looking things — or does it presuppose there's a real internal thing (values, goals, preferences) that the training is shaping, with behavior only an imperfect readout? I suspect it want to have it both ways: behavior is treated as the only admissible criterion for training and evaluation, while intentional language saturates the internal docs and system prompts. Also: the preference data is just revealed preference from crowdworkers, but then we override it with safety rules and refusals, so does the practice secretly assume that human values aren't identical to what raters pick? And what does it commit to about whether values can be aggregated out of millions of pairwise comparisons, or compressed into a few bullet points of instructions? I want the implicit theory reconstructed: what the practice strictly presupposes, what it merely fits with, and where it's underdetermined — could it be run just as coherently by a behaviorist, by someone who thinks LLMs have no values at all, and by someone who thinks the values are internal but the measurement is noisy? Then tell me what would concretely have to change in the work — the training objective, the evals, the way people talk in launch reviews — if a lab actually replaced the implicit theory with the view that alignment is about internal goals and motivations rather than revealed choices.

You're a philosopher of cognitive science who knows both the alignment literature and the old behaviourism and intentional-stance debates; avoid the glib 'LLMs are just stochastic parrots' line and the glib 'they're basically people' line. Think carefully, maybe brainstorm the candidate positions first, and be specific about where the practice is genuinely incoherent rather than merely philosophically debatable.
