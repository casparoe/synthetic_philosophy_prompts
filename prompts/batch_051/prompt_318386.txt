I am reporting on a procedural dispute inside a citizens' assembly convened to advise a funder on outdoor climate intervention research, and I want to understand what the dispute actually turns on. The assembly's standing orders, written when it met in person, include the following rule: "Where a member's contribution presupposes a proposition not already recorded as common ground, that proposition shall be entered into the record upon the chair's recognition of the next contribution, unless a member has objected before that recognition." The drafters plainly had a live session in mind: a chair who can see who is attending, a rhythm of turns in which silence is itself a signal that the room has let the presupposition stand, and the possibility of raising an objection the moment a contested premise is floated.

The assembly has since moved to an asynchronous online written-comment platform. During a 48-hour comment cycle, one delegate posted a contribution that presupposed, without ever asserting, that the assembly had already set aside sulfate-based stratospheric aerosol injection; no such decision had been taken. The cycle closed over a national holiday weekend. When the deadline lapsed, the software triggered the recognition of the next contribution, and the secretariat, following the rule, entered the presupposition into the record. Several delegates were offline throughout and insist there was no point at which the body could be said to have accepted anything; others reply that no one objected, and the rule does not require anyone actually to be in the room. I am less interested in the secretariat's reading than in what each main approach to interpreting the rule would give: the letter, the purpose, what the drafters would have said had they foreseen the platform, and what a reasonable delegate would take the words to mean. Is the presupposition now in the record, in the only sense of "record" that this language game actually has? And does the incident show that accommodation is a social act that bookkeeping can simulate but not perform, or that a conversational score can legitimately be moved by deadlines and clocks?
