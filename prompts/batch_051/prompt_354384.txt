I've been teaching the Mozi against the Confucians for years, and I keep getting stuck on the same problem. On paper I'm convinced by the Mohist line: partiality is what produces conflict, so the cure is jian'ai — impartial care, equal concern for everyone's welfare rather than equal liking — and it's simple, universal, and gives a clean test for action. I also grant the Confucian side when it's put well: graded love radiating outward from the family starts from attachments that are actually there, and talk about everyone in the abstract can become a way of caring for no one in particular. Still, my firm intuition is that I ought to care more about my own daugher than about a stranger's daughter, and that a theory that calls that a bias is asking me to be a worse father, not a better philosopher. I don't want to be told which side wins. I want a negotiated verdict: does jian'ai actually contradict that intuition, or only a sloppy reading of it? What would justify amending the Mohist theory to carve out room for special obligations, and what would justify dropping the intuition as parochial? Is there a reading of care, as affect rather than as action, on which both survive? Make your final line a one-sentence summary of your verdict, in the form \answer{...}.
