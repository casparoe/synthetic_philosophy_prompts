I am a teaching assistant in a third-year cognitive science course, and next week I have a fifty-minute section on the timing of conscious experience. The students already know the basics of apparent motion and can distinguish iconic from working memory, but they have no background in philosophy of mind. The session should run from the color phi phenomenon to the Orwellian/Stalinesque contrast, and it should end by putting pressure on what makes the two accounts look like genuine rivals rather than by adjudicating between them.

By the color phi phenomenon I mean this: a red dot is flashed, and then shortly afterward and nearby a green dot is flashed, and observers sincerely report a single dot moving continuously and changing from red to green around the midpoint of its apparent path. The puzzle is that the experienced color change seems to occur before the green flash itself, so any account of the timing of experience must say how information from the second flash ends up shaping what the subject takes to have happened earlier. The two accounts I want the students to hold in tension are the Stalinesque account, on which experience is delayed until both flashes are in and then presents one already-edited sequence, so the revision happens before anything reaches consciousness; and the Orwellian account, on which the flashes are experienced in real time and memory is rewritten immediately afterward, so reports and recollections are of a revised past rather than the events as first experienced. Both accounts predict the same sincere report.

I know the material and I am not asking for the explanation itself. What I need is a plan for teaching it in the fifty minutes, in the following terms:

- The order in which the pieces must be introduced and the dependency structure between them: what must students already have accepted before each next move makes sense? In particular, when should the puzzle become unavoidable, when should the two accounts become available as the only two natural options, and when should their apparent status as rivals be challenged?
- The single concrete case that should carry the discussion, and why it should be the color phi rather than either plain phi motion (two same-colored flashes) or a purely verbal statement of the backward-referral puzzle. Say what the case must have and what it must lack — for instance, nothing about it should let students dismiss the report as guessing.
- The misconceptions to expect and where in the sequence to handle each. I am particularly worried about these: reading the Stalinesque account as precognition rather than delayed construction; reading the Orwellian account as the subject lying or merely misremembering later, rather than there being no earlier record left to consult; assuming that better timing data or neural measures would settle which account is true; and concluding that the debate is merely verbal because both accounts predict the same sentence. For each, say whether it should be headed off before it appears or allowed to surface and then be shown to misfire, and why at that point.
- What I should openly refuse to settle at this level — I am thinking of whether the framework carries over to the Libet experiments on the timing of conscious intention, and of the question how long a delay is too long for backward referral — and how to mark that refusal without letting the refusal become the lesson.
- A final question or small exercise, for the last three minutes, that would tell me whether they understand that both accounts are revisionist and why they seem to be rivals, rather than merely having memorized the labels Orwellian and Stalinesque.
