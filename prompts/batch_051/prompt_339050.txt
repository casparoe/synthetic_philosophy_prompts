I just spent a long evening arguing with a friend about naked statistical evidence, and I came away convinced by his position on the standard cases but stuck on what feels like an obvious next move. He pressed the blue bus case—80% of the buses in town belong to Blue Bus Co., a bus injures you, and you cannot identify the bus—and the gatecrasher case—501 of 1,000 rodeo attendees did not pay, and you pick one at random. He persuaded me that the plaintiff should lose both, on the principle that a bare population statistic cannot tie this particular defendant to this particular event, and so cannot carry a verdict.

But I do not see how to reconcile that with DNA evidence. A lab reports that the crime-scene sample matches the defendant's sample, and the force of that match is presented as a random match probability: the chance that a randomly chosen person would match is, say, one in ten million. My friend says DNA is different because the sample came from the defendant and from the crime scene. Yet the inference from a match between the defendant and the crime-scene sample to the conclusion that the defendant is the source still seems to be a probabilistic inference from population frequencies. If the blue bus statistic is naked, why is the random match probability not equally naked? And if it is equally naked, why should it be able to support a criminal conviction under the beyond-reasonable-doubt standard when the blue bus statistic cannot support even a civil verdict under the preponderance standard?

I am less interested in black-letter law than in whether my friend's principle is coherent. If I have misdescribed the cases, or if my question rests on a confusion about what a DNA match actually is, tell me that plainly instead of answering the question as posed. Please answer as a philosopher of evidence. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating of the question.
