I'm an undergraduate taking a course called Concepts of the Infinite, and I'm drafting a short argumentative essay for it. I'd be grateful for comments on the argument below before I revise. I'm particularly interested in whether the position I sketch is internally stable, where it leans on a slide or an ambiguity, and whether the distinction I draw does the work I claim it does.

Background. In class we have been contrasting actual and potential infinity, working through the Hilbert's-hotel thought experiment, Cantor's diagonal argument, and a range of finitist responses. My essay's working thesis is that finitism is usually attacked for the wrong target. The interesting version of finitism is not the claim that we can never quantify over infinite domains; mathematical practice does this constantly, and a bare prohibition looks unmotivated. The interesting version is narrower: it denies that an infinite totality is ever present as a completed object, surveyable or inhabited as a whole. I'll call this the no-completed-totality thesis.

My central move is a reading of the Hilbert's-hotel story. The story, as we have presented it, is that a hotel with rooms numbered 1, 2, 3, and so on, all occupied, can make room for one additional guest by moving the guest in room n to room n+1. This is often treated as evidence that an actually infinite collection is coherent, and even that such a collection can be full while still admitting new members.

I want to argue that the thought experiment is being overread. What it rigorously shows is that the mapping from n to n+1 is a well-defined rule over the natural numbers, and that no finite enumeration of guests exhausts the rooms. A finitist can grant both points without strain. What the experiment does not show is that there is a completed whole, the hotel with all its rooms, whose size is unaffected by unbounded rearrangement. That further conclusion requires treating the infinite collection as a finished object whose cardinality can be compared across arbitrary bijections, and that is precisely the step the no-completed-totality finitist declines to take.

I make the same move with the diagonal argument. As I read it, the proof gives us a schema: from any proposed enumeration of infinite binary sequences, we can specify a sequence that differs from every sequence in the list. That rules out certain claims about possible enumerations and is an important fact about finite rules and representations. But it does not by itself force us to say that the set of all infinite binary sequences is a completed object with a definite cardinality, any more than the Hilbert's-hotel case forces us to speak of a completed hotel. The finitist, as I understand the position, accepts the schema but refuses the metaphysical topping.

The worry I have, and would especially like comments on, is whether this refusal is merely verbal. A critic might say that once the finitist agrees to quantify over natural numbers, accepts the function sending n to n+1 for every n, and agrees that no enumeration of binary sequences succeeds, they have already admitted everything the defender of actual infinity wants. If so, my no-completed-totality finitism is not a substantive alternative to the standard view but only a change of emphasis, or a restriction on pictures rather than on mathematics.

Could you comment on that? Specifically:

(1) Is my reading of Hilbert's hotel fair, or does it shift the burden illegitimately between a mathematical claim and a metaphysical one?

(2) Does the finitist reading of the diagonal argument, as a schema rather than a theorem about a completed set, really avoid the commitment it claims to avoid, or does stating the schema already quantify over the disputed totalities?

(3) If the position does collapse into standard mathematical practice, is that a victory for the finitist, a victory for the defender of actual infinity, or a sign that the debate was misdescribed all along?
