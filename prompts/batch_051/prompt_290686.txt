I've been trying to get clear on a distinction that keeps surfacing in discussions of authoritarian-leaning developmental states: the difference between actually being entitled to hold power and merely being good at delivering results. The concept I have in mind is performance legitimacy, understood roughly as the claim that a government can earn a genuine right to rule by consistently providing economic growth, public order, security, and competent administration, even when it restricts political competition, civil liberties, and meaningful accountability. I don't want a general overview of the concept; I want to test it against a concrete case, because I suspect the theory tends to slide over the gap between "people tolerate this regime" and "this regime is actually legitimate."

Take Singapore from independence in 1965 through roughly the end of Lee Kuan Yew's premiership in 1990, extending beyond that point where it is useful. The salient facts as I understand them: a resource-poor city-state expelled from Malaysia, facing mass unemployment, a housing shortage, and ethnic tension; the People's Action Party under Lee Kuan Yew pursued export-oriented industrialization, large-scale public housing, a meritocratic education system, and a reputation for relatively clean, technically competent government, producing one of the fastest rises in living standards of the twentieth century. At the same time, the government constrained press freedom, assembly, and organized opposition; used preventive detention under the Internal Security Act against political opponents; and maintained an electoral system in which the PAP's legislative dominance far exceeded its share of the popular vote.

Analyze this episode through the lens of performance legitimacy. Specifically: (1) By the theory's own lights, what trade-offs did Singapore's leadership face, and did the theory genuinely require the restrictions as a condition of the performance, or is that connection the theory's proponents merely assert? (2) Which features of the case does the theory explain well—public quiescence, material improvement, regime durability—and which does it misdescribe or leave out, such as coercion, the inheritance of colonial-era legal instruments, and the difference between consent and resignation? (3) Run the analysis in the other direction: does Singapore confirm performance legitimacy, expose it as a post hoc rationalization by those who won, or reveal something more awkward—for instance, that it is a real but contingent and perishable basis of support once prosperity stalls or expectations rise? Be disciplined about the mapping between theory and case, and where the theory is silent rather than speaking to an important feature of the case, say so explicitly.
