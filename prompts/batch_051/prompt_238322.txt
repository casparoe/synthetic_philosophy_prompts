I have come to think that the derogatory force of a slur is part of its literal, conventional meaning: a slur does not simply pick out the same people as its neutral counterpart, and a speaker who used the word competently without grasping that it was derogatory would be missing part of what the word means. I would like you to tell me what, precisely, would count against this view. I am not looking for a rehearsal of the obvious reply that in-group speakers sometimes use slurs among themselves without offence; I want a map of the position's exposure. Which linguistic and social facts should most worry a defender of the semantic view — for instance the behaviour of slurs under negation and inside questions, conditionals, and reports of speech; the way children actually acquire them; the ease with which a neutral counterpart can be substituted without loss of truth; the variation in derogatory force across languages and over time; and the existence of genuine non-derogatory uses? Distinguish what would be decisive from what would be merely awkward, and say which alleged counterexamples a committed semantic theorist would reasonably absorb by appeal to ambiguity, idiolect, presupposition, taboo, or tone. Include whether the semantic view really makes different predictions from a purely pragmatic rival, or whether the dispute is partly verbal. Be willing to conclude either that the view is well insulated or that it is exposed, and say whether that insulation is a defect in a theory of word meaning. Please also tell me how confident you are in what you claim and which single point you are least sure of. Write in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as if you were writing a letter.
