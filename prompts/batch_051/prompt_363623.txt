My philosophy of law seminar has 16 advanced undergraduates and a single 90-minute session to spend on self-amendment. I want a game rather than a lecture, simple enough to run in about 60 minutes and driven by a point incentive rather than real money, in which students amend the rule that governs how rules are amended and are then forced to answer, in practice, by which rule their amendment was valid. By the end of the session, they should have felt rather than merely discussed why a rule of change that applies to itself unsettles the line between an ordinary legal change and a change of the system.

I want the game built around this passage from Peter Suber's Nomic: A Game of Self-Amendment, where he says what the game was meant to show:

For me, it was intended to illustrate and embody the thesis of my book, The Paradox of Self-Amendment, that a legal "rule of change" such as a constitutional amendment clause may apply to itself and authorize its own amendment.

The debrief should return to this passage and test whether the game confirms it, complicates it, or fails to secure it.

Please structure your answer in the following sections, in this order:

1. The Game (roughly 250 words): materials, roles or seating, the initial rules, the point incentive, and a timed sequence leading to the debrief.
2. Where They Should Get Stuck (roughly 150 words): the precise crisis your design is engineered to produce.
3. The Debrief (roughly 300 words): sequenced questions that map what actually happened onto the structure of self-amendment in the passage.
4. What Will Go Wrong (roughly 200 words): likely failure modes and how to handle each.
5. What the Game Cannot Reproduce (roughly 150 words): the feature of self-amendment the facilitator must still supply because the game cannot produce it.
