In the Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (Second Section), Kant rejects the golden rule in its traditional negative form as a candidate for the supreme principle of morality. According to his argument there, the rule that one should not do to others what one does not will done to oneself cannot serve as a universal moral law, because it contains no proper ground for duties to oneself, no ground for duties of love or beneficence toward others, since a person might gladly forgo receiving help from others if that also excused him from having to help them, and no ground for strict duties owed to others, since a criminal could invoke it against the judges who would punish him. Write an essay on whether these objections really defeat the golden rule, or whether they succeed only against an uncharitable version of it. Consider whether the standard repairs—replacing the negative formulation with a positive one, idealizing what the agent would will, adding an impartiality requirement, or building in a condition of rational willing—can preserve what is distinctive about the golden rule rather than silently turning it into Kant's own categorical imperative. Reach a definite conclusion about which, if any, version of the golden rule survives Kant's criticism. In the course of the essay, say how confident you are in the claims you make and single out the point you are least sure of.
