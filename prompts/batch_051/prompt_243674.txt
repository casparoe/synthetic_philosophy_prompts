Construct a decision tree for a handout in an intermediate ethics seminar. The tree should sort the principal philosophical responses to Nozick's utility monster—a being stipulated to derive vastly more welfare from each additional unit of a resource than ordinary people do—so that a student can locate their own view before any position has been named.

The question the tree sorts is this: should an agent be prepared, even in principle, to direct an ever-larger share of resources toward a single being simply because that being converts resources into welfare with supernormal efficiency, and what considerations could block such a conclusion?

Build the tree from the logical space of answers rather than from a list of labels. Each internal node should be a concrete yes-or-no question about a stipulated case or a specific commitment, answerable by someone who does not yet know the names of the theories. Label each branch with the question asked at that node; never phrase a node as self-identification with an -ism, and do not appeal to authors, citations, or authority.

Use at least these three cases among the nodes: (a) Nozick's original monster, which gains vastly more welfare from each marginal unit of a resource than anyone else; (b) an inverse monster, whose suffering from the loss of each marginal unit is vastly greater than the happiness anyone else would gain from it, so that even a small deprivation produces an enormous net loss; and (c) a trade-off in which thousands of people could each receive a barely noticeable benefit that only when summed is declared to outweigh one person's serious harm.

The tree must include these leaves, unless you explicitly mark a combination as unoccupied and explain why: classical total act-utilitarianism as the bullet-biting response; average utilitarianism; prioritarianism; leximin; sufficientarianism; Nozick's side-constraint rejection of utilitarianism; and Taurek-style anti-aggregation, if you judge it distinct enough from the Nozickian rejection to deserve its own leaf.

For each leaf, give the standard label, or write unnamed if no standard label exists, and add one sentence stating the implication of that position that its own defenders would find hardest to accept once the monster has been stipulated.

After the tree, list any combinations of answers that the logic leaves open but that no one actually defends, and for each say whether it is empty because the combination is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Then flag any pair of leaves that the decision questions cannot separate—for example, positions that agree on all fixed-population reallocations and diverge only when the creation of new beings is at issue—and specify the additional case or question that would separate them.

Keep the instrument to at most five decision questions and seven leaves. It should be self-contained and usable without consulting any outside source; do not include reading lists or bibliographic suggestions.
