I keep noticing a move in forecasting discussions, and I'd like to know whether it has a name of its own. I'm familiar with the inside/outside view distinction; I don't mean either side of it. I mean a particular lopsided way of moving between the two.

Two examples. A doctoral student is asked to estimate how long his dissertation will take from this point. He has finished data collection, and he can list the mechanical tasks that remain. Told that the base rate for dissertations in his program is two years, he explains why every slow case in the class was importantly different — a supervisor change, a resubmission, a part-time student — while his case is different along just the dimensions that matter. Yet when the same student estimates the likely impact of that dissertation, he reaches for a broad base rate about placement and citations and does not ask whether those cases are like his.

A hospital administrator considering a new urgent-care site explains away the unfavorable failure rate for such sites by narrowing the comparison class step by step — only teaching hospitals, only this region, only sites opened after a particular regulation — until the class fits her plan fairly well. In the same meeting she defends a favorable estimate about readmission reductions by citing an average across all such sites, including many she would not otherwise count as similar.

The pattern underneath these cases seems to be directional switching. Base-rate information is treated as decisive when it supports the conclusion the person already favors, and as crudely inapplicable when it does not. Specific features of the case are treated as decisive when they cut one way, and dismissed as anecdote when they cut the other. The very same fact that is invoked to make this case importantly atypical is not allowed to do the same work against a base rate the person happens to like. I am also struck by a second-order feature: people making this move often advertise it as epistemic modesty, saying they are taking the outside view even while they quietly select the comparison class, which tends to make the unevenness hard to spot.

Does this configuration have an established name? It seems plainly adjacent to base-rate neglect, motivated reasoning, and the problem that no comparison class is uniquely correct, but none of those captures the directed, switching character of the move. If it does have a name, I'd like it plus a precise account of how it is distinguished from those neighbors; if it does not, I'd rather have a coined name and a clean definition than a bare label, including the boundary where this move ends and a legitimate, evenhanded decision to set aside a base rate begins.

Please respond in American English.
