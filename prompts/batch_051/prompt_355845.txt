So I'm a journalist who mostly covers science and ideas, and I got roped into guest-leading one session of a first-year political concepts seminar. The topic they handed me is communism as an ideal: what would make it desirable if it worked. The students are bright but they keep treating the whole thing as already settled by twentieth-century history, and a few of them fall back on "human nature" the moment anything uncomfortable comes up. What I want them to actually get is that the question of whether the ideal would be good is seperable from whether it could ever work or has worked, and that you can hold those two apart without being naive. I dont want to give a pro-communism talk; I want to teach that distinction and the thinking it unlocks.

Plan the arc for me rather than the lecture itself: what has to be in place before the question makes sense to them, what depends on what, and in what order to introduce the pieces; the one example or scenario that carries the idea and what that example has to have and what it has to lack; the misconceptions or objections they'll raise and at which point in the sequence each should be headed off; how to check by the end that they've understood the question instead of just getting quiet or defensive; and what to leave out at this level and say so. Also please flag through the plan where you're drawing on what's settled among people who work on this versus the genuinely contested parts, so I don't present one side's contested view as if it were the consensus.
