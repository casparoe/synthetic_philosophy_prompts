In the course of reporting a piece on the legal and critical disputes over AI-generated images, I found that both sides kept talking past each other about a single word — creativity — and I ended up constructing a small conceptual apparatus that I would like an outside assessment of. I have been calling it the trace criterion. The core claim is that creative credit attaches to a person for a feature of an artifact only to the extent that the feature is a trace of that person's discriminations: the feature was one live option among alternatives, and it entered the work because the person, at some point, noticed a difference, preferred one option, and could in principle have chosen the other, such that the work would have been different had the choice gone differently. A painter's placement of a line satisfies this; so does a photographer's decision about where to stand and when to release the shutter; so does a jazz musician's decision to take or refuse a note in a solo. When someone prompts a generative image model, by contrast, the features that typically draw praise — the composition, the handling of light, the rendering of surfaces and anatomy — are not options the prompter ever held and rejected. Those were settled inside the model between prompt and output. The prompter does hold and reject options at the level of wording, parameter settings, and the acceptance or rejection of a whole generated image, so the criterion does not deny that prompting requires skill, nor that it can be creative in the way writing a haiku or giving a brief is creative. What it denies is that the resulting image, in its full visual detail, inherits that creativity.

I am aware this resembles the familiar intentionality objection to machine creativity, but I mean it to be more fine-grained: the question is not whether a machine has intentions, but whether the features we praise in an object are answerable to anyone's discriminative choices. What I want to know is this. Is the trace criterion already a named view in aesthetics or the psychology of creativity, merely restated in my vocabulary? Where does it break — does it wrongly demote collagists who trust chance, surrealists who practice automatic drawing, or artists who rely heavily on assistants, and does it wrongly promote merely laborious but unoriginal craftsmanship simply because it involves thousands of small unforced choices? And finally, is the counterfactual test doing real work, or have I smuggled in the conclusion that AI images are not creative by defining creative credit in terms of individual human choice from the outset? I am prepared to be told the criterion is unsalvageable, but I would like to know exactly which part fails and what the nearest better view would be.
