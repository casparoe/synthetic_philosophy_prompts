I write about science and ideas for a general readership, and I am drafting a piece on the precautionary principle. I have a working grasp of statistics: significance tests, Type I and Type II error, power, confidence intervals, and the basic machinery of decision theory such as loss functions and, where they can be specified, priors. What I would like from you is a translation exercise. Take the precautionary principle in its strong form — the view that when a proposed technology or policy could bring serious or irreversible harm, uncertainty about whether it will do so should not count as a reason to proceed, and the default should be restraint until the proponent has shown adequate safety — and restate it as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary of statistical error control and decision theory: your null hypothesis and alternative, the two kinds of error, error rates, power, the loss function, and the burden of proof. Then give me a residue report. Which commitments of the principle translate cleanly into that vocabulary; which ones survive only if you bend them or silently import assumptions; and which ones have no expression there at all? Pay particular attention to what the statistical framing does with the principle's talk of ignorance, irreversibility, and who must prove what. End with your committed judgment, not a survey: once the translation is done, is the precautionary principle best understood as a rule about asymmetric error costs and where the burden of proof lies, with the rest mostly rhetorical, or does the untranslated residue show that the principle is saying something the statistical framework cannot say? Please put your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
