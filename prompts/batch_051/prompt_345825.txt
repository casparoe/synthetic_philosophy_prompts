The standard objection to counterfactual history runs as follows. Because we cannot rerun the past, a counterfactual claim—that without event E, outcome O would not have occurred—is untestable. It is also radically underdetermined: the more one alters the antecedent, the more one must silently fix background conditions, and those stipulations look arbitrary. The standard defense has been a minimal-rewrite rule: vary only a small, well-documented event and hold everything else fixed except what is causally downstream of it. Now suppose a research group builds a large agent-based model of a past polity, calibrated on digitized tax records, parish registers, and climate data, and the model reproduces several known macro outcomes in periods not used in its calibration. The group then runs thousands of counterfactual simulations—deleting a single harvest failure, delaying a single letter—and when a target outcome still occurs in most runs, they report this as evidence that the outcome was overdetermined or nearly inevitable. Does the classic objection survive this development, need repair, or is it dead? Identify which premise of the objection is under the most pressure: untestability, underdetermination, or the assumption that counterfactual history is a single-case thought experiment rather than a modeling enterprise. If a version of the objection survives, state its strongest repaired form; if it is dead, say precisely what kind of counterfactual or inevitability claim the calibrated simulations do and do not license.
