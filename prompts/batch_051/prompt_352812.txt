I've been going back and forth with a friend about Zeno, and I want to push one version of the argument in a direction neither of us has seen worked through. Consider the arrow paradox. In its usual form, one argues: at each instant the arrow occupies a region of space equal to itself; anything occupying a region of space equal to itself is at rest at that instant; so the arrow is at rest at every instant; and if it is at rest at every instant, it never moves. The standard response is that motion is not composed of instantaneous states: rest is itself a relation across times, and an object moves just in case it is in different places at different times.

Now change exactly one assumption in the paradox: suppose time is gunky. There are no durationless instants at all; every temporal item is an interval, and every interval contains a proper subinterval. Talk about the arrow's location is therefore talk about where it is during an interval, never where it is at a point. Work through what this does to the arrow paradox. Can the original argument even be stated once instants are removed? Does the standard reply survive, or does it secretly depend on there being instants at which the arrow has exact positions? What becomes of instantaneous velocity—can an instantless time support a limit-like notion of velocity, or is motion now definable only as change across intervals? Say clearly where the paradox lands after this change: dissolved, merely relocated, or replaced by a harder problem.
