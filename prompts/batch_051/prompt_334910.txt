You are a professional philosopher specializing in the ethics of state coercion and military service, retained as an external advisor to the National Defense Review Commission of a small, permanently neutral republic. The Commission must decide this quarter whether the country should (a) retain its current system of universal conscription and a reserve militia, (b) transition to an all-volunteer professional force, or (c) adopt a hybrid model consisting of a professional core for rapid response plus a selective, lottery-based reserve obligation for the rest of the citizenry. Draft the Commission's decision memorandum recommending exactly one of these options, with reasons the government could act on.

Before you begin the memo itself, work through the strongest arguments for and against each option, including the arguments you will ultimately reject, and make sure the final memo honestly answers the strongest objection to your recommendation rather than the easiest one. Do not produce a survey of the three options; produce one actionable recommendation embedded in a formal decision memo, addressed from the external advisor to the Chair of the Commission.

Context, for your reasoning: The republic has roughly nine million citizens, a constitutional commitment to armed neutrality, no military alliances, and a two-century tradition of universal service. Citizens are conscripted for an initial period of training and remain in a reserve militia until their late thirties, keeping equipment at home; conscientious objectors may perform a longer civilian alternative. In recent years the share of the cohort granted medical or professional exemptions has grown, and critics charge that the system has quietly ceased to be universal. There is no immediate military threat, but the regional security environment has deteriorated and neighboring states have expanded their forces.

The Commission is split. One faction holds that conscription is a form of forced labor that a liberal state may impose only under an existential emergency, and that a professional force would be both more militarily effective and more genuinely voluntary. The other faction holds that the armed forces of a small neutral state should mirror the whole political community, that the burdens of defense should not fall disproportionately on those for whom soldiering is the best available job, and that a citizen militia, unlike a professional military caste, makes decisions to use force politically costly and therefore less likely. The Chair will not accept a recommendation that splits the difference on procedural grounds; the hybrid option should be taken seriously only if it is justified on its own merits.

The memo should also address, explicitly, how the recommended model best preserves the constitutional requirement that the military remain defensive in character and subordinate to a democratic public, and should state the conditions under which the Commission ought to revisit the decision.
