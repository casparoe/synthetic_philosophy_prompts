I am a PhD student working on Peirce's theory of inquiry, and I have been trying to think through how his framework would handle systems he never could have anticipated. Peirce held that inquiry aims at the settlement of opinion, distinguished four ways of fixing belief—tenacity, authority, the a priori method, and the method of science—and took the method of science to be the only one that lets experience correct our beliefs. He also understood truth as the opinion that would be reached by an unlimited community of inquirers if inquiry were carried far enough, and he insisted that genuine inquiry must never block the road to further inquiry. Suppose we now consider a community whose scientific consensus is largely mediated by large language models: predictions, literature summaries, and even candidate hypotheses are produced by systems that have no first-person experience and no way of being surprised by the consequences of their own commitments. From the internal logic of Peirce's position, how would he classify the role of such systems within inquiry? Would reliance on them resemble one of the four methods of fixing belief, could it constitute a new method, or would Peirce deny that they are inquirers at all? Where, if anywhere, would his framework have to be extended to accommodate them? Please reason from the details of his view rather than by generic analogy.
