I have been working out a view of personal identity that I would like you to test against a hard case. On this view, a self is neither a soul nor a body; it is a pattern — a characteristic weave of perceptual habits, values, turns of phrase, emotional reflexes, and memories. Such a pattern can be copied, in coarser or finer grain, into another person's mind through shared experience, imitation, or study. So the question whether someone continues to exist after death is not an all-or-nothing matter; it is the question of where, and at what resolution, that person's pattern still does work.

I propose four criteria for saying that a person L's self exists in another person K:

1. Recognizability. K carries enough of L's specific dispositions — ways of noticing, valuing, framing, and responding — that the pattern in K could be identified by someone who knew L well as L's pattern, not merely as a generic influence.

2. Descent. The pattern was caused by L through some channel of transmission, such as conversation, teaching, or imitation, rather than developed independently by K.

3. Activity. The pattern is not an inert archive; it functions inside K's mind, criticizing, redirecting, overriding, or stabilizing K's thoughts, choices, or emotional responses.

4. Presence. K experiences this material as L's — as an internalized other with its own voice and weight — rather than as an ordinary part of K's own thinking, even though K knows that the voice is internal.

Now the case. K studied musical composition with L for fifteen years, from adolescence until L's death. L was a severe and precise teacher, and K learned to anticipate her objections phrase by phrase. But K knew L almost entirely within the studio: he has few memories of her outside it, knows little of her friendships or domestic habits, and never watched her compose. After L dies, K continues to compose. When he is about to take an easy path, he often hears L's voice, in her exact manner, saying, "You haven't earned that." He argues with it, sometimes gives in, and is unsettled by how often giving in improves the piece. Once, when a student of his made a familiar mistake, K laughed because he caught himself about to explain the fault in L's old idiom. He does not believe that L is a ghost. He says: she is in my head, but only the part of her that was my teacher.

Work through my four criteria honestly. Say where K's case satisfies each, where it satisfies each only along a narrow dimension, and where it fails. For the vague or partial verdicts, say whether a partial satisfaction should count at all, and why. Then answer two further questions. First, is it more accurate to say that L's self exists in K, that an aspect of L's self exists in K, or that K merely remembers and imitates L? Give the strongest argument for and against the first answer before you settle. Second, does this case expose a problem in my criteria — for example, that the fourth criterion demands too much, or that the first criterion needs a threshold I have not supplied?
