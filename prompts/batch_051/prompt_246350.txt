I want to run Tolstoy's theory of art from What Is Art? against a concrete case, and the one I can't get out of my head is the 1913 Paris premiere of The Rite of Spring. The story is common knowledge: Stravinsky's score, Nijinsky's choreography, and an audience that supposedly exploded into a riot. As I understand Tolstoy, real art is a kind of infection—the artist has a feeling, the work transmits it, and the audience catches it—and then he adds two more tests: the feeling has to be sincere, and it has to be morally serious and accessible to ordinary people, not just refined pleasure for a bored elite. So run the Rite through that. Would Tolstoy have to count the riot as successful infection, since a whole theater full of people got strongly worked up? Or would he treat the evening as a perfect example of the corrupt, artificial art he thought the modern West was producing? Does it expose the infection test as too broad to separate art from a punch in the face, or do the sincerity and accessibility tests save it?
