Im taking a course on logic and epistemology this term and we just did Fitch's paradox of knowability, and its honestly been bugging me all week. The seminar handout laid out two claims side by side that I'd always assumed just went together. The first is the anti-realist, verificationist-flavored one: truth and knowability can't come apart, so if a proposition is true, it must be possible for someone at some time to know it. The second is the Moorean-sounding observation that there are clearly truths nobody will ever know, like how many hairs were on Napoleon's head at Waterloo, or what the last uncontacted person on earth ate for breakfast this morning. On their own they feel totally compatible: a truth can be knowable in principle without ever actually being known. But the derivation we worked through seems to show they can't both be true. Take a truth p that nobody ever knows; by the principle, the conjunction p-and-nobody-knows-p would have to be knowable. But knowing that conjunction means knowing both p and that nobody knows p, and knowing the second conjunct is impossible if knowledge is factive: if you know that nobody knows p, then nobody knows p, yet you also know p, so somebody knows p. Contradiction. So the supposedly knowable unknown truth turns out to be unknowable, and the only consistent options look like denying that any truths go unknown forever, giving up the principle, or messing with the underlying logic.

I keep going back and forth on whether there's a real reconciliation here or whether every fix is just special pleading wearing a formal costume. The moves people in my study group threw out were: restricting the principle to some properly-behaved class of propositions, going intuitionistic so the derivation doesn't land the same way, or just biting the bullet that all truths are in fact known (which feels absurd for Napoleon's hair). I'd love a point-by-point pass: lay out the main candidate reconciliations, say what each one costs and which cost is the least embarrassing, and give me an honest verdict on whether the two original claims can survive together in any form. Lead with your bottom line in a sentence or two, then the candidates. Keep it under 400 words — I need to reread it before Thursdays tutorial.
