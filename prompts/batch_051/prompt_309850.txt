I write about science and ideas, and lately I've been going around in circles on Good's theorem — the old argument that a rational agent should never turn down free information, because at worst you can just ignore it, so the expected value of information is never negative. The proof's usual setup has you receiving a signal from something like a neutral source: a test result, a market price, an image from a satellite. But nearly all the information that actually reaches me now comes through engagement-optimized feeds, where a recommender has already picked what I see, in what order, and with what framing and thumbnails. What I cant work out is whether Good's theorem survives that change. Is the information still free in the sense the proof needs, or has the recommender made it not-free without charging me money? Does the theorem just keep working because the feed showing me this is itself an observation I can condition on? Or does it need a repair that says something weaker, and if so which premise is the one that actually snaps? I'm interested in what happens to the argument, not in the platform-regulation debate.
