I keep noticing a structural parallel that I cannot decide is deep or superficial. In algorithm design, for many NP-hard optimization problems we give up on exact optimality and settle for an approximation algorithm: the optimum is computationally out of reach, so we replace it with a weaker, checkable guarantee—a bound on the ratio between our solution's value and the optimum's. Herbert Simon's satisficing under bounded rationality looks to me like the same replacement on the practical side: maximizing utility is out of reach for a bounded agent, so the agent adopts an aspiration level, a threshold testable option by option, and takes the first option that passes. But there is an asymmetry that bothers me. Approximation algorithms carry performance guarantees, while satisficing carries nothing analogous to a bound on how much utility the stopping rule leaves on the table. Is this a deep identity of structure—in which case we should expect or demand something like an approximation ratio for satisficing—or does the analogy break somewhere essential, for instance because an aspiration level is not a solution concept in the same sense? Spell out exactly where the mapping holds and where it comes apart.
