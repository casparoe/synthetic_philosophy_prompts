I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm editing a feature on what formal results about rationality do and don't show about real disagreement. Two researchers I've been reporting on—one who thinks Aumann's agreement theorem is a deep constraint on rational agents, the other who thinks it's a formal curiosity with almost no real-world bite—have agreed to produce an adversarial joint statement instead of the usual point-counterpoint. Draft the statement they could both sign. Begin with a numbered agreed-claims section that states the theorem precisely, lists its assumptions, identifies which assumptions are doing the work, and records what both sides think follows about actual disagreement, if anything; make sure it distinguishes common knowledge of each other's posterior probabilities from merely exchanging announced estimates, and common priors from shared broad worldviews. Then provide a disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is stated in wording each side would accept for its own view. Then name the cruxes—the sub-questions whose answers would move at least one side—and for each crux give a concrete scenario, fictional or real, that both sides accept as a fair test, with each side's prediction stated in advance. Where a disagreement is merely verbal, say so and offer the neutral restatement. End with \answer{...} containing a one-sentence summary of the deepest non-verbal crux.
