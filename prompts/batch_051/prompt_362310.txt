Below is a draft essay for a general philosophical audience. Please give me detailed comments on it.

The essay asks whether prosperity has moral worth beyond what it buys, and it defends a qualified "yes."

---

**Abundance and the Worth of Standing Reserves**

It is common to treat prosperity as morally important only derivatively. On this view, a society's wealth matters because it buys health, education, leisure, and relief from the struggle for subsistence; if we could secure those same goods with fewer resources, the remaining abundance would have no further moral claim on us. This essay resists that conclusion by distinguishing two faces of abundance.

The first face is material plenty: the availability of goods and services that answer to identifiable needs and wants. Its value is mostly instrumental. A second car, a faster delivery service, or a larger house is valuable chiefly because of the specific benefits it provides.

The second face is slack: the presence of unused reserves, such as spare time, spare capacity, spare credit, extra attention, and room to fail without ruin. A society can be materially rich while running with almost no slack, as when every person and institution operates at the edge of its capacity, absorbing shocks only by passing them on to someone else.

The essay's central claim is that slack has moral worth beyond the particular goods it buys, for three reasons. First, slack lowers the cost of mercy. When a landlord, employer, or enforcement system has reserves, leniency need not threaten its own survival or the survival of those who depend on it. Second, slack changes which mistakes are recoverable, and therefore changes what people can learn and whom they can trust. Third, slack underwrites patience in political and interpersonal judgment, since haste is so often a product of scarcity.

The hard question is whether these three benefits are still just instrumental goods, thinly spread. I want to argue they are not, because slack is not consumed by the benefits it makes possible. It is a standing condition whose value lies partly in being available, not only in being used. A community that keeps hidden margins may be better even when no one ever draws on them, much as a witness who would believe you can be better even if you never need to call on her. The worth of slack, like the worth of that standing trustworthiness, is partly constitutive of a certain kind of relation rather than merely productive of it.

A critic might reply that this is an aesthetic preference for robust societies dressed up as a moral argument. The most serious version of this objection is that standing conditions can always be redescribed as portfolios of possible future goods, so their value remains purely instrumental after all. The essay's final move is to accept the redescription but deny its force: value can be both instrumental and non-fungibly tied to the way a good is held. A reserve that everyone knows exists but no one can see exerts a different kind of pressure on social life than a list of future payouts does.

---

Please comment specifically on the following:

1. Does the distinction between material plenty and slack hold up under closer inspection, or does slack collapse into one more good that prosperity buys?
2. What is the strongest objection to the claim that slack's value is partly constitutive rather than purely instrumental, and does the final paragraph adequately answer it?
3. Identify the least persuasive move in the argument and suggest how it could be strengthened without appealing to empirical or historical studies.
