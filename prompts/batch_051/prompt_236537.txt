For a reading group I run, I want to settle what the word nihilism actually covers, because our arguments keep slipping between positions that seem to have little in common. I am asking for a working definition, not a survey of authors. The hard cases we have been fighting over are these:

(1) A moral error theorist who denies that any moral proposition is true, but who is a strict vegan and volunteers every weekend in disaster relief while insisting these are just strong preferences with no authority over anyone. (2) A mereological nihilist who denies that tables, chairs, and persons exist in any fundamental way, but who is a moral realist and says her children have objective moral claims on her. (3) Someone who holds that human life has no cosmic purpose or plot and finds this exhilarating rather than crushing, and who is full of local projects and attachments. (4) A person in a depressive episode who reports, and apparently believes, that nothing matters, but who still flinches at an oncoming car, keeps appointments, and feels humiliated by a public failure. (5) A hard determinist who denies free will and moral responsibility, yet still resents a colleague who plagiarized his work. (6) A political radical who says all existing values are corrupt and should be torn down, but only because he is convinced that new and better values can be built in their place.

My own intuitions are not stable: I would call (1) a moral nihilist, I would not call (2) a nihilist in any ordinary sense despite the label, and I cannot decide whether (4) and (5) are nihilists or just people with inconsistent outlooks. I also want the definition to speak to whether nihilism is livable — that is, whether a person can sincerely hold the deepest form of the doctrine and still act as though some things are worth doing, and whether the label should track the doctrine, the person's affective state, or their actual conduct.

Propose a definition of nihilism, or, if you conclude the word does more than one job, a small set of distinguished senses. Run the definition through all six cases, say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and then revise it or defend the surprising verdicts. The deliverable is the definition itself, with the cases used to test it. Please answer in American English.
