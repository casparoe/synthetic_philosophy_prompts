I'm an editor at a mid-tier mathematics journal, and I want a standing referee procedure for submissions whose main theorem depends on a computation that no single human can survey by hand. These submissions now arrive roughly monthly, and each one is high-stakes: an acceptance we can't later stand behind damages the journal's reputation permanently, and a rejection drives good and possibly important work to a competitor. The procedure will be run by editors who are working mathematicians but not specialists in proof assistants, automated reasoning, or formal verification, and by volunteer referees who cannot be assumed to have those skills either; we have about three weeks per decision, and every acceptance has to be signed off by the editorial board, which is more conservative than I am. Referees will not re-run large computations or audit thousands of lines of code in full, and the procedure must not pretend they will.

Please give me the procedure as an ordered workflow: the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the process terminates in reject, revise-and-resubmit, or accept-with-conditions, and — explicitly — where the procedure stops being able to settle the matter and hands the decision to a human because the question has become one of values rather than verification. I want it grounded in what the philosophy of mathematical proof actually has to say about surveyability, authority, delegation of checking, and the difference between certifying that a theorem is true and giving a proof that produces understanding — including what the debates over the four-colour theorem and the Kepler conjecture established about unsurveyable proofs — but translated into operational rules my editors and referees can apply without reading the philosophy.

Then run the procedure on three hypothetical submissions: (a) a theorem reduced to 40,000 finite cases checked by a single bespoke C program, with source deposited and an independent implementation in a different programming language confirming the result; (b) the same style of case-check performed by a proprietary, closed-source solver whose output is summarized in a twelve-page appendix; (c) a proof fully formalized in an interactive theorem prover, so the final theorem carries a mechanically checkable certificate, though no referee has actually read the formalization. Include at least one worked case where the procedure tells me to reject or impose conditions that my board and I would find genuinely unwelcome despite the result's likely truth and importance, and say plainly what the procedure cannot do — for example, what it cannot restore or guarantee no matter how carefully it is followed.
