I have recently found myself in a new situation: I keep a vegetable garden, and most mornings I now make small life-and-death decisions about aphids, cabbage loopers, and squash beetles. The experience has left me pulled in two directions at once, and I would like to be shown what each possible resolution costs before I settle.

On one side, I find the scale argument for treating insect welfare as a massive moral consideration close to compelling. Its premises seem individually right: what gives a being a claim on me is sentience rather than size or species; when I am unsure whether a being is sentient, I should weigh that possibility by its probability rather than rounding it to zero; and when an act affects an enormous number of beings, my assessment of it should somewhere register that number. But put those premises together and routine acts — walking across a lawn, driving at dusk, spraying a row of beans, perhaps tending a garden at all — begin to look like they carry expected harms that rival or exceed the direct harms I would never dream of inflicting on a bird or a mammal. The numbers are simply that large, and the probability of insect sentience need only be nonzero rather than negligible for the arithmetic to become enormous.

On the other side, when I actually crush a cabbage looper between my fingers, I cannot bring myself to believe I have just done something of the moral weight the theory assigns to it, let alone that my garden, taken as a whole, is a moral catastrophe. The intuition is not that insects are certainly unconscious. It is that a vast number of quite small probabilities of a fairly low-grade harm, spread over very simple lives, should not be allowed to swamp everything else in my moral life. And I distrust the thought that the correct lesson is simply that I have been radically wrong all along and must now reorganize my life around avoiding insect deaths. That feels less like moral clarity than like a failure of the decision procedure I was otherwise inclined to trust.

I do not want to be told which side wins. I want to know, with the strongest versions of both on the table, what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes — what would have to be true about its likely sources for me to discount it as scope insensitivity or motivated reasoning — and what it would take for the theory to be amended rather than abandoned: a risk-weighted treatment of tiny probabilities, a threshold of sentience-relevant complexity, a principled limit on aggregation across barely conscious lives, a shift from expected value to something more ambiguity-averse, or a refusal to trade across the enormous differences in the kinds of good at stake. Finally, I want a candid verdict on whether there is a reading of the theory on which both survive — for example, one on which it gives me a standing reason to make cheap reductions in insect suffering without licensing the conclusion that my garden is a moral disaster.
