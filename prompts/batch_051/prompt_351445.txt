I'm building a little world for a strategy game some friends and I have been tinkering on, and I've gotten stuck on what should have been a flavor decision: the colonists need a calendar, which means they need to pick the year they're living in — their year zero (or year one; I can't even settle that, but it's the smaller problem). It turns out I have strong, slightly incoherent feelings about this, and the arguments with my friend have made me realize I can't state the priniciple I'm actually relying on.

Here are the judgments I keep defending. Using the founding of a particular nation or religion as year one feels wrong for a pluralist colony; it would quietly make one group's story the official timekeeping. Using the arrival year also feels wrong, even though it's clean: it's too local and too accidental — if the ship had been delayed a decade, every date in the colony's records would just slide. Picking the Big Bang feels like the least arbitrary choice, and I keep being drawn to it, but it makes ordinary years enormous and pretends to a precision we don't have; I'm not even sure "the Big Bang as a date" is a real date rather than a placeholder. Something like the start of the Holocene — the "human era," roughly — gives me the nice property that all of recorded history is positive and small, but I worry I'm just picking the period I happen to like and calling it natural. The Unix epoch feels absurd for a generation ship, yet I can't say exactly why it's worse than the Holocene: one is recent and arbitrary, the other is vague and ancient. A rolling epoch tied to the current constitution or ruler feels the most political and the least stable, but it's also the most honest: it admits the calendar is a living convention rather than a discovery.

The half-formed principle I've been using is: an epoch should be as neutral as possible, as stable as possible under new discoveries, and should make the dates a civilization actually uses come out small and positive — but those three are in tension, and I suspect something has to give. I want you to either sharpen that principle or replace it. Which of my judgments does the best principle have to overturn? What should the colonists do, in your view, and is there a case for running two calendars with different jobs? Test whatever you propose on a case I keep going back and forth on: fixing year zero to the origin of self-replicating life, which is cosmic-ish but deeply uncertain, versus fixing it to the heat death of the universe and counting backward, so that all of human history would have negative years. Please also tell me what would change your mind about your answer: what argument or case would lead you to a different principle, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail. End with a line of the form \answer{...} containing a one-sentence version of the principle you'd defend, or the epoch you'd pick if you think the principle yields one.
