For years I have taught a course on probability and the philosophy of science in which the deflationary account of coincidence has been one of my set pieces. The mathematics is decisive as far as it goes: with enough opportunities, events that would be wildly improbable on any single occasion become antecedently likely in the aggregate, and the birthday result is the cleanest exhibit — with only twenty-three people, a shared birthday is already more probable than not. To this I add the familiar selection effects: we remember the old friend who calls just as we were thinking of her and forget the hundreds of times no call came; the denominator against which we compute the odds is retrospective and unprincipled; and a pattern-hungry mind supplies the appearance of meaning. On that basis I have long maintained that an anecdote is not evidence for hidden connections but evidence about the psychology of the person who finds the anecdote remarkable.

And yet there is one event in my own past from which this theory cannot extract me. As a graduate student I arrived in a large foreign city having arranged nothing, and within an hour walked into a café to find, at the next table, the single person from my undergraduate years with whom I had lost all contact — and whom I had spent the previous week trying, unsuccessfully, to locate. The deflationary account tells me that in a life of thousands of arrivals, searches, and encounters, some such collision is near-guaranteed, and that the preceding search is exactly the kind of salience that manufactures the illusion of design. I cannot make myself believe it. The specificity of the event — the failed search, the first hour in a new country, the one person so sought — has an evidential force that the statistical story seems to explain around rather than explain away. I notice the same thing when students, colleagues, or family members bring me their own coincidences: my theory tells me to discount the anecdote, while my judgment refuses to treat it as nothing but a selection artifact.

I do not want a verdict that simply chooses sides; I want a clear statement of what each option costs. The strongest form of the deflationary view will say that the felt evidential force of a coincidence is itself one of the artifacts the theory predicts. The strongest form of the intuition will say that at least some anecdotes carry weight that no generic reference-class argument specifies away, and that they can be evidence even if they are frequently misread. So: what would it take, in principle, for the intuition to be the thing that goes, and what would it take for the theory to be the thing that goes? Is there a negotiated reading on which both sides survive — for example, one that distinguishes the availability of a chance explanation from the absence of evidential value, or one that requires a debunker to specify the reference class against which the event is supposed to be unsurprising rather than merely gesturing at one? I would like a final answer to say what an anecdote could be evidence for, under what conditions, and how one tells the difference between an anecdote that functions as a data point and an anecdote that functions as a symptom of miscalibrated surprise.
