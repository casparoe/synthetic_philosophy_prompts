im in a course on self-sufficiency and autarky this term—we've done the Stoic version where autarky is an ethical ideal, then some pretty skeptical treatments of national autarky, and now I'm stuck on whether the ideal even makes sense once you scale it down to a face-to-face group. My co-op housemates (25 of us next year) are semi-seriously designing rules for the house, and I'd rather get a walkthrough of how the thing would actually behave than the usual vibe-level take.

Here's the proposal. (1) All shared dinners have to be cooked from inputs grown in the house garden, made by members, or bartered within the house; anything else requires spending leakage points. (2) Everyone gets 20 leakage points a month. Outside purchases for personal use—takeout, a new pan, detergent, whatever—cost points roughly proportional to price, and points are tradable between members. (3) Each month the house calculates what it saved against the university meal-plan baseline, and pays out that "autarky dividend" in proportion to logged production hours, but each log needs another member to vouch for it and can be challenged at house meetings. (4) A point deficit has to be worked off with verifiable production or paid into the common fund.

What I want to know: what does this do to who cooks, who gardens, who just pays to check out, and who ends up running a little points arbitrage business? Where does it get gamed first, and what's the failure mode that actually kills it—domestic labor accounting, fake logging, resentment, complexity? Is there an adjusted version that preserves some genuine self-sufficiency without becoming a grim little planned economy or bookkeeping hell? And please separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who know this literature, rather than presenting your own read of autarky as the consensus.
