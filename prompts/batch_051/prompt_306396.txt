I'm TAing an undergrad decision theory class and I've got one problem-set answer I keep going back and forth on. I don't want to tip my hand about what I think, since I'm trying to grade it fairly and I'd like an independent read. Here's the homework question:

Newcomb's problem. A transparent box contains $1,000. An opaque box contains either $0 or $1,000,000, depending on what a predictor predicted before you choose. The predictor is 90% reliable: if she predicted that you would take only the opaque box, she put $1,000,000 in it, and if she predicted that you would take both boxes, she put $0 in it. You know all of this, and you choose after the prediction has been made. (a) State the dominance argument for taking both boxes. (b) State the evidential expected-utility argument for taking only the opaque box. (c) Explain where causal decision theory (CDT) and evidential decision theory (EDT) part ways in this case, and argue for which one gives the right answer.

And here's what one student wrote in response:

Dominance: whatever the predictor predicted, taking both boxes makes you $1,000 richer than taking only the opaque box. If she predicted one-boxing, the opaque box has the million, so both boxes gives $1,001,000 and one box gives $1,000,000. If she predicted two-boxing, the opaque box is empty, so both gives $1,000 and one box gives $0. So taking both boxes strictly dominates.

The evidential reasoning goes the other way. Since the predictor is 90% accurate, if I take only the opaque box, the chance she predicted one-boxing is 0.9, so the expected value is 0.9(1,000,000) + 0.1(0) = 900,000. If I take both boxes, the chance she predicted two-boxing is 0.9, but there's a 0.1 chance she predicted one-boxing anyway; then the expected value is 0.9(1,000) + 0.1(1,001,000) = 101,000. So one-boxing has much higher expected value.

CDT and EDT split over how to handle that correlation. EDT says to evaluate acts by the probabilities of the states conditional on the acts. One-boxing is strong evidence that the million is there, so EDT takes one box. CDT says only what your act causally affects matters for its expected value. Since your act comes after the prediction and can't cause the million to be in the box, the probability of the million should be treated as the same either way, and then the dominance argument takes over. So CDT takes both boxes.

I'm on team CDT. EDT's mistake is that it reasons as if taking one box could reach back and make the predictor have predicted one-boxing, but the prediction already happened. What one-boxing does is merely indicate that the million is probably there; it doesn't bring the million about. Once the states are causally independent of your act, dominance is the only valid principle, so the right answer is to take both boxes.
