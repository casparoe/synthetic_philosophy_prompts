I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and this week I'm sitting on a decision that doesn't reduce to ordinary fact-checking. An anonymous source sent me a video that appears to show a public official, in what looks like a private exchange, admitting to something career-ending. The file arrived without metadata. I asked two forensic analysts I've used before to examine it: one says the artifacts are most consistent with a sophisticated deepfake, the other says they are more consistent with genuine footage, and both told me, independently, that within a few years the best synthetic video will often be indistinguishable from real recordings. My editor wants to publish the clip with explicit caveats about where it came from. His argument is that our job is to report what we have, make our uncertainty transparent, and let readers judge. My instinct pulls the other way, and it troubles me that I can't articulate it cleanly. I keep thinking that if we publish, we will have done something larger than relay one possibly-fabricated clip: we will have demonstrated to our audience that a leaked recording can no longer settle a factual dispute, and we may thereby spend down a shared epistemic resource that the public depends on. But I can't tell whether that thought is a genuine philosophical objection or a high-minded way of dressing up caution.

Walk me through the strongest version of each position, and then tell me what you would advise. What is a recording supposed to provide, epistemically, that a verbal report does not, and what exactly is lost when recordings can no longer be presumed authentic? Is it legitimate for a publication to take into account the effect of its coverage on public standards of evidence, or does that slide into treating readers as less than competent judges? I'd like the answer to be usable by someone deciding a particular editorial call, not a survey of media ethics. And please separate what is settled among people who have thought carefully about this from what remains genuinely contested, and avoid presenting a contested view as the consensus.
