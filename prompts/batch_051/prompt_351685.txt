For the past several months my partner and I have been preparing for our wedding with both a rabbi and a priest, and the conversations keep pulling me toward an old distinction I was taught to use when sorting ways of thinking about the divine. A conception is transcendent insofar as: first, the divine exists of itself, so that its reality does not depend on there being any world; second, the divine is other than the world, neither a part of it, nor made of its materials, nor merely one of its features or processes; and third, the divine stands to the world as an external cause or ruler, acting on it from outside the world's own causal order. A conception is immanent insofar as: first, the divine cannot be without the world, or at least cannot be without some world; second, the divine is not other than the world but present in it as its own depth, structure, or life; and third, the divine acts only in and through the world's ordinary processes, never by intervention from outside.

A friend has been describing a view that seems designed to frustrate these tests. She is an ecologist, and she thinks of God as the emergent whole of reality, the way an ecosystem is neither a separate thing standing behind the organisms that compose it nor merely the list of organisms. God, on her account, is not one item in the inventory of the world and not a supernatural being attached to the world's edge. The world depends on God for its existence, because parts are what they are only within the whole, and the whole exerts a downward influence on its parts; yet the whole also requires some parts or other, so God could exist without this particular world though never without any world. She denies that God occupies a place, and she says the world is in God rather than God in the world, while denying that this makes God spatially or causally outside the world. When I asked how such a God could act, she said that what people call miracles are not intrusions into nature but reorganizations of the whole that the parts could not have produced or predicted on their own. She is emphatic that God is not identical with the sum of finite things: a sum, she says, is no more a whole than a heap is, and only the whole has the unity that the sum lacks.

Work through the three tests one by one and say, for each, whether the view lands on the side of transcendence or immanence, or whether the test fails to determine an answer. Then tell me what I should conclude: that her view is an immanent conception with transcendent-sounding decoration, that my inherited tests break down at this case, or that the distinction itself is tracking something other than the spatial language of inside and outside.
