I'm a philosophy of religion PhD student, and I've been trying to turn a vague worry I have about the two-natures doctrine into an actual argument, because I suspect the vagueness has been doing a lot of work for me. The doctrine I mean is the standard conciliar one: Christ is one person with two complete natures, divine and human, united without being mixed together or pulled apart, with each nature keeping what belongs to it. Here's the argument I've ended up with.

(1) Christ is one person, a single subject of predication.
(2) That one person has two complete natures, one divine and one human.
(3) Having the divine nature entails certain properties: being uncreated, being impassible, being omniscient, and the like.
(4) Having the human nature entails certain properties: being created, being passible, having a limited or developing mind.
(5) Some of the properties in (3) and (4) are not merely different; they would be contradictory if predicated of one subject at one time in the same respect.
(6) So if the doctrine is to avoid contradiction, every problematic predication has to be read as qualified by a nature: Christ is impassible qua divine and passible qua human, but neither, it seems, simpliciter.
(7) But if nothing is ever true of Christ simpliciter except 'has two natures', then the one person has dropped out: the doctrine becomes a story about two natures sharing a label rather than one subject.

So the doctrine seems caught between contradiction and vacuity. Is this a valid argument? If it is, say which premise I should give up and what the strongest defensible version of the doctrine is. If it isn't, tell me exactly which step fails and why, and whether a repaired version gets the same conclusion. And could you seperate the parts of your answer that are just you choosing a philosophical account of what a nature is from the parts that are fixed by the conciliar definitions, so I don't end up presenting one contested reading as the consensus in theology.
