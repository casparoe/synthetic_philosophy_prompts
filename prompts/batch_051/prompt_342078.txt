An upper-middle-income country with low tax compliance and widespread distrust of government announces an attempt to import the Nordic model by manufacturing trust through what it calls a "Solidarity Ledger." Every adult receives an annual itemized statement showing what their personal taxes bought in the previous year, resolved down to named local facilities and services: the specific clinic, school, road segment, nursing-home places, and debt-service share funded by their payments. The state also publishes a publicly searchable good-standing register, updated quarterly, recording whether each citizen is up to date on taxes, and access to a range of nonessential public goods—public-library cards, municipal building permits, subsidized adult education, priority scheduling for elective medical care—is made conditional on appearing in the register. Finally, each taxpayer may direct one percentage point of their income tax to any registered communal good, with their name displayed on or beside the funded object. Treat the scheme as a mechanism rather than as a reform to be endorsed or condemned: what does it incentivize among taxpayers, employers, local officials, and the poor; how would it be gamed or captured; what does it do to the social meaning of taxpaying and to horizontal trust; and which failure modes, if any, could be fixed without abandoning the underlying idea?
