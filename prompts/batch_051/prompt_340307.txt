I've been reading the preface to Fernand Braudel's The Mediterranean and the Mediterranean World in the Age of Philip II, and something keeps bugging me. Braudel says, roughly, that historians have the hierarchy backwards: the events we obsess over are just the surface froth, the tiny crests on a wave, while the real explanatory work is done by slow-moving structures — geography, climate, sea routes, harvest cycles, the long rhythms of trade — that carry those events along. I'm paraphrasing, but the image in the preface is of events as surface disturbances riding on the strong backs of longer historical tides.

Now here's the asymmetry I can't shake. When we explain the past, we mostly do it through events and individuals: Sarajevo starts the war, the Emancipation Proclamation ends slavery, Napoleon's mistakes decide a campaign. When something dramatic goes wrong in the present — a famine, a migration crisis, a financial collapse — the newsroom reflex is to find the decision, the person, the summit, the vote. But when a disaster is slow and structural, we call it "context" or "underlying causes," and people's attention glazes over. Same split in moral judgment: we hold persons responsible for quick harms, but a trade policy that slowly hollows out a region over thirty years is treated as nobody's fault in particular, just the way things go.

My question: is there any principled justification for that asymmetry, or is its just a cognitive bias for the fast and the visible? If Braudel is even half right, we should maybe flip our judgments — treat the slow structures as the real agent-like causes and the events as symptoms. But flipping has strange consequences for moral and legal judgment: we'd have to start blaming monsoons and harvest cycles, not generals and ministers. I want the strongest case you can make for keeping the ordinary asymmetry, then the strongest case for flipping it, and a clear verdict on which side you'd defend. If you think the right answer is "neither, the dichotomy itself is bad," tell me what the third position looks like.
