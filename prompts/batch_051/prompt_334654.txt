As a doctoral student in philosophical logic, I have developed a settled way of responding to requests for the justification of basic inference rules, and I want to know whether it is defensible or a sophisticated way of protecting my own presuppositions. My policy is this: when someone asks what justifies modus ponens, or conjunction elimination, or universal instantiation, I do not attempt to give a justificatory argument. I instead diagnose the request. Following Carroll’s fable of Achilles and the Tortoise, I point out that any argument for a basic rule must either assume that rule at some step, or assume something at least as strong, and that a merely discursive explanation of the rule does not bind an interlocutor who refuses it. From this I conclude that the request is not a demand for a missing piece of information but a category confusion: inference cannot be validated from outside inference. I treat the only honest remaining task as descriptive—saying what one is committed to in inferring—rather than normative.

The policy has become more than a classroom stance. I have caught myself applying it to local and comparative questions as well: when a colleague asks why I favour a natural deduction formulation over a sequent calculus formulation of the same logic, I tend to reply that there is no fact of the matter about which formulation is justified, because the two formulations articulate the same inferential commitment at different resolutions. A fellow student recently objected that my policy is a rule for refusing questions, and that the Tortoise’s regress shows only that a certain kind of foundationalist justification is unavailable, not that no genuine normative question remains.

I would like an analysis of whether this policy is defensible, and where it should stop. More specifically: under what conditions does the Carroll diagnosis genuinely dissolve a pseudo-question, and under what conditions does it overgeneralise? I notice in myself an asymmetry I cannot justify: I accept the diagnosis readily when it protects modus ponens or conjunction elimination, but I resist it when a paraconsistent logician uses the same move to fend off a challenge to disjunctive syllogism, or when an intuitionist brushes aside a request to justify the law of excluded middle. Is there a principled distinction between those uses? Could a restated version of the policy keep the therapeutic benefits without insulating logic from legitimate criticism, or should I abandon the policy altogether and accept that I am simply choosing to leave certain commitments unexamined?
