I am a graduate teaching assistant for an upper-level undergraduate metaphysics course, and I have been asked to lead a seventy-five-minute seminar on Bradley's regress and the unity of the proposition. My students can translate simple predications into first-order logic and are comfortable treating properties, relations, and propositions as theoretical posits, but they have not read Bradley, Frege, or Armstrong, and I will not assign any background reading beforehand. I understand the material myself; what I need from you is a plan for how to teach it, not an explanation of the material to me, and not a list of readings or citations.

The plan should cover the following. First, the order in which the components have to be introduced, and what depends on what: for instance, how early I need to separate a relation as a constituent of a complex from that relation's actually holding, and at what point I can introduce the thought that instantiation might not itself be another relation. Second, the one carrying example that should do most of the work: what it has to have, what it has to lack, and whether the obvious 'Socrates is wise' should be avoided and why. Third, the misconceptions the students can be expected to bring or form — I suspect at least these: that the regress is just a harmless infinite series, that the right response is to posit instantiation as one more relation, that the whole problem is an artifact of the grammar of the copula, and that any answer which stops the regress with a primitive is simply relabeling rather than a real solution — and the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. Fourth, one final question or exercise that would tell me whether they have understood why the unity problem is a genuine problem rather than merely learned to recognize the word regress. Fifth, what I should openly decline to settle at this level — for example, whether the correct endpoint is a Bradley-style monism, a Frege-style unsaturatedness, or treating facts as unanalyzable primitives — and how to say so without making the session feel unfinished.
