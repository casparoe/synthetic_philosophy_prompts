I work in communications for a policy institute. Recently I had to produce a short public statement on a policy issue in a matter of hours, so I asked a large language model to draft it, made a few light stylistic edits, and published the result under my own name. The text was fluent and superficially polished, but it was assembled almost entirely from stock phrasing: “a perfect storm of challenges,” “in today’s fast-paced landscape,” “cutting-edge approaches,” “at the end of the day,” “going forward.” I felt uneasy, and I was reminded of Orwell’s rules in “Politics and the English Language” (1946), the first of which runs: “Never use a metaphor, simile, or other figure of speech which you are used to seeing in print.”

That rule was written for a human author who selects words and can be held responsible for choosing them. A language model is not an author in that sense: it predicts probable token sequences from a corpus, so it is, if anything, optimized to emit exactly the figures of speech that are constantly seen in print. I want you to help me think through how this rule applies to a case Orwell could not have foreseen. Did I “use” those stock phrases in the sense the rule targets when I signed and published the model’s draft? Did the model violate the rule? Or does the rule fall silent when no individual mind selected the words? More deeply, does Orwell’s underlying worry—that ready-made language is both a symptom and a cause of shallow, evasive thought—still have force when the text is produced by a system that has no thoughts at all, and what would it mean to obey the spirit of the rule while using such tools?

Act as a philosopher of language with expertise in Orwell’s views on language and political thought and in the interpretation of prescriptive rules. Work through at least three approaches: a literal reading of the wording, a reading centered on Orwell’s stated purpose, and a “what would Orwell have said if asked” or reasonable-reader approach. Then give your most defensible answer about what the rule requires of someone in my position, and address the strongest objection to that answer.
