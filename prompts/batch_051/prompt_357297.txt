I am a high school student, and I have recently been reading about Alfred North Whitehead's philosophy of education. The idea that has stayed with me is his claim that learning has a natural rhythm that passes through three recurring stages. As I understand them, romance is the stage where a subject first seizes your interest and you encounter it as a whole, exploring freely without yet worrying too much about exactness. Precision is the stage of disciplined mastery of details, rules, and technique. Generalization is the stage where the mastered details become almost automatic, and you can apply them fluently to new problems — a kind of return to the freedom of romance, but now with real skill behind it. Whitehead thought these stages should cycle rather than occur only once, and he warned that precision imposed too early or without romance produces inert ideas: knowledge that is received, stored, and never actually used.

I came to this partly by wondering why some of my classes make me curious while others simply exhaust me, and I now suspect Whitehead's stages might diagnose the difference. I am less interested in a summary of him, though, than in a practical judgment. Suppose I were asked to redesign a year-long high school course so that its time is explicitly divided among romance, precision, and generalization. What fraction of the year should go to each stage? Please give me three fractions that sum to 1, and explain the reasoning behind your allocation. Then, thinking of the ordinary high school classroom: rank the three stages from most to least under-supplied, and say in a sentence or two why each stage lands where it does.
