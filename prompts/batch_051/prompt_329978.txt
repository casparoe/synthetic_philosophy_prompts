I have been reconstructing, for a graduate seminar on Marx and machinery, an argument that seems to me now to prove far too much: I can see that its conclusion is unacceptable, but I cannot locate the step at which the overreach occurs. Reconstructed as a numbered chain, the argument runs:

(1) The value of a commodity is the socially necessary labour-time required to produce it.
(2) Machinery and raw materials — constant capital — transfer at most their own wear to the product; they do not add new value. Only living labour adds new value.
(3) Profit is, in the aggregate, the monetary expression of surplus value, and surplus value is the new value created by living labour in excess of the value of labour-power.
(4) When a firm introduces labour-saving machinery, less living labour is expended per unit of output; therefore less new value, and hence less surplus value, is created per unit.
(5) If every firm in the branch adopts the improved technique, the socially necessary labour-time per unit falls throughout the branch, so the branch's aggregate surplus value shrinks.
(6) Therefore the general adoption of labour-saving machinery lowers the general rate of profit, swells the reserve army of the unemployed, and pushes capitalist accumulation toward crisis.
(7) Since nothing in steps (1)–(6) turns on specifically capitalist property relations, the same reasoning shows that any economy that systematically automates production to make goods more cheaply is cutting off the source of its own surplus and making its technical progress self-defeating.

I would most like to keep (1) and (2), which strike me as Marx's actual commitments, and I cannot accept (7); my conviction is that a machine that cheapens the necessities of life is not, just for that reason, an economic self-inflicted wound. But I want to know exactly which premise or transition must be rejected or qualified, why it looked valid, and what it costs to give it up — including whether the cost is different if one insists on keeping (1) and (2) intact.

Please give the diagnosis in the following structured form. First, a table with one row for each step or transition in the argument, with these columns: (a) Step or transition; (b) Reading under which it is plausible; (c) Diagnosis — what, if anything, goes wrong at that step, or "none"; (d) What rejecting or qualifying it costs, stated in terms of which Marxian commitments or parts of the original argument must be surrendered. After the table, give a short paragraph naming the single least costly correction and saying why that is where the argument should yield.
