I keep chewing on this puzzle about evidence and stubbornness. Suppose I genuinely know something — not just believe it, know it. Then any evidence against it has to be misleading, because the thing is true and the evidence says otherwise. So doesn't that mean I'm entitled to ignore counterevidence? But that sounds exactly like being a crank who can't be corrected by anything.

Then it hit me that this is basically how a cache works. A cache holds a value it trusts, and until its told to refetch, it treats incoming data from the source as noise. All the real engineering is deciding when to invalidate and go back to the source. "I know this, so counterevidence is misleading" looks like a cache with no invalidation policy.

So is there a real structural analogy here or am I just pattern-matching? If it is real, does anything from cache design transfer to how I should update my beliefs — time-to-live, invalidate-on-write, that sort of thing? And where does the mapping come apart? For instance, a cache tracks a source that can change, whereas if I really know a truth maybe nothing ever needs invalidating — but that seems too convenient. I'd also like one thing this analogy would predict about good reasoning that's actually false.
