I'm the lead analyst for a county-level flood-management agency, and I keep running into the same wall. Our office makes maybe six decisions a year about whether to require property-level floodproofing in a new development, recertify a levee, buy out repeatedly flooded houses, or tighten building rules in a particular watershed, and the elected board that votes on our recommendations wants a single number: the probability of a given flood over the life of the project. The honest situation is nearly always that a single number is not supported. The stream gauge record is short, the climate is clearly drifting away from the historical distributions, and the two or three hydrologic models we run plus the outside engineers we sometimes pay all give materially different answers to the same question. I have about two weeks per decision, no in-house statistician, and the board treats any range I hand them as indecision, which usually means they either postpone the decision or pick whichever end of the range fits what they already wanted to do. I have read enough formal epistemology to know that there is a live view according to which your uncertainty, when the evidence underdetermines a unique probability, should be represented by a set of probability functions rather than one function, and that choices under such imprecision are then evaluated by rules such as maximin, interval dominance, and their less conservative relatives, with different rules expressing different attitudes toward the worst case. What I have never seen is that idea turned into something a working office can actually run.

Please write a decision procedure my office could apply to each of these decisions. It should be grounded in the imprecise-probability view rather than in the usual practice of forcing one number, and it should keep the distinction that actually matters, between imprecision in our evidence and mere sloppiness in our modeling, while remaining usable by a small staff with no formal background in decision theory. The procedure should specify the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, how to extract the probability set from disagreeing models and experts without pretending the disagreement away, which decision rule to use when the set remains wide, and what each choice of rule quietly commits us to. It should also say where the procedure stops with a no: where the evidence cannot support a recommendation, where the real issue is a value trade-off that belongs to the elected board rather than to my analysis, and where the pressure to produce a single number is itself the thing the procedure must refuse to satisfy. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure rather than technical results.

Then apply the procedure to three cases: a levee-recertification decision in which our two models give flood probabilities of 0.03 and 0.11 for the critical elevation and an outside engineer adds a range of 0.01 to 0.20; a proposed buyout of forty repeatedly flooded houses where estimates of future inundation run from 0.05 to 0.50 and the main disagreement is about whether the historical record is still informative; and a proposed development moratorium where the board wants one number to justify both the moratorium and its eventual lifting, and where I suspect the correct answer is that no single number can do both jobs. Include at least one case where the procedure gives me an answer I will not like. If you think this whole request rests on a mistake, for example that imprecise probabilities cannot be turned into a two-week procedure without becoming a caricature of themselves, or that my board's demand for a single number is a political problem no technical procedure can solve, say so plainly rather than handing me something that looks rigorous and would mislead my board. Write your answer as continuous prose, the way you would write a long email to a colleague, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
