Here is the test I have been using to decide whether someone is complicit in a wrong rather than merely present for it. I count a person as complicit when all four of the following hold: the wrong is reasonably foreseeable to them; they voluntarily do or publicly say something, rather than only thinking or feeling it; what they do is tied to the wrongful project either by helping it along in some way or by expressing approval of it; and they could decline to do it without serious personal cost. I deliberately left out causation: on this test a person does not need to make the difference between the wrong happening and not happening. To stress-test that omission, imagine a clerk in a records office who learns that her supervisor is quietly issuing false certificates stating that convicted violent offenders have no criminal record, so that the offenders can obtain gun permits. The clerk never touches those certificates; her assigned work is to enter routine change-of-address forms into the same database. Her entries are always accurate, the database would be maintained whether or not she did the work, and she could move to a different assignment with no loss of pay. Knowing what the office is using the database for, she keeps entering address changes. Under my test, is she complicit? Go through the criteria one by one; wherever the test returns a verdict you think is wrong, say whether the criterion is at fault or the case is being forced into the wrong category, and say whether the test needs an additional condition—perhaps something about a person's role in the institution as a whole.
