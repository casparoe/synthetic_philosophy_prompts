I cover science and ideas for a living, and a philosopher at a conference backed me into a corner about love. The argument seems to show that love is either a trading floor, where your rationally obligated to switch partners whenever a higher scorer shows up, or it's a coin flip. Neither sounds true, but I can't find the step where it slips. Here it is, numbered the way he gave it to me:

(1) If you have reasons to love the person you love, those reasons are facts about that person — her wit, her patience, her crooked smile, whatever.
(2) Facts like that are instances of properties. If a fact about A's wit is a reason for you to love A, then the corresponding fact about B's wit is a reason for you to love B, and a greater degree of the same property gives you a stronger reason.
(3) If you have a stronger reason to love B than A, then, other things equal, you ought to love B rather than A.
(4) So if love is based on reasons, then whenever someone comes along who instantiates your beloved's lovable properties to a higher degree, you ought to switch your love to that person.
(5) But no one ought to do that. Someone who loved by constantly scanning the room for a higher scorer on the relevant traits would be making a mistake about love, not being admirably rational.
(6) Therefore love isn't based on reasons.
(7) And if love isn't based on reasons, then it's arbitrary: there's nothing that makes loving this person rather than that one appropriate or inappropriate.
(8) Love isn't arbitrary.

So the whole setup forces a choice between love as a trading floor and love as a coin flip, and I don't believe either. The only exit I can see is rejecting one of the steps, and each rejection costs something. I'd most like to keep (5) and (8), becuase they're the only ones that sound like actual life, but I suspect that's the expensive choice. I gather the live positions in the debate have names — the appraisal view, the bestowal view, the relationship view — but I can't tell which premise each one rejects or what they pay for it. Where does the argument actually go wrong, and what does the least bad fix look like?
