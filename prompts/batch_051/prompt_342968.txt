I keep noticing a parallel and cannot decide whether it is deep or only a matter of shared vocabulary; I want it checked at the points where it matters.

On one side is masterless replication in a distributed database. A value survives the loss of any particular node because it is held on several nodes at once; there is no privileged copy, since the datum just is what the agreeing replicas jointly store. Survivability is a function of the number and placement of replicas and of the protocol that keeps them consistent, and failover works because each replica stores a complete, self-sufficient copy.

On the other side is Hofstadter's claim about the self. A self, on his account, is not the brain that realizes it but a coarse-grained, self-referential pattern that could in principle be realized elsewhere. When one person lives intimately with another, each brain comes to run a degraded but recognizable copy of the other's pattern; after death, those lower-resolution copies persist in the people who knew the person. Survival of the self is thus partial and graded rather than all-or-nothing, and it does not require the original brain to keep running.

The mapping seems clean: an entity's identity is distributed across multiple realizers; the loss of the original is survivable; each realizer holds a full if imperfect version; persistence is a matter of degree rather than a single binary.

But I suspect this is pattern-matching over the word 'copy'. Database replicas are bit-identical by design and are reconciled by deterministic anti-entropy and quorum rules, while Hofstadter's copies are active, drifting, lossy simulations of a mind. The identity conditions of a database record are fixed externally by a schema, whereas in the self case the hard question is exactly which copies, at what fidelity, still count as the person.

Is this a genuine structural parallel, or does it bottom out at a metaphor? What would have to be true of selves, given their drift and the absence of any synchronization mechanism, for concepts like consistency level, quorum, or split-brain to translate? And name something the parallel predicts that turns out false: for instance, it seems to predict that a person's degree of survival is like a replication factor, with more and higher-fidelity copies meaning more survival, whereas the database side has abrupt thresholds below which the datum is unavailable rather than gradually fainter. Which side should give way, and why?
