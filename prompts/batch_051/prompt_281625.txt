Here is a position I have been defending and want stress-tested. It is a view about what a person's real interests consist in and about what any system acting on a person's behalf should be built to serve. The view is that neither revealed preference nor occurrent desire is the right target: revealed preference can be manufactured by constraint, adaptation, and manipulation, while present desire is too shallow, transient, and self-conflicting. The right target is the person's idealized preferences — roughly, the preference structure they would converge on if their deliberation were freed of coercion, false belief, fatigue, and manipulation, and if they had become more fully the person they would, in their better moments, want to be. This is close to what is called coherent extrapolated volition, though I mean to defend the core idea rather than any particular implementation.

I want a map of what counts against this view. I am not mainly interested in computational impracticalities except insofar as they bear on the philosophical claim itself. Which possible cases, arguments, intuitions, empirical findings, or formal results would show that the idealization is incoherent, indeterminate, unfaithful to the person it is meant to serve, or otherwise untenable as an account of real interest? Which would be merely awkward? Which would a committed defender reasonably absorb? Which concessions would reveal that the view has been insulated by its own qualifications, and would that insulation itself be a defect? I also want to know which verdicts the view commits me to that revealed-preference, actual-desire, or objective-good rivals avoid. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward.

For my part, I am most moved by the threat that the idealization constructs a self rather than uncovers one — especially where the idealizing process must pass through experiences that alter the evaluator, leaving different and equally defensible choices of idealizing conditions to yield different endpoints, each with a claim to represent what the person really wants. But treat that as one entry in the map, not an assumption.
