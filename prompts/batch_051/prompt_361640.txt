I'm a novelist, and I've been stuck for weeks on the moral logic of something in my current book, so I started inventing vocabulary to get a handle on it, and now I'm worried I've talked myself into a theory that's just a plot convenience with a fancy name. The concept I've been calling "rescue lien" — or "beneficiary debt," I keep going back and forth — goes like this. A supererogatory act is usually analyzed from the doer's side: it's optional, praiseworthy, beyond duty. But I want to say it also creates a real, non-optional debt on the recipient's side, and that debt is sized by what the act cost the doer, not by what it actually gave the recipient. So if a stranger dives into a river and ruins a thousand-dollar phone to save my dog, I owe them a debt the size of that phone plus a risk premium, even though I never asked to be saved, and even if the dog probably would have made it to the bank on its own. In the novel, my protagonist rescues a woman from a fire, and she ends up resenting him because his optional, heroic act has — without her consent — put a lien on her, the way an unrequested favor from a parent does. I told my editor its just a character beat about pride and obligation, but I've started to think it points at a real blind spot in how people talk about supererogation: everyone argues about whether the hero was obligated to act, and almost nobody asks what the beneficiary now owes.

So here's what I want checked. Take the theory in its strongest form: supererogatory acts are optional for the doer but generate nonconsensual, cost-proportional debts in the beneficiary. Is this already a thing under some name I don't know — it feels too obvious not to be, though "duty of gratitude" doesn't seem to capture the cost-sizing part? What does it get right, and where does it break? Does it secretly collapse into gratitude, or does it absurdly turn every unwanted kindness into an enforceable debt?
