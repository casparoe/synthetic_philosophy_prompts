I spent my career in molecular biology before retiring, and I've only recently started reading philosophy of mind seriously. I keep coming back to a passage in David Chalmers's 2003 paper "Consciousness and its Place in Nature" (freely available on his site). He has just introduced what he calls the "easy problems" of consciousness—discriminating stimuli, reporting information, controlling behavior—when he writes:

    These are important phenomena, and there is much that is not understood about them, but the problems of explaining them have the character of puzzles rather than mysteries. There seems to be no deep problem in principle with the idea that a physical system could be "conscious" in these senses, and there is no obvious obstacle to an eventual explanation of these phenomena in neurobiological or computational terms.

In the next paragraph he sets this against the hard problem: the problem of experience. I saw the same divide acted out in the lab all the time. Nobody blinked at the idea that some bit of cellular machinery was doing something clever and that we would eventually say how; but the moment somebody said the word "experience," people switched to a different register and started talking about the limits of science. That asymetry feels right to me—but so do a lot of biases. So here is what I want: give me the strongest principle you can find that could justify treating those easy problems as mere puzzles while treating experience as a mystery in principle. Then tell me honestly whether that principle draws the line where Chalmers draws it, or whether it either makes one of the easy problems mysterious or makes the hard problem tractable. Say whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up.
