In the course I'm taking this term on the history and theory of sovereignty, we've been using the Montevideo criteria as our working definition of statehood: a permanent population, a defined territory, a government, and the capacity to enter into relations with other states. For a paper, I want to work through a constructed borderline case, and I'd like you to treat it as a genuine borderline case rather than dispose of it with a one-line verdict.

Consider a for-profit joint-stock enterprise, the Meridian Chartered Company, which in 1874 received a royal charter over a coastal enclave and three adjacent islands. The charter assigns it exclusive jurisdiction there: it levies taxes, appoints judges, operates police and prisons, and maintains an armed force for defense and for suppressing piracy. It issues its own passports, mints its own coin, and, within the matters covered by the charter — commerce, transit, and extradition — negotiates and signs agreements with neighboring states in its own name. Those agreements take effect upon ratification by the crown, but ratification has never once been withheld. The territory has surveyed borders settled by longstanding agreement with the adjoining sultanates, and it has a permanent population of several thousand colonists and a much larger native population. The company is governed by a governor, whom the crown appoints, and a board of directors, whom the shareholders elect. The charter is perpetual but revocable by the crown for gross misgovernment, a power that has never been exercised. The crown reserves high foreign policy — war, peace, alliances — to itself, stations no officials in the territory, and prohibits the company from ceding territory. The company exists for the profit of its shareholders; its net earnings are paid out as dividends, and it has no public purpose beyond the good order the charter demands of it. The case is loosely modeled on the chartered companies of the seventeenth to nineteenth centuries, but I have altered the details, so treat everything as stipulated.

Walk through the four Montevideo criteria one by one and say whether the Meridian Chartered Company satisfies each: permanent population, defined territory, government, capacity to enter into relations with other states. Then give your final judgment: is it a state? If you judge that it satisfies all four while still not being a state, identify which criterion is doing hidden work, or argue for a fifth criterion and state it. Address as well whether the attitudes of third states should count: every neighboring state treats the company as a subordinate organ of the crown, and none has recognized it as an independent state.

End with a single machine-readable line and nothing after it, consisting of exactly one of the following labels:

\answer{state} or \answer{not a state} or \answer{underdetermined by the criteria}
