Suppose we adopt, as a working checklist, the four main traditions in the philosophical and psychological study of humour — superiority, incongruity, relief, and benign violation — and treat them not as rival accounts but as a cumulative set of conditions that must all be present, at least to some degree and in one package, for something to count as a genuine instance of humour.

1. Superiority: someone in the situation — the laugher, the speaker, or an onlooker — finds amusement in a person's being lowered, bested, defective, or unfortunate relative to someone else.

2. Incongruity: the case joins two frames, scripts, or expectations that do not fit together, and the humour lies in the collision between them.

3. Relief: the laughter discharges tension that had been accumulating around a source of anxiety, embarrassment, or prohibition, so that the joke works as a licensed way of touching what is normally kept solemn or unsayable.

4. Benign violation: the case presents a violation of a norm — physical, moral, social, or conceptual — but the violation is simultaneously appraised as harmless, acceptable, or done in play.

Now consider the following case. A woman with a terminal diagnosis and roughly a month to live has gathered her closest friends in her hospital room. With careful timing and the manner of someone long practiced at using jokes to manage other people's discomfort, she tells a polished, self-deprecating joke at her own expense that turns on the fact of her imminent death: she says she has finally stopped worrying about how she will pay for old age, since that problem has now solved itself. Her friends laugh, uneasily at first and then fully; she laughs hardest of all. The delivery is controlled and plainly meant to be funny rather than a disguised plea for reassurance.

Does this qualify as a genuine instance of humour under the four-part checklist? Work through the checklist criterion by criterion, and for each criterion say whether the case clearly satisfies it, clearly fails it, or sits genuinely between the two. In particular, address the following. Does the superiority criterion require a butt distinct from the teller, or can the teller satisfy it by standing above her own fear, her former self, or her audience's discomfort? Can a real and near death be appraised as benign simply because the teller frames it in play, or does the benign-violation condition fail whenever the violation is the teller's own actual destruction? If the verdicts split across the criteria, is the right conclusion that the case is humour under some theories and not others, that the conjunction is too strict, or that one criterion is silently doing all the work? Conclude by saying which criterion, if any, carries the judgement here, and whether the four-condition checklist needs revision — and if so, exactly how.
