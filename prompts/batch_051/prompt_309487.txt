My brother Marcus and his wife Dana have been having the same fight since their daughter's school launched a program where families sponsor refugee kids' school fees. I've had a front-row seat because I'm their daughter's godfather and I'm staying with them this fall — and my own kid goes to the same school, so I've been staring at the same sponsorship form.

They can afford the sponsorship or their daughter's piano lessons, not both. Dana wants to take the piano money and sponsor two refugee children. She says ranking their daughter's music lessons above another child learning to read is favoritism with a nice name. Marcus says no: your first job is the child you brought into the world, and someone who spreads care equally ends up caring for no one well.

Dana brought in Mozi: impartial care means a stranger's child counts the same as your own, so the warm feeling you have for your own kid is a fact about you, not a reason to shortchange other people's kids. Marcus countered with Confucius: love is graded, it starts in the family and spreads outward, and forcing the farthest rung to feel like the nearest is either self-deception or it kills the relationships that make care possible at all.

Then it got sharper. Dana said she isn't talking about how love feels, but what they owe; special relationships aren't a source of duties so much as a background condition. Marcus said that's absurd: families are the paradigm of unchosen obligations, and if she watered those down to 'every child counts the same,' their daughter wouldn't have parents, she'd have two people hedging. Dana said that's question-begging — whether their daughter is entitled to piano lessons at the expense of a refugee child's schooling is exactly what's at issue. Marcus came back that even a morality of impartial care has to be lived by partial human beings, and Mozi's rule would mean never buying their daughter anything while any child anywhere lacks the basics. Dana replied that only follows if the rule is a moment-by-moment optimizer, which nobody is proposing; the sponsorship is one discrete choice, and protecting piano lessons is what needs a justification.

He said the justification is that parental partiality is constitutive, not incidental — a father who didn't rank his daughter first wouldn't be in a father–daughter relationship at all. She said fine, but that just tells impartial care to count relationship goods when it calculates; it doesn't show piano lessons beat school fees, and it definitely doesn't show Confucius beats Mozi.

At that point they both looked at me to settle it. I'd filled out the same form and erased it twice, so I just shrugged. Can you go through their exchange and adjudicate the actual moves — which claims from each hold up, which ones slide past each other or beg the question, what they're both missing, and who's more right overall?
