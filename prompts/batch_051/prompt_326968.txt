Hempel's ravens is driving me nuts, and I need something I can actually defend in front of forty undergrads.

Here's my rolling list of cases, with my gut verdicts in parentheses:

(a) A black raven on a birdwatching trip confirms "all ravens are black." (obviously yes)
(b) A white raven disconfirms it. (obviously yes)
(c) A white shoe confirms "all non-black things are non-ravens," and since that's logically equivalent to "all ravens are black," you seem forced to say the shoe confirms the raven hypothesis too. (my gut: no — or yes but only in some empty, useless sense)
(d) A green frog: same structure as (c), a non-black non-raven. (same gut as (c))
(e) A black shoe. It's not a raven, and it's not non-black, so Nicod's condition — an F that's G confirms "all Fs are Gs" — gives no verdict either way. My gut says it should at least rule out a possible counterexample and count for something. (I have no idea)
(f) A careful survey of a known raven roost finds all 100 ravens black. (strong confirmation)
(g) A careful survey of 100 shoes finds them all non-black and non-ravens. (feels like you've learned nothing worth calling confirmation, formal story be damned)
(h) A raven that a prankster spray-painted white. (my gut says this doesn't disconfirm, but I can't say why without sneaking in background knowledge)

If there's no single definition, I'd rather you split the term into two notions then force one.
