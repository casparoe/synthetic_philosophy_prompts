Consider a set of verdicts Machiavelli hands down in The Prince. He presents Cesare Borgia as the proper model for a new prince, even though Borgia received his state through his father's fortune and lost it when fortune turned against him; all the intervening success is credited to Borgia's own skill. He denies that Agathocles of Syracuse possessed virtù, Machiavelli's term of art: Agathocles rose from the lowest condition by murder and treachery, kept his state, and gained empire but not glory. He praises Hannibal's cruelty as necessary to holding his army together, ranking it among Hannibal's virtues, while blaming Scipio's excessive mercy for the revolt of his soldiers in Spain. And he claims that fortune favors the impetuous over the cautious, yet also that one who could match his conduct to the times would never fail.

Now imagine Machiavelli is asked to judge this new case: a prince who inherits a small state, never takes a decisive risk, enlarges nothing by his own enterprise, and yet keeps power for forty years by making exactly the timely concessions, reversals, and promises each new moment requires. He is perfectly adaptable, perfectly dependent on fortune's current, and never cruel. Would Machiavelli hold him up as a model or as a cautionary example?

I want less the bare prediction than the rule behind it. Identify at least two rival rules that fit the historical verdicts equally well, show how they diverge on the cautious adaptable prince, give your best guess together with the reason for preferring its reading of Machiavelli, and name one further case that would settle which rule was really doing the work. Please respond as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
