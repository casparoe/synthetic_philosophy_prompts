I'm a retired geologist, so I keep reaching for the idea that your own mind is something you can read off like a decent instrument. I've been reading around in epistemology, and two slogans keep bothering me. One is the KK principle: if you know something, you know that you know it. The other is that our own mental states are luminous, meaning that if you're in one of them, you're in a position to know you're in it just by attending to yourself. Ive collected a bunch of case judgments and I cant tell what principle, if any, holds them together. I want you to find it, or tell me which of my verdicts I have to abandon.

Here are the cases, as close to how they strike me as I can get them.

If I glance at a jar of sand, I know it has more than fifty grains, and I know that I know it; asking how I know that I know feels like a joke. So there, KK works and the state is luminous.

In a psych lab years ago I did a tachistoscope task: a letter was flashed for a few milliseconds and I had to say which of four it was. Every single trial felt like a blind guess, but I ran around seventy percent correct where chance was twenty-five. I think on those trials I knew the letter, but I didn't know that I knew. My feeling of guessing was simply miscalibrated. So KK fails there, and my first-order state wasn't transparent to me.

On a winter morning I get into a cold car and the heater slowly comes up as I drive. I don't think there's a last instant at which I feel cold and a first instant at which I feel warm; it's a smear. But if feeling cold were fully luminous, there would have to be a last instant at which I knew I felt cold and a first instant at which I knew I didn't, and I don't think those exist either. So something near the boundary just isn't luminous.

A physicist I know learned a classification theorem in graduate school and used it routinely. Ask him to reconstruct the proof on the spot and he fumbles, goes quiet, and says he supposes he doesn't really know it. I'm convinced he did know it before you asked and knows it after; at the moment of panic he simply couldn't see that he knew it. So knowledge outruns knowledge of knowledge again.

Ask me the capital of Uzbekistan right now and I know that I don't know it, no hesitation. Negative introspection is free for me in a way positive introspection isn't, and I have no idea what to make of that.

Now the pair that really bothers me. A radiologist friend looks at a film and before she can name any feature is sure there is a fracture; it is confirmed. I'd unhesitatingly say she knew the fracture was there, and she knew that she knew. Another friend, a field geologist, gets a whiff near a slope and has a sudden, unshakeable hunch about a gas vent; he's right, and he's been right by that same feeling before. I'd call that a reliable hunch, but I wouldn't say he knew the vent was there, and I certainly wouldn't say he knew that he knew. I cannot articulate the difference between these two, and I'm not sure there is one.

My working hunch is that second-order knowledge is a separate, fallible measurement of your own first-order state, with its own error bars, which would explain the tachistoscope and the physicist. But it doesn't explain why the radiologist gets second-order knowledge for free, or why negative introspection is costless while positive introspection isn't.

Here's what I want. Take these verdicts, or most of them, and tell me whether there is one principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures them. State the principle precisely, say which clause is doing work for which case, and then tell me what it says about some further cases I haven't considered, like a chess player who unhesitatingly says a move is right without calculating, or a recovered memory I am only halfway confident in. Above all, be honest about which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, whether that means overturning one of them or else saving them all at the price of a complicated, ad hoc mess. I would rather learn which verdict is the cheapest to drop than be told everything fits. And no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables in the answer; just continuous prose, the way you'd write an email.
