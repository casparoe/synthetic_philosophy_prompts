Here's the thing I can't get past in a column I'm drafting. Last month I interviewed an astrophysicist who told me she doesn't lose much sleep over modified-gravity alternatives to dark matter anymore — not because they've been refuted one by one, but because the whole shape of the evidence tips one way. I noticed I was nodding, and I noticed that my own stated view makes that nod suspicious. I buy the underdetermination thesis: for just about any body of evidence, you can keep a rival theory alive by adjusting auxiliary assumptions, so no amount of data can literally force a unique theory on a rational person. But as someone who covers science and ideas for a living, I also cant shake the feeling that the evidence really did decide dark matter, and that treating the two programs as if they're on an equal footing would be false balance, not open-mindedness. So one of these has to bend — the thesis or the intuition. Give me the strongest case for the thesis and the strongest case for the intuition that the data sometimes genuinely single a theory out, then a negotiated verdict on which should yield and under what sort of pressure. If they're compatible, show the reading that makes both true; if not, say which one I'd be rational to revise and why. No citations, no bibliography — I'm after the argument. Structure it in four sections, in this order: 'The case for underdetermination' (about 250 words), 'The case that the evidence decides' (about 250 words), 'What would justify each move' (about 150 words), and 'My verdict' (about 200 words).
