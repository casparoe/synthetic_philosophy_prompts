In the circles I read, people are generally comfortable ceding weighty decisions to unelected experts. A central bank raises interest rates and may throw millions out of work; a public-health official closes a school over the objections of its elected board; a fire marshal orders a restaurant shut. None of them stands for election, and each overrides a decision the affected people would have made for themselves, yet few call this a democratic crisis. When a constitutional court strikes down a statute that a representative legislature actually passed, however, many of the same people reach for the countermajoritarian difficulty as if it were a special usurpation. I have trouble seeing what underwrites the asymmetry. The usual distinctions — courts decide what rights mean, judges are generalists, judicial errors are hard to correct — all describe parts of the administrative state too, and a bad statute is usually easier for ordinary people to get changed than a bad interest-rate path. Is there a principle that generates these two judgments, or is the special objection to judicial review mostly a rationalization? I want the strongest principle you can find and an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up.
