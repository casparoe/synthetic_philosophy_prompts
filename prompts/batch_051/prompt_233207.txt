Right, here's a brief I've been trying to get right. I'm a PhD student in constitutional theory, and I teach an upper-level undergrad seminar on political obligation and constitutional emergencies — about 16 students, one 90-minute session. I want a strucutred classroom game or role-play that makes the students feel, not just discuss, the problem of constitutional gaps and interregna: when the written rules run out, or an office falls vacant with no succession procedure, who governs? My students keep slipping back into the assumption that there is always a correct answer somewhere in the text, or that a court will just sort it out. I want them to actually find themselves in a situation where the rulebook says nothing, and discover what they in fact do about it — before I name any theory.

The setting should be an artificial community with its own very small constitution, deliberately not modelled on the US or any real country, so nobody can reach for case law. You are an expert on constitutional theory and on designing classroom simulations. Please think carefully about the mechanics, and start with a short brainstorm of two or three candidate crisis mechanisms before settling on one.

Then give me a runnable 90-minute activity, with: (1) roles and materials; (2) the fragment of constitution or rules the students get, including the gap; (3) the crisis event that triggers the gap mid-flow — it should not be announced as "the gap", they should run into it while trying to do something they care about; (4) a payoff structure that uses points or sweets or some other small stake rather than real money, plus a built-in cost of deadlock so the situation has urgency; (5) a timeline; (6) a debrief that maps what actually happened onto the theoritical ideas: interregnum, the English doctrine that the Crown never dies, the rule of recognition, necessity, and de facto versus de jure authority, maybe even the old Roman practice of a rotating interrex as a contrast; and (7) an honest note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle it.

One constraint: I don't want the game to presuppose the answer, e.g. that it is always continuity or the strongest or best-organised faction that wins. It should be set up so that several different resolutions are live, and the interesting thing is what the group ends up treating as authoritative. Also be frank about which feature of the real phenomenon the classroom can't reproduce — my guess is the absence of genuine fear or threat of civil conflict, but there may be more.
