Scholars commonly describe Han Fei as the great theorist of rule by incentives rather than rule by virtue: where many Confucians of his era held that government should work through the ruler's moral cultivation, ritual, and example, Han Fei maintained that a ruler should govern through public and fixed legal standards, calibrated rewards and punishments, and the covert techniques by which he supervises his subordinates, on the grounds that people respond to self-interest and to the incentives their situation presents rather than to another person's virtue. I have been reading him alongside contemporary debates about algorithmic management, and I want to press on how far his commitments travel.

Suppose Han Fei were shown a modern labor platform of the sort that manages delivery drivers or ride-hail drivers through automated scoring: acceptance rates, on-time metrics, customer ratings, bonuses, and automatic deactivation, with almost no human supervisor exercising discretion over individual workers. What would he make of it? Which features would he recognize as a realization of his program, and which would he criticize as a departure from it? Would he, for instance, treat the substitution of algorithmic rules for a capricious human manager as an advance against personal favoritism, or would he regard a private company's opaque scoring formula as merely another arbitrary power, one that is worse in some respects because it cannot be questioned face to face? Would he view the workers' attempts to reverse-engineer, game, or exploit the metrics as confirmation of his own account of how subordinates respond to incentives; and would that lead him to demand that the rules be made public, or to advise the platform to keep its formulas hidden the way he advised rulers to conceal their techniques of control? Would his conviction that the levers of reward and punishment must ultimately remain in the ruler's hands commit him to saying that some named human executive must retain final authority over the algorithm, or would he accept a system that runs itself once it has been well designed?

In answering, keep clear track of what is reasonably settled about Han Fei's thought and what is genuinely contested among specialists, for example whether his public legal standards amount to anything like a modern rule-of-law ideal and whether his techniques of control serve a broader program of stable order or merely secure the ruler's power.
