I've been reading Frazer's chapter on the transference of evil in The Golden Bough, and there's a bit in there that's been bugging me. He says, roughly, that behind scapegoat rituals is the notion that the guilt or pollution of a whole people can be concentrated in a goat or a dog or some unlucky person and then removed—the sin isn't just declared forgiven, it's moved. The Leviticus 16 version makes this vivid: the high priest lays hands on a live goat, recites the sins of the Israelites over it, and that goat is sent out into the wilderness while a second goat is killed as a sin offering.

I want to know what theory of guilt and blame that practice actually presupposes. There are a few candidate readings: (a) guilt is a substance-ish something that can be transferred from person to animal, so after the ritual the people literally no longer carry it; (b) the ritual is a performative/symbolic act, and the underlying theory is still that guilt is a status of agents, but the community treats the goat as representing it's guilt; (c) the ritual is more about social cohesion, and there is no coherent single theory underneath. I keep going back and forth.

Reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can, and say what the ritual strictly presupposes versus what it merely fits well with. Specifically: can someone hold a fairly normal retributive view of personal blameworthiness and still perform this ritual without conceptual doublethink? If the underlying theory were replaced with a more agent-level account of guilt, what would actually have to change in the practice—maybe nothing, maybe the confession-over-the-goat step becomes incoherent? Also, am I just wrong to assume the ritual has to have one implicit theory at all?
