Exam question: Explain Quine's argument against the analytic-synthetic distinction and against reductionism in Two Dogmas of Empiricism. Distinguish what Quine takes himself to have established about each dogma, and show how the two criticisms are related.

Student answer: Quine attacks two dogmas of empiricism. The first is the belief in a principled division between analytic truths, which are true solely in virtue of meaning, and synthetic truths, which are true in virtue of how the world is. The second is radical reductionism, the view that each meaningful sentence can be translated into a logical construction out of terms referring directly to immediate experience. Quine holds that the two dogmas are ultimately the same dogma, because both rest on the verification theory of meaning, and since that theory is an article of faith with no empirical basis, both must be abandoned. He begins by dividing analytic statements into logical truths, such as No unmarried man is married, and statements such as No bachelor is married, which are supposed to become logical truths when a synonym is substituted for one term. He sets the logical truths aside and concentrates on the second sort, since they require an account of synonymy. He argues that no non-circular account of synonymy can be given: definitions merely report an already existing synonymy, and interchangeability salva veritate fails in an extensional language because coextensive but non-synonymous expressions such as creature with a heart and creature with a kidney are interchangeable in all extensional contexts. Adding a necessity operator would make bachelor and unmarried man interchangeable, but only because the claim that necessarily all and only bachelors are unmarried men is already analytic, so the criterion is circular. Quine concludes from this that there are no analytic truths. He also takes this result to settle the second dogma as a corollary, since reductionism presupposes a boundary between the linguistic and factual components of a statement, and that boundary is just the analytic-synthetic distinction. Quine then replaces reductionism with his holistic picture of a web of belief: our sentences face experience not individually but only as a whole, so the scale at which empirical significance attaches is the whole of science rather than the individual sentence. Within this web, empirical statements can be revised in light of experience, though Quine deliberately restricts that revisability to empirical science and leaves logic and mathematics as analytic truths that are immune to revision because they lie at the center of the web. He therefore takes himself to have shown that the analytic-synthetic distinction is a dogma with no empirical warrant and that reductionism is untenable.
