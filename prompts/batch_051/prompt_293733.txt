My roommate has been on a Kant kick and built an argument he calls the "maxim shredder": he says it shows the "what if everyone did that?" test gives opposite verdicts on the very same action, so it isn't a real test at all. I keep trying to push back and keep getting tangled, so I'd like a careful diagnosis of where it goes wrong, if it does.

The argument, as he gives it:

(1) The universalizability test evaluates an action by evaluating the maxim under which it is performed: the action is permissible just in case you can will that everyone act on that maxim.

(2) The same action can be brought under many different maxims. Not paying tax on some freelance income could be described narrowly, as "understate freelance income from the winter quarter, when a client paid me late and I was short on rent," or broadly, as "advance my interest by whatever means works out best for me." Same action, different maxims.

(3) For nearly any action, you can find a maxim specific enough that universalizing it is harmless: if everyone in exactly those circumstances did the same, nothing much would happen.

(4) For nearly any action, you can also find a maxim broad enough that universalizing it is a disaster: if everyone acted on "further my own interest, never mind the rules," the practice in question — tax compliance, promising, whatever — collapses.

(5) So the very same action is both permitted and forbidden by the very same test, depending only on which description we happen to pick.

(6) Since there's no principled way to pick the maxim, the verdict is arbitrary — any act can be made to pass by narrowing or fail by broadening.

(7) So the test isn't testing actions at all, only descriptions, and the whole family of views that lean on universalizability — Kant's, Hare's prescriptivism, the generalization-argument moves in Singer — loses its engine.

I'd most like to keep (1), or something near it: I think "what if everyone did that?" really does catch free-riding like tax dodging. So I suspect the damage is somewhere in (2), (3), or (5), but whenever I say "you can't just narrow the maxim," he says: fine, then tell me what fixes the right level of description. I don't have an answer. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, what would a theory of maxim individuation — or of picking the relevant act-description — have to do to keep the test from being arbitrary?
