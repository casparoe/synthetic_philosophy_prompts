In the comparative politics course I am taking, the case for proportional representation is often introduced through an analogy: an electoral system should work like a mirror for the electorate. First-past-the-post is said to produce a distorted image—magnifying some groups, shrinking others, and leaving some out of the frame entirely—while proportional representation is said to reflect the political complexion of the public more faithfully, so that the legislature is a recognizable image of the voters. Our instructor does not use this merely as illustration but as an argument: if the legislature is supposed to stand in for the whole public, then fidelity of reflection looks like a constitutive virtue of the system, not just one aesthetic consideration among others.

I would like you to audit how far this mirror analogy can actually carry that conclusion. Please do the following. First, spell out the mapping explicitly: what is meant to correspond to the object being reflected, the mirror's surface, the reflected image, and the sources of distortion; and what the optics quietly imports—for example, that there is a single determinate shape of public opinion out there waiting to be reflected, that reflection is passive rather than partly constituting what it represents, and that a good image is one that alters the original as little as possible. Second, identify where the analogy breaks down. A mirror does not deliberate, form coalitions, or make decisions that bind the thing it reflects; its distortions are mechanical rather than chosen trade-offs; and real proportional systems impose thresholds, district magnitudes, apportionment formulas, and rounding rules, so the image is always partly constructed. Third, distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the intended normative use from the ones that are merely cosmetic. Finally, say whether the conclusion—that proportional representation's fidelity of reflection gives it a strong advantage over first-past-the-post—survives once the analogy stops doing the argumentative work, and whether some better analogy would do that work more honestly.
