I'm an undergraduate in a course on mathematical practice, and I've developed a policy for my own study that I'd like an honest audit of. I refuse to accept that I understand a definition unless I can do three things with it: (1) give a clear example that satisfies it, (2) give a near-miss example that fails exactly one condition, and (3) explain, in terms of what would go wrong in the surrounding proofs, why that condition is there rather than some weaker or simpler alternative. If I can't do all three, I treat the concept as not yet learned, even if I can state the definition verbatim and solve standard exercises. I know this policy slows me down, and in practice I sometimes have to suspend it to keep up with lectures. Is this a defensible way to manage the trade-off between motivation and rigor, or have I dressed up an avoidance of difficult formalism as a principle? Are there kinds of definitions for which it systematically misleads, and is there a version of the rule that keeps the benefits without the costs?
