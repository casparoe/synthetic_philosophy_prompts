Retired now after thirty-five years in human genetics, so this is the kind of thing I used to see from the lab side and only recently started reading philosophy on. Here's a puzzle I can't shake.

Take a couple doing IVF. The clinic screens the embryos and finds one carries a dominant mutation for a nasty, early-onset disorder, say Huntington's. Almost nobody blinks if they discard that embryo and transfer a healthy one; plenty of people would say they'd be negligent or even cruel to knowingly implant it. Now take the same couple, same batch of embryos, and suppose the clinic offers a polygenic score for height or educational attainment, and they pick the embryo with the best score. Suddenly it's "designer babies" and liberal eugenics, and a lot of people object, or at least feel queasy.

I have trouble seeing what actually does the work in that shift. Both cases are selecting which possible child to bring into existence on the basis of the child's predicted genes. Both involve discarding embryos the parents would otherwise, presumably, have loved. Is the difference that disease selection prevents suffering, while trait selection merely gives a relative advantage? Fine, but then why is it acceptable to select against a fairly mild condition like moderate hearing loss, and why is it queasy to select for a stronger immune system or a gene that lowers later-life cancer risk? If the principle is "avoid harm to the child," the enhancement cases don't harm the child. The child selected for height is better of than the one who wasn't born, if anything. And a social objection, that enhancement selection will stratify the gene pool or advantage the rich, seems to apply to disease selection too, since both are mainly available to people who can pay for IVF in the first place.

So my question is whether there's a principled line between what ordinary people accept (avoiding disease) and what they reject (selecting for traits), or whether the therapy/enhancement distinction is mostly a comforting label. If you defend the asymmetry, defend it against the hard cases I just named. If you can't, tell me which side you'd revise: accept enhancement selection, or reconsider disease selection.
