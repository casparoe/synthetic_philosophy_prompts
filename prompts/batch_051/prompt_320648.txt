I manage the small grants program at a foundation that funds work across global health, animal welfare, and AI safety, and my recommendations go to a board whose members have sharply different moral commitments. I have maybe six hours per decision and need to make a call every other week, usually between $50,000 and $1,000,000. I have absorbed the usual LessWrong and EA Forum discussions of moral uncertainty and expected value, but they are mostly too theoretical to use under board pressure. The thing I keep circling back to is Henry Sidgwick's The Methods of Ethics, specifically the early part of Book IV, where he insists that the utilitarian standard is the happiness of all sentient beings affected by an action, then almost immediately concedes that applying this standard forces us into rough, probable estimates of consequences, and that even well-informed, well-intentioned people will make those estimates differently. He names the problem but does not solve it.

I want that gap turned into a reusable procedure for someone like me: a decision checklist for approving or rejecting a grant when I am uncertain about moral theory, not merely uncertain about the empirical consequences. It should connect Sidgwick's point with the modern moral-uncertainty idea that I sometimes ought to act on a moral theory I give, say, thirty percent credence, even if I mostly reject it. The procedure must be usable by a non-philosopher in under three hours per decision, must specify what to input, what to compute, what to write down for the board, and which steps are genuine judgment calls dressed up as mechanics rather than mechanics themselves. Work it through three hypothetical cases: one in which a grant helps current humans in a measurable way but may be bad on the best animal-welfare view; one in which a grant has a tiny probability of an enormous effect many centuries out; and one in which a grant would build capacity inside our own foundation and is therefore instrumentally tempting but extremely hard to evaluate. I especially want to see a case where the procedure returns an answer I will not like, and I want an honest statement of what the checklist cannot do, including where it may simply move the uncertainty around or create false precision.
