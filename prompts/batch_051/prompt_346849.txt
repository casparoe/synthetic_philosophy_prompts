I've been reading Darwin's On the Origin of Species on my own—not in school, just got into it—and this passage has been bugging me for weeks:

"[I]t will be seen that I look at the term species, as one arbitrarily given for the sake of convenience to a set of individuals closely resembling each other, and that it does not essentially differ from the term variety, which is given to less distinct and more fluctuating forms. The term variety, again, in comparison with mere individual differences, is also applied arbitrarily, and for mere convenience sake."

Here's my problem. Darwin seems to be saying "species" is just a convenience label with no sharp line between species and variety. But biologists still talk like species are real units—they count them, protect them under law, and argue about whether two fossils are the same species. So which is it?

I want a working definition of "species" that doesn't dodge the mess. Please act as a professional philosopher of biology. Start by brainstorming a few candidate definitions, then commit to one and test it on these cases:

1. A chihuahua and a Great Dane: same species or not? They can't physically mate without human help, but they're both dogs.
2. A horse and a donkey: fertile offspring? No, mules are sterile, so most people say different species.
3. Two populations of birds that overlap in a narrow zone and produce fertile hybrids there, but remain distinct outside that zone.
4. A ring species: populations A and B interbreed, B and C interbreed, C and D interbreed, but A and D overlap and don't interbreed.
5. A bdelloid rotifer (a microscopic freshwater animal) that reproduces asexually and hasn't had sex for millions of years. What's an interbreeding definition supposed to do with it?
6. Bacteria that swap genes sideways instead of only passing them from parent to offspring.
7. Neanderthals and modern humans: we know they interbred, but most scientists still call them separate species.
8. Two groups of frogs that are genetically very different but look and sound identical to a careful human observer.

Run your definition through each case, say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and then revise it if you need to. Finally, tell me whether your definition makes species a real natural kind or a convenience label, and whether Darwin was right that it doesn't essentially differ from variety.

I'm fine if the answer is "the term does several jobs and no single defintion gets everything" as long as you show why.
