I'm a science writer working on a piece about whether the boundary we draw between one person's well-being and another's is partly a technological accident. Here's the counterfactual I keep turning over. Suppose that, starting around 2035, a cheap, reversible, over-the-counter device becomes available in rich countries that lets any two consenting adults "link" their affective systems for a stretch of up to a week. While linked, each feels the other's bodily pleasures and pains roughly as their own — a stubbed toe on one side hurts both, a good meal on one side satisfies both — but their thoughts, memories, and inclinations remain seperate, and when the link ends each person remembers the shared feelings without continuing to have them. Assume it spreads the way smartphones did, becoming ordinary within a decade.

The old philosophical worry I want to run this against is the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism: roughly, its fine for me to trade a pain now for a bigger pleasure later within my own life, but its not fine to impose a pain on you to buy a bigger pleasure for me, because separate lives have claims that separate moments of one life do not. What happens to that distinction once two people can temporarily share a single affective life? I don't want a story about the device; I want a reasoned prediction, over the next couple of generations, of how our moral and legal practices around interpersonal trade-offs actually shift.

Be concrete: which practices move first — consent to risk, compensation, medical triage, family law? Which near-universal norms stay stickier than the philosophy would predict, and why? Where does the analogy "same person at different times" vs "different people, linked" still come apart? And tell me which of your predictions you're least confident in, and what fact about human psychology or institutions, if it turned out differently, would flip them.
