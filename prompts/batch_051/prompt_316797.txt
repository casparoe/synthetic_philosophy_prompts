I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so maybe I'm seeing this sideways. The old craft dictum is that a chronicle says "this happened, then that happened," and plot only starts when there's a "because" between them. But in practice I do something stranger: I never write the because. I write two events in an order, close together, with a few details made salient, and the reader supplies the causality, often without noticing. If I put a man buying rope before a body is found in the river, readers will connect them even though the page only contains the two scenes; if I reverse the order, the very same two events get read as a consequence instead of a cause. The causal relation, as far as I can tell, is not on the page at all; its manufactured by the reader according to regularities I exploit. That resembles what Hume says we do with nature — all we observe is constant conjunction, not a causal glue — and what Russell presses further, that causation is something a mature science could do without. In the novel's case there's a wrinkle: the author (me) selected and ordered the events, and I know the "because" is projected by the reader, because I never put it in the text. In the real world there's presumably no such selector.

So my question is whether the parallel between constructing plot out of sequence and constructing causation out of constant conjunction is deep or just flattering literary metaphor, and whether it should move me at all in the debate about whether causation is fundamental, reducible, or eliminable. What would have to be true of both sides for my craft observation to transfer into the metaphysics of causation? Does the fact that a novel has an author disqualify the analogy, or does it turn the novel into a kind of controlled experiment in how regularity plus salience manufactures the sense of because? And run it the other way: if the regularity or eliminativist view is right, should it explain why I can make a reader supply a causal connection the text never states, and why reversing the order reverses the inference? I want to know where the structure matches and where it comes apart — and please say how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point you are least sure of.
