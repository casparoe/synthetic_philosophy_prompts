I'm working through the formal epistemology literature on belief change, and I've tied myself in knots over Bas van Fraassen's Reflection principle. In the form I've seen it, the principle says that your current credence in a proposition A, conditional on the supposition that your later self will assign A probability r, should itself be r. It is often glossed more loosely as the claim that your present probability should equal the expected value of your future probability. I am aware of the standard counterexamples involving anticipated forgetting, drugs, or cognitive decline; those make sense to me as failures of deference to a future self. But I don't understand how the principle is supposed to be compatible with ordinary learning.

Suppose I now assign credence 0.8 to the claim that a trial will meet its primary endpoint, and I have good reason to think—from how I tend to respond to this kind of interim data—that after reading next week's report I will assign only 0.5 to that claim. Reflection then seems to tell me that I should already be at 0.5. Generalize this, and it looks as though any anticipated change of mind is irrational: whenever I can foresee that the evidence will move me, my current view is wrong unless it has already moved to where I can see it will end up. That makes rational belief revision impossible, since every update would be either a confession of present irrationality or a violation of Reflection.

I suspect the mistake is something like conflating knowledge of my future credence with knowledge of the future evidence, or failing to distinguish cases in which the future belief-forming process is one I should defer to from cases in which it is not. As an expert on Bayesian epistemology, could you think this through carefully and tell me exactly where the argument goes wrong? In particular: does Reflection forbid changing your mind, and if not, what does it actually constrain? Please work through the relevant distinctions rather than just restating the principle.
