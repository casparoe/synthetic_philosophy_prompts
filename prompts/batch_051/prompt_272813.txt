I've recently started modding a medium-sized online community, and we keep going in circles about whether to remove someone for stuff they did off-platform, or whether that's just mob justice. Someone dropped a reference to John Stuart Mill's On Liberty, chapter 4, where he distinguishes legal punishment from the ways society punishes people through social exclusion, contempt, and refusal to associate. I don't have the text on me, but as I remember it, Mill is trying to work out what society may do to people whose conduct it deems harmful, and he insists that the harsher, less regulated forms of social punishment are at least as serious as legal penalties and need their own justification, not just a community's strong feelings. That distinction has really been bugging me.

Can you make me a which-position-do-you-actually-hold quiz, maybe eight or nine questions, on deplatforming and social sanction as punishment? Each question should be a concrete case (leaked private messages, offensive old tweets by a public figure, a comedian being shouted down, doxxing a harasser, a professor denied a speaking invite, an ordinary person losing their job over an ill-advised Facebook post, etc.) with two or three answer options. The point is to sort people by how they actually answer cases, not by whether they call themselves free-speech absolutists or pro-accountability or whatever.

The scoring key should map people onto something like four to six positions located relative to Mill's distinction. For example: a strict Millian view that extra-legal sanctions should track the harm principle and should only be used where legal punishment is unavailable or hopelessly slow; a due-process view that social sanctions are legitimate but need some procedural safeguards against rumor and pile-ons; a communitarian view that a group's expressive norms can justify exclusion even where no individual rights were violated; and a skepticism view that social sanctions are almost never justified because they are disproportionate, unaccountable, and hard to reverse. But don't just take those: build the leaves from the logical space of answers first, then note which positions actually show up and which combinations of answers are unoccupied, and whether that's because they're incoherent, unattractive, or just underexplored. Also flag any pair of leaves the quiz can't reliably seperate.

The quiz should force people to confront the Millian worry directly: at least two of the cases should involve conduct that is harmful in some diffuse way but not illegal, and at least one should involve a social sanction that seems harsher than any legal penalty the person could have faced. Don't include questions that just ask people to endorse a principle in the abstract.
