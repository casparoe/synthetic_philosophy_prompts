So I spent a whole evening arguing with a friend about whether the state is basically just a corporation, and we kept coming back to the East India Company as the test case, and neither of us would budge. Take the standard criteria for statehood — permanent population, defined territory, a government that actually administers the place, a monopoly on legitimate violence, and the capacity to deal with other states as an equal — and the British East India Company in, say, India around the 1830s seems to nail almost all of them. It governed most of the subcontinent, collected taxes, ran courts, had an army larger than most European states', minted coin, and made treaties with Indian princes. But it was also a chartered joint-stock company, owned by shareholders in London, answerable to Parliament, and for a long time it ruled at least nominally in the name of the Mughal emperor. My friend says this proves his point: if a corporation can do all the things a state does, then "state" was always just a legal fiction with guns, and the Company counts. I kept saying no, it's a franchise, the sovereignty was delegated and revocable, so calling it a state stretches the word until it snaps — but I cant actually say which criterion it fails.

So run through the criteria one by one and tell me whether the Company around 1830 qualifies as a state. Where the answer is no or murky, say whether the problem is with the criteria, with the case, or just with the criteria being underdetermined. And if you had to add or change one criterion to handle this case properly, what would it be?
