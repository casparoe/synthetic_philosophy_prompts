I write about science for a living, and I keep coming back to an asymmetry in the way we handle simultaneous discovery, becuase it strikes me every time a priority fight flares up in my inbox.

Take the Darwin–Wallace case. When Wallace's paper landed on his desk, Darwin wrote to Lyell on 18 June 1858: "I never saw a more striking coincidence. if Wallace had my M.S. sketch written out in 1842 he could not have made a better short abstract! Even his terms now stand as Heads of my Chapters." A few lines later he adds: "So all my originality, whatever it may amount to, will be smashed."

Here's the asymmetry I can't settle. When we explain why two people reached the same idea at nearly the same moment, we use exactly Darwin's first language: the time was ripe, the idea was in the air, somebody was bound to find it. We treat independence as evidence that the discovery was due anyway — the next step the field was going to take, not a fluke of one person's private genius. But when we decide who gets the credit, we flip. We honour a discoverer as if the insight were a personal achievement, and priority disputes are fought over days and weeks. Darwin does both in the same paragraph: "a more striking coincidence" is the language of inevitability, while "all my originality... will be smashed" is the language of something personally owned and lost.

So: is there a good justification for holding both attitudes at once, or is this just an inconsistency we put up with? If the discovery was genuinely inevitable once the evidence was available, what exactly is the first discoverer being credited for — and if that means we ought to stop crediting firsts, why do the alternatives (credit all, credit no one, credit the community) seem even less satisfactory? I never quite know whether the fix lies in giving up inevitability talk, giving up priority honours, or keeping both and accepting the strain. Which way would you jump?
