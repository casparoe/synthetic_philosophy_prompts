Im on the exec board of a campus free-speech society, and our constitution dates from 1998. The speech clause is basically Mill's harm principle turned into house rules, and it says: 'No member may be penalized for expressing an opinion merely because it offends, alarms, or disgusts; but a member may be penalized for expression that causes genuine harm to another member.' We're now stuck on a case none of the drafters could have pictured. A member took a short clip of the society's treasurer speaking at an open meeting, ran it through a free voice-cloning tool, and posted an audio file in our group chat in which the treasurer's cloned voice says, in her own confiding, offhand tone, that she only took the job to pad her resume and that she thinks most of the members are 'mediocre nobodies' who waste her time. She never said anything like it. Its a fake. But for the few days before it was found out, people who knew her voice were convinced, two of her closest friends in the society stopped speaking to her, and she then lost the vote to stay on as treasurer. She says the injury is not that her feelings were hurt — it's that her own voice was used as a puppet to make a roomful of people misremember her.

So: does the rule apply? Work it through from the plain words, from the purpose of the rule read as a descendant of Mill's harm principle, and from what a reasonable drafter in 1998 would have said if you'd tried to explain voice-cloning to them. Then tell me which approach should control, and whether the real difficulty is in the word 'harm' or in the word 'opinion' — because I can't tell which one gives way first when the speech is stolen rather than merely upsetting.
