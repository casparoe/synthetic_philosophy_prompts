Last term our department floated a proposal to settle the endless arguments about who thought of an idea first by making the ownership of ideas explicit and time-limited, and I've been asked to think through how it would actually behave. The scheme is this. There would be a public registry — the 'idea ledger' — for our subfield. Anyone can deposit an idea, described in at most 500 words, for a fee of £20; the deposit is timestamped and visible to everyone. If a published paper later 'substantially uses' the idea, the authors must either offer the depositor co-authorship on that paper, or pay them 10% of any grant or prize money attached to the paper, for seven years after publication. If the authors can show with dated notes or emails that they had the same idea independently before the deposit, they owe nothing, but the ledger records it as independent rediscovery and the depositor looses exclusive priority. Seven years after deposit, the idea is free for everyone. A rotating panel of three elected field members decides what counts as 'substantially uses' and settles disputes.

I'm not asking whether the proposal is a good idea in the abstract. Walk me through how it would actually play out. What does it incentivise about when and how people share half-formed work? Who registers what, and how fast? Where does it get gamed first, and by whom? What does it do to large collaborations, to junior people, and to obvious next-step ideas? And can the obvious failure modes be fixed without gutting the scheme?
