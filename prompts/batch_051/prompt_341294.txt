I’m a philosophy of biology student and I keep tripping over the way molecular biology talks about DNA as a code. Genes “code for” proteins, the genetic code gets “transcribed” into RNA and then “translated” into protein, mutations are “misreads” or “misprints.” I know this way of talking is usually traced back to Francis Crick’s 1958 paper On Protein Synthesis, where he states the Sequence Hypothesis. From what I remember, he says roughly that the specificity of a piece of nucleic acid is expressed solely by the sequence of its bases, and that this sequence is a code for the amino acid sequence of a particular protein. I’m paraphrasing from memory, but that’s the gist.

What I want to understand is: what philosophical commitments does this practice actually presuppose? Does calling DNA a code commit you to something semantic or intentional in the cell—like a message being read by an interpreter—or can it just be a systematic mapping between base triplets and amino acids? Does the practice rule out any views of information, for example the idea that information must be for some agent or consumer? And what would have to change in how biologists talk if we replaced the code metaphor with purely causal or structural language? I'm less interested in what the term "code" means in ordinary English and more in what the practice of using it so confidently presupposes, and whether all its parts presuppose the same thing or whether transcription, translation, and coding are actually doing different philosophical work.
