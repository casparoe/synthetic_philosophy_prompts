I am a PhD student in political theory, and I have been developing a short essay around an idea I would like to pressure-test before a seminar. The essay argues that sortition and term limits, though often treated as complementary remedies for the pathologies of career politicians, actually rest on conflicting diagnoses of what is wrong with political professionalism—and that this tension matters for whether a single constitutional system could coherently adopt both at once.

Here is the core of the argument as it stands. Term limits, on my account, treat accumulated office-holding as the danger: the longer officials serve, the more they master the levers of power in ways that serve incumbency rather than the public, so the cure is forcibly interrupting careers before that mastery turns into self-dealing. Sortition, by contrast, treats the electoral race rather than the holding of office as the corrupting filter: it assumes that the qualities that make people good at winning elections—ambition, name recognition, fundraising ability, coalition management—are largely independent of, or even negatively correlated with, the qualities needed for sound public judgment, so the cure is to eliminate the filter and draw a representative sample instead. On this reading, term limits aim to stop professionals from governing for too long; sortition aims to stop the wrong kind of professional from governing at all. The two reforms converge in what they oppose but diverge in what they presuppose about political experience: term limits presuppose that political experience is real enough to be dangerous, while sortition presupposes that the apparent value of experience is largely an artifact of a selection process skewed toward the wrong traits.

I would be grateful for comments on whether this contrast is fair or too clean. In particular: Is it plausible to say that the two reforms really conflict in their underlying commitments, or are they better seen as targeting different moments in the same career pipeline and therefore as natural complements? Does my characterization of sortition unfairly downplay the way its defenders address competence—through briefing processes, expert witnesses, rotating oversight, and the internal division of labor in mini-publics? And is there a stronger way to frame the underlying disagreement, perhaps as a disagreement about whether governing competence is scarce and hard-won or widely distributed and best protected by diversity rather than expertise? I would rather have the essay fail now, in front of a colleague, than later in front of my committee.
