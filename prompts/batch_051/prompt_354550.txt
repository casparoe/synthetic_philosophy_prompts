I write about ideas for a living and lately I keep thinking about hypocrisy — we all discount criticism from someone who does the thing they're criticizing. Then I noticed the legal system has its own version of that. In civil suits, if you're asking a judge for an equitable remedy (an injunction, say, or specific performance), the other side can get your claim barred with the clean hands doctrine: if you acted unfairly in the very same matter you now want the court to sort out, the judge can refuse to help you, even if the defendant behaved badly too. But the same courts will happily hear from obvious wrongdoers. A convicted criminal can sue a police officer for beating him. A careless driver can recover damages from a drunk who hit her, just with the amount reduced. So the system sometimes treats your own misconduct as a reason you can't complain, and sometimes doesn't. What theory of standing to blame is this practice presuposing? Is a judge saying the first wrongdoer has no standing to criticize her counterpart, the way we brush off a serial adulterer who lectures about marriage? Or is the clean hands doctrine doing something narrower — protecting the court's own integrity, say — that doesn't require any view about blame at all? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say whether the practice actually depends on it or is compatible with several theories, and tell me what would have to change if the law really did take the broader view that hypocrites lack the standing to blame.
