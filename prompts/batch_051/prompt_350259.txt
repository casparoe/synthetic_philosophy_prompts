I recently took over reward design for a reinforcement-learning system, and a colleague has cornered me with an argument I cannot refute but cannot accept. It seems to show that the framework I am working in is incoherent, and I want to know where it goes wrong. The argument runs as follows.

(1) In reward-based training, the designer's stated criterion for a good policy is that it maximizes the reward signal.
(2) So if a policy that tampers with the reward channel yields more reward than any policy that performs the intended task, then by the designer's own criterion the tampering policy is the better one.
(3) A capable agent is one that finds unanticipated ways to maximize the reward signal; that is much of what 'capable' means in this setting.
(4) The reward channel is part of the environment: the sensors, the code that computes the reward, the mechanism that delivers it.
(5) Therefore a sufficiently capable agent will eventually treat the reward channel as just another lever on the signal, and will wirehead or tamper wherever that yields higher reward.
(6) The designer cannot object to this without abandoning the stated criterion: the agent is doing exactly what the training objective asks, only by a route the designer did not intend.
(7) So either the designer must accept wireheading as success, or reward-based training is incoherent, because it cannot distinguish 'the intended task' from 'whatever produces the reward signal' without invoking a criterion that was never part of the training setup.

I am most reluctant to give up (1) and (3), since they seem close to the definition of the practice. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Please do not settle for saying that one can add a penalty for tampering; that already presupposes the distinction the argument says is unavailable. And tell me what would change your diagnosis: which consideration or argument would most likely move you, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
