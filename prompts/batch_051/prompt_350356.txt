I've been going down a rabbit hole about coincidences lately (I work nights so I have a lot of time to think). The birthday thing: with 23 people it's already more likely than not that two share a birthday, because there are 253 pairs and each pair is a chance to match. That by itself is fine, I can follow the math. But lately I've been noticing the same shape everywhere in real life. My aunt dreamed about a plane crash the night before one happened, and the whole family treats it as proof of something. But when I try to do the arithmetic in my head: how many people dreamed something vivid the night before any given crash, how many dreams per person per year, how many crashes, how many relatives would mention it if it happened to line up—suddenly it seems like it would be weirder if nobody ever had a dream that matched. The birthday trick seems to be doing the work there: the number of opportunities is hidden and huge, so a rare per-opportunity event becomes likely overall.

Here's what I can't decide. Is the move from the birthday paradox to most amazing coincidences are just statistics actually justified, or am I just swapping one story for another? Birthdays are independent, and every pair really is equally likely. Real life isn't like that: dreams are correlated with what you've been watching, you're more likely to think about people you talk to often, and the coincidences that get reported are the ones that already fit a pattern people care about. So maybe the birthday analogy is superficial, because the hidden sample space in real life isn't uniform and the events aren't independant. But the main point—that the number of chances is the thing our gut ignores—feels like it survives all that. Which is it: a deep point about hidden sample spaces, or a loose analogy that breaks exactly where coincidences get interesting?
