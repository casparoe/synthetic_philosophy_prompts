I am a novelist, and I have been asked to give a ninety-minute craft seminar at a writers' conference. The audience will be fiction writers — most of them can discuss point of view, constrained form, and free indirect discourse in their sleep — but almost none of them play games. My working title is Choice as a Material: What Games Do That Novels Cannot. I do not want you to explain the topic to me; I want you to produce a teaching plan for the seminar.

The position the plan should land is this: the distinctive aesthetic achievement of games is not story, spectacle, or interactivity in the abstract, but agency — the design of a constrained space of possible actions in which the act of choosing is itself timed, weighted, and shaped the way a novelist shapes a sentence, so that the player experiences their own choices as aesthetically charged rather than merely functional. I want the audience to leave seeing agency as a designed material, not as authorial freedom handed to the player.

Plan the arc: what concept has to be in place before the idea can land, and in what order the remaining concepts must follow, with the dependencies made explicit. Pick the single example that will carry the whole session: say what features it must have and what features it must lack, and say why you are rejecting the more obvious candidates — a branching narrative on the page, or a big-budget story game whose choices mostly gate content. Map the misconceptions these particular writers will bring — for instance, that agency means more options, that interactivity is the enemy of artistic form, that a game with choices is just a novel with a branch — and tell me exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off rather than allowed to harden and then refuted. End with the one question or short exercise that would show me whether they have understood agency as a designed aesthetic object rather than as "letting the player do what they want." Finally, tell me what I should openly decline to explain at this level.

Act as a philosopher of art who also understands narrative and game design from the inside, and think carefully about the dependency structure before you settle on an order. Above all, be committed: choose one definition of agency, one arc, one example, and defend those choices, rather than offering me a menu of options.
