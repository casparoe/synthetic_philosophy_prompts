I spent most of yesterday evening arguing with a friend about how to run the enforcement division I direct in a mid-sized city. His position, which he kept grounding in Han Feizi, was that I keep making a category mistake: he said the ruler of a state should not depend on the benevolence of subordinates, because people are self-interested and virtue is too scarce and too hard to detect to build a system on. What actually governs behavior, he insisted, is reward and punishment applied through clear published standards, with those two handles kept firmly in the ruler's own hands. I pushed back that some of our best work — deciding a genuinely ambiguous safety case, refusing a bribe no camera would ever catch — comes from professional character rather than from incentives, and that an aggressive regime of quotas and penalties will be gamed, chase only what is measurable, and crowd out the judgment I prize. By the end I had convinced him of the crowding-out risk, but he had convinced me that I currently alternate between "be good" and "here is the bonus" with no rule for which applies.

I want you to turn that dispute into a procedure I can actually use. I direct a city department of roughly forty people doing permits and inspections. Decisions come up dozens of times a week; stakes run from trivial paperwork to life-safety enforcement; I can spend at most an hour with my two deputies on any single classification decision; and I must sign off on any change to how staff are supervised, paid, or disciplined. Please give me a reusable procedure, grounded in Han Feizi's legalist argument but taking the crowding-out worry seriously, for deciding whether a given function, role, or kind of decision should be governed primarily by incentives (rewards, penalties, quotas, audits), primarily by appeals to virtue and professional norms (hiring, mentoring, discretion), or by some ordered mix of the two — and for flagging where one regime is corrupting the other. Make it a checklist or an ordered set of tests usable by someone with no philosophical background, and mark clearly the points where judgment cannot be eliminated and the cases on which the procedure will misfire. Apply the procedure to three cases: tying inspectors to numeric citation targets; evaluating and rewarding a senior inspector whose value is mostly mentoring and detecting corruption that no metric records; and deciding whether to keep an informal norm of discretionary leniency for first-time minor violations or replace it with fixed fines. At least one application should give an answer I will not like, if that is what the procedure yields. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the procedure and the reasoning that supports it, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
