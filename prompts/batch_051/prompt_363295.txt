I'm a PhD student in ethics, writing on whether misleading is morally different from lying, and I feel like I can enumerate the standard moves but not see past them. I was rereading Augustine's On Lying and this bit has been bugging me—its not one of his famous lines, but it should be—from section 3: "But the fault of him who lies, is, the desire of deceiving in the uttering of his mind; whether he do deceive, in that he is believed when uttering the false thing; or whether he do not deceive, either in that he is not believed, or in that he utters a true thing with will to deceive, which he does not think to be true: wherein being believed, he does not deceive though it was his will to deceive: except that he deceives in so far as he is thought to know or think as he utters." That definition makes nearly all misleading come out as lying: if you utter something you know to be true while intending the other person to form a false belief, you've got the double-hearted act Augustine is describing. So I'm collecting arguments for the claim that misleading is morally on a par with lying, because I want to test whether Augustine's definition is doing the work or whether there's a better argument for moral equivalence that doesn't lean on his particular terminology.

Here's the list I already have, and I think these are all the standard ones:
(1) The intentionalist argument: the wrong is entirely in the intention to induce a false belief, so whether you do it through assertion or implicature is morally idle.
(2) The consequence argument: lying and misleading produce the same foreseeable doxastic harm, and the mode of delivery is not itself what matters.
(3) The virtue argument: both express the same vice of deceitfulness or double-mindedness; a truthful person wouldn't do either.
(4) The conversational-commitment argument: in normal conversation, what a speaker is responsible for includes the implicatures, so a true sentence used to convey a false proposition is still a false representation in the overall communicative act.
(5) The line-drawing argument: there's no non-arbitrary boundary between explicit false assertion and more indirect deception, so any moral distinction built on that boundary is suspect.

I need one more argument for the same conclusion—misleading is not morally better than lying—that has a different structure from all five. Just to be clear, "different structure" means it can't be a restatement of one of them, a special case of one, or a combination of two. Then say whether you think it actually succeeds, and how it interacts with Augustine's sentence above: does it vindicate his way of putting it, or does it show his definition is too broad or narrow to capture what's really at stake?
