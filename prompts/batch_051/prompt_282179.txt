I help moderate a mid-sized online forum for local politics and housing, and I'm caught in a dispute about our code of conduct. The clause at issue was drafted years before any of the current moderators joined, and it reads:

"3.4 Prohibited Hateful Content. Users may not post content that expresses hatred, contempt, or derision toward any person or class of persons on the basis of race, ethnicity, or national origin. This includes, but is not limited to, racial slurs and epithets, negative racial or ethnic stereotypes, and claims that any group is inferior, criminal, or unwelcome. Moderators may remove such content and sanction the account that posted it."

The drafters plainly had overt material in mind—slurs, swastikas, pseudoscientific claims about group intelligence. Now we have a case they did not anticipate. A long-time user, across many threads about schools, crime, and zoning, keeps posting variants of the same sentence: "The character of these neighborhoods always changes for the worse once the [named highway] stops being a boundary." The sentence never mentions race, and the user insists it is about "community character" and "crime, not race." But the posting pattern is severe and consistent, and a substantial part of the forum's readership—including several moderators—reads the phrase as a dog whistle: a coded way of saying, to those in the know, that Black residents are the problem. Half the moderation team treats this as obviously covered by 3.4; the other half says a clause aimed at hateful content cannot reach a sentence whose literal wording says nothing about any group.

My question isn't so much "what would you do" as: does §3.4, read coherently, cover this user's posts? Take the time to reason through the case as a philosopher of speech and interpretation. Please start by laying out at least three competing readings of the clause—the plain words, the drafters' purpose, and what a reasonable reader of the forum would understand—and say, for each, whether the dog whistle falls inside or outside the prohibition. Then defend a final classification, and be explicit about the point at which your reasoning is doing the heavy lifting: is it a claim about what "content" and "expresses" mean, about whether meaning includes uptake by part of the audience, about whether effect can substitute for intent, or about something else? Where would a coherent reading most plausibly fail to reach this user, and what would you have to add to the clause to make the coverage unambiguous?
