heres one that's been bugging me for a while, and I want the conceptual answer rather than a literature review. The standard overshoot-and-collapse argument says a society is basically one big integrated system with a carrying capacity, and when it hits the ceiling the elite double down (more monuments, more marginal land, more tribute) instead of backing off, so the whole thing fails suddenly: population crashes, institutions disappear, and the society is gone. That's the story for the Classic Maya, Easter Island, and the Late Bronze Age, more or less.

Now here's the development I cant stop thinking about. The old picture was built on elite records and big ceremonial sites: when the monuments stopped and the king disappeared from inscriptions, the society had 'collapsed.' The newer toolkit (lidar, household archaeology, isotope chemistry, careful settlement survey) lets us follow ordinary people rather than dynasties. And when you follow ordinary people, you keep finding mobility and re-organization, not disappearance: people move to less water-stressed places, farm differently, swap a divine king for a smaller local council, stay Maya (or whoever they were), and keep trading. Suppose we had an especially clean version of this revisionist picture for all three cases, strong enough to rule out a universal die-off or a clean end. Does the overshoot-and-collapse argument survive that, or does 'collapse' stop being a real category and become just a dramatic name for ordinary change?

If it survives, tell me which premise the new data actually undercuts and which premise is left holding up the conclusion, and state the revised claim in one or two sentences. If it doesn't survive, tell me what should be said instead about what happened when a state thins out and people disperse, and whether anything from the old story is still worth keeping (carrying capacity, elite misreading of stress signals, the brittleness of specialized complex systems). And one last thing: if fine-grained data kills 'collapse,' does it kill the opposite word 'resilience' too, or do the two terms fail in different ways?
