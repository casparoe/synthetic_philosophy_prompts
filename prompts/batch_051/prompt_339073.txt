I have been trying to figure out what I actually believe about fairness when it comes to buying, selling, and the way people come to have money, and my reactions to particular cases are pulling me in different directions. I would like you to help me find a principle, or a small set of principles, that captures my verdicts, and to tell me plainly which of my verdicts have to be revised.

Here are the cases.

1. A classmate wants a limited-release pair of sneakers, knows what they usually resell for, and still buys them from me for twice the retail price. This seems fine to me.

2. A different classmate uses a bot to buy up most of the tickets to a concert the moment they go on sale, then resells them at three times face value to fans who pay willingly. I think this is not fine, even though I cannot find a clean difference between this and the sneaker case. That bothers me.

3. A star basketball player in our town negotiates a deal in which two dollars from every ticket sold to watch him play goes directly to him. People attend voluntarily, and he ends up making far more than his teammates and coaches. I think this is fine.

4. A classmate inherits a large sum from her grandmother, who earned the money honestly. I think this is fine, even though my classmate did nothing to earn it.

5. At a flea market, an adult buys a rare comic book from a ten-year-old for five dollars, knowing it is worth two hundred. I think this is not fine; the kid did not understand what he was giving up.

6. A classmate whose family is struggling agrees to do yard work for a neighbor for far less than the work is worth, because he needs the money. The neighbor knows he is underpaying. I think this is not fine, even though both agreed.

7. Someone steals a phone and sells it to an innocent buyer who does not know it was stolen. When the original owner finds out, I think the buyer should have to give it back, even though the buyer paid for it.

I started with the idea that a holding is fair if it came about through a chain of exchanges in which everyone involved consented, and no one was forced or lied to. That principle handles cases 1, 3, and 4, but it seems to say that cases 5 and 6 are fair, which I do not believe, and it is unclear what it says about case 7, since the innocent buyer consented and was not lied to, but the chain began with a theft.

Is there a better principle, or a small set of them, that gets all or most of these cases? If some of my verdicts have to go, I want to know which ones and why. I would also like the principle to settle a further case I keep going back and forth on: whether it would be fair for our school district to put a small surcharge on the basketball player's extra earnings to pay for lunches for students who cannot afford them. I lean toward saying the lunches should be funded, but I cannot see how that does not treat his fair earnings as up for grabs.

Please do not force my cases into a neat framework if they do not actually fit. If I am wrong to expect one principle to do all of this work, or if some of my verdicts are simply inconsistent with one another, I would rather be told that directly.
