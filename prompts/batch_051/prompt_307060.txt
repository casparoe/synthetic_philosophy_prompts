You are a philosopher of mind who works on the epistemology of consciousness science, with particular familiarity with the access/phenomenal consciousness distinction, global workspace theory, and the debate over phenomenal overflow. Think carefully before committing; begin by steelmanning both sides of the dispute I describe, then give your own view.

I have an epistemic rule for deciding which systems I treat as having morally relevant conscious experience, and it is this: only access-conscious properties count as evidence. Contents that are not available for report, reasoning, or the guidance of behavior — no matter how vividly introspected or confidently described — are excluded from my judgment procedure. I adopted the rule after reading about Block's phenomenal/access distinction, global workspace theory, Sperling-style partial-report results, change blindness, and the more recent no-report and overflow literature. What struck me was that every piece of evidence in that literature is ultimately read out through access-dependent channels: a forced-choice response, a behavioral cue, a neural signature selected because it tracks reportable content. The hypothesis of phenomenal consciousness outrunning access never seems to contribute an observation of its own; it appears only as an explanation of observations that access itself already licenses. Since I cannot inspect phenomenology directly and do not have unlimited time, the rule "build only on access profiles" looked to me like a sensible division of labor, perhaps even an unavoidable one.

A colleague I respect says the rule is not an epistemic shortcut but a disciplined-sounding way of begging the question. She says I am confusing the channel through which evidence arrives with the target of the inquiry. A rule that treats access-transcending phenomenal states as inadmissible guarantees that any overflow hypothesis cannot lose, because its distinctive predictions — that there is unreportable phenomenal character — are classified as unusable by construction. She also says the rule can do ethical damage: if there are states that matter morally but are not globally broadcast, not available to executive control, or not reportable — in certain disorders of consciousness, in infants, or in non-human animals — my policy would license ignoring them, not because they lack the relevant property but because they cannot present evidence through the one channel I have decided to trust. She thinks I have smuggled global workspace theory into my evidence standard and then counted its fit with the record as independent support.

I want to know whether my rule is defensible as a policy: a shortcut for a bounded agent who cannot inspect phenomenology directly, not an attempt to settle the metaphysics. I am not asking for a survey of the consciousness literature, and I am not asking you to resolve the access/phenomenal debate. I am asking you to assess the shortcut itself.

Specifically:

(1) Construct the strongest version of my policy, stated precisely enough that it makes sense to ask where it fails.

(2) Give the strongest case against it, including the conditions under which it would be systematically misleading.

(3) Say whether a repaired version can keep the practical benefits — refusing unfalsifiable claims, avoiding over-crediting introspective intensity, keeping decisions tractable — without deciding the access/phenomenal debate in advance. If such a version exists, spell out what it would instruct me to do differently in a concrete case, such as a patient with a disorder of consciousness, a non-human animal, or a candidate AI system.

(4) Propose at least one observation, test, or diagnostic — introspective, behavioral, or conceptual — that would tell me whether I am using a defensible epistemic division of labor or just protecting a prior, and say what each outcome would imply.
