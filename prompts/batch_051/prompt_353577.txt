I chair my university's academic technology review commitee, which is a boring way of saying I sit through vendor demos and then help decide whether some piece of teaching software gets switched on for about eight thousand students. We meet twice a month in term time, and once a tool is in the LMS it is basically impossible to rip out, so we're making fast decisions that last for years. The dean gives us ninety minutes per tool and signs off on anything over ten grand, and the rest of the committee — an economist, a statistician, the registrar, two students — will never read Foucault; I've accepted that.

I'm a philosophy professor, so I keep trying to bring Foucault into the room, but every time it collapses into the question of whether the tool has a camera pointing at students, and I know that's wrong. What actually matters in Discipline and Punish, as I read it, is the combination: hierarchical observation, normalizing judgment, and the examination, the thing where each person gets turned into a case with a record, and the record feeds back into how they're treated and how they come to manage themselves. Plus the power/knowledge point that the proctoring suite or the dashboard isn't just watching — it's producing official knowledge about the student that changes what can be done to them and by them. My problem is I've never managed to turn that into something the registrar and the two students can run in twenty minutes.

So write me a standing decision procedure, a checklist in words with questions in order, that the committee can use on any proposed teaching technology to decide whether it imports disciplinary power in something like Foucault's sense, and whether that means no, yes-with-safeguards, or "this has become a values question and a person has to decide." Each question should say what counts as a red flag and what counts as passing. It has to be usable by people who have never read a page of Foucault, in about twenty minutes, before the vendor's enterprise pricing arrives. And I want you to be honest about three things: which of Foucault's distinctions you kept and which you flattened or dropped to make it usable, where the procedure is likely to misfire because of that, and where the procedure has to import some minimal normative stance to get from "this is disciplinary" to "we should not adopt it" — since Foucault is doing genealogy, not procurement policy, and the step from description to decision is were the real disagreement lives.

Then run the procedure on three tools in front of us this term. One, an AI proctoring suite that watches students on webcam during exams, flags gaze direction and background noise, and auto-generates a misconduct report for the instructor. Two, a student success dashboard that pulls LMS clicks, card-swipe data, and grades, assigns every student a dropout-risk score, and nudges advisors to intervene. Three, a classroom response app with a participation leaderboard that shows who hasn't answered — the one the committee already loves and wants to approve, so I'd like at least one worked case where the procedure tells us to reject or fence the tool we're inclined to like. Finally, say what the procedure can't do, and where it will punt or sort something wrong.
