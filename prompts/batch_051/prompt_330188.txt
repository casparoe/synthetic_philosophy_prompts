Something I keep going back and forth on, and I dont have any training in this so forgive me if its muddled. A while back our government issued a formal apology for something the state did about a hundred years ago, before anyone now alive was born. People I know found it genuinely moving, and quite a few said it was long overdue. But if my neighbour's grandfather had swindled my grandfather out of the family farm, and the neighbour apologised at a family gathering, most of those same people would say that's awkward or even a bit hollow, since he didn't do it and I never actually suffered the loss. So we seem to think a country can meaningfully apologise for what long-dead officials did, but a person can't meaningfully apologise for what a long-dead relative did — and more than that, I'd be annoyed if the state refused to apologise, while I'd feel no such expectation toward the neighbour. Is that difference defensible, or is it mostly stage effects? Give me the strongest principle you can find that lets states apologise across generations without making the neighbour's apology meaningful too, then tell me honestly whether it survives that test.
