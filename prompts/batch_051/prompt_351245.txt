ok so i'm doing a presentation on Augustine's Confessions, Book XI, for my hs philosophy class, and i've been collecting the standard ways people read the part where he recites a psalm from memory to explain how he measures time. the passage i'm working from is:

"I am about to repeat a psalm that I know. Before I begin, my attention is extended to the whole; but when I have begun, as much of it as becomes past by my saying it is extended in my memory; and the life of this action of mine is divided between my memory, on account of what I have repeated, and my expectation, on account of what I am about to repeat; yet my consideration is present with me, through which that which was future may be carried over so that it may become past. Which the more it is done and repeated, by so much (expectation being shortened) the memory is enlarged, until the whole expectation be exhausted, when that whole action being ended shall have passed into memory. And what takes place in the entire psalm, takes place also in each individual part of it, and in each individual syllable: this holds in the longer action, of which that psalm is perchance a portion; the same holds in the whole life of man, of which all the actions of man are parts; the same holds in the whole age of the sons of men, of which all the lives of men are parts."

the readings on my list so far are:

(1) the psychological one — he's cashing out his earlier claim that past and future exist only in the mind, so "measuring time" is really measuring the stretching-out of his own attention (the distentio animi), not something in the world outside him;

(2) the theological one — the example matters because of the contrast with eternity: expectation shrinking while memory grows shows the soul as scattered and stretched across time, unlike God's eternal present where nothing slips away, so the psalm is supposed to make you feel that difference;

(3) the performative one — augustine isn't just describing how attention carries the future into the past; the reader has to do exactly that with the passage itself (holding the start in memory, anticipating the end), so the text demonstrates what it says.

i need one more genuine reading of this passage, not a rewording, special case, or combo of those three. i keep half-remembering one about the nested levels he lists at the end — syllable, whole psalm, whole life, whole age of history. is that actually a standard reading in its own right, or is it just (1) again? if it is its own thing, say what it adds that (1)–(3) miss; and if its not really distinct, give me the actual fourth reading i'm missing and say whether it's any good.
