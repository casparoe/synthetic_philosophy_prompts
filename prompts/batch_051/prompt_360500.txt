Over the past several years I have come to accept what is now usually called the singleton hypothesis, though I want to state it more carefully than it often is. The hypothesis, as I hold it, is this: if Earth-originating civilization survives long enough, the highest level of decision-making will tend to become a single integrated agency — not necessarily a single mind or a literal world government, but a unified system of control, coordination, or enforced norms with which no rival agency is in a position to compete on equal terms. I think of this as a default trajectory, an attractor, rather than merely one possibility among many. The reasons are broadly structural: as the destructive power of the most capable agents grows, the cost of coordination failure rises, and the returns to unified control increase; competitive multipolar orders generate arms races, collective action problems, and destructive conflict, and those pressures select for either extinction or unification. I do not claim that a singleton would be good, only that it is where the dynamics point. And I want to know what would count against this.

What I am asking for is not a bibliography or a list of sources, and not a discussion of whether a singleton would be desirable. I want a map of the hypothesis's exposure: the observations, arguments, formal results, historical analogies, and conceptual objections that would most undermine it, ranked from those that would be fatal to those that would be merely awkward. I want you to be precise about the difference between evidence that should lower my confidence and evidence that should make me abandon the framework altogether. A committed proponent will concede that certain things genuinely count against the hypothesis while dismissing others as attacks on a strawman; I want you to say which is which, and why.

In particular, please address the following. First, what does the hypothesis predict that a robust multipolar alternative does not, and which of those predictions could actually be disconfirmed? Second, is there any reason to think that a competitive multipolar order could itself be a stable long-run equilibrium once the stakes are very high, and do the historical, biological, or strategic records give us any genuine evidence either way? Third, has the hypothesis — or have I, in the way I just stated it — been qualified so that no possible outcome would count against it, and if so, is that a problem for a hypothesis of this kind, or is it the appropriate modesty of a view about the very long run? Finally, I would personally be most moved by evidence that, under conditions relevantly similar to the ones the hypothesis invokes, uncoordinated or loosely coordinated systems have persisted, thrived, and kept the relevant failure modes in check without converging on a unified decision procedure; but I do not want the whole answer organized around that preference.

The deliverable should be a structured assessment of where the singleton hypothesis risks being false, and whether it risks anything at all.
