For a postgrad seminar on causation I'm planning to teach Pearl's ladder and the do-calculus properly, not just wave at it. Before I read any more of the technical literature, I want the desiderata: what any adequate formal framework for causal inference has to deliver. Give me the list — the cases it must get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the things it has to be able to say — with the case or example behind each. I can think of a few myself (separating prediction from intervention: a barometer reading predicts rain but you can't make it rain by fiddling with the barometer; conditioning on a collider and creating a spurious association; saying what would have happened for a particular unit, not just the average treatment effect; knowing when an effect is identifiable from observational data alone and when you actually need an experiment), but I want the full spec. Then mark which of these are genuinely forced and which are one camp's preference — I'm suspcious that the third rung, counterfactuals about individuals, gets treated as an obvious requirement when it's a much stronger commitment than people admit. Then score Pearl's SCM plus do-calculus against the list, briefly. And if any pair of desiderata can't be jointly satisfied, say so; that's the bit I most want to see.
