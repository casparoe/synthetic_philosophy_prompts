Here's an argument I keep half-convincing myself with, and I can't tell whether it's sound or whether one of the steps is doing something sneaky.

(1) A person can be genuinely harmed without any physical injury or loss of property: being systematically humiliated, deceived into a one-sided relationship, or having one's reputation destroyed are all real harms.
(2) What makes such wrongs real harms is that they set back a genuine interest of the victim — in dignity, autonomy, trust, or social standing.
(3) People hold genuine interests of these very kinds inside virtual worlds: they care about their standing in a guild, about promises other players make to them, about not being conned or coerced out of what they've built up.
(4) So a player's action in a virtual world can set back another player's genuine interests in just the same way that offline humiliation or deception does.
(5) If a player's action sets back another player's genuine interests in just the same way that offline humiliation or deception does, then it harms them really, not merely 'within the fiction'.
(6) Therefore at least some virtual harms are real harms, and 'it's only a game' is not always a defence.

I suspect the trouble is either an equivocation in (4) between haveing the same type of interest and having the same interest, or a slide in (5) between the harm being real and the object of the interest being real. But I can't make either objection stick. Is the argument valid? If not, say exactly which step fails and under what reading, and give the strongest repair of that step. If it is valid, say what that means for the line 'it's only a game' — does it come out false, or just much weaker than people think? Please write the answer in British English.
