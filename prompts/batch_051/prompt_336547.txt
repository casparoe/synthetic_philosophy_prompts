I've been chewing on a parallel between decision theory and criminal law and I want to know whether its deep or just pattern-matching. In the Ellsberg paradox, people will pay a premium to bet on an urn whose proportions are known over an urn whose proportions are not, even when the expected values are identical: the aversion targets the ambiguity (unknown probabilities), not the risk (known probabilities). The void-for-vagueness doctrine seems to have the same shape. A criminal statute is invalid when its boundaries are too indefinite for a reasonable person to know in advance whether a kind of conduct falls inside them. The defect is the uncertainty about the line itself, not whether some acts the statute reaches deserve punishment—as if vague rules carry an ambiguity premium that the legal system refuses to pay. Is that a genuine identity of structure, or a loose analogy? I'm not asking for case law or citations; I want the mapping. So lay out the mapping carefully: what corresponds to the known urn, the unknown urn, the premium, and the decision-maker. Say what would have to be true of both sides for the results to transfer. And name at least one thing the parallel predicts that turns out false—somewhere the law does not behave like an ambiguity-averse agent. Finally, tell me what would change your mind about whether the parallel is deep or superficial, and where your mapping is most likely to break.
