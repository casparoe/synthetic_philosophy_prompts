Here's a counterfactual I've been chewing on. Suppose that around 1985, a wealthy democratic country passes a constitutional amendment saying that every health, safety, and environmental regulation must be justified by the minimax regret criterion. By that I mean: for any proposed rule, the agency has to lay out the feasible options and possible states of the world, compute for each option its regret in each state (the shortfall between what that option delivers and what the best option would have delivered in that same state), and then choose the option whose worst-case regret is smallest. Expected-value reasoning is out: no multiplying harms by probabilities, no cost-benefit analysis, no admissable way to smuggle probabilities back in. The amendment's drafters, knowing agencies need something tractable, also require that for a single rule the states of the world be reduced to two: roughly "the rule is needed to avert the harm" and "the rule is pointless or over-cautious." The two kinds of mistake—failing to regulate a real harm and regulating when there was nothing to prevent—have to be put on a common scale (monetized lives, QALYs, whatever the agency can defend). A constitutional court can strike down a regulation if it finds the agency's regret arithmetic demonstrably wrong, but the court may not substitute its own judgment about which harms matter more.
