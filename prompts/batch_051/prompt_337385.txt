Everyone in my circle keeps saying LLM hallucination is best understood as bullshit in Frankfurt's sense, not lying and not ordinary error. The argument, as I hear it, goes roughly: a liar has to know or at least believe the true answer and aim away from it, so calling a model a liar smuggles in intent; Frankfurt's bullshitter is different, because what defines him is that he just does not care whether what he says is true or false. Since a language model will happily emit whichever continuation the distribution favours and never checks it against the world, they say the term 'bullshit' fits it exactly, and 'hallucination' is a dodge because it suggests a mis-seeing rather than a lack of concern for the truth. I've seen this used to conclude that we should stop evaluating models primarily on truthfulness and start thinking of them as bullshit artists.

I want you to audit that analogy rather than just accept or reject it. Give me a correspondence table: speaker, belief, intention to deceive, the self being presented, the audience being managed, and the indifference to truth. Then lay out the disanalogies and seperate the ones that matter for the intended conclusion from the ones that are just a difference in furniture. In particular: can a system with no mind be indifferent to anything, or does that already break the analogy? Does the bullshitter's effort to hide that he is not trying to get things right have any counterpart in a transformer? And if the analogy fails at the level of the model, does it succeed at the level of the training process or the people who ship it, which is maybe where the real bullshit sits? End by saying what the best category actually is for LLM hallucination and whether the original conclusion survives. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
