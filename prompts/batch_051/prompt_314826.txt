I'm a PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science, and I've been slogging through the multiple realizability literature for a qualifier. Here's what's bugging me. The argument is more than fifty years old and somehow its still going: Putnam's original point was that one and the same mental state — pain, say — could be realized by a brain, an octopus nervous system, or a silicon system, so psychological kinds can't be identified with physical kinds. That was supposed to underwrite functionalism and block reductionism. Fine. But the debate has since settled into three camps that have barely moved. The reductionist camp says multiple realizability is an artifact of individuating mental states coarsely and neural states finely; match the grains and the examples dissolve. The "real but inert" camp grants that kinds are multiply realized but says that does no anti-reductionist work — Kim's disjunction problem, Shapiro's point that wildly multiply realized kinds are bad news for a science of functional kinds, and Polger and Shapiro's book-length version arguing that genuine multiple realization is much rarer than the textbooks say, because most alleged realizers differ in ways that matter to the realized kind. The defenders, now a minority, reply that those objections define "relevant to the kind" too narrowly and miss how evolution reuses a capacity across genuinely different substrates — the main recent defense from Aizawa and Gillett treats realizational relevance as multi-dimensional, so variation along one dimension doesn't make the realizers irrelevant. Everyone concedes there is some variation in how brains implement capacities; nobody moves about whether it matters. The examples are allways pain, the octopus, and a silicon simulation; the grain objection gets re-raised; the same moves get re-replied.

My working theory is that "multiple realizability" is actually three different claims wearing one name. Sometimes it's an empirical bet about how variable realizers actually are across species. Sometimes it's a modal claim about merely possible systems, settled by intuition rather than data. And sometimes it's a methodological norm about how fine-grained an explanation of a capacity has to be. If each camp is defending a different one of those claim-types, the debate would look exactly as stuck as this one does.

So diagnose the structure of the disagreement. Is it a hidden empirical question, a verbal dispute, an ill-posed question, or a clash of starting intuitions about what counts as "the same mechanism"? And given the diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a cross-species dataset at a fixed grain, an explicit convention about grains, a formal result about disjunctive kinds, or changing the question? Or should we expect no progress, and why? Lead with the bottom line, not a literature review. Max 500 words.
