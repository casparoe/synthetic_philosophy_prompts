I've been rereading the Grand Inquisitor chapter in The Brothers Karamazov, and I keep getting stuck on how the old cardinal's argument maps onto the way I actually live. He basically says, if I'm remembering it right, that Christ overestimated human beings by offering them freedom instead of miracle, mystery, and authority. Most people, the Inquisitor thinks, are too weak to bear the burden of choosing; they'd rather be happy and secure, with someone else making the hard calls, than be left alone with the terrible gift of freedom. Give them bread and someone to bow to, and they'll hand over their conscience gladly.

But here's the thing. When my cousin stays with a partner who controls her money and decides who she can see, I'm horrified and tell her she should leave. I think she shouldn't trade her agency for comfort. Same with my brother who joined a church that tells him what to read, who to marry, and what to think. I say it's bad that he's happy because he surrendered his judgment. Yet I'm also glad the government makes seatbelts mandatory, taxes cigarettes hard, and won't let me buy certain drugs. I don't object to those restrictions by saying I have a right to choose my own risks. And if a friend said the state is treating us like children, I'd kind of shrug.

So I have an asymmetry in my ordinary judgments: I condemn the individual-level trade of freedom for happiness or security, but I accept, maybe even endorse, the state-level trade of freedom for health and safety. Is there a principled difference? Or does the Grand Inquisitor's point that most people would rather be happy than free force me to either accept that my cousin should stay and my brother is fine, or reject seatbelt laws as an insult to adult judgment? What would it take to keep the asymmetry without just being inconsistent?
