Write a dialogue between two friends, Mina and Paul, who are arguing about a passage from the Zhuangzi, in James Legge's translation of the “The Full Understanding of Life” chapter. Here’s the passage:

“For those who wish to avoid caring for the body, their best plan is to abandon the world. Abandoning the world, they are free from its entanglements. Free from its entanglements, their (minds) are correct and their (temperament) is equable. Thus correct and equable, they succeed in securing a renewal of life, as some have done. In securing a renewal of life, they are not far from the True (Secret of their being).”

The background issue is the paradox of trying not to try: making effortless action a goal seems to add effort. Mina thinks this passage is a response to that paradox—it says stop trying to manage your life, and effortlessness comes when you give up the whole project. Paul thinks the passage just reinstates the paradox at the level of world-abandonment, because “their best plan is to abandon the world” is still a plan, something you can aim at, fail at, and become self-conscious about. Make them argue it out. Both characters should be genuinely good at philosophy, and neither should be a strawman. They should respond to each other’s actual moves, not just take turns making speeches. Let the dialogue go at least six substantial exchanges, and make them get somewhere—maybe they discover a distinction between trying to abadon the world and simply ceasing to be the kind of agent who relates to it that way.
