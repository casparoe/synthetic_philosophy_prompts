I'm a philosophy of science PhD and the graduate rep on my university's research-integrity committee, and I need to circulate a short memo before Thursday's meeting. The case is a psychology professor — call her Alvarez — who published a result four years ago showing that a certain training intervention reduces confirmation bias. The effect was predicted by a computational model she developed with two other labs, and the model has held up in several independent tests. Last month a postdoc elsewhere posted a preprint with three failed replications of the intervention. Alvarez replied that the failures must have a hidden flaw, and she has refused to share her task materials or rerun the analysis. Her reasoning, roughly: she knows the effect is real, and since she knows it, any apparently negative evidence is by definition misleading, so there's no scientific obligation to chase it. Our policy lists reckless disregard for the accuracy of the research record as sanctionable misconduct. Half the committee thinks refusing to engage a direct replication failure is exactly that; the other half thinks sanctioning her would punish well-founded confidence and turn every preprint into a subpoena.

I keep feeling this is just the dogmatism paradox wearing a lab coat, and I'd like the memo to work through that question rather than hide from it: does knowing-level confidence license you to dismiss counterevidence, and what should an institution do when that stance collides with a norm of engaging replications? Please draft the memo with a concrete recommendation among (a) close the matter with no action, (b) issue a letter of concern requiring her to share materials and run a preregistered direct replication within six months, or (c) open a full misconduct inquiry. Make sure the memo honestly presents the strongest consideration on the side you don't recommend, not a strawman. And since I'll have to defend this in the meeting, tell me how confident you are in the recommendation and single out the point you're least sure of.
