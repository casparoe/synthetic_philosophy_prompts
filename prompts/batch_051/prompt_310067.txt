I'm running a small epistemology reading group with a mix of grad students and people who've never read any of the anti-luminosity literature, and I want a handout that sorts people by what they actually think before we get into the argument. The topic is the KK principle and luminosity. Just to fix terms: KK is the thesis that if you know p, you're in a position to know that you know p; a condition is luminous when, whenever it obtains, you're in a position to know it obtains.

Could you write a "which self-knowledge position do you actually hold?" quiz—say eight to ten questions—in the form of a decision tree or a scored inventory? Every question should be a concrete case, not a request for a label: a room warming through imperceptible increments while someone is asked each minute whether they feel hot; a dial you can read only to within a few notches; being asked whether you know that you know something under time pressure; a first-order judgment that's true only by luck. Build the tree from the commitments that actually split the positions, not from a list of names. The branching commitments I can see are: whether any nontrivial condition is luminous; whether phenomenal states like feeling cold are luminous even if knowledge isn't; whether knowledge requires a margin for error; and whether the word "knows" changes content between the first and second order in the way contextualists describe.

The leaves I need at least: the strict S5-style KKist who accepts both positive and negative introspection without exception; the phenomenal-luminosity defender who still denies KK because knowledge isn't among the luminous conditions; the full anti-luminosity margin-for-error view on which no nontrivial condition is luminous and KK fails; the contextualist or indexicalist who says KK is fine in any fixed context and apparent failures are context shifts; and an acquaintance view on which asking do you know that you know is a category mistake for basic self-knowledge. If the logical space has combinations of answers no one of these leaves fills, flag the empty cells and say briefly whether each is empty because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. And flag any two leaves the questions can't actually seperate. No citations or reading lists needed—write it all in your own words.
