A friend has been pressing me with an argument that, he says, shows we should prefer that the people we love never grieve, and that anyone who resists this has not thought clearly about what it is for an emotion to be bad. I find the conclusion almost unbearable, but the argument is cleaner than I would like. Here it is as I understand it.

(1) If a mental state is intrinsically bad — bad in itself, and not merely because of its causes or effects — then, other things equal, a life with less of it is a better life.

(2) Grief is intrinsically bad. Whatever it may do for us, its felt character — the pain, the disorientation, the way it narrows attention — is not an unfortunate accompaniment; that is what grief is.

(3) Grief is not a means to any further good that could not be had without it. It arrives after the loss, so it cannot prevent the loss; and it tends to impair, rather than serve, the repair of a life.

(4) So, other things equal, a person who suffers a loss and grieves is worse off than a person who suffers the very same loss and does not grieve.

(5) Therefore, if I could choose for someone I love between two otherwise identical lives — one containing their loss of a person they love followed by grief, the other containing that same loss without grief — I should choose the second.

(6) But that verdict seems to me perverse. A person who loses someone they loved and does not grieve has not escaped something bad; they have failed to love properly. Part of what it is to love someone is to be changed, stopped, disoriented by losing them. A love that left no such mark would be a shallow love.

(7) So the argument appears to show that, if I love someone, I should prefer for them a life in which they love shallowly. That cannot be the right conclusion.

I suspect the trouble lies somewhere between (4) and (5), in the move from other things equal less grief is better to prefer the no-grief life, but I do not trust that suspicion, and I know it may simply be where I would most like the mistake to be. I am unwilling to give up (2): grief really does hurt. And I am unwilling to give up (6): the absence of grief after such a loss would be a defect, not an improvement. Tell me which premise or step you reject, and what rejecting it costs. Then say how confident you are in that diagnosis, and identify the point you are least sure about.
