I've been going around in circles on something and I want it checked. I work a bit on AI alignment, and I've been calling this idea 'past-extorsion' (maybe 'self-blackmail' is better): every surrogate goal is an acausal threat issued by your past self against your future self. The setup: you (or an AI) can't directly optimize the terminal thing, so you install a proxy — sweetness for calories, status for fitness, a smile-count for "human flourishing" — as a way of binding your own future behavior. The past self can't causally reach in and enforce that, so the enforcement has to be acausal: the future self keeps chasing the proxy not because it still tracks the original goal but because dropping it would mean the earlier self's whole architecture of choices stops making sense. The threat, roughly, is "if you abandon this, you're not the same agent anymore," and it works because the future self has inherited the precommitments.

My stronger claim is that there is no principled difference between terminal and surrogate goals, only a difference in how long the hostage has been held. Reproduction is a surrogate for fitness, fitness is a surrogate for whatever made fitness count back in the day, and at the bottom there's no terminal value, just an old precommitment whose randsom terms we've forgotten. I know there's a literature on hyperbolic discounting and the self as a bunch of bargaining agents (Ainslie, Schelling), and I know "instrumental convergence" for AIs, but I haven't seen the specific move of saying surrogate goals just *are* acausal threats across time.

So: is that move coherent? Has someone said it under another name? Does it actually collapse the terminal/surrogate distinction, or is there a stable difference I'm missing? Give me the three strongest objections and tell me which, if any, is fatal rather than just uncomfortable.
