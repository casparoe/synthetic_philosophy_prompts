In my fiction I keep using, and noticing in the novels I read, a structural device that I can feel but cannot name, and I want to know whether criticism or philosophy of language has carved it precisely. Three instances. A novel whose first sentence declares that everything after it is true: the declaration is not part of the story it introduces but an instruction about how to take the story. A character who finds a letter: the letter's contents matter, but so does the fact that it is a letter, because that wrapper tells the reader to treat its sentences as one character addressing another from a particular moment and with particular motives. A narrator who presents the book that follows as a recovered manuscript: the act of presentation changes the kind of object the inner text is before any of its sentences are read.

I understand that these outer announcements carry meaning of their own, and that the same sentence can change meaning when moved from inside one wrapper to inside another. What I lack is an account of where the meaning resides, how deeply such wrappers can nest, and why some wrappers feel internal to the work while others feel like mere scaffolding. Is there an established concept for this distinction between the message of the wrapper and the message of what is wrapped, and for the way one layer tells the reader how to decode the next? If the nearest concepts only partly fit, I want the boundaries drawn: how does this differ from ordinary narrative framing, from an unreliable narrator, or from saying as opposed to showing—and at what point does the nesting stop being meaningful rather than merely clever?
