I have been using Asimov's Three Laws of Robotics in a course on the value specification problem, and I keep coming back to a difficulty that deserves a much more careful treatment than it usually gets. It is easy to say that the Laws fail, but it is harder to say precisely how far one could get by taking a single word in the First Law seriously and trying to specify it. The First Law, roughly, forbids a robot from injuring a human being and also treats standing by while a human being is harmed as itself a violation. The word doing the unacknowledged work is harm. The Laws treat harm as though it were a detectable property that could be handed to an engineer, but harm is contested moral vocabulary, and any workable alignment proposal must either specify it or admit where the specification stops. I want a working definition of harm to a human being that an autonomous robot with excellent perception and causal reasoning could apply, where this definition would serve as the robot's only specification of what it must avoid, and that sorts the following cases correctly.

Let me fix two endpoints before giving you the hard cases. If a robot breaks a patient's arm to prevent him from leaving a locked psychiatric ward without authorization, I consider that a clear case of harm, even if one ends up thinking the robot did the right thing. If a robot beats a person at chess and the person is left feeling humiliated and disappointed, I do not consider that harm, although the robot could have predicted the unpleasant feeling. On the remaining cases I genuinely do not know what I think. A robot truthfully tells a terminally ill patient, at her request, a diagnosis she had been shielded from, and she falls into a severe depression. A robot hands a cigarette to a competent adult who asks for one, knowing that it raises her long-term risk of cancer. A robot amputates a trapped hiker's hand against his shouted protests, because the alternative is that he will die before rescue arrives. A robot, at the instruction of an owner who appears to be in the grip of a manic episode, deletes the only photographs of the owner's deceased grandchild. A robot lets a person with serious heart disease order and eat an extremely rich meal whose risks she understands and says she accepts. And a robot stands by while a person attempts suicide, because the person has repeatedly and sincerely instructed it never to interfere with her decisions.

I want you to propose a definition, run it through all eight cases, say where it yields a verdict you would not want to defend, and then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict. In doing so, take a position on whether harm covers a consented-to increase in the risk of future harm, psychological suffering, the impairing of a person's interests without their awareness, and physical damage inflicted to prevent a greater harm. Also say what your definition implies for the First Law's clause about allowing harm through inaction. Please write your answer in continuous prose, as though in a letter to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no tables, and no bold or italic type. Use American English throughout.
