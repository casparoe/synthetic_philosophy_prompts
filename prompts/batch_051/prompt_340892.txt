Ok so I just got back from a three-hour argument with a friend about Sellars and the myth of the given, and I'm panicking a little because I have a talk next month defending a Sellarsian line and my Q&A prep is basically nonexistant. The friend kept pushing the baby-and-dog objection: a preverbal kid can see a red ball and know where it is, a dog knows when the bowl is empty, so perceptual knowledge can't require the heavy conceptual capacities Sellars says it does. I kept coming back with "it's a cause, not a reason," but he pointed out that this makes perception epistemically idle, and I didn't have a clean answer. That exact exchange is what I'm going to get after the talk.

The thesis I'm defending is this. Every attempt to carve out a pre-conceptual, epistemically basic stratum of experience — sense data, raw feels, perceptual seemings, nonconceptual content — is just the myth of the given coming back in a new outfit. My argument: the moment you treat a state as a justifier, you are already placing it in the space of reasons, which means assigning it a content that stands in rational relations to the belief it justifies. That content has to be conceptually articulated in exactly the way the given was supposed to avoid. If instead you refuse to assign it conceptual content so it can stay a bare feel, then it can cause belief, but causes don't justify.

The audience is not a Sellars-specialist crowd, so I'm not worried about exegetical objections to how I read him. It's a mixed philosophy colloquium, mostly epistemologists and philosophers of perception, with a few specific tribes in it:

- three or four people who are basically dogmatists about perceptual justification: they think an experience as of a red cube in good light gives you immediate, defeasible, non-inferential warrant for believing there's a red cube, with no need for prior justification of any reliability claim.
- one person who works on animal minds and will run the baby-and-dog objection much more carefully, probably with a continuity argument about perception evolving before language.
- someone who works on nonconceptual content and will bring up the fineness of grain of color experience — you can discriminate two shades you have no names for — and ask why that difference can't itself justify a comparative belief.
- a couple of people who want a middle way: perceptual experience is already conceptually shaped, so it can justify without being a raw given. They'll probably say I'm attacking a strawman.
