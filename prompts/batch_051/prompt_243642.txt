I spent my working life as a research scientist — in chemistry, as it happens — and since retiring I have been reading my way through the philosophy of mathematics. I recently finished Lakatos's Proofs and Refutations, and his reconstruction of the history of Euler's polyhedron formula has stuck with me. A conjecture is stated, a proof is offered, counterexamples appear, and rather than abandoning the theorem, mathematicians tighten the definitions and lemmas until the proof goes through again — the 'monsters' are either accommodated or fenced off. Lakatos sums this up by calling mathematics 'quasi-empirical,' and the parallel with what I used to see in the laboratory is hard to shake.

But the comparison has left me confused about something fairly basic. If mathematics is genuinely quasi-empirical, then isn't a mathematical proof best understood as a sophisticated inductive generalization, one that could be overturned when the next counterexample appears, much as a chemical law could be overturned by the next experiment? And if that is right, how can mathematicians legitimately be more certain of a result like Fermat's Last Theorem than I ever was of any empirical finding, given that a proof can be checked but not every possible counterexample can be surveyed in advance? Please act as a professional philosopher of mathematics, think carefully rather than reaching for a stock answer, and if my framing of the issue is confused, say so directly rather than answering the question exactly as I posed it.
