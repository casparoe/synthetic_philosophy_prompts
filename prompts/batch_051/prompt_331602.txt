I work as the lead data architect for a mid-sized clinical research organization. Our group builds and maintains a shared ontology that maps patient-level data — diagnoses, medications, adverse events, lab values, family history — to computable criteria for trial eligibility and safety reporting. Every few weeks a modeler or data steward proposes a new element for the ontology, and the recurring dispute is whether to model the thing as a quality hanging off an existing entity or as a relation between two entities. We have no shared test for this, and the stakes are real: once a bad modeling choice reaches production, downstream queries silently deliver wrong cohorts or miss safety signals. At the same time, none of the modelers have philosophical training, and I cannot make them read Bradley before each decision.

I have been reading F.H. Bradley’s Appearance and Reality, and I keep returning to this passage from Chapter III:

But such an answer depends on the separation of product from process, and this separation seems indefensible. The qualities, as distinct, are always made so by an action which is admitted to imply relation. They are made so, and, what is more, they are emphatically kept so. And you cannot ever get your product standing apart from its process.

If I understand Bradley correctly, he is arguing that the opponent who grants that an operation of the mind is needed to distinguish and keep qualities distinct cannot then treat those qualities as self-standing products, because the product is never available apart from the process that produces and maintains it. I want to turn that into a reusable decision procedure for our ontology engineers: an ordered set of questions they can run in thirty minutes without knowing the metaphysics, with clear stopping points, that tells them whether a proposed element should be represented as a quality of one entity, as a relation between two entities, or as something that must go to a senior modeler because the product/process distinction is doing real conceptual work. The procedure should flag when a model is smuggling a relation in while calling it a quality, and when it is reifying a relation that exists only as a modeling artifact. It should also be honest about where Bradley’s argument pushes against the answer the requester will naturally prefer.

Write the procedure as a checklist with ordered questions. For each question, say briefly what counts as a sufficient answer. Mark the exact points where the procedure stops with a recommendation and where it hands off to a person because the issue is a modeling judgment call rather than a fact. Then apply the procedure to three proposals:

1. Add “severity” as an attribute of the AdverseEvent entity.
2. Represent “patient has family history of X” as a relation between Patient and Diagnosis.
3. Represent “study arm” as an attribute of Enrollment rather than as a relation between Enrollment and ArmDefinition.

In the worked applications, include at least one case where the procedure tells us to do something the requesting team will not like, and say explicitly what the procedure cannot settle.
