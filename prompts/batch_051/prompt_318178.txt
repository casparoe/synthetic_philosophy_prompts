The long-running debate over higher-order evidence has reached a familiar impasse over epistemic akrasia: the state of believing p while judging that one's total evidence does not support p. One side treats an enkratic requirement as close to axiomatic—roughly, rationality forbids knowingly holding a belief one takes to be unsupported, so a subject who says "I believe p, but my evidence doesn't support p" is in a state that cannot be rational. The other side presses cases of detectable first-order malfunction—a reasoner who knows she has taken a reasoning-impairing drug, or knows her judgment is being driven by wishful thinking—and argues that a subject can be correct at the higher level ("my evidence counts against p") and yet rationally retain the first-order belief, because rationality cannot demand what the subject cannot do and the two levels answer to different norms. Both sides appear to accept the same case descriptions, and yet the arguments have not moved anyone in years.
