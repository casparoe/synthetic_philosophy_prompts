I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I've been trying to work out whether my views about disagreement actually hang together. I'd like an audit rather than a lecture: identify which pairs of the following commitments are in genuine tension, which only seem to be, and rank the genuine tensions by severity. For each genuine tension, say what would have to give and what the least costly revision would cost; then tell me which single revision would do the most to make the set coherent.

Here is the package I've ended up with:

(1) Conciliationism about peer disagreement. If I learn that someone who is roughly my equal in intelligence, honesty, and familiarity with the relevant evidence disagrees with me, I should lower my confidence substantially, and in many cases suspend judgment. I was led toward this partly by Aumann's agreement theorem (roughly, rational agents with a common prior and full knowledge of each other's conclusions cannot agree to disagree).

(2) The flaw-spotting exception. If I have worked through the reasoning myself and can identify the specific step where the other person's argument goes wrong, I do not have to conciliate; I am entitled to keep my original confidence.

(3) This feels especially forced on me in my own work: when a respected colleague disagrees with me about a technical design decision and I can spell out exactly why my proposal handles the failure modes better, I think it would be epistemically wrong, not merely practically inconvenient, to move to 50/50.

(4) Uniqueness for factual questions. For a fixed body of evidence and a fixed proposition, there is exactly one maximally rational degree of confidence; when two people disagree about a factual matter, at most one of them is being maximally rational.

(5) Permissivism about engineering judgment. Two equally smart, equally well-informed engineers can rationally disagree about whether a codebase is clean or which framework is better for a project, and I do not take that to be a form of relativism.

(6) Domain neutrality. I do not want the epistemology of disagreement to contain a special rule that lets engineers be steadfast in a way I would not accept for two doctors disagreeing about a diagnosis.

(7) Anti-skepticism. Given that expert disagreement is pervasive, I also take it as a firm constraint that an acceptable epistemology cannot require us to suspend judgment on nearly everything.

Please walk me through which of these actually pull against one another, which only appear to, how severe each genuine tension is, and what the cheapest fix would be for each — and then say which single change makes the whole set hang together best.
