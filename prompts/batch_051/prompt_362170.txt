A criminal-law reform commission in a mid-sized jurisdiction is weighing a proposal to abolish fixed statutes of limitations for most nonviolent offenses — fraud, embezzlement, environmental violations, and certain assaults — and replace them with a graded decay scheme. Under the proposal, prosecutorial authority would never formally lapse. Once the old limitation period for an offense has run, however, three things happen. The maximum permissible sentence declines by one-tenth of the statutory maximum for each full year of further delay, down to an absolute floor of one-tenth of the original maximum. The prosecution must also adduce one additional independent item of corroborating evidence — a contemporaneous document, a forensic finding, or a disinterested witness — for each five years of delay beyond the old period, up to a cap of four additional items. And the defendant gains a rebuttable presumption that the delay has prejudiced the defense, rebuttable only by a showing that the defendant materially caused the delay through concealment, obstruction, or flight.

Treat this as a mechanism to be analyzed rather than as a moral position to be defended or attacked. What would it actually do? In particular: how would prosecutors time charges, and when would they rationally prefer to wait years rather than file at the earliest responsible moment? How would defendants' post-offense conduct change — in document retention, public statements, cooperation, and the way they live after the relevant period? How would victims, whistleblowers, and insurers respond to the fact that the same offense is worth progressively less punishment as time passes, but is also harder to prove the longer one waits? What gaming strategies become attractive, and which of them would be difficult to distinguish from the scheme working as intended? Finally, which predictable failure modes — such as divergent effects on sophisticated and unsophisticated defendants, or the use of late, weak charges in plea negotiations — could be repaired by adjusting the discount schedule, the corroboration rule, or the prejudice presumption, and which repairs would effectively reintroduce the all-or-nothing limitation period the scheme was meant to replace?
