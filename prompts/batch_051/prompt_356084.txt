Here's something I've never gotten sorted. A person who'd cross the street to avoid seeing someone else weep, and who'd pay real money not to feel sad for an afternoon, will put on a song later that same day because it wrecks them, or start a film they know will make them cry, and afterward call the experience 'good' — not just 'worth it,' but good. They'll do it again the next week. Yet the same person mostly won't seek out art whose whole pitch is to make them feel bored, nauseated, or humiliated, and they'll even avoid movies wich they expect to be 'just depressing' rather than 'sad but moving.' So it isn't a blanket appetite for any negative emotion, and it isn't an appetite for sadness in every form either.

I want the strongest single principle you can build that generates both sides of this: why fictional sadness is sought and called good while real sadness is avoided; why fictional boredom or disgust doesn't get the same treatment; and where the line between 'moving' and 'just depressing' lands. Test your principle against nearby cases — cringe comedy that people do seem to enjoy, grief memoirs that are valued without being called enjoyable, the small gore audience, whatever you think applies. Then commit: is this an asymmetry we should keep because something justifies it, or is it just a habit we've dressed up as a paradox? Don't hand me a survey of the usual candidates and leave me to choose — give me your answer and stand by it.
