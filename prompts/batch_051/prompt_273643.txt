I'm in a philosophy of biology course and we've spent like six weeks on the functions debate, and I keep having this feeling that the whole thing has stalled in a way the papers don't quite admit. The etiological folks (Wright-style, selected effects) say the function of a trait is what it was selected for: hearts are for pumping blood, even a broken heart that never pumps anything is still for pumping blood. The causal-role folks (Cummins-style) say functions are contributions to a capacity of a containing system: a heart's function is pumping blood because that's the contribution that explains how the circulatory system can move stuff around. Both sides handle the same textbook cases—hearts, wings, kidneys—and both agree not every effect of a trait is its function (a nose can hold up glasses but that's not its function; a heart happens to make sounds but sounds aren't its function). Yet after fifty years nobody has really moved. My working theory is that they're answering two different questions—one about what a trait is supposed to do, with normativity and malfunction built in, the other about how to decompose a system into explanatory parts—and "function" just gets used for both. But I can also imagine it's a substantive disagreement about which of those is the real biological notion, or even a hidden disagreement about whether you can do biology at all without talking about selection history. So I want a diagnosis, not a verdict: is this verbal, is it two questions sharing a word, or is there a substantive core one side could in principle win? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress—a distinction, a hard case the two accounts classify differently, a formal result, or just changing the question? Also, please separate what's actually settled among philosophers of biology from what's genuinely contested, and don't present a contested view as the consensus, because my prof talks like the selected-effects account is basically settled and I'm not sure thats right.
