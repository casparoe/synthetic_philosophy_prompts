Construct a thought experiment that forces apart two candidate varieties of free will—the leeway variety and the sourcehood variety—so that a leeway theorist and a sourcehood theorist give opposite verdicts about the same person on the same occasion. First, pin down the two positions in a sentence each, in wording both sides would accept: leeway theories say the free will worth wanting is a robust ability to do otherwise at the moment of decision (the agent would have done otherwise had she judged otherwise, and she could have judged otherwise); sourcehood theories say what's worth wanting is being the ultimate source of one's own choices, and last-minute alternatives are at best a proxy for that. Constraints: no demons, perfect predictors, or mind-control gadgetry; whatever indeterminism the case needs has to be the boring kind (neural noise or the like), not exotic physics. The agent should be a normal adult making a charged but realistic decision, and you can't rig the disagreement by giving her an obvious excuse—coercion, psychosis, a gun to the head—since the whole point is to show the two views coming apart on there own terms. Keep the scenario itself to one paragraph. Then walk through how each side reaches its verdict, point to the one structural feature of the case that does the separating, say how each verdict would shift if you removed that feature, and close with how the side that comes out looking worse would attack the case. The case is for a paper on whether the ordinary two-way-power picture of free will survives the disappearing-agent worry—the worry that an undetermined last step isn't yet an agent doing something.
