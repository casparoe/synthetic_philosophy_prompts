For a novel I am drafting about a translator who misleads others while almost never uttering a falsehood, I would like a consistency audit of the views I find myself holding. I cannot tell which of these commitments are doing the real work, and I do not want to discover the conflict only after the book is written.

1. Lying is always pro tanto wrong, even when it is all-things-considered justified, because it exploits the hearer's trust in assertion. Misleading — bringing someone to a false belief through literally true speech or omission — need not be wrong in that same way, because the hearer draws the false conclusion himself.

2. Writing fiction is not lying. A novel signals its own fictionality and does not assert its contents as true.

3. What makes a deception wrong, and how wrong it is, is fixed by the deceiver's intention to produce a false belief and the degree of control over another person's picture of reality that this achieves, not by the linguistic vehicle, whether assertion or implication.

4. In the novel, I treat a character who carefully tells only truths while arranging to be misread as just as culpable as a character who lies outright.

5. Ordinary trust rests on an asymmetry: we rely on others not to assert what is false, but each of us remains responsible for the inferences we draw from true statements. This asymmetry is why misleading is not the same wrong as lying.

Please audit this set. Which commitments are genuinely in tension, which pairs are only apparently so, and which commitments would have to give, in order of severity, to leave the set coherent? I am asking for a diagnosis, not a lecture, and I do not know in advance which item I should revise.
