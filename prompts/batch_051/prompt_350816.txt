Question: "A valid act of consent transforms an otherwise wrongful act into a permissible one. Explain the conditions under which consent is valid, and compare the roles of deception and coercion in defeating consent: do they undermine the same condition of validity, and is the test for invalidity the same in both cases?"

Student's answer: "Consent is valid when a competent person authorizes another to do what would otherwise be a wrong against her. Three conditions must be met. First, the person must be competent: she must be able to understand the information relevant to the decision, appreciate its consequences, and make and communicate a choice. Second, the person must be adequately informed: consent is not valid when the person is unaware of facts that a reasonable person would regard as material to the decision, such as a partner's infection with a communicable disease or the failure to use agreed contraception. Third, the choice must be voluntary: coercion undercuts consent when one party obtains agreement through a threat of serious harm that leaves the other no reasonable alternative but to comply.

Deception and coercion are, however, best understood as defects of the same underlying condition, namely voluntariness. In deception, the agent's will is misdirected toward a false picture of the situation, while in coercion the will is overborne by pressure. The test in both cases is therefore a single counterfactual: the influence defeats consent if, and only if, the person would not have consented to the act as it actually was. This explains why a trivial lie—for instance, about being an excellent cook—does not invalidate consent, since the person would have consented anyway, whereas a lie about contraception or infection does invalidate consent, since the person likely would not have consented on those terms. The same test also explains why the seriousness of a threat matters: the more serious the threatened harm, the less likely it is that the person would have consented absent the threat."
