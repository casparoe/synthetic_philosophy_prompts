I've been chewing on a parallel between the way physicists defend string theory and the way a circumstantial case gets built in criminal law, and I can't decide wether it's deep or just a shared word like "coherence." On the physics side, nobody has directly observed strings or compact extra dimensions, and direct tests are probably out of reach for a long time. Still plenty of smart people think string theory is the best bet, roughly because: it's the only known framework where a spin-2 massless particle shows up automatically (so gravity "falls out"); it has the right low-energy limits; it recovers gauge-theory structures; and decades of trying to find a consistent rival have pretty much failed. The idea is that these non-empirical virtues—uniqueness, internal consistency, explanatory coherence—count as a kind of confirmation. Then the landscape shows up and seems to say there are a huge number of vacuum solutions instead of one, which some people use to motivate a multiverse and others think this guts the uniqueness argument. In a criminal trial, a circumstantial evidence case has a similar shape: no single item directly proves the defendant did it, but the pieces are supposed to lock together into a story that excludes reasonable alternatives, and the law says that can be enough for conviction.

What I want to know is whether these are genuinely the same structure or only superficially alike. Where does the mapping actually hold, and where does it snap? Is "survived decades of hostile mathematical scrutiny" really analogous to a witness surviving cross-examination, or is the physics community it's own defense attorney? What would play the role of the prosecutor's burden of proof, the defense counsel whose only job is to invent innocent alternative stories, the rule that a chain of inference is only as strong as its weakest link? And if the landscape supplies a gigantic set of alternative narratives that fit all the same evidence, why doesn't that function like reasonable doubt? I'd like an answer that states the mapping carefully, says what would have to be true on both sides for results to transfer, and names at least one thing the parallel predicts that turns out false.
