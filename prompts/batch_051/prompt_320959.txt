I have a standing policy about whether to move from merely probabilistic support to outright acceptance, and I want to know whether it is defensible or just a way of evading an inconsistency I find uncomfortable. In a fair lottery with exactly one winning ticket out of a million, I will not accept outright that any particular ticket loses, however high the probability; I regard the move from 0.999999 to acceptance as mistaken here, because I know with certainty that one ticket wins and I cannot coherently accept, of each ticket, that it loses. But when it comes to my own book manuscript, I do not apply this policy. I accept each individual claim I make in the book, and I also accept the preface sentence, understood as the claim that at least one of the book's claims is false, even though I cannot identify the error. I take this combination to be rational rather than incoherent. The two cases look formally parallel: each involves a large set of propositions each of which is extremely likely, together with a further proposition equivalent to the negation of their conjunction. Yet I treat the lottery case and the preface case asymmetrically, accepting the conjunctive-risk claim in one and refusing the individual high-probability claims in the other. Is this asymmetry defensible? Is there a principled difference between the two cases, in the kind of evidence involved, in the source of the expected error, or in the role of stipulated certainty, that rationalizes treating them differently? Or does my policy, once stated carefully, commit me either to accepting lottery propositions or to abandoning the preface attitude? Please give me the most defensible version of the rule, or tell me honestly that no such version exists.
