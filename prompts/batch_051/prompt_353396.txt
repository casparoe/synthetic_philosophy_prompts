I am presenting a paper next month at a philosophy of science colloquium, and I need a Q&A prep sheet rather than more revisions. My thesis is that the problem of old evidence is not a bug to be repaired but a correct result: data already known with probability one cannot, by themselves, confirm a newly proposed theory, and apparent counterexamples either involve data that were never actually integrated into the evidential background or involve crediting a theory for explanatory unification rather than confirmation. My argument, briefly, is that the standard repairs — recomputing confirmation relative to a prior from which the old evidence has been withheld, or adding low-level propositions about what has been learned — either beg the question or misdescribe what scientists report as confirmed, and that the real locus of confirmation is the theory's subsequent survival of new tests. The audience includes two committed subjective Bayesians who will treat any appeal to explanatory virtue as abandoning Bayesianism, several philosophers of physics who know the Mercury-perihelion case technically and will press on whether an unexplained anomaly was ever old evidence in the required sense, and a historically minded philosopher of science who will suspect me of a presentist, after-the-fact account of discovery. Draft the prep sheet: objections ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased as this audience would phrase them, each with a short reply and a note on where that reply is weakest, an indication of where I should concede and where I should hold, and the question I should be hoping nobody asks.
