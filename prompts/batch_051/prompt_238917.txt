I work at a policy foundation that advises middle-income governments on social spending, and every time we float something Nordic-flavoured — universal child benefit, free tertiary education, the high-tax-high-service bargain — I watch the same argument break out and then go nowhere. One camp says the model travels fine: what built trust in Scandinavia was the welfare state itself, with universal programmes that treat everyone the same, so copy the institutions and the trust will follow. The other camp says that's backwards: those taxes and programmes are only politically survivable because generalized trust and a culture of rule-following were already there, so importing the policies into a lower-trust society just gives you high taxes without the compliance or the public support. Each side has a stock of evidence — cross-country correlations, a few historical cases — and each side has a standard move for explaining away the other side's evidence, usually some version of "your correlation is reverse causation" or "your history is cherry-picked." We've been having exactly this argument internally for years and I no longer think its progressing.

I'm not asking you to take a side on whether the model can be exported. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is. My working theory is that it's really three different disagreements stacked under one heading: a historical one about which came first, the trust or the institutions; a conceptual one about what "trust" even means here — generalized interpersonal trust, confidence that state agencies aren't corrupt, willingness to be taxed; and a political one about what counts as having exported the model, passing the laws or getting the outcomes. The debate loops because each side picks the layer where its evidence is strongest and keeps dragging the conversation back to it.

Is that hypothesis right? If so, which layers could actually be settled by evidence and which are just definitions or values? And concretely: what would count as progress on the empirical layer — what kind of study or data — or is even that layer unfalsifiable in practice because the causal loop is too tight? If some layer is genuinely empirical but settled in a way one side won't like, what would the losing camp actually have to give up? I want less a survey of the evidence and more an account of the structure of the standoff, and what, if anything, would unstick it.
