I've been going around in circles about gauge freedom and I came up with a picture I want you to beat up. In classical electromagnetism the vector potential A is only defined up to a gauge transformation: you can add the gradient of any scalar and the E and B fields don't change. So A has surplus structure — extra mathematical degrees of freedom that don't correspond to anything you can directly measure. The standard move is to say that only gauge-invariant quantities are real: E and B themselves, or the loop integrals of A around closed curves (holonomies), which is what the Aharonov-Bohm effect seems to be tracking. Here's my idea, though. The redundancy in A isn't just bookkeeping; its escrow. The local value of the potential at a point is like money held in an escrow account during a sale: it isn't what anyone ultimately ends up with, you can't go spend it directly, but the sale won't settle correctly unless it's sitting there — because the slack in A is exactly what lets you write the dynamics locally and covariantly. So A isn't real the way a field is real; it's real the way an institutional enabling condition is real, the way an account exists so that a payment can happen. I've been calling this gauge escrow, and I've half-convinced myself it dissolves the whole realism question. The reason it feels importantly different to me is that it explains why the two usual camps talk past each other: the eliminativist is right that A's local value isn't a property of the world the way E and B are, but the substantivalist is right that you can't just delete A without paying a price in how the physics gets written.

Now make me less convinced. Act like a philosopher of physics who actually works on the metaphysics of gauge theories. First steelman gauge escrow as hard as you can: what's the strongest possible version of the claim, and what would it get right? Then tell me whether it's a genuine third position between eliminativism (only invariants exist) and substantivalism about the potential (A is a real field, full stop), or whether it's just instrumentalism wearing a finance metaphor. Does it already exist under some standard name — the usual 'surplus structure' talk, or the view that the connection is real but its pointwise values aren't? And where exactly does the analogy break? My own nagging worry is Yang–Mills: if gauge fixing itself isn't unique (Gribov copies and such), then even the escrow account isn't a single well-defined account, and the metaphor stops pulling its weight. Give me the best objections you can muster and sort them into 'this kills it' versus 'this only needs fixing.'
