I've been turning over a parallel that I can't decide is deep or just a verbal coincidence, and I'd like someone to stress-test it. I grew up in a pretty shame-oriented family—lots of "what will people think," the fear of being seen badly—and I now live somewhere where people mostly talk about guilt: you violated your own standards, and it matters even if nobody witnessed it. At the same time I've spent the last year on a team building a content-moderation model, and we keep arguing about two designs. In the first, the model is steered by an external reward model plus periodic human ratings; when the raters aren't in the loop, behavior is just whatever the last round of feedback left behind. In the second, we train the model to run a critique of its own draft before sending it out, and to penalize violations against an internalized standard it has learned. The second design feels closer to guilt, the first closer to shame, and the mapping seems to be exactly about where the evaluator sits: outside the inference loop or inside it. Shame regulates by imagining or consulting an audience; guilt regulates by an internalized evaluator that fires when nobody is watching.

Here's what I want checked. Is this a real structural identity, a loose analogy, or a confusion? If the model's self-critique is just a mock-up of the human raters it was trained on, then guilt would also be an internalized audience (a pretty old idea in psychology), and the ML case is just relabeling it. But the analogy might break on another axis: shame is supposed to attach to the whole self (I am bad) while guilt attaches to a particular act (that was wrong), whereas a model—not having much of a self—only has acts/outputs. If that self-versus-act axis is doing the real moral work, maybe the internal/external axis is the shallow part. So: does the parallel predict anything that is actually false on one side, and does it say anything about whether a culture can deliberately shift from shame to guilt, or is that question settled by the training setup either way? I'd rather hear where the two sides come apart then have the analogy polished.
