I’m a PhD student in cognitive linguistics, and I’m trying to figure out what I actually think about metaphor by going through cases rather than by starting with a theory. I’d like you to work out whether some principled account captures my verdicts, or close, and to tell me where I’d have to revise.

My gut reactions:

1. If someone says of a colleague, “She’s a fog bank,” I don’t think there’s a definite metaphorical content I could state as a set of propositions. The metaphor does something — it makes me notice a family of qualities: she’s hard to read, she blurs boundaries, she chills the atmosphere. But I’d resist saying the sentence means any one of those, or their conjunction.

2. If someone says, “We’ve been spinning our wheels on this project,” I think there is a fairly stable content: prolonged effort without progress. I’d expect any competent hearer to recover that, and I could paraphrase it without loss.

3. “The committee has been chewing on the proposal for weeks” also seems to communicate something determinate — sustained, effortful, possibly repetitive processing that hasn’t yet produced a decision. The paraphrase isn’t just a loose effect; it feels like what the speaker intended.

4. “The infection outran the antibiotics” strikes me as partly agentive: it encourages me to think of the infection as eluding pursuit. But I’m unsure whether the agentive framing is part of what the sentence says or just a way it nudges my attention. The literal counterfactual — the infection continued despite the antibiotics — seems to be the main point.

5. “The manager bulldozed through the agenda” feels to me as if it imposes a spatial structure on the meeting: the agenda is a terrain, the items are obstructions, and the manager is a force moving through them. I can articulate that structure, but I doubt ordinary hearers have to articulate it to understand the sentence.

The tension is obvious. Case 1 pushes me toward Davidson’s view in “What Metaphors Mean”: a metaphorical sentence has only its literal meaning, and the rest is a causal effect on the hearer, not propositional content. Cases 2 and 3 push me toward conceptual-metaphor theory, where at least many metaphors carry conventional conceptual content that speakers can mean and hearers can recover. Is there one account of metaphor that gives me the right verdicts on all five cases, or do I need to sort metaphors into kinds? If I need kinds, what is the principle that assigns a case to one kind rather than another?

I also want you to apply your proposed account to a case I haven’t settled. If a child asks whether a loud argument “exploded,” does that metaphor carry a determinate claim about suddenness and violence, or does it only prompt a way of imagining the event? Say what your account predicts and why.

Finally, please be explicit about what would change your mind. Which step in the reasoning is most vulnerable, and what sort of argument or evidence — linguistic, experimental, or philosophical — would lead you to revise the account?
