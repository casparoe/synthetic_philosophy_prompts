I am a novelist, and for the past year I have been researching a novel set in a provincial university. While following the work of its Centre for the Study of the Book, I was invited to dinner after a council meeting at which the senior management had proposed cutting the Centre’s funding. The Centre teaches palaeography, codicology, and the editing of medieval and early modern manuscripts. Two academics at the table argued about the proposal, and I listened without being able to settle the matter. I would like you to adjudicate their exchange.

Dr Helen Ashworth, a reader in early modern history who helps run the Centre, said the proposal was a mistake because it judged the Centre by the wrong standards. Professor Michael Trench, a chemical engineer on the university’s finance committee, said the university had no choice but to apply ordinary tests: student recruitment, completion, research income, and impact beyond the university. By those tests the Centre failed, and sentimentality could not justify a budget line.

Ashworth replied that Trench was treating the university as a business. A university, she said, is the one institution charged with preserving and transmitting knowledge that has no immediate purchaser; if it will not do that, no other body will. Trench answered that scarcity is real. Every pound spent on obscure manuscripts is a pound not spent on students, laboratories, or widening access. A defence of useless knowledge, he said, is easy until one has to choose whose salary to cut. Ashworth countered that his principle, applied consistently, would also cut pure mathematics, theoretical physics, and much of philosophy, since those also lack an immediate purchaser. So either his principle was too broad, or he was drawing a line he had not admitted.

At this point Ashworth put her case in terms of the training of the intellect, and quoted a passage from the seventh discourse of John Henry Newman’s The Idea of a University: “We know, not by a direct and simple vision, not at a glance, but, as it were, by piecemeal and accumulation, by going round an object, by the comparison, the combination, the mutual correction, the continual adaptation, of many partial notions, by the employment, concentration, and joint action of many faculties and exercises of mind.” What the Centre actually teaches, she said, is exactly this practice. Its students must hold many partial and conflicting readings in mind at once, distrust the quick glance, and work patiently through recalcitrant evidence. That is a transferable intellectual habit, not a narrow technical skill.

Trench replied that Newman was writing before mass higher education, when a university could be a place for forming gentlemen. Today the institution is publicly funded and accountable. He also said the claim about transferable habits is empirical and unproven. Many difficult practices—watchmaking, choir singing, chess—train patience and attention, yet a university does not fund departments for them. If the Centre’s defenders want to claim special epistemic virtue, they need evidence that palaeography produces better judgement than other demanding activities. Ashworth answered that the burden was not only on her. The proposal implicitly claims that the disciplines that survive the cuts produce enough measurable benefit to justify them, and the university’s own mission statement still uses the language of liberal education. If the only answer is that we cannot measure it, so we will not fund it, then the university has quietly adopted a crude verificationism about value. Trench said that at some point one must act on imperfect evidence. Refusing to cut anything whose value cannot be measured is a recipe for never prioritising, and prioritising is what a budget is.

Please go through this exchange move by move and adjudicate it. I want to know which of each speaker’s claims are sound, where each overreaches, what the strongest available version of each position would be, and who is more right overall about the decision before the council.
