I've been chewing on this for a while and need somebody to tell me where it breaks. I call it structural theodicy. The issue is evolution and the doctrine of a good creation: billions of years of predation, parasitism, starvation before any human sin. The usual move is to say animal death is not a moral evil, but that always felt too quick to me. My version is stronger. I think the goodness in question was never a property of each creature's experience. It's a property of the whole order: that lawful, self-organizing processes can produce bodies, minds, ecosystems. The same features that make the order stable enough to be called good also make harm possible and, in a finite world, actual. So animal suffering before the Fall isn't a glitch in a good creation or a consequence we have to retroactively blame on sin; it's the cost of there being the kind of structure that can be called good at all. The Fall, on my account, is not when suffering entered but when humans became able to experience it as loss and to refuse their place in the order—so it's a change in the creature, not in the physical process.

I want a few things from you. (1) Give me the strongest version of this idea, even if it's stronger than mine. (2) Tell me if it's already a named position somewhere, because it feels too obvious not to be; if it is, tell me what the standard objections to it are. (3) Give me the hardest case for it and say whether it's fatal or just uncomfortable. The hardest case I can think of is a fawn burning in a forest fire long before any human, and a God who looks at that and still calls the whole thing good. Does my view actually have anything to say there, or does it just bite the bullet and call the bullet the point?
