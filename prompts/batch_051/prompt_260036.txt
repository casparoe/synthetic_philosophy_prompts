I cover science and the history of ideas for a national magazine, and I am trying to settle what I actually think about historical objectivity before I commit it to a long essay. I have been reviewing recent public-history projects and recording my reactions; they do not seem to add up to any theory I can name, and I would like to know whether a single principle lies beneath them. Here are the verdicts.

1. An exhibition on the history of eugenics moves in one clean arc from Galton through early twentieth-century sterilization laws to the Nazi program and its later scientific repudiation. It is unapologetically moralized, omits many complications, and stops short of present-day genomics, though a whole other gallery could be built from what it leaves out. I thought it fair, and possibly more truthful than an even-handed account would have been.

2. A gallery on the discovery of insulin is factually impeccable at the level of individual sentences: every date and quotation on its walls checks out. Yet the story attributes the discovery almost wholly to two researchers, and visitors leave with a picture of credit that the very documents in the adjacent display case, if read attentively, contradict. I think it is misleading.

3. A biography of Darwin reconstructs his private hesitations and his state of mind during the Beagle voyage with an assurance that letters and journals cannot support; it reads as a recovery of deliberation rather than an inference from evidence. I do not trust it as history.

4. A documentary on the 1919 eclipse test of general relativity presents the expedition as a clean confirmation and its lead astronomer as an uncomplicated hero. It does not mention the difficulty of reading the photographic plates, the contemporary and later dispute about how the results were handled, or the criticism of the heroic version of the story. I find it objectionable in a way the eugenics exhibition was not, even though both are selective leading narratives.

5. A podcast on the history of climate science, made by two activists, announces at the top that it is using history to explain present-day politics and has chosen its sources accordingly. Its selection is the most tendentious of anything I reviewed. I nevertheless find it more honest than the insulin gallery and, if I am being strict, more honest than the eugenics exhibition.

6. A chronology gallery on the history of germ theory offers nothing but dates, images, and names, with no connecting argument. I do not object to it, but it leaves me cold, and I suspect it is deceptive in its own way: visitors will take the bare sequence to be what the history is.

The case I keep going back and forth on is a two-hour radio series on the history of genetic modification. It lets advocates and critics each tell their own history, leaves the disagreement unresolved, and says only that historians too disagree. I cannot decide whether it is objectivity or a form of false balance.

I want to know whether one account of historical objectivity, somewhere along the axis from the past being recoverable as it actually was to history being made rather than found, can deliver all six verdicts. If it can, state it precisely and tell me what it says about the radio series and any further case you think it settles differently than I would expect. If it cannot, say which of my verdicts I should overturn, why that one, and what the repaired account says about the radio series.
