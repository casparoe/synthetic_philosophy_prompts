i'm in a grad seminar on philosophy of mathematics and we've been working through Lakatos's Proofs and Refutations, and i've found myself reaching for a term I made up that I want pressure-tested before I use it in a paper. I call it "counterexample debt." The idea is that a proof never actually finishes off its theorem; it just leaves the theorem with a list of unpaid obligations. All the would-be counterexamples that the current definitions and lemmas push just outside the boundary — the solid with a tunnel through it that gets declared "not really a polyhedron," the awkward case that gets exception-barred, the hidden assumption in the "remove a face" lemma that nobody has checked — these aren't killed by the proof. They're put on a tab. Over time a field either settles the debt (lemma-incorporation, strengthening the proof so the excluded case gets covered) or restructures it (redefining the terms so the counterexample no longer counts, which is monster-barring in a nicer suit). So "proved" starts to look like a balance-sheet claim about how much unexamined boundary a theorem is currently carrying, rather than a binary property.

The obvious objection is that a machine-checked proof in a proof assistant seems to have zero debt — everything is checked, there's no hidden lemma. Is that fatal, or does the debt just move somewhere else, into the definitions, the formalization choices, the axioms? Also, is my "counterexample debt" actually distinct from what Lakatos already says, or am I just re-describing proofs and refutations in accounting language? And does the framing buy anything — e.g. a way to compare two proofs of the same theorem, or a reason to prefer one kind of open problem over another — or is it a metaphor doing no real work? I want the three strongest objections and a candid verdict on whether I should keep the term or drop it.
