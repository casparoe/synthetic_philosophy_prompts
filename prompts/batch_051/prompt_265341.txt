I teach a second-year elective called Paradoxes and Infinity, mostly philosophy and math majors, about 18 students, 80 minutes a session. In two weeks I want the class on Russell's paradox to be a game rather than a lecture. The thing I want them to actually feel is how natural the move is from the thought that you can collect all the things satisfying any sharp condition, to the contradiction: the collection that contains exactly the collections that do not contain themselves both does and does not contain itself, and this isn't because anyone was slopping around. I want that to happen to them in the room, before I name it, not as a trick I pull at the board.

My working seed is the catalog or library version, but I worry it plays like a desk puzzle. Each student is a catalog, they write on an index card which catalogs they list, some list themselves and some do not, and then a designated student has to compile the master catalog that lists all and only the catalogs that do not list themselves, and cannot decide whether to write their own name on their card. I'd rather have them physically sort themselves into groups, trade cards, maybe with a scoring rule so they care about whose list gets approved. I also worry they'll file it under the liar paradox or the barber joke and never feel the specific pressure that forced early set theory to abandon unrestricted comprehension.

You are a philosopher of logic with a background in interactive teaching. Design a runnable 80-minute activity. Start by brainstorming two or three candidate mechanisms, say wich one you're choosing and why. Then give the rules, materials, a group-size arrangement, a payoff or scoring scheme that doesn't require real money, and a debrief that maps concrete moments in the game onto the formal structure: naive comprehension, the Russell set, the contradiction, and the usual repairs such as restricting comprehension, typing, or seperating levels of discourse. Tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, especially the student who immediately proposes just banning self-reference. And be frank about what the game can't reproduce and what I'll have to supply myself by lecturing at the end.
