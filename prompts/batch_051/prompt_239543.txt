I've been thinking about privacy on my own after reading Samuel Warren and Louis Brandeis's essay, "The Right to Privacy," and trying to work out where I actually stand. I keep returning to one passage in particular, because it seems to make a strong claim about what privacy is for:

"The intensity and complexity of life, attendant upon advancing civilization, have rendered necessary some retreat from the world, and man, under the refining influence of culture, has become more sensitive to publicity, so that solitude and privacy have become more essential to the individual; but modern enterprise and invention have, through invasions upon his privacy, subjected him to mental pain and distress, far greater than could be inflicted by mere bodily injury."

Here is the set of commitments I seem to have taken away from it, and I would like to know if they actually hang together:

(1) Privacy is, at bottom, about control over access to personal information: someone wrongs me when they acquire, use, or disclose information about me without my permission.

(2) But a privacy violation can happen even when no information is acquired, recorded, or used. If someone rigs up a camera pointed at my bathroom, the wrong is in the exposure, even if the camera is never turned on.

(3) The reason such exposure matters is the mental pain and distress it causes. Warren and Brandeis seem to make exactly this point in the passage, and it feels right.

(4) Yet I also think privacy can be violated even if the person never discovers the exposure and therefore never feels any distress at all.

I cannot tell whether this is a coherent package or whether I am helping myself to something in one commitment that the others rule out. Please treat these as my actual commitments and give me an audit: which pairs are in real tension and which only look that way, how severe each tension is, and what the least costly revision would be. I would rather know which single change would do the most to make the whole set cohere.
