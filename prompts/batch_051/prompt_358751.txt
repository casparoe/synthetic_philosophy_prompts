I'm taking a philosophy of law course and I can't stop chewing on the blue bus case. A bus hits a car and flees; we know 80% of the buses in that city are Blue Bus Company and 20% are Red. Everyone agrees this isn't enough to find Blue Bus liable in a civil suit, even though the standard is just preponderance of the evidence and 80% is well past 50%. But if one eyewitness testifies that it was a blue bus, and we'd rate the eyewitness only about 75% reliable, a jury would find Blue Bus liable without much trouble. So we accept the weaker individualized evidence and reject the stronger statistical evidence, wich seems backwards. Is there a real principle that justifies this asymmetry, or is the statistical/individualized distinction just something we find psychologically comfortable? Give the strongest justification you can, then see whether it survives a case where the eyewitness is only 60% reliable and the base rate is 90%, and say briefly whether your answer would change if the case were criminal and the standard was proof beyond a reasonable doubt. Should we keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or drop it?
