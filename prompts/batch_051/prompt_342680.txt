I'm a PhD student working on Aristotle's De Anima, specifically the stuff on phantasia and thinking, and I keep going back and forth about how much of it is supposed to be empirical. Aristotle seems to maintain that the soul never thinks without phantasia — without, roughly, something imagistic. The way I was taught it, even when you think a universal like justice or a triangle, you need a phantasm to do the thinking with, the way a geometer uses a drawn figure as a stand-in for the abstract triangle. So phantasia is doing a lot of work: it's not daydreaming or free association, it's supposed to be a basic ingredient of any thought at all.

What's been bugging me is the recent-ish literature on aphantasia, people who report having no mental imagery — no mind's eye, no picture of their mother's face, nothing — even while they reason about geometry, philosophy, all the usual stuff. Taken at face value, aphantasia looks like a live counterexample to Aristotle's claim: here are people thinking perfectly well with no images at all. But I can't shake the thought that this is to fast, and that the real question is whether phantasia in Aristotle's sense has to be conscious imagery, or whether maybe aphantasics still have phantasmata in a thinner, functional sense (unconscious, nonvisual, schematic, whatever), in which case the claim survives but only by reinterpreting what it was really about. So here's what I want: does Aristotle's claim that thought requires phantasia survive what the aphantasia research seems to show? Which premise does that finding actually put pressure on, and is there a repaired version of the claim that keeps the original philosophical point without becoming unfalsifiable? Or does 'phantasia' just end up meaning 'whatever it is that makes thought possible,' and if so was the original claim ever saying anything substantive? I'd rather have a diagnosis than a survey of De Anima or the aphantasia literature.
