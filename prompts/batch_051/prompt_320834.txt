I'm a PhD student TAing an early modern philosophy course and I'm grading midterms. The question I set was: in Leviathan, explain why Hobbes thinks covenants entered into in the state of nature are void, and then evaluate whether this undermines the covenant that institutes the sovereign, since that covenant seems to be made before a common power exists. Here's one student's answer:

According to Hobbes, the state of nature is a condition of universal war, where people are roughly equal in power and driven by competition, fear, and the desire for glory, so they cannot trust one another. In this condition covenants made on mutual trust are void, since no one can be assured the other will perform. The laws of nature still bind in conscience, but not always in outward action, because obeying them without security would make a person a prey to others. The solution is that everyone makes a covenant with everyone else to give up the right of nature to a sovereign, who is not a party to the covenant. The objection is that this original covenant looks like exactly the kind of mutual-trust covenant Hobbes says is void before a common power exists. I think the problem dissolves because the covenant is self-validating: by creating the very common power that makes performance assured, it becomes binding as soon as the sovereign is instituted, and it binds retroactively on the parties who made it.
