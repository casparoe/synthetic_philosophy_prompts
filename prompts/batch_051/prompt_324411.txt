I'm a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, and I keep noticing that "social construction" does double duty there: sometimes it is a debunking move, and sometimes it is a way of saying that something is real but not natural. In a recent exchange, a friend made an analogy that struck me as load-bearing. Money, he said, is just paper, metal, or ledger entries; its value is not in the physical token. It exists because people collectively accept it, institutions stand behind it, and everyone coordinates on the expectation that others will keep accepting it. Race, he argued, is like money in exactly this respect: it is real because people classify bodies, institutions record and enforce those classifications, and everyone plans around them — not because race has a biological essence. The conclusion he wanted to license was that race could in principle be abolished the way a currency can be demonetized or replaced, and that treating race as a natural kind is a category error. He treated gender as a nearby case, but I would rather keep race and money in focus.

I would like you to audit this analogy rather than simply affirming or rejecting it. Give the mapping explicitly: what in money corresponds to what in race, and where the mapping breaks. At minimum, consider the role of collective attitudes; the difference between explicit institutional rules (legal tender, central banks, redenomination) and informal, contested, unevenly enforced classification; whether race has anything analogous to a currency issuer or a decision to replace the system; how involuntary ascription and the role of bodily appearance, ancestry, hierarchy, and accumulated advantage differ from participation in a monetary system; and whether "socially constructed" means one thing in the money case and another in the race case. Then say which disanalogies actually matter for the intended abolitionist conclusion and which are merely incidental. Finally, say how much of the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether some other analogy would serve my friend's claim better.

Please separate what is well established about social ontology and race from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
