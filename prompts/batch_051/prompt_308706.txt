A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about naked statistical evidence, and we came apart not on the examples but on what the examples show; I would like your number, but only in order to see the structure behind it. Take the standard cases. In the blue bus case, a bus injures a pedestrian; the pedestrian can prove that the Blue Bus Company operates four out of every five buses on the relevant route at the relevant hour and the Red Bus Company the remaining fifth, and no witness can identify the color. Most people — my friend and I included — would not allow recovery against Blue Bus on a preponderance standard, despite a probability near .8. In the gatecrasher case, a rodeo has a thousand attendees but only 499 paid; a randomly selected attendee is pursued for the unpaid admission. The chance that this person gatecrashed is barely above .5, and again most people refuse to find against them. Neither of us defends those verdicts. What we fought about is why they should not be entered — and whether the why is epistemic or only practical.

Here is the proposition on which I want your credence: the reluctance to find against a defendant on bare statistical evidence is at least partly justified by an epistemic defect in that evidence — something about whether it can properly ground a singular finding that this defendant did the act, as opposed to grounding only a high probability that some member of a reference class to which the defendant belongs did it — and this defect cannot be fully explained by practical considerations such as the cost of false findings, incentive effects, the verdict's expressive meaning, or the need to preserve the legitimacy of state coercion. I currently sit at about .7 that such an epistemic component is real and doing justificatory work; my friend sits at about .2 and thinks the whole phenomenon dissolves once you set the probability threshold appropriately and price the costs of error. It would count heavily against the proposition if a complete decision-theoretic model that used only the stated probabilities and properly specified asymmetric error costs predicted the standard pattern of reluctance with no epistemic remainder.

I am asking you to commit rather than survey: pick a number, stand behind it, and give me the derivation. Decompose the question into the sub-questions that matter for you — for example, whether there is a stable distinction between statistical and individualized evidence or whether the reference class problem dissolves it; whether the blue bus case and the gatecrasher case fail for the same reason or for different reasons; whether raising the threshold, adding a sensitivity or safety condition, or using an asymmetric error-cost model captures the reluctance; and whether the analysis behaves the same at the civil preponderance standard and at the criminal beyond-a-reasonable-doubt standard. Assign a rough probability to each sub-question you regard as load-bearing, say how they combine into your overall number, identify which of them your final credence is most sensitive to, and say where the disagreement between someone at .7 and someone at .2 most plausibly lives. End by telling me what evidence, argument, or variation on one of the cases would move your number the most in either direction.
