I hold the following view, and what I want from you is a map of what would count against it — not a defense of it, and not the stock objection that the question is fuzzy. The view: the question "When did Latin become Italian?" has no determinate answer in the history of the language itself. Latin passed into Italian, along with the other Romance vernaculars, through a continuous generational accumulation of changes across a dialect continuum, with variation present at every stage, so that no non-arbitrary point exists at which a speaker, a text, or a grammar crossed from one language into another. The candidate "first Italian" texts and dates are retrospective selections reflecting when the written record happened to register the spoken vernacular, not when the language changed; the boundary itself is an artifact of later national historiography rather than something linguistics could in principle recover.

Now tell me what would have to be true for that view to be wrong. I want the defeaters sorted by how decisive they would be, from fatal to merely awkward. Work through at least these possibilities: a demonstrable break in intergenerational transmission that would place the boundary between cohorts; a measurable collapse of mutual intelligibility between earlier and later stages, and how one could measure that for dead speech communities; the Carolingian-era recognition that the rustic Roman vernacular was now distinct from the Latin of the schools, with preaching instructions and reforms responding to failed comprehension, as evidence of a real socially recognized boundary rather than a convenient convention; the fact that Italian later became a deliberately codified literary standard built on Tuscan, which suggests that at least the standard language has a dateable beginning even if the spoken continuum does not; and the status of the usual candidate witnesses of early Romance in Italy, such as the Veronese riddle and the Placiti Cassinesi — whether a non-circular set of diagnostics could classify them as no longer Latin and thereby locate the boundary in a determinate window.

For each defeater, say whether a defender of my view should concede that it counts against the position, and how that concession could be limited without turning into a retreat into unfalsifiability. End with an honest assessment of whether the view survives by redefining its terms, and whether that kind of survival is a problem for a position of this kind. If it helps you to know what I would personally find most persuasive: I think evidence of a genuine acquisition break between adjacent generations would move me most, while evidence that "Italian" is an instituted object with a real beginning would move me least — so tell me explicitly if I have that weighting wrong.
