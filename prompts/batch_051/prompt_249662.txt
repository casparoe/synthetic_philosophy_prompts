So I was talking to a friend about divine foreknowledge and free will, and he gave me his capsule version of Boethius like this: 'Boethius's whole trick is saying God is outside time, so he dosen't forsee anything — he just sees all of history at once, like someone looking down at a whole landscape from a mountain. So calling it fore-knowledge is the mistake. And just as me watching you walk down the street doesn't make you walk, God's seeing your future actions doesn't cause or force them. The problem only looks real because we imagine God sitting back in the past peering ahead; once you drop that picture, the conflict just disappears. It's basically the same move as a friend who knows you well enough to say exactly what you're about to order, and nobody thinks that robs you of free will.' I'm inclined to think the broad strokes are right but the last analogy feels like it's smuggling something in, and maybe 'the conflict just disappears' is too fast. Is this a fair summary of what Boethius actually argues? Go claim by claim and tell me what a careful reading of the Consolation would accept, what it would wince at, and whether the knows-you-well analogy does the same work as the outside-time point or is doing something genuinely different.
