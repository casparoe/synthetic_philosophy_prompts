Two philosophers of work and association, one defending an individual-authorization view of the right to organize and the other defending a group-authority view, have agreed to produce an adversarial collaboration. The individual-authorization view holds that the right to organize is ultimately a bundle of individual rights—freedom of association, freedom of contract, and each worker's right to withhold their own labor—so collective action is justified only to the extent that each worker it binds has actually authorized it. The group-authority view holds that the right to organize is a collective right of employees as a body, grounded in the need to correct the structural asymmetry of power between employers and employees; once a collective has reached a decision through fair majoritarian procedures, that decision may permissibly bind holdouts, because an isolated withdrawal of labor is close to powerless and individual exit is often illusory. The specific question they are working on is whether a fair majority vote to strike may legitimately impose obligations on employees who voted against the strike, or subject them to organized social pressure to respect the picket line.

Write the document such a collaboration should issue. It should contain: (1) a numbered "Agreed claims" section carrying substantive claims both can sign about labor markets, employer power, workers' interests, the conditions under which strikes are at least presumptively legitimate, and the legitimate interests of dissenting workers, stated so as to be informative rather than vacuous; (2) a "Disagreements" table whose rows state each live question, the individual-authorization answer in wording that position would accept, and the group-authority answer in wording that position would accept, with a final entry judging whether that row is substantive or partly verbal; and (3) a "Cruxes" section identifying the sub-questions that would most move the disagreement, and for each crux a concrete case or observation both parties agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Where a shared formulation emerges, the collaboration should note it; where a dispute dissolves under more precise wording, say so explicitly. Aim for a document both parties could put their names to, not a debate transcript and not a verdict on who is right.
