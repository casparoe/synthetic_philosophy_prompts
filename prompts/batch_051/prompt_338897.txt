I teach a graduate seminar in formal epistemology, and I have spent much of this term puzzling over a tension in my own thinking about rational disagreement. On one side lies a body of formal work that I find enormously attractive. The common prior assumption says that before two ideally rational agents receive their private information, they would assign the same probabilities to the relevant hypotheses. Once that assumption is combined with the requirement that both agents update those priors by conditionalization, every later difference in their beliefs becomes attributable to a difference in the information each has seen. In its sharpest form, the associated agreement theorem adds a stronger result: if rational agents share a common prior, then they cannot stably agree to disagree, because once each agent's probability for a proposition is common knowledge between them, those probabilities must already be equal. The appeal of this picture is not merely formal. It converts the messy fact of disagreement into a diagnostic instrument: when two well-informed people continue to diverge, the theory instructs us to look for hidden information or a lapse in reasoning, and it rules out in advance the idea that transparent disagreement among rational people could be a brute, irreducible feature of intellectual life. I find that tidiness difficult to resist.

At the same time, I hold a firm intuition that refuses to accommodate itself to the theory. Consider two careful intelligence analysts who have read essentially the same reporting and historical record and yet assign quite different probabilities to a foreign power's willingness to use nuclear weapons, or two economists who disagree about the long-term effects of a proposed policy after reviewing the same studies. My conviction is not merely that such disagreement must be tolerated for practical or political reasons. It is that neither analyst and neither economist need be irrational, and that the divergence is not best explained by some datum known only to one of them. The difference seems to live in the priors themselves: in background judgments about which historical analogies carry evidential weight, which classes of models are plausible, and how much trust to place in various institutions and methods. Those prior judgments do not strike me as the kind of thing rational people must share, and the demand that there be a uniquely correct starting point common to both parties looks to me like a modeling convention dressed up as a requirement of rationality. Yet the theory's entire diagnostic power appears to depend on that convention.

I am not asking for a verdict on which side should win. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that yields, what it would take for the common prior assumption to be weakened or abandoned, and whether there is a formulation on which both survive — for instance, one on which a common prior is genuinely required only for a restricted class of propositions, such as those needed for stable cooperative planning or shared calibration, while divergent priors remain permissible across the open-ended hypotheses that animate serious inquiry. Is the claim that every persistent disagreement traces ultimately to hidden information or error a genuine consequence of the formalism, or does the formalism simply stipulate away the very phenomenon it was meant to explain?
