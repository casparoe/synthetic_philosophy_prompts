Two colleagues of mine disagree about the thesis sometimes called the gene's-eye view of evolution: the gene, or the gene-lineage, is the fundamental unit of selection, and the organism is only a transient vehicle that genes build in order to replicate themselves. The first colleague treats this as an empirical claim about the causal structure of evolution. On her view, it predicts that wherever the persistence of a stretch of DNA and the survival or reproductive success of the organism that carries it diverge, the DNA's fate will dominate, and the visible design of adaptations is ultimately design for the benefit of gene-lineages rather than organisms. The second colleague treats it as a bookkeeping convention. On his view, any selective episode can be redescribed at the level of the organism or the group without losing information, so no observation could force us to adopt the gene-level description or refute it.

I want to know whether any observation could settle this. Design the strongest empirical test you can — a real or thought experiment, ideally in an artificial or controlled population — of the thesis that selection acts at the level of the gene rather than at the level of the organism. The disagreement should sharpen around cases where the two levels appear to part company, such as a genetic element that biases its own transmission into the next generation while lowering the reproductive output of the organism that carries it. Specify the experimental setup, say which result would favor each colleague's interpretation, and then state precisely what a positive result would still not settle. In particular, address whether a successful gene-level prediction would show that genes are the causal units of selection or merely that gene-level bookkeeping is a useful description for that case.
