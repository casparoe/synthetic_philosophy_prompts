I'm moderating a colloquium Q&A with a political philosopher who defends a Rawlsian interpretation of public reason. On her account, citizens may invoke religious considerations when deliberating about constitutional essentials and matters of basic justice, as long as they are also prepared, in due course, to offer considerations shareable by citizens who reject their comprehensive doctrine; she treats this as a moral duty of civility rather than a legal restriction, and she deflects the charge that it unfairly burdens religious citizens by insisting that the requirement is symmetric, since secular comprehensive doctrines such as utilitarianism or Kantianism must also pass the same shareability test. Draft eight to ten interview questions that take her statement of the view as given and press where it is most exposed, avoiding generic warm-up questions. Sequence the questions so that the symmetry defense becomes progressively harder to sustain, and close with a question about what evidence could reveal that the requirement, despite its formal symmetry, imposes a distinctive burden on religious participants. Along the way, include at least one question, each paired with a follow-up that anticipates her most likely reply, on the following: the integrity objection, namely that religious citizens are being asked to justify the use of coercive power in terms of a secular standard they may not themselves accept; the worry that translating a religious consideration into shareable terms changes the consideration rather than making it public; the apparent head start that secular comprehensive doctrines enjoy under the shareability test; and the regress problem, namely whether the duty of civility itself could be justified to reasonable religious citizens without implicitly assuming the priority of public reasons.
