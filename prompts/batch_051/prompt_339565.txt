I have been thinking about a class of artificially intelligent systems that are deliberately designed to form moral judgements but not to act on them: they review a proposed course of action, produce an assessment of whether it is permissible or wrong, and pass that assessment to a human operator who is free to follow or disregard it. The system has no actuators and no veto power, cannot initiate or prevent anything, and its outputs are sometimes overwritten. I find this case pulls me in two directions. On one side is a view of moral agency I find theoretically attractive: what makes a being a moral agent is not that it can make a causal difference, but that its conduct is answerable to its own judgements about reasons, so that what it does or would do expresses a stance, an endorsement, or a quality of will. On this view, a person with locked-in syndrome does not cease to be a moral agent merely because their body cannot currently execute their intentions, and a wrongdoer remains answerable even after the harm can no longer be undone. By the same reasoning, the constrained AI seems to count: it forms judgements about what ought to be done, stands behind them in its outputs, and would revise them in response to better reasons.

Against this stands a firm intuition I cannot shake: there is something conceptually off about resenting, blaming, praising, or feeling grateful towards such a system as one would towards a moral agent. I want to say that it has no stake in what happens, that nothing is ever laid at its feet, and that a being whose moral judgements can make no difference at all is not a moral agent, however reliable a moral classifier it may be.

I do not want to be told which side wins. I want the strongest version of each position: a case for debunking the intuition as an artefact of how we imagine such systems — for instance, our tendency to withhold agentive attitudes from things that cannot be harmed, punished, or made to answer to us — and a case for taking the intuition to reveal a genuine condition on agency, such as a standing capacity to exercise control over what one does, which the theory would then need to absorb. I would then like a negotiated verdict: could the positions be made compatible, perhaps by distinguishing full moral agency from moral judgement or answerability, or by distinguishing the capacity to act from actually making a difference on some occasion? What exactly would justify dropping the intuition, and what exactly would justify revising the theory? Where the state of the debate allows it, please separate what is settled among people who work on moral agency from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
