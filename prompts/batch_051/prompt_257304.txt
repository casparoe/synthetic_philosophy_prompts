Take the role of a philosopher of language who is comfortable with the basics of classical physics, and think carefully; begin by brainstorming the proposed mapping before you judge whether it holds. I am a retired physicist who has recently begun reading philosophy of language, and I keep noticing a parallel I cannot yet evaluate. In physics, coordinate systems are arbitrary choices: the laws can be stated in coordinate-free form, yet to compute anything concrete one must choose coordinates, and that choice supplies a privileged origin and axes that are not themselves part of the invariant content. Indexicals — I, here, now — look to me like the same device in natural language. A speaker chooses herself as the origin of a context; the proposition expressed varies with that choice, while something like the character of the indexical stays fixed, just as a transformation rule stays fixed while the coordinates vary. The essential-indexicality examples seem to push the parallel further: a person can know every objective, indexical-free description of a situation and still lack the one piece of information she needs in order to act, namely that she is the one who is here now — much as an invariant, coordinate-free description of a physical situation does not by itself tell an observer which of the points in it is her own. Is this a genuine identity of structure, a loose analogy, or a confusion? State the mapping explicitly, say what would have to be true on both sides for results to transfer between physics and semantics, and identify something the parallel predicts that turns out false.
