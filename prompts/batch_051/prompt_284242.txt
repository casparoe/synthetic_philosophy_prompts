You are a philosopher of language who works on indexicals and context-sensitivity, brought in as a consultant. Think carefully, and start by brainstorming the main distinctions and likely failure cases before you write anything.

I lead a team drawing up the message format for a fleet of disaster-response drones. All operational messages are automatically logged and may be replayed later—hours or days after they were sent—to make decisions, and indexicals have already caused failures. A stored message like "I am trapped now at these coordinates" was replayed an hour later and sent a rescue crew to the wrong location. We are considering whether to simply ban pronouns and indexicals from the message vocabulary, replacing "I" with a stable unit identifier, "now" with a timestamp, "here" with map coordinates, and "this" with an explicit object descriptor. I suspect a blanket substitution rule will sometimes change what a message claims.

Give us a reusable procedure that a working engineer with no philosophy background, under time pressure, can follow to decide, for any operational message, whether rewriting it in indexical-free, timestamped form preserves what the message claims, and what to do when it does not. The procedure should be a checklist or decision tree in words, usable without the underlying theory but faithful to what is actually known about how indexicals behave. Work through two or three examples, including a replayed message that looks safe but is not, and a case where the procedure forces us to accept something we do not want to—for instance, a class of messages that cannot be safely archived at all. Explicitly flag the steps where the engineer must exercise judgment rather than follow a rule.

Finally, tell me how confident you are in the procedure as a whole, and single out the step or judgment you are least sure about.
