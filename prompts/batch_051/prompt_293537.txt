I am a PhD student in political theory, and I am leading a single 80-minute session for 18 upper-level undergraduates in a democratic theory course. I would like you to design a classroom simulation that makes students feel, rather than merely hear, a central idea from the sortition and lottocracy literature: that election and random selection are not simply two methods for choosing the same kind of decision-making body, but two different ways of constituting that body, with different selection effects, different distributions of competence and local knowledge, and different sources of legitimacy.

The exercise should be structured as a two-round comparison. In one round, the class should constitute a small decision-making body by election; in the other, by lottery. Each body should then decide a matter that has real, though small, stakes for the whole group, and on which the group as a whole has dispersed private information or heterogeneous interests. The task should be designed so that the two mechanisms sometimes predictably pull in different directions, but it should not be rigged so that the lottery always wins; both mechanisms need a fair run. The students should run into the differences themselves, before any conceptual vocabulary is introduced.

Please provide me with the following:

(1) Full rules and materials, including how to divide the room between the elected and allotted bodies, how to handle the fact that both rounds involve the same people, and how to prevent the second round from simply imitating the first.

(2) A realistic minute-by-minute timeline for the 80-minute session.

(3) A no-money payoff or incentive scheme that gives participants genuine stakes in the outcomes without involving cash.

(4) A debrief with a loose script, mapping specific moments in the exercise onto the conceptual distinctions at issue: ex ante equal chance of selection versus ex post electoral accountability, descriptive representation versus authorization, the competence objection, and the legitimacy of being selected by lot.

(5) An account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and how I should handle each failure.

(6) An honest note on which feature of lottocracy cannot be reproduced in an 18-person classroom and must be supplied by me in closing remarks.

The main idea should emerge from what the students do and observe, not from anything I say before the exercise. By the end, they should see concretely that introducing a lottery changes not only who governs but what the governing body knows, which interests are present, and what its members are answerable to.
