I lead the economic appraisal unit of a national infrastructure agency. Every month we set a discount rate for proposals whose costs land within a decade and whose benefits accrue over fifty to several thousand years: flood defenses, grid decarbonization, vaccine stockpiles, geological waste repositories. Analysts currently choose a rate by habit, and project rankings flip depending on who runs the numbers. Write a decision procedure my analysts can actually run in half a day with the project file. It should be a sequence of questions, each specifying what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the remaining step is a value judgment rather than arithmetic. Draw on what is genuinely established about four things: pure time preference; discounting because the affected people will be richer; discounting for the probability that the future payoff never arrives; and the behavioral finding that people discount the near future more steeply than the far future. The procedure should treat separately benefits that return to the same people who bear the cost and benefits that fall mostly on people not yet born. At the step where a pure rate of time preference is set, require an impartiality check: the analyst must write the justification as if they did not know which birth cohort they belong to, and record whether the rate survives that test. Then apply the procedure to three proposals: a coastal flood barrier whose benefits begin in twenty years; a decarbonization investment whose avoided climate damages mostly fall after 2100; and a pandemic vaccine stockpile that pays off only if a severe outbreak occurs. Make sure at least one application gives an answer my leadership will not want to accept, and state plainly what the procedure cannot decide.
