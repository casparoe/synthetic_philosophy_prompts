Two friends of mine got into it at a bar about whether you can legitimately take moral views from other people the way you take factual claims from experts, and they went several rounds with neither one budging. I was just listening and honestly couldn't tell who had the better of it, so I want a disinterested verdict. Here's the back-and-forth as I remember it.

(1) Dan opened with a division-of-labour point. He recently changed his mind about buying fast fashion because a friend who works in garment supply chains showed him how wage theft and pollution get hidden in the books. He said: I trust engineers about bridges and a doctor about my knee, so I can trust someone who's actually looked at a factory's accounts. If you insist that every moral view be worked out from first principles, you're just choosing to be ignorant.

(2) Maya said he was running together two different things. When you accept an engineer's verdict, you're getting information you couldn't easily get otherwise, and you still know roughly what would make it true. Taking over a moral verdict on say-so doesn't transfer the moral understanding. If Dan can't say why the fast-fashion thing is wrong beyond "my friend says so" — if he doesn't genuinely grasp the reasons — then his belief isn't moral knowledge, it's moral deference, a kind of fake competence.

(3) Dan replied that she was holding moral beliefs to a higher standard then she holds anything else to. Does she understand the mechanism of the vaccine she took, or why the plane stays up? No; she trusts and still calls it knowledge. Saying moral views need something extra is just special pleading.

(4) Maya said the cases aren't parallel. With a vaccine I can identify what I don't know and what test would settle it. But a borrowed moral view, if it's wrong, is my conduct going wrong, since I'm the one acting on it. So outsourcing moral judgment is irresponsible in a way outsourcing factual judgment isn't. She added that there's an autonomy problem: the whole point of a moral judgment is to make it yourself, not to be a mouthpiece for whoever talked to you last.

(5) Dan came back that she was making the perfect the enemy of the good. Nobody is self-made: she didn't derive her views on animal suffering from first principles, she got them from parents, teachers, essays. If "decide for yourself" means rediscover everything, she's no better off than him. And if it instead permits learning from people who give you arguments — which is what she does — then the only question is how much reasoning you have to internalize, which is a matter of degree, not a bright line. His friend gave him reasons, not just a conclusion. Maya was drawing a circle around her own reading habits.

(6) Maya said the circle is real: in an essay you get considerations you can weigh, while testimony hands you a conclusion and expects credit. Dan said that's wishful. When you read an essay you trust the author's choice of what to mention, how to frame it, what to emphasize — the verdict is already in the selection. At some point it's testimony all the way down.

Adjudicate this move by move. For each of (1) through (6), rate it from clearly right to clearly wrong, or somewhere in between, and justify each rating. Then step back and say what the argument is actally about and what they're both missing — whether the expert-comparison does real work, whether understanding is required for moral knowledge or for knowledge generally, whether essays are just testimony by another name, and whether the autonomy objection survives the fact that nobody authors their own moral views from scratch. It's fine if your conclusion is that one of them won early and neither noticed, or that the whole dispute is partly verbal.
