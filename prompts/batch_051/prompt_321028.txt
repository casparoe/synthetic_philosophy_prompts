I teach a graduate historiography seminar, and every year the discussion stalls in the same place. One side argues that historians should treat non-human phenomena—epidemic disease, long-term climatic shifts, major volcanic eruptions—as historical actors in their own right rather than as inert backdrop. For them, the eruption of Mount Tambora and the so-called year without a summer that followed, or the arrival of Old World pathogens in the Americas, did not merely set conditions for human events; they redirected those events, and any account that treats them as mere context is incomplete. The other side replies that this is a category error dressed up as theoretical sophistication. A volcano or a pathogen can be a cause, even a decisive cause, but only something that receives information and adjusts its conduct in response can be an actor; calling a climate shift an actor is a metaphor that quietly licenses environmental determinism, dissolving human choices into rainfall averages and mortality curves. Both sides have good cases, so the argument recurs, with each side accumulating examples without changing position.

I would like a diagnosis of this impasse, not a survey of the literature. My working hypothesis is that the two sides are engaged in three different disputes at once and do not notice that they pull apart. First, a semantic dispute about what the word 'actor' should mean in historical explanation: does it require intentions, or only the power to make a difference? Second, a substantive dispute about explanatory standards: must a historical explanation run through human purposes and institutions, or is it enough to identify a non-human process that changed the options available to people? Third, a dispute in starting intuitions about control and responsibility: how much of the human story should we credit to human agency at all, and what are we doing to historical accountability when we admit the climate into the cast of characters? Are these three questions genuinely independent, or do they collapse into one? Is there a hidden empirical question doing the real work—for example, whether counterfactual histories in which a particular epidemic never occurred support strong actor-language, or only weaker causal claims? And what would count as progress given the diagnosis: a definition, a distinction, a standard for weighting causes, a change in terminology, or something else? If your honest conclusion is that the debate is ill-posed and should be dissolved rather than settled, defend that conclusion rather than pretending to resolve it. Please answer in American English.
