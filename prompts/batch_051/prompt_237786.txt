I am a novelist, and I am working on a book in which two moral philosophers, after a decade of publishing against one another, are compelled by the terms of a research award to conduct a six-month adversarial collaboration on a single question: whether the numbers count when we must choose whom to save. One of them, the aggregator, holds that, other things being equal, we should save the greater number because five lives saved are a better outcome than one. The other, in the spirit of the position associated with John Taurek, denies that the numbers count in themselves: the five are five individuals, not a single fivefold beneficiary; each of the five and the one has, absent special obligations, an equally serious claim to be saved; saving the five is permissible but not required; and he is drawn to resolving the choice by an equal lottery. In the novel, the document the two finally sign is reproduced as an appendix, so it has to read like a real philosophical paper rather than a fictional prop.

Please draft that joint statement as the deliverable of a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Begin with a numbered agreed-claims section, substantive enough that both parties are genuinely staking something and not merely signing truisms. Then provide a disagreements table, ideally with columns for the crux, each side’s signed wording, whether the disagreement is substantive or merely verbal, and the agreed test with each side’s prediction recorded in advance. Treat, at minimum, the standard drug-allocation case in which one person needs the whole dose to survive and five each need a fifth of it; variations in the ratio of one to many; and the question of whether a fair procedure should give the single person and each of the five an equal individual chance of being saved or instead give the two alternatives an equal chance. Write the document itself, not a description of it, and use British English throughout.
