I write about the science of prediction, and for a piece I am working on I keep returning to a disagreement about the inside view and the outside view. One researcher has been telling me that these are not merely two ways of estimating a quantity but two genuinely different stances toward one's own judgment: the outside view, in the strong version of the claim, works precisely because it enforces epistemic modesty, a suspension of the conviction that one's own case is special. A more skeptical source counters that every sound outside-view analysis reduces to three steps—identify an appropriate reference class, take its base rate, and adjust only when one can justify the adjustment—so that talk of a distinct stance or a distinctive virtue of modesty is decoration for ordinary statistical reasoning.

I would like you to design the strongest test you can of whether taking the outside view is a distinct mode of judgment, and whether it improves forecasts or decisions in a way that cannot be captured entirely by base-rate updating. Be concrete about the task, the comparisons, and the outcome measures; specify what result would favor the distinct-stance interpretation and what result would favor the base-rates-only interpretation. Then say what even a favorable result would not settle—in particular, whether epistemic modesty is a real cognitive stance or merely a useful label for good statistical hygiene. Do not lay out the options and stop: take a definite position on which way you believe the strongest version of the evidence would come out, and state how confident you are in that verdict and what would change your mind. Please write your answer in American English.
