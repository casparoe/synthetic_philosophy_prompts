I'm an engineer by training — most of my career has been in build systems and developer tooling — with no formal philosophy background, so I want to make sure I'm not reinventing something obvious or dressing up a cognitive bias as a mechanism. I have a concept I'd like assessed honestly.

I've been reading around bounded rationality and the account of satisficing developed by Herbert Simon. As I understand it, satisficing models choice under limited time and information roughly as: set an aspiration level for what counts as acceptable, search until an option meets it, then stop. What I want examined isn't that model itself but a specific failure mode in how the aspiration level moves across repeated decisions.

I call this failure mode the satisficing ratchet. The core claim is that each accepted "good enough" outcome tends to silently re-anchor the standard downward for the next decision. Once a team ships something, the fact that it shipped starts functioning as evidence about what its standards actually were; the output becomes the new prototype of acceptability. Next time, the bar is set not where it was deliberately placed but where the last outcome happened to land. The movement is asymmetric: standards drift down by default, because lowering a standard requires no explicit act — it just stops being noticed — while raising one requires a deliberate, effortful act of redefinition against the grain of habit.

A concrete case: the definition of "done" on a long-lived codebase. On the first release, a team agrees that done means tested, reviewed, documented, and free of known regressions. They ship under deadline with three known workarounds, and the release is accepted. Three months later, "done" functionally means it builds, the main pathway works, and the workarounds are tracked somewhere. No one ever formally revised the definition; it decayed through a sequence of individually reasonable, good-enough decisions, each of which took the previous output as its input for the next judgment. The same shape appears in alerting: after a noisy production page, the first instinct is to widen or silence the threshold rather than make the signal reliable, which slowly leaves the system blind to exactly the slow regressions it was meant to catch.

My stronger claim is that this mechanism explains part of why competent teams grow mediocre without any change in personnel, incentives, or constraints. The constraints don't tighten; the standard crawls down to meet the outputs, and because each step is small, local, and defensible, nobody registers the cumulative drift. I suspect this connects to things in philosophy like adaptive preferences, but I don't have the vocabulary to place it.

I'd like an honest assessment. In particular: (1) Is this a coherent concept, or am I smuggling in hindsight bias, status quo bias, or ordinary rationalization under a new name? (2) Does it already exist under another name — in the literature on satisficing, aspiration adaptation, organizational learning, or adaptive preferences — and what, if anything, is left over once I grant those antecedents? (3) Where does the idea plainly break, and what would a defender have to concede? (4) What are the three strongest objections, and are any of them fatal rather than repairable? Don't bother with citations or a reading list; I'll track those down myself if I develop this further. I care more about whether the mechanism itself holds up.
