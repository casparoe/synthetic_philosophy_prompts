A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about solar geoengineering, and we came away with the usual tangle rather than a position. He kept insisting that even the serious prospect of stratospheric aerosol injection is dangerous because it is a moral hazard: governments and firms will treat the option as a reason to keep emitting. I kept pressing the opposite worry, that if a climate emergency ever arrives and there is no prior agreement, a single capable state may deploy it alone and everyone else will merely experience the consequences. We also kept returning to termination shock: if a deployment is interrupted, the warming it had been masking is expected to return quickly, and that seems to change what consent and exit even mean. By the end it was obvious we were not really arguing about the technology; we were arguing about who, if anyone, could be entitled to decide, on what basis, and with what recourse for everyone else.

Before I read any more proposals for how a deployment decision should be made — a Security Council vote, a club of the most affected states, a supermajority treaty, a default ban with an emergency override — I want the specification written out first. I need the desiderata that any adequate account of who decides about solar geoengineering must meet. Give the cases it must handle and the distinctions it cannot collapse: for example, and without treating my list as exhaustive, the difference between small short-lived field experiments and sustained deployment, between a state acting on a broad scientific consensus and a state acting on its own emergency claim, between those exposed mainly to benefits and those exposed mainly to harms, and between present people and future generations. For each desideratum, give the consideration or case that forces it. I suspect the three worries we argued about each translate differently into requirements on a decision procedure, and I want that made explicit: whether moral hazard is a constraint on legitimate procedure or a prediction about how agents will respond to it, and whether termination shock is a constraint on deployment, on exit, or on both.

Then flag which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable constraints of legitimacy and which are negotiable preferences of particular camps, and say for each whether it is a requirement of legitimacy or only of wise policy. If some subset of the desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied — for instance, if a procedure cannot be both fast enough for an emergency and inclusive enough for the affected — say so, because that is the part I most want to see. Finally, tell me which single requirement, if any, excludes the most candidate proposals, including any that would exclude unilateral deployment by a technologically capable state acting under a claimed emergency. Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this area, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Write in American English.
