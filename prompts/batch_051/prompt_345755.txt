I've been in a working group on foreign-policy decision-making, and we keep going in circles about whether historical analogies do more harm than good. Everyone agrees that Munich got invoked to justify escalations that later got called another Vietnam, and that the Thucydides trap now does something similar in U.S.-China talk, but nobody can agree on what a fix would actually look like. We're explicitly not asking whether analogy is good or bad; we want to know how one specific institutional rule would behave.

Here's the proposal we can't seem to kick apart. Every policy memo that invokes a named historical analogy—Munich, Vietnam, the Thucydides trap, 1914, Suez, whatever—must be filed along with an analogy disclosure form. The form has three boxes. (a) State the causal mechanism that you think made the original case come out the way it did. (b) List at least two observable indicators that, if absent now, would show that mechanism isn't operating in the present case. (c) Name at least one rival analogy that points toward the opposite policy, with it's own indicators. A rotating three-person Analogy Review Committee scores the form on how falsifiable and non-tendentious it is. Memos that pass get an "audited analogy" tag; memos that fail still circulate but with a "raw analogy" warning; repeated failures suspend your right to invoke analogies in memos for a quarter.

I want you, as a political scientist or historian who actually studies how analogical reasoning works inside institutions, to think hard about how this would play out. Don't tell me whether it's a good idea in the abstract. Tell me what it incentivizes: among the analysts writing memos, among the senior principals who read them, among the reviewers on the committee, and among outside critics. Where does it get gamed first—box-ticking, reverse-engineering the indicators, capturing the committee, smuggling analogies in under other names? Does it change which analogies survive, or just make them more elaborate? And can the failure modes be fixed without turning the form into another bureaucratic ritual? Please separate what is actually settled about historical analogy from what is genuinely contested among people who know the literature, and don't present a contested view as the consensus. Start by brainstorming the likely failure modes, then work through the strongest ones.
