For an interdisciplinary workshop on intertemporal choice and long-term policy, I have been asked to write down my working position on discounting so that others can stress-test it. I want an audit of whether the following commitments hang together, not a general survey of the topic.

(1) Pure time preference—counting a unit of well-being as less important merely because it arrives later in time—is rationally indefensible. Temporal position is no better a ground for discounting than spatial position would be.

(2) Welfare analysis should nonetheless respect people's expressed time preferences. If a competent adult persistently values present consumption far more than future consumption, policy should not override that valuation; doing so would be objectionably paternalistic.

(3) For public investment and climate policy I favour a declining social discount rate: a modest positive near-term rate, in the region of 3–4 percent, falling to a floor of around 1 percent over a century or so.

(4) The parameters of the Ramsey discounting formula should, where possible, be calibrated to market interest rates and survey evidence rather than fixed by philosophical argument alone.

(5) Present bias—the hyperbolic tendency to reverse one's own earlier plans as the immediate future approaches—is a defect rather than a legitimate preference. Where it produces self-defeating behaviour such as chronic undersaving or procrastination, defaults and nudges are a justified corrective.

(6) Where a person's present and future selves conflict, the future self's retrospective judgment is authoritative when the person would later endorse the correction; absent such later endorsement, I would not override a deliberate, informed choice to discount one's own future.

(7) Impartiality across time commits me to the view that the well-being of people living two centuries from now counts, in itself, no less than that of people alive today; the rate used for long-run climate damages therefore must not encode mere impatience.

I do not have a settled view about where the strain lies, and the numbering carries no significance. Please go through these one pair at a time: which commitments are genuinely in tension with one another, and which only appear to be? Rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious, and for each say which commitment is doing the pulling and what the cheapest revision would be. Finally, tell me whether a single revision would defuse the worst cluster while preserving the core of the view, and what it would cost.
