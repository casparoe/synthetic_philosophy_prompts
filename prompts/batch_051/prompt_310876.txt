Act as a philosopher of science who has thought carefully about both the psychology of attitudes toward death and the arguments for and against radical life extension.

I am a journalist who covers longevity science for a general-interest magazine, and I am preparing a long piece on the case for treating aging as a medical condition. In the research and advocacy community I cover, the term deathism is used for the view that aging and death are natural, inevitable, or even necessary for life to have meaning, and that radical life extension would therefore be bad. Many of the researchers I interview say deathism is not a real position at all but a bias: a status-quo bias, a terror-management defense, or a sour-grapes rationalization from people who know they are going to die. I find that explanation increasingly convincing, and I now tend to treat the familiar objections—death gives life its urgency, mortality is what makes us human, only a limited lifespan makes our choices matter—as symptoms of that bias rather than as arguments that need to be weighed. Before you answer, think through the strongest version of the case on each side. Then give me exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion, on whether I am justified in dismissing those objections as deathism, and if not, how I should handle them in my reporting.
