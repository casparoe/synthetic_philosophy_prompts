I'm a retired atmospheric scientist (aerosol chemistry, mostly) who has only recently got into philosophy, and I'm trying to think carefully about solar geoengineering instead of just reacting to headlines. The thing I can't get past is how often the 1991 Mount Pinatubo eruption gets wheeled out as nature's own field trial of stratospheric aerosol injection (SAI). So I want an analysis of that episode through the three ideas that keep dominating the philosophical side: the moral-hazard worry, termination shock, and the 'who decides' problem.

For this exercise, take the salient facts as given: Pinatubo was the largest stratospheric injection of the twentieth century (around 15–20 megatonnes of SO2), noone chose it, it lowered global mean temperature by roughly half a degree for a year or two, and the signal faded as the stratosphere cleaned itself out. I don't need more volcano detail than that. Treat it as nature running SAI once and then phasing it out on nature's own schedule.

What I'm after is a two-way case analysis, not a summary of the eruption. Specifically:

(1) Moral hazard. The standard worry is that a credible backup plan weakens mitigation. But Pinatubo wasn't a technology; it was a free proof-of-concept. Does a natural proof-of-concept carry the same moral hazard as a deployable technology? Please distinguish a soft version (the existence of research or a plan displacing mitigation effort) from a hard version (deployment becoming a positive justification for continuing to emit), and say whether the 1990s give any real evidence either way.

(2) Termination shock. The worry is that if SAI is stopped abruptly, warming catches up fast. Pinatubo didn't stop abruptly: it ramped down as the aerosols settled out on nature's schedule, with nobody's politics involved. So is it a poor test of termination shock, or does it reveal something about the difference between a scheduled phase-out and an interrupted deployment? I genuinely can't tell, and I'd like an honest account of what a single pulse like this cannot show about a sustained programme.

(3) Who decides. A volcano imposed a global intervention without consent, compensation, or anyone to blame. Does the fact that nobody chose Pinatubo make consent look more or less central to the ethics of SAI, and what does it do to the 'free driver' worry — a single state or company able to do it on the cheap without asking?

Then I'd like you to say explicitly where the analogy breaks, and end with a verdict: does the Pinatubo case actually move any of the three debates, or do both sides just use it as a prop?
