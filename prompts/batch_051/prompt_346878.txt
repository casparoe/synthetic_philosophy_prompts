I have been defending a position in a reading group over the past few weeks and would like it subjected to a genuinely searching stress test rather than a sympathetic restatement. The position concerns whether a person can coherently assign well-calibrated probabilities to her own not-yet-made choices. The view I have been defending is that there is something structurally unstable about doing so: a genuine choice is not the sort of event I can stand outside of and predict in the way I predict the weather or another person's behaviour, because deliberating about whether to do A while simultaneously holding a credence that I will do A seems to treat me both as the author of the decision and as its spectator. The stronger version of the claim, which is the one that interests me, is that deliberation crowds out prediction. As soon as I take my own credence seriously as part of my evidence, it feeds back into my practical reasoning in a way that should dissolve its status as a prediction, and any attempt to render the credence consistent merely postpones the instability. This is not the mundane observation that I already know what I intend; it is the stronger claim that, for an agent who has not yet settled the question, there is no determinate fact of the matter about how likely she is to choose each option, and that attempts to assign such probabilities are either disguised intentions, expressions of current inclination, or a form of self-deception.

I would like you to present the strongest honest case against this position. What would have to be true for it to be false, or for its scope to shrink to vanishing point? Please distinguish the considerations carefully: some would be decisive if established, some merely awkward, and some only apparently damaging. You might draw on cases in which self-assigned probabilities seem genuinely useful rather than incoherent, formal or decision-theoretic considerations, the phenomenology of deliberation, the distinction between prediction and intention, the evidential relevance of my own past behaviour and stable psychological traits, and the possibility that the view has been quietly insulated by qualifications until it risks nothing at all. Where a defender of the position would himself concede that a consideration counts against it, say so, and where he would reasonably refuse to, explain why; I also want an assessment of whether the view, on its most defensible formulation, is exposed to empirical or argumentative defeat at all, and whether its insulation would be a virtue or a vice.

For my own part, I would find the most persuasive kind of objection to be a concrete, live case in which an agent plainly benefits from holding a determinate probability over one of her own future choices without thereby abandoning the stance of a deliberator, so I would ask you to weigh that possibility especially carefully. Please write your answer in British English.
