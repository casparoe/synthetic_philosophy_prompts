I've come to hold the view that ordinary weakness of will, what philosophers call akrasia, is best explained as a phenomenon of hyperbolic discounting. On this picture, the weak-willed agent does not, at the moment of action, act against her own simultaneous, all-things-considered judgment about what would be best. Instead, her preferences are indexed to times, and because her discount function is hyperbolic rather than exponential, a smaller, sooner reward can temporarily displace a larger, later good to which she was, and later will again be, firmly committed. Socrates' claim that akrasia is impossible and Aristotle's more concessive account of being overcome by appetite both miss what is really going on: the agent is not simply ignorant, and passion is not doing the work; she is, in effect, a sequence of temporally indexed selves whose preferences can reverse as the delay to a reward shrinks. I take addiction and compulsion to be importantly different and want to set them aside, but whether that boundary can be defended is itself part of what I want tested. The view predicts, among other things, that clear-eyed akrasia, choosing against one's own simultaneous, fully informed, occurrent better judgment, should be rare or nonexistent, and that felt regret should follow a U-shaped pattern over time.
