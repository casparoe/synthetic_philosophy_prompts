I spent my working life fitting models to noisy data in the natural sciences, and I have only recently begun reading the philosophical literature on induction. I keep returning to the no-free-lunch theorems from machine learning, which I understand to show that, averaged over all possible target functions, every learning algorithm performs exactly as well as every other: any advantage on one class of functions is paid for by a matching disadvantage elsewhere. I have a number of settled verdicts about inductive practice, and I want to know what principle, if any, captures them.

1. If I fit planetary positions, I regard a two-parameter inverse-square law as far better supported than a ninety-parameter polynomial that fits the data exactly, even though the polynomial fits better.

2. If two teams report the very same fitted curve, but one fixed the functional form before seeing the data while the other searched thousands of forms afterward and reported the best, I trust the first team's curve for prediction more than the second's.

3. If a model is derived from an already successful physical theory, I trust it in a new domain more than an empirically equivalent model with no theoretical backing.

4. Yet I also trust a crude empirical rule that has succeeded across many unrelated domains—say, taking the long-run seasonal average—more than an elegant but untested rule in a new domain, even when the elegant rule is simpler.

5. The no-free-lunch theorem leaves me completely unmoved as a skeptic: it does not lower my confidence that a well-tested regularity will hold in the next instance. But it does force me to admit I cannot state what justifies that confidence, once I have granted that no rule is better than any other over the whole space of possible worlds.

Is there a single principle that delivers all five verdicts, and if not, which should I abandon? Please state the principle precisely, say what it predicts for a case I have not listed—for instance, a model chosen by an automated search procedure that was itself selected because it worked well on earlier data—and say honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate. Finally, say how confident you are in the principle and single out the point you are least sure of.
