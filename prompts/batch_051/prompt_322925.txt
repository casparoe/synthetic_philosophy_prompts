I keep coming back to Bertrand's chord paradox and I'm not sure I've ever seen a fair account of what it acutally shows. My friend sent me this summary from some blog and I can't tell if it's right:

"The principle of indifference says that when you have no reason to favor one possibility over another, you should assign them equal probability. Bertrand's paradox shows this principle is incoherent. In the chord problem, three different ways of picking a chord at random — random endpoints, random radius, random midpoint — are all equally licensed by the principle, but they give probabilities of 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4 for the same event. Since the same principle leads to contradictory answers, classical probability is refuted, and the only consistent way out is to treat all probabilities as subjective degrees of belief."

I'm inclined to believe the last bit because everyone says Bertrand killed the classical interpretation, but the summary feels too glib. Go sentence by sentence: which parts would a careful philosopher of probability accept, which are overstatements, and is the conclusion about subjectivity a consequence of anything true in the summary? Also, is it fair to say the three methods are "all equally licensed by the principle," or is that doing all the work? What would actually count as a resolution of the paradox — a better symmetry argument, a distinction about how "random chord" is specified, or something else?
