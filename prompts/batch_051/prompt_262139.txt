I recently became the sole adjudicator for a university emergency fund that makes one-time grants to students in acute financial distress. The fund's charter says it exists for bad luck rather than bad choices, but offers no operational definition, and I decide several cases a week, alone, usually in under half an hour, without legal or philosophical training. Write me a reusable decision procedure, grounded in luck egalitarianism, for sorting each case into brute luck or option luck: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, how to proceed when I cannot establish what risks the student actually faced, and the exact points at which the procedure must hand the decision back to my own judgment. Flag where the underlying theory is unresolved or where the procedure will systematically misfire, such as expensive tastes, conditions with mixed genetic and behavioral causes, and background disadvantages that look like bad options rather than bad choices. Then run the procedure on three cases, including at least one where it tells me to deny aid even though I would rather grant it: (a) a student who lost the term's rent in a crypto gamble after watching an older sibling do the same; (b) a student whose family home was destroyed by a flood and whose parents can no longer help with rent; and (c) a student with a painful but manageable chronic condition who spent their savings on an unproven treatment a friend recommended.
