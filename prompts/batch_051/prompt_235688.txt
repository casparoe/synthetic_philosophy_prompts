I've always taken the rule of law to mean, among other things, that a law on the books is a law, full stop. If a legislature passed it and never repealed it, a court should apply it; otherwise the state is holding a pile of dormant statutes it can dust off whenever it wants to single someone out, which is basically arbitrary power with better posture. On that view the defect is never the enforcement, only the failure to keep the statute book clean. Enforce it or repeal it, but don't leave it lying around as a snare. That view feels nearly axiomatic to me.

And then I think about desuetude — the idea that a law can lapse through long non-enforcement — and my intuition won't go along. If someone gets prosecuted today under a 150-year-old statute that no one has enforced in living memory and that everyone has treated as a dead letter for generations, I think that's an injustice, even though the statute was validly passed and never formally repealed. They had no notice in any sense that matters; they couldn't have organized their life around it. I want to say the state shouldn't get to do that.

So I've got a theory and an intuition pulling opposite ways. The clean rule-of-law view says desuetude is just a symptom of legislative laziness: the code says X, so the only honest options are apply X or repeal X. The intuition says applying a zombie law is itself an attack on law's point of guiding conduct instead of ambushing it.

Lay out the strongest version of both and tell me which should give. Is my intuition just squeamishness about enforcement against sympathetic defendants, so the principled answer really is 'repeal it, but until then it's law'? Or is my 'law on the books is a law' principle too crude, and a proper account of the rule of law should make room for desuetude? Is there a reading on which they're compatible — say, non-enforcement is itself a form of legal change through custom or estoppel, without any formal repeal? And whichever way it goes, how do I tell a genuinely dead law from a live one that's merely under-enforced, since that line seems to do all the work but I can't see where to draw it. What would justify each move — dropping the intuition, amending the theory, or declaring them compatible — rather than just reaching for the one that feels comfortable?
