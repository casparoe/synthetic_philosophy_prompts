My company's employee handbook has this line I've been chewing on since our last promotion cycle. It says: "Promotions and compensation increases will be awarded strictly on the basis of merit, where merit means the quality and impact of the employee's work during the review period." Okay. Here's the case I don't think anyone writing that line imagined. Last year our main competitor imploded in a scandal and basically stopped competing for about six months. Our sales team didn't do anything different; the accounts simply fell into their laps. Two reps finished the year at roughly triple their targets, their merit ratings got computed from those numbers by the formula, and both were promoted with max bonuses. One of them is widely known internally to be medicore at the actual job — disorganized, slow, customers find her hard to deal with — she just happened to own the territory where the competitor's biggest accounts came loose. Is she owed that promotion under the rule? Walk through what the words say, what the purpose of the clause probably was, and what the people who drafted it would have said if somebody had asked them about a windfall. Does the rule presuppose that merit is about producing outcomes no matter how they came about, or can it be read so that merit tracks effort and skill, in which case a lucky mediocre rep doesn't have it? And if those readings come apart, which one should govern, and what would the company have to change in the writing for it to govern consistently?
