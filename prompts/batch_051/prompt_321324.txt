I've been going around in circles with a friend about Hayek-style price-as-information-aggregation versus the no-trade theorem, and I want to see somebody actually reason through a clean hypothetical instead of just reciting the theorem. Here's the setup. A foundation sets up an electronic prediction market for a single binary contract, say whether a carbon tax bill passes the U.S. Senate before 2027. The only people allowed to trade are 100 recruits who are, by construction, perfect rational Bayesians: they share one prior on the bill passing (40%, let's say), they're all risk-neutral and there purely to make money, there is no hedging demand, no liquidity needs, no fees, no market maker, and no noise traders. Before the market opens, each of the 100 privately sees a different informative signal about the bill's fate, drawn from a known distribution and independent conditional on the true outcome, and it's common knowledge that all of that is true. The order book is public, so every bid, ask, and trade is visible to everyone in real time; the market opens with an empty book and no seed price. I'm not asking whether real prediction markets trade, they obviously do. I'm asking about this artificial setup. Would any trade ever occur? If yes, describe the first hour as concretely as you can and explain what exactly lets it happen despite the no-trade logic. If no, explain the mechanism that stops the first person from posting a quote that somebody else would take, and say what that implies for the Hayekian idea that a market price can aggregate information no single trader has. For each link in the chain, keep apart what follows pretty robustly from the setup and what depends on details like how fast quotes update or whether people can condition on the absence of quotes. Then, if your answer is that trade never gets going, tell me which single assumption you'd relax first to get an information-revealing price, and why that exact assumption is doing the work. Formatting request: just write continuous prose, like an email; no headings, bullets, bold, or tables.
