I keep running this argument and I can't find the bad step, but the conclusion feels intolerable. Suppose we have to choose between two energy policies: conservation leaves future people very well off, while depletion leaves the people born two centuries from now with lives that are still worth living but clearly worse than the lives people would have had under conservation. Everyone says depletion is wrong because of what it does to those future people. But:

(1) An action is wrong only if it harms some particular person.
(2) An action harms a person only if it leaves that person worse off than they would have been had the action not been performed.
(3) The people alive two hundred years from now under depletion owe their existence to that choice; if we had chosen conservation, different people would have been born, through different timing and different pairings, so these particular depletion-born people would never have existed.
(4) A life worth living is not worse then never existing.
(5) So no depletion-born person is worse off than they would have been without the depletion policy: the alternative for them is nonexistence, not a better life.
(6) So the depletion policy harms no one.
(7) So the depletion policy is not wrong.

I find (1), (2), and (3) each plausible, and (4) hard to deny, but (7) strikes me as false. I half-suspect (2) is doing something sneaky, but I can't make the charge stick. Is the argument valid? If it is, which premise do I actually have to give up, and what is the strongest objection to each of the plausible exit routes? If it isn't valid, which step sneaks something through? Be specific about the step and the reading of it.
