Act as a philosopher of technology who works on technological change, induction, and modern AI risk, and who is comfortable distinguishing historical claims from philosophical ones. Last night I had a long argument with a friend about whether historical analogies can tell us anything reliable about transformative AI. He is skeptical that analogies are anything more than seductive stories. I ended up defending the following argument, and now I am not sure whether it survives his objections.

(1) The printing press did not merely speed up the copying of books; by making texts widely reproducible, it undermined centralized control over interpretation and eventually helped restructure religious and political authority.

(2) The Industrial Revolution did not merely raise output; by changing the returns to capital and labor, it transformed class structure, patterns of urbanization, and what states had to be able to do.

(3) Nuclear weapons did not merely make one kind of bomb much bigger; by making total war between the major powers seem unwinnable, they shifted great-power conflict into deterrence, proxy wars, and technological competition.

(4) These cases share a pattern: when a technology sharply lowers a deeply embedded cost, its most consequential effects tend to be institutional and political restructuring, not merely the specific tasks it was first applied to.

(5) AI sharply lowers the cost of performing a wide range of cognitive and predictive tasks.

(6) Therefore, AI's most consequential effects will likely be institutional and political restructuring, not merely the particular applications or accident scenarios that currently dominate debate.

(7) Therefore, forecasting AI's specific harms from present use cases is unreliable in principle, while historical analogies still let us know the general shape of what is coming.

My friend attacked step (4) as a wild induction from three hand-picked cases, step (5) as smuggling in the claim that AI is general-purpose without an argument, and step (7) as self-defeating because it uses analogies to support exactly the kind of broad forecast it says cannot be made in detail. I half-suspect he is right that something goes wrong in the move from (6) to (7), but I cannot locate the exact failure.
