In our moral revolutions course we have been comparing three candidate drivers of norm change—explicit moral argument, material interest, and honor—using abolition and dueling as the standard cases. I would like to stress-test that framework on the end of foot-binding in China, working from the following facts. Bound feet were for centuries central to a daughter's marriageability and to family status across much of Han Chinese society, and the practice extended through many social classes; earlier bans by decree and later moral arguments that the practice was cruel had little visible effect. The decline began in earnest only with anti-foot-binding societies whose members publicly pledged both not to bind their daughters' feet and not to accept bound-footed wives for their sons, and with reformers' reframing of bound feet as proof of China's humiliating backwardness in foreign eyes. Urban and elite practice then collapsed within roughly a generation, with formal state prohibition arriving late and being only unevenly enforced. Please analyze the episode by asking what each driver explains and what each leaves out. In particular: can the honor mechanism be separated from material interest here, given that the marriage market was simultaneously a status system and an economic institution; does this case force any revision to the view that moral argument only consolidates changes already driven by interest or honor; and does it suggest that moral revolutions are one kind of process or several?
