I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on property, and we spent last week on Locke’s labor theory of acquisition in the fifth chapter of the Second Treatise of Government. The class split into two camps: one side treated his proviso as a constraint that money and the growth of an economy simply make obsolete, and the other side treated it as the real normative core of the theory, with surprising distributive consequences if applied to contemporary holdings. I want to show them that the terrain is finer than that, and I’d like to give them a sorting quiz that returns something more informative than “propertarian” versus “radical.”

Act as a political philosopher who works on Locke’s theory of property and its modern descendants. Think carefully before writing, perhaps by first listing the distinct questions on which readers of Locke actually divide and then assembling combinations of answers into a logical space. Your deliverable should be a decision tree or short sorting quiz, roughly eight to twelve leaves, that takes someone from concrete judgments about cases to a named position. Use branch-node questions that students can answer without already knowing the labels, such as: Does labor generate a right only in small, pre-monetary conditions, or does the same principle operate now in attenuated form? Does the spoilage limit constrain only the waste of physical things, or also the indefinite accumulation of money or claims? Does the requirement to leave enough and as good mean same-quality raw resources, comparable opportunities, or merely no one left worse off? Is original acquisition a source of full title, or only a use-right that the commonwealth may revise? Build the tree from combinations of answers first, then locate positions in the resulting space. Include at least Locke’s own position, a Nozick-style historical entitlement view, a left-libertarian equal-share view, and a common-ownership view; add other leaves as needed. Flag any positions your tree cannot separate, and note combinations that no one occupies, with a brief diagnosis of whether the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

The final answer should be in American English. Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a contested interpretation as the consensus. Also flag places where your reading of Locke leans on one side of a scholarly dispute.
