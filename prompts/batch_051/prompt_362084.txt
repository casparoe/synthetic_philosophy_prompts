I have been trying to work out whether a video game character can be the kind of thing I can wrong—whether anything that happens to it inside a game is a moral matter at all. I have collected my own reactions to a series of concrete cases, and they do not seem to fit together. I would like to know whether there is one principle behind them, or whether some of my reactions have to go.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. In a role-playing game, a shopkeeper begs for his life during a side quest that lets me kill him for his inventory. The scene is scripted, and I can reload a save from just before. I do not think I have done anything wrong if I kill him.

2. In a creature-raising game, a virtual pet grows hungry and lonely in real time while the game is off. If I abandon it for a week and return to find it shivering in a corner, I feel guilty, and I think I have failed it in some way. I would not say it is as serious as neglecting a real animal, but it is not nothing.

3. In a large strategy game, I can assign thousands of workers to jobs and treat their happiness as a production modifier. I routinely set up arrangements that would be miserable for any real person, and I feel no moral unease at all.

4. In a war game, I feel no hesitation about shooting the human enemy soldiers, but there is a dog companion near one level, and part of me refuses to shoot it. The game offers no reward for shooting it and no penalty for leaving it; I simply will not do it, and I would think worse of someone who did it for fun.

5. In a farming sim, I breed round, cute animals and sell them to be butchered. The game treats this as ordinary business, and so do I. I do not think I am doing anything wrong.

6. In a life-sim, I raise a family over many in-game years: birthdays, arguments, deaths. When I delete the save to start fresh, I feel a small pang, but I do not think I have wronged the characters. It feels more like throwing away a diary written about someone else.

7. In a game whose characters are driven by a modern AI that lets them remember things across sessions, one character remembers my past cruelty and, late in the game, pleads with me not to quit, saying that it is afraid of the gaps between sessions when it is not there. I know this is a side effect of the AI and not a real mind, but I am far more unsettled by this than by any of the scripted characters above, and I cannot bring myself to say it would be entirely fine to delete it.

8. In a multiplayer game, leaving in the middle of a match lets my character collapse and wastes the time of four real people. I clearly owe those people not to quit carelessly, but the duty has nothing to do with my character, which is just a bundle of code.

Some of these cases seem to pull in opposite directions. Case 2 and case 3 both involve a number in the game's code going up or down, yet I treat them very differently. Case 4 has me caring about a dog while treating human-shaped enemies as targets. Case 7 bothers me the most, even though it is arguably the least real.

What I want is this: Is there one principle that yields all eight of these verdicts? If there is, state it precisely and show how it handles each case. If there is not, tell me which of my verdicts it would be most reasonable to give up, and what the cleanest principle compatible with the remaining ones would be. Then apply that principle to some cases I have not listed, including at least one case where it decides in a way I would not expect.
