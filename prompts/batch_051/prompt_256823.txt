He reckons the whole thing is basically a statistical artifact: the countries that score high on gender equality have different occupational classifications, more women do STEM in places like Algeria because those are the economically secure jobs rather than because of freer choice, and the paradox apparently disappears once you control for GDP and how "STEM" is defined. I don't buy it. I think in more egalitarian countries people are freer to choose according to their actual interests, and those interests differ on average between men and women, which is why you get more gender-typical career paths in Scandinavia, not less. The argument got a bit heated.

The proposition is this: in countries with high levels of gender equality, the tendency for men's and women's career choices to be more gender-typical than in less egalitarian countries is best explained by stable average differences in interests or preferences with a substantial biological or evolved component, rather than by lingering socialisation, discrimination, or measurement problems.
