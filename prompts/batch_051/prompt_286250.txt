I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in moral philosophy, 16 students, 90 minutes. Design a classroom activity that makes them feel the force of the idea that someone can be complicit in a serious wrong without any single act of theirs causing it. The activity should be built around a concrete, mundane institutional routine, such as an eviction, a procurement chain, or an audit, in which the harm emerges from how several individually defensible tasks interlock, so that no participant's contribution is necessary or sufficient for the outcome. Give me the rules, roles, materials, and a timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened onto the distinction between causal contribution and participatory involvement in a joint practice, including the thought that one can share in a wrong by acting within a structure even when one's own act is harmless in isolation. There should be no lecture up front, and the terms "complicity," "causation," and "wrongdoing" should not be used until the debrief. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be explicit about which element of the idea the activity cannot make students feel and that I will have to supply. End with a single sentence beginning "Summary:" that captures the activity's core mechanism and the idea it is meant to make felt.
