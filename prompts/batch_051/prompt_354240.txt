You are a professional philosopher with expertise in early modern political thought and contemporary epistemology. Think through the following case carefully; do not just give a yes-or-no verdict. First brainstorm the conditions under which the policy tracks truth, then the conditions under which it merely tracks power.

Blaise Pascal, in the *Pensées*, has a fragment on justice and force whose argument I remember roughly as follows. It is right that what is just should be followed, but it is necessary that what is strongest should be followed. Justice without force is powerless; force without justice is tyrannical. Justice is subject to dispute, while force is easily recognized and not disputed. Therefore, since we cannot make what is just strong, we make what is strong just.

I have turned that into a standing epistemic policy for my own thinking about contested political and moral questions. When I cannot independently determine which side's claim of justice is correct, and one side's rule is actually backed by effective enforcement while the other's is not, I treat the enforced position as my working belief about what is just. My rationale is that I have no epistemically neutral access to justice before force, so the only available concrete sign of justice is what survives and stabilizes through force. I do not endorse the stronger side merely because it wins; I use Pascal's point that force is the one thing not subject to dispute as a tie-breaker.

Is this policy defensible? Under what assumptions would it be a reasonable heuristic rather than a bias dressed up as a principle? Can it be restated to keep whatever insight it has without rationalizing submission to power?
