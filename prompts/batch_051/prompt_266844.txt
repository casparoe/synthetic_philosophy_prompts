I spent most of my working life in the natural sciences, specifically in condensed matter physics, where symmetry arguments were part of the daily furniture and where I picked up Noether's theorem in the way one does: as a piece of mathematics one uses rather than reflects upon. Since retiring I have been reading my way into the philosophy of physics, which is partly familiar and partly unsettling, because I keep discovering how much philosophical weight can be hung on results I once treated as machinery. Recently I came across the following paragraph in a popular book on the philosophy of science, written for a general audience by someone who, so far as I can tell, is not a practising physicist:

"Emmy Noether's theorem shows that every symmetry of nature carries a conservation law with it. If the laws of physics are unchanged from one moment to the next, that is why energy is conserved; if they do not care about direction, that is why angular momentum is conserved. What this means is that symmetry is not merely a convenient bookkeeping device but the deep physical explanation of conservation. A system possesses a conserved quantity exactly when it has the corresponding symmetry, and the invariance is, in a real sense, the cause of the conservation. The fact that the laws of physics are the same at every instant is therefore the reason the total energy of the universe is constant."

I have the uneasy sense that this passage is right enough to be persuasive and wrong enough to mislead. My own training tells me, for instance, that 'every symmetry' cannot be right as stated, since I spent years invoking discrete symmetries that do not issue in the way the author describes; and I have never been comfortable with the leap from a mathematical identity to the words 'explanation' and 'cause'. But I do not trust four-decade-old technical habits to tell me where the philosophical claims come apart, and I have no wish to be the retired scientist who confidently corrects a book from a half-remembered textbook chapter. Could you go through the paragraph sentence by sentence and give me a committed verdict rather than a menu of possibilities: which parts a philosopher of physics would accept, which are harmless simplifications, which are overstatements or conflations of distinct claims, whether the causal and explanatory language is warranted by the mathematics, and whether the final sentence about the energy of the universe survives scrutiny? I would like your considered judgement on whether the paragraph as a whole is fair to what Noether's theorem actually shows, and I should add that I would prefer the answer in British English.
