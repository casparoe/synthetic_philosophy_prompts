I am a retired biochemist who spent forty years working on the kinetics of multi-step enzyme-catalyzed pathways, and I have only recently begun reading philosophy of science. I have been working through the account of mechanistic explanation associated with Machamer, Darden, and Craver. As I understand it, their core claim is roughly this: to explain a phenomenon is to describe the entities and activities that produce it, from start or setup conditions to termination conditions, where activities are what bring about change and entities are the things that engage in those activities; these components must be organized spatially and temporally, each stage must give rise to the next in productive continuity, and an adequate explanation bottoms out at whatever level further detail would no longer make a difference to the phenomenon being explained.

I keep catching myself translating this into the language I actually think in, namely chemical kinetics: elementary steps, reactants, products, intermediates, transition states, catalysts, rate constants, steady-state flux, and rate-limiting steps. Please restate the Machamer–Darden–Craver position as precisely as you can in that vocabulary, and then give me a residue report: which claims map cleanly onto the chemistry, which ones you had to distort or fudge, and which parts of the philosophical account have no counterpart in how chemists describe reaction mechanisms. I specifically want to know whether my inclination to read their term mechanism as the chemist already does, as a reaction mechanism, is adequate, and whether the elements that fail to carry over are signs that the target vocabulary is too coarse or that the philosophical claims were doing more work than I took them to.

As you go, tell me how confident you are in the translation at each of the main steps, and single out the point about which you are least sure. I am fully fluent in the chemistry; assume I need no explanation of rate laws or elementary steps.
