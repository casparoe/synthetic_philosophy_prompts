I have been working through judgment aggregation, and the discursive dilemma has left me with an argument I find both compelling and impossible to accept, and I want to know exactly where it breaks. The standard case runs as follows. A three-judge panel must decide whether a defendant is liable for breach of contract. Liability (R) obtains just in case two findings both hold: that a valid contract existed (P) and that the defendant breached it (Q), so R if and only if P and Q. Judge 1 accepts P and Q, and so accepts R. Judge 2 accepts P, rejects Q, and so rejects R. Judge 3 rejects P, accepts Q, and so rejects R. Each judge is individually consistent. But when we poll the panel proposition by proposition, a majority (Judges 1 and 2) accepts P, a majority (Judges 1 and 3) accepts Q, and a majority (Judges 2 and 3) rejects R. The panel therefore accepts each premise while rejecting the conclusion that those premises entail.

The case seems to force the following argument on me: (1) A collective judgment is determined wholly by the judgments of the members, with no source of information beyond theirs. (2) The only non-arbitrary way to aggregate those judgments is proposition-wise majority rule, since it treats every voter and every proposition alike. (3) The case shows that proposition-wise majority rule can yield an inconsistent collective judgment set, accepting P and Q and rejecting R. (4) An inconsistent judgment set cannot be the view of a rational agent. (5) Therefore, in this case, the panel is irrational. (6) But a panel cannot be irrational when every member is individually rational and the tension arises only from combining their consistent votes. (7) Therefore proposition-wise majority rule, despite being the only non-arbitrary rule, is not an adequate account of collective judgment. (8) And the apparent alternatives — premise-based procedures that first settle P and Q and then derive R, conclusion-based procedures that settle R directly and leave the premises wherever they fall, or any weighted or tie-breaking rule — all privilege some propositions or some voters over others, which is exactly the arbitrariness that step (2) ruled out. (9) Hence there is no adequate account of collective judgment at all. But courts, boards, cabinets, and electorates produce collective judgments all the time, so (9) makes ordinary multi-member decision-making either impossible or systematically irrational. I cannot accept that, so something in the argument has to give.

Where exactly does it go wrong? If forced to choose, I would most like to keep (2), because abandoning equal treatment of propositions feels like gerrymandering the agenda toward one's preferred result; but I want to know what keeping it costs. I am not looking for a bare verdict about which premise to reject. Give me the best case for the strongest candidate diagnoses, name the step or equivocation doing the real work, and explain what a workable account of collective judgment must give up in order to avoid the dilemma.
