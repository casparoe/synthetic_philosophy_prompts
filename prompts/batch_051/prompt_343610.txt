I'm a philosophy professor, mostly ethics, and I've been trying to work out what I actually think about simplicity as a virtue in ethical theory. My verdicts on cases don't hang together, and I want a principle that captures the pattern — or an honest list of which verdicts have to go. I'm not after the literature, just the principle itself.

If two theories fit my considered intuitions equally well and one has two principles while the other has eleven, I prefer the two-principle theory, and I don't think that's just aesthetic laziness. But I've basically never found an equal-fit pair in real life, and I don't think an elegant theory that gets lots of cases wrong beats a messy theory that fits them. So simplicity looks like a tiebreaker, but ties never happen — yet it doesn't feel like it's doing nothing. Ross's short list of prima facie duties feels principled to me, not gerrymandered, but a commonsense morality with a separate principle for every familiar kind of situation feels like my intuitions being read back to me; I can't say why seven-ish is fine and twelve-plus isn't. I count utilitarianism's single principle as a real point in its favor, but when I see how much downstream machinery it needs to actually deliver decent verdicts about promising or punishment, I stop trusting the elegance: the complexity hasn't disappeared, it's just been moved. I also distrust a theory more when someone adds a new clause after I give a counterexample than when the clause was there from the start, even though rational reconstruction shouldn't care about the order in which the theorist thoguht of it. And a single principle with lots of adjustable weights — "balance liberty, equality, and welfare as the situation demands" — strikes me as flexible but not simple at all.

Is there a coherent account of when parsimony counts, and how much it is worth against fit with intuition, that gets most of these? If not, which verdicts should I overturn and why? State the principle precisely if you can. And do some work I haven't: give me the resulting account's verdicts on a one-principle theory with a baroque theory of value versus a five-principle theory with a spare theory of value.
