Hi, I'm a grad student in medieval philosophy and I've been volunteered to run a dead philosophers Q&A session where we pick a historical figure, state their view, and then press them on it with follow-ups. I got Maimonides, and I want to interrogate him about his account of animal sacrifice. The position I'd be attacking, which I'll state for the audience first, is roughly this: God never wanted the sacrificial cult for its own sake; it was a concession to the fact that the ancient Israelites were steeped in sacrificial worship and wouldnt have been able to make the jump to imageless devotion to God all at once. So the sacrificial laws are a kind of weaning strategy, and the real goal is the intellectual love and knowledge of God. Can you write me a sequence of, say, seven questions that makes this view progressively harder to hold onto? I'd like it to exploit the obvious soft spots: the biblical text itself presents sacrifices as something God commands and finds pleasing, not as a second-best stopgap; the view turns a big chunk of an eternal Torah into a time-bound instrument; and its awfully convenient that the real meaning of the law ends up being exactly your own intellectualist ideal. For each question, build in the follow-up that anticipates his likely deflection—for instance, that the Torah speaks in human idiom, or that the plain sense is for the masses—and don't let him get away with it. Make the last question the harshest: if the Temple were rebuilt in a messianic future, would he actually reinstate all of it, and if not, what do we do with the plain sense of the text?
