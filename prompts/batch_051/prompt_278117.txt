One of my close friends, Maya, has spent two years on our parish council's safety committee, and she's ended up with a weirdly consistent record on exactly one question: when you're allowed to put other people at risk without asking them. I've been keeping notes, partly because me and my mate are about to go in front of the committee ourselves and I want to predict how she'll vote.

Her calls, as I remember them:

- Beehives: a bloke on the estate wanted to keep two hives at the bottom of his garden, directly backing onto a family with a kid who's anaphylactic to bee stings. He offered to screen them off. Maya voted no.
- 5G mast: the phone company wanted to put a small mast on the corner of the green. Maya voted yes, said the measured exposure is tiny and the whole village benefits from the signal.
- Delivery drones: last year a delivery company wanted to run drones along a corridor over our back gardens, with insurance and height limits. Maya voted no, said we never agreed to be overflown and a parcel could go through someone's conservatory roof.
- Speeding: she knows one of the farmers drives a old diesel Land Rover past the primary school at a few mph over the limit, and she's never minded. She says the added risk to any particular kid is negligible.
- Balcony smoking: when the flats on the edge of the green proposed banning smoking on balconies, Maya was strongly in favour, because smoke drifts into open windows and people can't practically keep their windows shut all summer.
- Fireworks: the council's annual firework display on the green always gets her vote, even though she admits there's a tiny risk of a rocket landing on a roof, because it's once a year and it's for everyone.

Now our thing: me and my mate have a decent drone and we want permission to fly it over the village green on weekend afternoons to sell photos to families, with insurance and a 30-metre no-fly zone over the playground. The risks are vaguely similar to the delivery drones (some chance of it coming down) but the people below are anonymous park users rather than specific householders, and the benefit comes to us rather than a big company. I can't tell if that makes it better or worse in Maya's book.

So: how do you think Maya votes on our application? More importantly, what principle is she actually running? I can see at least two or three rules that fit all six of her old calls — something about identifiable victims versus statistical ones, something about who benefits, maybe something about consent in your own home. Which rule fits best, what does each candidate rule say about our drone over the green, and what single future case would cleanly split the top two?

And answer in British English please.
