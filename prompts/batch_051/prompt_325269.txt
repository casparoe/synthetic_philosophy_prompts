I recently argued at length with a friend about whether the standard explanations of the paradox of tragedy can survive the way people now consume true crime, disaster documentaries, and grief memoirs. The classical solutions were designed for an audience that knowingly watches fictional suffering: catharsis, the conversion of painful feeling by aesthetic form, the safety of distance, and the view that our sadness in fiction is only a quasi-emotion because we know the events did not happen. But much of the painful material people now seek out and say they enjoy depicts suffering that is real and known to be real. Does the classic explanation still go through in that setting, or does it quietly depend on a premise the real-victim case undercuts? If a repaired version can cover both fictional and nonfictional tragedy, say what it keeps and what it abandons; if the two cases call for different explanations, say where the line falls. I am interested in the structure of the explanation, not in whether such enjoyment is morally defensible.
