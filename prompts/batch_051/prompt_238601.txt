I am a doctoral student in philosophy of biology working on the fitness concept, and I have been reconstructing the propensity interpretation's answer to the tautology objection. In reconstructing it I produced an argument that now seems to prove too much: it appears to force a dilemma according to which the propensity interpretation either collapses back into the tautology it was introduced to escape or renders fitness claims empirically undetectable. I cannot accept that conclusion, in part because the same reasoning would show that a die's bias, a drug's efficacy, and a material's propensity to fail under stress are all empirically empty whenever those properties are understood probabilistically. But I cannot find the mistake, or even establish that there is one. The argument, as cleanly as I can put it:

(1) The tautology objection is best met by defining fitness not as actual reproductive success but as a propensity, that is, the probabilistic disposition of an organism or trait type to leave a certain number of offspring in a specified range of environments.
(2) A propensity is not identical with its manifestation, so on this view a less fit type can, in any finite population over any finite number of generations, out-reproduce a fitter type purely by chance.
(3) For any finite sequence of reproductive outcomes and any proposed fitness ranking of two types, there exists an assignment of propensity values that makes that sequence likely while ranking the types in the opposite order; so no finite sequence of outcomes is logically inconsistent with any given fitness ranking.
(4) A hypothesis has empirical content only if some possible observation could count against it, so if no possible finite observation could count against an assignment of fitness differences, then the claim that one type is fitter than another is empirically empty.
(5) It follows from (2) and (3) that no possible finite observation counts against any assignment of fitness differences.
(6) A claim with no empirical content is just the tautology objection returning at the level of evidence, which means the propensity interpretation has not solved the problem it was meant to solve.
(7) The only way to restore empirical content is to let actual reproductive success determine fitness after all, but then the principle that fitter types reproduce more is true by definition, and the tautology returns directly.

Conclusion: either the propensity interpretation is empirically empty, or it collapses into the definitional view it replaced; either way, the tautology objection cannot be answered by going dispositional.

I would most like to keep (1) and (2), since abandoning them means abandoning the propensity interpretation outright, but I have no settled view about where else I would be willing to pay. Where does the argument first go wrong, and what does rejecting that step cost?
