I have been reconstructing a proof that I initially took to be a trick, but each step still looks compelling, and the conclusion seems impossible to live with. Let Kp abbreviate "it is known, by some thinker at some time, that p." Assume two principles about knowledge that seem beyond reproach:

(1) Factivity: if Kp, then p.
(2) Distribution: if K(p and q), then Kp and Kq.

Now add the characteristically anti-realist claim:

(3) Every truth is knowable: for any proposition q, if q is true, then it is possible that Kq.

Take any true proposition p, and consider the proposition "p and not-Kp" — the proposition that p is true but never known. If this proposition were true, then by (3) it would be possible that K(p and not-Kp). But K(p and not-Kp) cannot obtain. For by (2), it would imply Kp and K(not-Kp); by (1) applied to the second conjunct, not-Kp; hence Kp and not-Kp together, which is a contradiction. So it is not possible that K(p and not-Kp). By the contrapositive of the relevant instance of (3), the proposition "p and not-Kp" is false. Since p is true, it follows classically that Kp. Therefore every truth is known.

This seems to show that the modest-sounding principle that every truth is knowable collapses into the claim that there are no unknown truths. I would like to keep classical logic, (1), and (2), and I suspect the unrestricted formulation of (3) is where the damage happens — but I do not see how to restrict it without baldly excluding the problematic instances, and I worry that any such restriction gives up what made the principle interesting. Where, if anything, does the argument go wrong? If it is valid, which premise should anti-realists reject, and what is the least ad hoc way for them to state their position afterward?
