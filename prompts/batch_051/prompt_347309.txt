I direct a small research programme in the epistemology of mathematics — three people, three years of funding — studying what changes when proofs are too large or too computational for a single mathematician to survey. The concrete cases on the table are the Appel–Haken computer-aided proof of the four-colour theorem (1976), the Robertson–Sanders–Seymour–Thomas reduction of its case analysis to 633 configurations (1997), Gonthier's verification of the theorem in the Coq proof assistant (2005), Hales's 1998 proof of the Kepler conjecture, and the completion of the Flyspeck formal proof of that conjecture in 2014 using Isabelle and HOL Light.

My question: which open problem or line of work in this area is most worth settling first, and why — in the sense that the most other questions depend on it, or that progress on it would most change how mathematicians should treat machine-checked or unsurveyable proofs?

Among the candidate questions I already see — and you should add others as you see fit:
(1) Is there an epistemic difference in kind, or only in degree, between a traditional surveyable proof and a proof no single mathematician can survey? What does surveyability actually contribute to mathematical warrant, and does its loss make the warrant partly empirical?
(2) In a computer-assisted proof, exactly where in the reliability chain — the mathematical specification, the algorithm, the implementation, the compiler, the hardware, the proof assistant's kernel, the formalized library — does empirical content enter? Can formal verification remove that empirical content, or does it merely compress it into fewer, smaller trusted components?
(3) Did the formal artifacts — Gonthier's Coq proof of the four-colour theorem and the Flyspeck proof of the Kepler conjecture — solve the original worry, or relocate it? What epistemic difference does a machine-checkable proof make, given that both the checking and the checked object are computational artifacts?
(4) Can an unsurveyable proof produce mathematical understanding, or only establish that a theorem is true? Should understanding be a norm for acceptable proof at all, or merely a desideratum?
(5) Under what conditions is the mathematical community rationally warranted in accepting a result whose proof no single member can survey? Is acceptance of the four-colour theorem a rational response to evidence, or a pragmatic convention about trust?
(6) Does shrinking the computational part of a proof, as the 1997 re-proof of the four-colour theorem did, change its epistemology, or is that merely presentational? Should mathematicians invest in "hybrid" proofs that isolate the unsurveyable part?
