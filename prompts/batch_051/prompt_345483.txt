You are a decision theorist and a professional philosopher who works on collective action, institutional design, and risk. You have designed decision procedures for grant-making bodies and boards. Think through the problem carefully before writing anything: trace how the failure arises in my situation, consider the obvious fixes, and say why each one is insufficient or how it can be made sufficient.

I am a senior program officer at a medium-sized philanthropic foundation. Our six program officers each have the authority to approve, on their own signature, a grant of up to $500,000 for speculative, hard-to-reverse projects: a field trial of a novel climate intervention, a high-visibility policy report with named partners, or an early-stage dual-use technology. Decisions like this come up several times a month, and once made they are difficult to unwind—money moves, partners hire staff, and public commitments are made. We cannot bring each one to the full board, and the officers work under serious time pressure.

We have settled into a pattern I now believe is an instance of the unilateralist's curse: because any one of us can act alone, the person who acts tends to be the person who is most optimistic about the project. The median officer, if honestly polled, would often have said no, but nobody was asked. We keep approving a stream of grants whose expected value is marginal or negative by the team's own lights.

Write a reusable decision procedure our program officers can follow before making a unilateral call. The procedure must be executable by a busy non-philosopher in under an hour, with the steps in order, a short checklist, the hard cases the procedure has to handle, and explicit markers for the points where judgment cannot be eliminated. Ground it in what the unilateralist's curse actually implies, not in generic risk-management advice. Address these specific questions:

- When should an officer be required to elicit the private estimates of the other five before acting? In what sequence, and should the officer reveal their own estimate first or last?
- How should the collected estimates be aggregated—majority, median, supermajority, unanimity, or something else—and why does that choice matter given the curse?
- What should change when the action is reversible rather than irreversible, and when the group's estimates are plausibly correlated rather than independent?
- Where should the procedure deliberately slow an officer down, and where should it leave room for genuine emergencies?

Apply the procedure to three worked cases: (a) a $450,000 pilot for a climate field trial that two officers privately think is reckless and three are on the fence; (b) a low-cost, fully reversible but reputationally visible public statement that one officer strongly favors and the others mildly oppose; and (c) a time-sensitive co-funding opportunity for a promising vaccine platform where waiting two weeks to poll the team could lose the deal. Make at least one worked case come out in a way I will not like, and say plainly that this is that case.

After the prose, provide a JSON object with this exact schema:

- "procedure_name": string
- "steps": ordered array of objects with fields "step_number" (integer), "instruction" (string), and "type" (one of "check", "estimate_elicitation", "aggregation", "timing", "override", "record")
- "checklist": array of strings, each a yes/no item
- "hard_cases": array of objects with fields "case" (string) and "handling" (string)
- "judgment_points": array of strings, each naming a place where a human must decide and why the procedure cannot decide for them
- "worked_examples": array of objects with fields "case" (string), "procedure_trace" (array of strings applying the steps in order), and "outcome" (string)
- "limitations": array of strings, each stating something the procedure cannot fix

Be specific about the institutional mechanics: who must sign off, what gets logged, what a "second look" consists of, and what happens when the procedure is skipped. Say where the conceptual content of the unilateralist's curse survives the simplification and where it is deliberately dropped.
