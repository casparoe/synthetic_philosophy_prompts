In patent law, if you independently reinvent a machine that someone else has already patented, your ignorance is normally no defense: you can be sued for infringement and made to pay. In copyright law, by contrast, independent creation is a complete defense: if you write a song that coincides with an existing song but you have never heard it, you have copied nothing and have not infringed. And in trade secret law, independently deriving the same formula, or reverse engineering a product you lawfully bought, is generally permitted; what is forbidden is misappropriation, such as theft or breach of confidence. So the law says that arriving independently at the same poem or chemical process creates no obligation, but arriving independently at the same patented machine does.

There is a further oddity. The patent holder gets this stronger protection only by disclosing the invention to the public, while the trade secret holder discloses nothing and yet gets only the weaker protection against misappropriation. What coherent view of owning an idea could yield all three results? Is there one theory that explains why patents, copyright, and trade secrets differ in exactly these ways, or is the pattern a historical accident? Give me the strongest justification you can construct for the status quo, test it against the independent inventor and the reverse engineer, and then say honestly whether the law should keep this asymmetry, or whether one of the three regimes should move toward the others.
