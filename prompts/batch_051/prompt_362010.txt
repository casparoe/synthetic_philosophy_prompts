I'm a software engineer on an ML reliability team, with no formal philosophy background, and I'd like an analysis of a proposed anomaly-triage mechanism. The rule: whenever our model monitor flags an output as anomalous, an automated attribution pass extracts the causal subgraph of the model that produced the output, and we keep a library of subgraphs that human reviewers have already cleared as benign. A new anomaly is auto-closed as "known benign mechanism" if its extracted subgraph is within graph-edit distance δ of any cleared subgraph; otherwise it escalates to a human. Crucially, δ is not fixed once and for all: every month it is recalibrated so that exactly N anomalies per week reach the human queue. Walk through how this would actually behave over a year, in concrete terms. What does it incentivize the engineers maintaining the attribution tooling to do, how does the cleared-subgraph library change character, how does "same mechanism" drift as a notion under distance thresholding plus constant-queue recalibration, where do genuinely novel failures get absorbed or missed first, and which of those failure modes can be fixed without abandoning the scheme?
