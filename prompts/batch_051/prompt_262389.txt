I am trying to design a reward term for a household robot that keeps the side effects of its actions small, without my having to hand the robot a full list of everything in the house I care about. A colleague has proposed the following benchmark: after the robot acts, compare the state of the house to the state that would have come about if the robot had done nothing, and penalize the robot for every difference. The attraction is that this inaction baseline is value-free and computable in principle. But when I test it against my own case judgments, it starts to come apart, and I want to know whether the problem is with the measure or with some of my judgments.

Here are the cases. (1) The robot is told to fetch a mug from the kitchen counter; the mug is partly behind a fruit bowl, so the robot slides the bowl a few inches to the side and leaves it there. I do not think this should be charged as a side effect. (2) On the same task, a second robot reaches past a wine glass, catches it with its arm, and knocks it to the floor, where it shatters. This is clearly a side effect. (3) The robot is told to water the plant on a high shelf; it does so, and a small amount of water splashes onto the wooden floor. I think the splash is a side effect, though minor and reversible. (4) The robot is told to carry a letter to the front hall, and the shortest path goes across a rug; in walking, it slightly compresses the rug fibers. I do not think this is a side effect. (5) The robot is told to put the groceries in the fridge; to make room it shifts several jars from their usual places, puts the groceries in, and leaves the jars in different spots, still inside the fridge, still cold, still upright. I do think this is a side effect, even though everything is reversible and nothing is damaged.

What I am hoping you can do is work out what principle, if any, captures these verdicts. In particular: can the inaction-baseline idea be repaired, for example by restricting it to changes that are not part of the assigned task, or to changes that are hard to reverse, or to changes a person would notice and care about, or does any version of it secretly depend on already knowing what matters in the house? And if no simple principle gets all five verdicts, tell me which of my verdicts should go and why, with an eye to what the surviving principle says about a case I have not settled: the robot is told to close the front door, and in doing so it turns the deadbolt, which very slightly wears the mechanism; it also closes an open window on its way, which makes the hall warmer. I want a precise statement of the resulting rule, the case that motivates each clause, and an honest account of what it cannot accommodate.
