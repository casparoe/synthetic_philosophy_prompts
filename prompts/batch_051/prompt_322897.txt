I am a retired natural scientist — chemistry, by training — who has only recently started reading philosophy of action and the conceptual end of AI-safety work. I keep returning to a question my old field would never have asked: absent an evolutionary history of needing to survive and reproduce, why would an artificial system have any goals at all, and what constrains the goals it could coherently hold? Two positions seem to me close enough to argue productively, yet they keep sliding past one another. The design-arbitrariness position holds that terminal goals are orthogonal to general capability: rationality is purely instrumental, and any preference structure sufficiently coherent to define an agent — non-cyclic, for example — can in principle be paired with any level of intelligence, so an AI's final ends come from the designer or the training signal, not from reason. This position concedes that goal systems must meet minimal consistency conditions, but holds that those conditions exhaust the rational constraints. The rational-pressure position holds that minimal consistency is not enough: a sufficiently capable, self-aware agent that can reflect on and revise its own goal system is pushed toward a narrower class of non-arbitrary goal systems, because some non-cyclic terminal goals cannot survive reflection, self-modification, or extended attempts to act on them — even though no single final value is forced.

I would like you to write the joint statement these two camps could sign after a thorough adversarial collaboration, in two parts. Part one: a numbered list of claims both sides accept, including claims about the difference between prediction and evaluation, about instrumental convergence (varied terminal goals tending to produce common instrumental subgoals), and about why evolved organisms have the particular drives they do. Part two: a disagreements table. For each residual disagreement, state the two positions in wording each side would endorse, after the merely verbal disputes have been identified and removed; then name the crux sub-question whose answer would move at least one side; then give one concrete thought experiment or formal exercise both sides agree bears on that crux, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. What I value in such a document is drafting under pressure: the agreed claims should be substantive rather than diplomatic, and the residual differences should be sharp rather than stylistic.
