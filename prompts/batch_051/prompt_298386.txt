A regional endowment makes grants for documenting endangered languages. Its charter defines eligibility in these words: "For the purposes of this endowment, a language is a system of conventionalized arbitrary signs, governed by a grammar and a shared lexicon, used by a human community as its primary medium of communication, and passed naturally from one generation to the next through the vocal-auditory channel." A small fishing community with an unusually high incidence of hereditary deafness has, over about sixty years, developed a village sign language. It has a rich lexicon and a systematic grammar of handshape, movement, and the use of signing space; it is learned as a first language, without formal instruction, by both Deaf and hearing children in the community, on the same maturational timetable as spoken languages elsewhere; and it functions as the community's everyday medium for negotiation, storytelling, and childrearing. It has no widely used written form and is produced and perceived entirely in the manual-visual modality, so it plainly fails the final clause of the definition. A researcher applies for a grant to document the language before younger community members shift to the dominant spoken language. The trustees split. Please argue the case through four lenses: (1) the plain text of the charter; (2) the evident purpose of the endowment; (3) what the drafters of the definition would most plausibly have said if asked whether a signed language qualifies; and (4) how a reasonable reader of the charter would understand its scope. Then say which of these approaches should control in this case, and what the disagreement shows about the sound-based definition of language itself.
