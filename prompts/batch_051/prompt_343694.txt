Question (10 points): A fair coin is tossed until it comes up heads. If the first heads occurs on the nth toss, the player receives 2^n dollars. Explain what the St. Petersburg paradox is. In your answer: (a) show that the game's expected monetary value is infinite; (b) explain what maximizing expected monetary value would imply about paying a fee to play; and (c) discuss whether replacing monetary value with expected utility, along the lines proposed by Bernoulli, resolves the puzzle.

Student's answer: Let n be the toss on which heads first appears. The probability of reaching toss n is (1/2)^n and the payoff is 2^n dollars, so the contribution to expected value is 1 dollar for every n. Summing over all n gives 1 + 1 + 1 + ..., so the expected monetary value is infinite. A decision maker who maximizes expected monetary value should therefore prefer playing the game for any finite fee to not playing at all. This is paradoxical because actual people typically would not pay more than a few dollars. The standard resolution is due to Daniel Bernoulli: people maximize expected utility, not expected monetary value, and money has diminishing marginal utility. Bernoulli proposed that utility is the natural logarithm of wealth, so the expected utility of the game is the sum over n of (1/2)^n ln(2^n), which equals 2 ln 2, a small finite number. Since any concave utility function has diminishing marginal utility, any risk-averse expected-utility maximizer will assign the game a finite value, and the paradox disappears. We can therefore say that the rational fair price for playing the game is 2 ln 2 dollars, or about $1.39.
