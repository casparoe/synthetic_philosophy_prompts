I'm not in law school, just someone who fell down a wiki hole about laws that stay on the books but are never enforced, and I hit a blog post defending something called desuetude — roughly, the idea that a law can die through long non-enforcement. The author's summary of the position goes like this:

"If a statute hasn't been enforced in decades and gets suddenly revived to nail one unlucky person, that person had no fair notice, so desuetude is just the rule of law applied honestly. The real law is what officials actually do, not what's sitting in the statute book; a law the state itself has ignored for a generation isn't law in any meaningful sense, and a judge confronted with it should strike it down, or at least quash the prosecution. The only people who resist this are formalists who care more about the words on the page than about people being singled out arbitrarily."

Something about this felt right, but also suspiciously smooth — I can't tell if its a bait-and-switch between "people rely on non-enforcement" and "the statute isn't law." I'd like a careful audit. Which parts of this would a serious defender of desuetude actually sign on to, and which are conflations or overstatements? In particular, does "not law in any meaningful sense" do the work of "a judge should refuse to enforce," and does the rule of law cut only one way here? If the blogger's version is unfair, could you also reconstruct the strongest form of the point he's fumbling toward? And when you reply, could you say how confident you are in the audit and flag the single point you're least sure about?
