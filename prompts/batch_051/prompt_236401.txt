I’m a PhD student working on decision theory, and in our reading group we’ve been arguing about what it takes for the expected value of a monetary gamble to be genuinely well defined. A visitor claimed that the St. Petersburg game is a special pathology of infinite expectation, and that once expected value is finite, the standard theory faces no further problem. I want to press this with a case I’ve been sketching, and I’d appreciate an audit of the criteria I’ve been using.

Here are the criteria I was taught for a gamble to have a well-defined fair price: (1) the possible outcomes form a countable partition of the state space; (2) each outcome has a determinate probability and a determinate numerical payoff, measured in a common utility or currency scale; (3) the expected value exists as an absolutely convergent sum of probability-weighted payoffs; and (4) the value is invariant under reasonable re-indexing or regrouping of the possible outcomes, so it does not depend on the order in which we happen to list the events. Now consider this game. A fair coin is tossed until it lands heads. If the first heads occurs on the nth toss, the player receives (−1)^(n−1) · 2^n / n dollars: on odd-numbered tosses this is a gain, and on even-numbered tosses it is a loss of that amount. The probability-weighted payoff is (−1)^(n−1) / n, so the natural sum over n is the alternating harmonic series, which converges to ln 2. But the convergence is only conditional, not absolute, so by reordering the same possible outcomes the sum can be made to approach any real number or diverge to either infinity.

Go through the criteria one by one and say whether this Pasadena game qualifies as having a well-defined expected value. Is the natural-order value ln 2 a legitimate fair price, or is it an artifact of the way I wrote the game’s description? Is criterion (3) too strong, or does the case show that a finite but conditionally convergent sum should not ground a fair price? Where the answer is no, say whether that is because of the criteria or because of the game, and whether the criteria themselves need changing. Please separate what is mathematically settled from what is genuinely contested among decision theorists, and avoid presenting a contested view as the consensus.
