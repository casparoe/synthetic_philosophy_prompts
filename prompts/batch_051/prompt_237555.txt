I have been returning to the Dennett–Caruso debate over just deserts, and I want to take one of its ideas down to the level of institutional design rather than settle the free-will question first. Suppose a mid-sized jurisdiction amends its constitution to provide that the criminal law shall remain entirely in force—same code, same sentencing ranges, same police powers, same prisons—but that no state actor may justify any coercive measure by appeal to desert, retribution, or moral blame. Every judgment of conviction and every custodial decision must henceforth be supported by written findings that cite only future dangerousness, general deterrence, or incapacitation. Courts and correctional authorities may not characterize offenders as blameworthy or as deserving to suffer; they may describe offenders only as sources of risk that the state is managing. Victim compensation is removed from the criminal process and assigned to a no-fault social insurance scheme, and victims are expressly told that the criminal courts are not where anyone's moral blameworthiness is assessed. The actual severity of sentences is unchanged. The amendment's drafters, who regard themselves as importing the public-health-quarantine insight associated with Caruso without adopting every detail of his program, expect the change in official justification to drain the system of retributive emotion and gradually humanize its practices. Dennett's institutional worry, as I understand it, runs the other way: a state that refuses to blame may either stop being able to call citizens to account in the way that sustains self-control, or it will keep doing retributive work under another name while deceiving itself about it.

I would like this treated as a mechanism rather than as a moral position. Walk through what the amendment incentivizes for judges, prosecutors, defense lawyers, correctional staff, legislators, victims, and the press. Identify the first places it is gamed—in the writing of risk findings, in the construction of dangerousness, in plea bargaining, in release decisions, or elsewhere. Ask whether the official prohibition on blame changes informal courtroom and prison norms or merely relocates them, and what happens to the public's distinction between being dangerous and being culpable once the state is barred from drawing it. Then say which of Dennett's specifically institutional predictions, as opposed to his compatibilist metaphysics, your analysis supports or undercuts, and whether the failure modes you find are fixable by better rules or only by restoring some public language of blame.
