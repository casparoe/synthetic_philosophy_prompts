I've been working through the standing-to-blame literature and seem to have built an argument whose conclusion I can't accept. Let me set it out.

(1) A person has standing to blame another only if her own conduct shows that she treats the norm she invokes as binding on herself, not merely on the person she blames.

(2) What shows whether someone treats a norm as binding on herself is what she does when the norm is costly for her — not what she says, what she believes, or how she describes her own principles.

(3) For nearly every adult blamer, and for nearly every occasion of blame, there is some norm in the same family as the one she invokes — honesty, consideration, fairness, promise-keeping, or something analogous — that she violates when it suits her, or would violate if the cost were high enough.

(4) By (2), then, nearly every blamer marks out a private exemption for herself somewhere in the very family of norms from which she blames.

(5) If a blamer marks out such an exemption within the relevant family, she blames from a position she has not earned — she demands of others a standard she does not impose on herself.

(6) Therefore, on (1), nearly every blamer lacks standing for nearly every instance of blame.

(7) But that result cannot be right. The standing objection was introduced to distinguish objectionably hypocritical blame from ordinary blame; if almost no one ever has standing, the charge no longer picks out any distinctive failing and the distinction collapses. So the standing objection proves too much and should be abandoned.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I'd most like to keep (1) and (2), because I think standing really is about what conduct shows rather than what people profess. But I don't see which later step I can reject without either making the standing objection toothless or smuggling back the exact distinction it was supposed to explain. Tell me which step gives way, why it looked sound, and what accepting your diagnosis costs.
