I'm in a philosophy of psychiatry course and we've been on Hacking's looping effects — the idea that classifying people doesn't just describe them, it changes them: people learn the label, respond to what the label leads others to expect, reshape their behavior around it, and the category then gets revised to fit the people it has partly produced, and around it goes. I want to stress-test a policy that's supposed to slow that loop down, and I'd rather you actually dissect how it would behave than tell me whether it's good or bad.

Here's the policy. A regional psychiatric system decides to delay the label. After the intake interview, clinicians must still enter a coded diagnosis for insurance and prescriptions, but for the first three months the patient recieves only a symptom-level description — "you're reporting persistent low mood, poor sleep, and loss of interest in things you used to enjoy" instead of "depression." During those three months the patient can't be told the code unless they file a written request, in which case a clinician must disclose it and read a short script warning that the label itself can shape how they see themselves and how others see them. After the three months everyone is offered the label with the same script. Online access to the chart is delayed for the same period, so no one can just check the portal. The stated goal is to give people time to form a sense of their difficulties before the classification starts looping.

Walk me through how this actually plays out. Who does it change most: first-timers, people with long histories, clinicians, families, support groups, researchers, the billing-audit side, online communities? Where does it get gamed first? Some worries I already have: patients can google their symptoms and self-diagnose in an afternoon, or badger the front desk for "my code"; people with a cluster of symptoms may just adopt whatever label is currently trendy online; and for some people a delay might be protective while for others not being told becomes its own stigma ("they won't even tell me what's wrong with me"). Also say whether the effect differs between a label the patient already arrived carrying and one they'd never heard of — the policy does nearly nothing in the first case and almost everything in the second. And tell me whether the failure modes can be fixed without gutting the policy. After your prose analysis, give me a table with these columns: Stakeholder or process; First-order behavior change; Gaming or failure mode; Net effect on looping (strengthens / weakens / displaces / neutral); Fixable, and if yes a one-line fix.
