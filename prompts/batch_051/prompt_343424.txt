I'm a PhD student in environmental philosophy, and I've been sat in a reading group that has been going in circles for six months about whether nature and wilderness have intrinsic value or relational value. I can't work out whether it's a substantive disagreement or just people refusing to use the word value in the same way.

It's not the older instrumental versus intrinsic fight. Everyone in the group agrees that pricing ecosystem services is not enough, and everyone agrees that oak woodlands, peat bogs, mountain ranges and so on matter in ways that are not just about what they do for us. The intrinsic value camp hold that nature has to be valuable for its own sake, independently of any valuer and any relationship, otherwise every claim about why a species or a landscape ought to be protected eventually bottoms out in human attitudes or preferences, which they see as instrumental value wearing a disguise. The relational value camp hold that value is not the sort of thing an object carries around on its own; they say it only arises within relationships and practices between people and particular places, and they insist this is not instrumental because the relationship itself is part of what is valued, not merely a means to some seperate benefit. Each side has a stock diagnosis of the other: the intrinsics say relational value is just anthropocentrism with extra steps, and the relationalists say intrinsic value is a spooky property nobody can observe and nobody actually uses when they decide what to protect. The peculiar thing is the two sides have nearly identical practical commitments in the cases we discuss. Yet nobody has moved.

My working theory is that the impasse is not about nature at all, but about what kind of thing value is in the first place: whether it is a property of things or a status that depends on relations. If that is right, the argument is a metaphysical dispute wearing an environmental ethics costume, and no amount of new cases about the last copse in a lifeless universe will settle it. But I could also see it as a difference in bedrock intuitions about value, or a hidden empirical claim about what actually motivates conservation, or just a verbal dispute about how to use value. I'm after a diagnosis rather than a verdict: what kind of disagreement is this really, using what each side actually says to make the case, and what would count as progress given that diagnosis? If the honest answer is that no progress should be expected and the question was badly posed, say so.
