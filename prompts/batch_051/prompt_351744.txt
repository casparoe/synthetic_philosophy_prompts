I am an undergraduate in a philosophy of mind course, and this week I have been asked to help mark a short-answer question on Gilbert Ryle. The question on the tutorial sheet reads: Explain Ryle's notion of a category mistake, reconstruct how he applies it to Cartesian dualism, and then evaluate whether his critique would also undermine the more modest idea that each person has introspective access to some of their own mental states. One student submitted the following answer:

Ryle's category mistake is the error of treating something as if it belonged to one logical type or category when it actually belongs to another. His illustration is of a visitor who has been shown the lecture halls, the library, and the administrative offices and then asks to be shown the university as well, as though the university were another building on the same list. Ryle applies the same diagnosis to Descartes: once Descartes has enumerated the body and its physical workings, he posits an additional non-physical substance, the mind, whose hidden inner acts are supposed to explain intelligent behavior. Ryle presents this as the picture of a spectral occupant housed inside a bodily machine. On Ryle's view, mental terms typically refer not to hidden episodes in a private theatre but to publicly observable patterns of action, ability, and inclination. To say that a person believes it will rain, for example, is not to report a private state behind her umbrella-carrying; it is to summarize a complex disposition to act and respond in certain ways. Ryle's argument therefore demonstrates that Cartesian dualism is false. It also shows that our ordinary talk of private conscious experience is misleading, because the introspectable states such talk appears to name are not real things.
