I am a PhD student in moral psychology, and I have been developing a distinction I have not seen drawn in quite this way. I call it the distinction between insulatory virtue and orchestrative virtue. Insulatory virtue is the capacity to act well when a situation is already pulling against that action: honesty under pressure to fudge a result, compassion under time scarcity, courage under direct threat. Orchestrative virtue is the capacity to select, shape, and exit situations so that such pressures are rare or weak in the first place: choosing transparent institutional roles, restructuring one's environment to prompt the relevant behavior, leaving settings that reliably corrupt.

My guiding thought is this. The classic situationist experiments—Darley and Batson's Good Samaritan study, Milgram's obedience studies, and the like—almost always hold the environment fixed and test whether a person resists a pressure introduced by an experimenter. They therefore test insulatory virtue. But the ordinary concept of a trait such as honesty does not require a person to remain honest in any arbitrarily hostile situation; it requires someone to act honestly over time and, plausibly, to be disposed to avoid or reshape situations that make honesty costly or psychologically unnatural. A scrupulously honest person who refuses a career in an industry that rewards systematic misrepresentation is displaying honesty, not cowardice, even if she would have failed the corresponding insulatory test. If that is right, the situationist findings, while important, underdetermine global skepticism about character because they leave the orchestrative component untested.

I worry, though, that my distinction either makes virtue unfalsifiable—since any observed failure can be redescribed as a failure of orchestration rather than a failure of virtue—or collapses into the familiar claim that virtue depends on external scaffolding and luck, given that the freedom to choose one's situations is not equally distributed. So I want an honest assessment.
