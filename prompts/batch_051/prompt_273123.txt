My officemate made an argument I can't shake, and it seems to prove that either events with probability zero happen all the time or the idea of flipping a fair coin forever is somehow incoherent. Here's how I reconstruct it. (1) A fair coin flipped forever will land on some specific infinite sequence of heads and tails. (2) Each particular infinite sequence has probability zero: with a fair coin, no single infinite sequence gets positive probability. (3) If an event has probability zero, it can't happen; if you said it might happen anyway, probability zero would be useless as a guide to what will occur. (4) So for each particular sequence you could name, that sequence can't happen. (5) But if no particular sequence can happen, then no sequence at all can happen, which flatly contradicts (1). (6) So one of (1)-(3) has to go. My officemate wants to reject (1) and says an actually infinite sequence of flips is borrowed mathematics that doesn't describe anything real. I don't want to go there, but rejecting (3) looks like it commits me to saying zero-probability events can happen, which sounds like probability giving up on tracking possibility altogether. Where does this argument go wrong, if it does, and what do I buy if I fix it at the step you pick?
