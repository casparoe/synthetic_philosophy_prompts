A colleague of mine works on the ethics of what we owe one another at the level of attitude rather than action, and in particular on the difference between recognition respect (regarding another person as a being with moral standing whose claims we must take seriously) and appraisal respect (esteem, admiration, or positive credit directed at someone for her specific excellences). She has a consistent record of informal verdicts on cases, but she has never stated her underlying principle explicitly. I want you to predict her verdict on a new case and make the reasoning behind the prediction explicit.

Here is her record.

1. A checkout clerk says and does everything the script of civility requires toward a customer while privately holding the customer in contempt. She said the clerk still withholds something that the customer is owed: minimally correct behavior is not a substitute for an attitude of recognition.

2. A surgeon is technically excellent and never mistreats patients in any outward way, but privately regards them as collections of failing organs rather than as persons. She said the surgeon fails to give patients something they are owed, even though no discrete action is wrong.

3. A man released from prison long ago now lives quietly in the neighborhood. His neighbors never harass him, but they treat him with cold suspicion and avoid ordinary pleasantries. She said the neighbors owe him ordinary civil recognition, but they do not owe him warmth, friendship, or trust, and cannot be blamed for not feeling those further attitudes toward him.

4. A deeply unremarkable accountant has done nothing wrong but has also done nothing distinguished. She said that nobody owes him admiration or professional esteem, and that manufacturing admiration for him would not be a moral improvement. He is owed the baseline of recognition owed to everyone, and nothing further.

5. A book critic privately detests a novelist and is assigned to review a novel that is, by the critic's own lights, a major achievement. She said the critic owes it to the novelist to say plainly in the review that the book succeeds on its merits; a review that accurately itemizes the book's virtues while never crediting the achievement would be defective, not merely as criticism but as something owed to the novelist.

The new case. A junior scientist privately despises her senior rival, whose latest result is, by the junior scientist's own lights, a genuine and important contribution. The junior scientist has no professional role that requires her to evaluate this rival: she is not a referee, editor, or assigned discussant. In her notes and in conversations with friends, she refuses to grant that the result is important, while accurately insisting that she has never said anything false about it and has never treated the rival unfairly in any professional decision. In formal settings she cites the result when necessary but conspicuously avoids passing on its importance.

Please answer the following. Would the colleague say that the junior scientist owes the senior rival the acknowledgment that the result is important? Identify the principle or principles the colleague is implicitly applying, identify the closest precedent among the five recorded cases, and give your best prediction with reasons for preferring it over the alternatives. Then construct one further case that would be especially informative if we could obtain the colleague's verdict on it, and state what each candidate principle would say about that further case and what her verdict there would show.

Throughout, separate what is settled in this debate from what is genuinely contested among people who have thought carefully about it, and do not present a contested reading of the recognition/appraisal distinction as the consensus.
