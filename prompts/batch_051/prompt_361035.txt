Since retiring from physics I have begun reading metaphysics, and a structural resemblance keeps troubling me. In the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics, as I understand it, the wavefunction never collapses: every outcome of a measurement is realised in some branch, all branches are equally real, and “this is my branch” expresses something merely perspectival. In Lewisian modal realism, the same pattern appears at the level of possibility: possible worlds are equally real and concrete, actuality is an indexical matter in roughly the way “here” is, and no single world is metaphysically privileged. Both views dissolve one privileged actual world into an enormous plurality and then reinterpret our sense of privilege as a fact about perspective rather than a fact about the world.

I know enough physics to suspect the resemblance may be shallow. Everett branches share a common history and emerge by decoherence within a single quantum state, whereas Lewisian worlds are supposed to be causally and spatiotemporally isolated and to cover logical possibility, not merely quantum-mechanical possibility. Their motivations also look different: modal realism is driven largely by the semantics of modal discourse, while many-worlds is presented as forced by the formalism of quantum theory.

Please treat this as a serious question in the philosophy of physics and modal metaphysics. Begin by setting out the two positions in your own words, however provisional, before comparing them. Then state the mapping precisely: which feature of each side corresponds to which feature of the other, where the mapping holds, and where it breaks. Tell me what would have to be true of both sides for a result from one debate to transfer to the other, and identify at least one thing the analogy seems to predict that is false on one side. In short: is the parallel deep, superficial, or somewhere in between?
