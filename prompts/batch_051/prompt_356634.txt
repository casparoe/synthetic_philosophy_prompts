I am a novelist assembling a collection of short stories about people who cannot stop what they have started, and I want you to draft one of the stories.

The story should be built on the formal skeleton of the dollar auction, though it should never name the term and should not be set in an auction. The structure I have in mind is this: two parties compete for a prize of fixed and modest worth; the rules require both parties to pay their bids whether or not they win; at each turn, each side treats the previous outlay as a reason to bid again rather than a reason to stop; and the process can carry both parties past the point where their combined payments exceed the value of the thing they are fighting over. The payments need not be money. They can be time, pride, goodwill, forbearance, or any other resource the characters value and cannot get back.

Set the story in an ordinary, close-range situation: a dissolving marriage, a failing family business, a quarrel between neighbors, a friendship coming apart over a shared project. Both characters should begin with something genuinely worth wanting and then slowly hollow it out through the attempt to secure it. Write in alternating close third person. Neither character should be a fool or a villain; each should be intelligent enough to see, at least once, that walking away now would cost less than continuing, and that recognition should be dramatized in a scene rather than summarized. In that moment, the motive for staying should be respectable and local: refusing to let the other person's unfairness stand, the conviction that the sacrifice already made must be made to mean something, the fear of appearing weak, or the belief that one more increment will finally settle things.

Let one character win. Make the victory arrive as an anticlimax, with the prize diminished by the campaign to hold it, and let the closing image carry the symmetry of the trap rather than a lesson about who was right. Avoid genre or speculative elements, avoid a twist based on hidden information, and avoid authorial commentary on the characters' folly. The structure should be legible to a reader who knows the underlying game, but the story should never announce it. Aim for roughly 2,000 words.
