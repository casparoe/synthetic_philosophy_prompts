I'm in a course on the uses of history in foreign-policy argument and one thing keeps bugging me. Every time we discuss a current conflict, someone reaches for Munich 1938, someone else says no, it's really Vietnam, and then a third person waves in the Thucydides trap, the power-transition account. The weird part is that the same people, with the same facts in front of them, never seem to move, and the debate just resets next week. My working theory is that the disagreement isn't actually about the historical cases or the current facts — it's about which features get to count as the 'core' of the analogy, and that choice is doing hidden argumentative work. I want a diagnosis rather than a verdict: is this a factual disagreement in disguise, a disagreement about what makes two situations relevently similar, or a values/policy dispute wearing historical costume? And if you had to say what would actually count as progress — an explicit method for weighing similarities, a taxonomy of analogical claims, or just admitting no resolution is possible — which is it, and why?
