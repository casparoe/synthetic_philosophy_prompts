I cover science and ideas, and I have been struck by an asymmetry in how we describe structurally similar forms of competition. When firms move production to jurisdictions with weaker labor or environmental rules because their rivals are doing the same, we call it a race to the bottom: each firm is responding rationally to the others, the resulting equilibrium is one none of them would choose if they could coordinate, and no firm can afford to be the first to stop. When firms cut prices or improve quality because their rivals are doing the same, we call it competition and treat it as the engine of consumer welfare, even though here too the individual firm would often prefer not to cut prices and cannot unilaterally opt out. Both cases fit the same sparse model of a multipolar trap: each actor's best response to the others' moves produces an equilibrium no actor would choose if they could coordinate, and exit is individually costly. Is there a principled difference between the two cases that justifies the asymmetry in our judgments, or are we mostly calling an equilibrium a trap when we dislike its outcome and competition when we like it? The strongest answer will test candidate justifications rather than merely list them: differences in payoff structure, in the distribution of gains, in externalities, in whether the process is adversarial or mutually beneficial, in whether the harms fall on third parties or on the competitors themselves. Test each candidate against nearby cases, such as a price war that bankrupts firms and strands workers, a patent race that burns resources without yielding a better product, an engagement race among platforms that degrades users' attention, and a standards war that leaves consumers better off. Then say which of the asymmetries in our ordinary judgments you would keep, which you would reverse, and which you would give up.
