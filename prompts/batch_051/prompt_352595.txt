I’m a PhD student in criminal law, and I’ve been trying to pin down what the void-for-vagueness doctrine actually forbids. In Johnson v. United States, 576 U.S. 591 (2015), the Court struck down the Armed Career Criminal Act’s residual clause, and the passage I keep returning to is:

> Deciding whether the residual clause covers a crime thus requires a court to picture the kind of conduct that the crime involves in “the ordinary case,” and to judge whether that abstraction presents a serious potential risk of physical injury.

The Court treats that pictured-abstraction inquiry as one of the defects. The background test has two broad prongs: a criminal law has to give ordinary people fair notice of the conduct it punishes, and it has to be structured enough that it doesn’t invite arbitrary or discriminatory enforcement.

Here’s the borderline case I want to think through. Suppose a state enacts a statute making it a misdemeanor for a company to “deploy an automated content-ranking system that, in light of reasonably foreseeable use by adolescent users, creates a substantial risk that a reasonable adolescent user will be induced to engage in physical self-harm.” The statute applies only when the company knows it has not implemented “reasonable prediction and mitigation measures.” On one hand, this is full of fuzzy terms: “substantial risk,” “reasonable adolescent user,” “reasonable measures.” On the other hand, it has a real mens rea element and it targets a defined class of actors, not everyone. Is this statute void for vagueness under the Johnson framework? Go through the fair-notice prong and the arbitrary-enforcement prong separately, and check whether Johnson’s worry about judicially imagined “ordinary cases” applies here or whether that worry is specific to statutes that classify prior convictions. Say explicitly where each prong runs out and whether the mens rea or reasonableness language does any real work.
