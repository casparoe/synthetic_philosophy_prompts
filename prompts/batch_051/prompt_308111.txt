I serve on the secretariat of a standing scientific advisory committee whose rules of procedure I inherited from a predecessor I never met. I have been asked to draft a short advisory note for the chair about a case that has arisen, and I find the question harder than expected. Clause 7.2 of those rules reads: "Where two or more equally competent and informed members of the Committee, considering the same evidence, reach conflicting conclusions on a question within the Committee's remit, the Committee shall not endorse any one of those conclusions as a settled ground for action; it shall instead record the disagreement, set out the grounds on which each conclusion can reasonably be maintained, and withhold any recommendation that presupposes the truth of one of them."

A case has now arisen that the drafters plainly did not have in mind. Two senior members with genuinely equivalent credentials examine the same body of toxicological data and disagree about whether the evidence warrants lowering a recommended exposure limit. Judged purely on the first-order data, the reading offered by one of them is by a moderate margin the better fit. But a third member then presents a pre-registered calibration study, drawn from a large archive of the committee's past forecasts, showing that the better-fitting member has a robust tendency to overestimate risk on exactly this class of question while the other is well calibrated. The challenged member does not dispute the record itself but argues that the study's scoring rule is the wrong measure of reliability. The committee therefore faces higher-order evidence about which of the two disputants is reliable, and it cannot tell whether the clause's precondition—"equally competent and informed"—is even satisfied without first taking a stand on evidence that is itself contested.

I want to know what the clause actually requires in this case. What does it require on a plain reading of its words, and what in light of its evident purpose? What would its drafters most plausibly have said if asked, and how would a reasonable reader of the code as a whole take the clause? Where those approaches diverge, which should govern, and what does the divergence reveal about the epistemic norm the clause was written to protect? In the course of the answer, mark the exact point at which a conciliationist about peer disagreement and a steadfastness theorist would read the clause differently, and say whether discounting the challenged member would violate the rule or merely apply its antecedent honestly. Finally, is there a stable rewrite of the clause that handles this case—one that neither licenses the committee to dismiss a member on contested grounds nor collapses into a mechanical peer-counting rule—and if no such rewrite exists, what does that tell us about the ideal of "reasonable disagreement" the clause presupposes?
