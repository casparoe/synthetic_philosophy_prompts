A theorist argues that a certain policy race — jurisdictions that drive down effective tax rates to keep capital, or credit-rating agencies that lower their standards to keep issuers — is a multipolar trap of the kind sometimes called Moloch, and not ordinary competition. By a multipolar trap she means three things at once: each participant is choosing the individually best available move given the moves of all the others; the resulting equilibrium leaves every participant worse off than some feasible alternative; and no participant can reach that alternative alone, because the first mover would be punished by the others' unchanged strategies. Design the strongest empirical or experimental test you can of whether a real case is genuinely of this kind, as opposed to being (a) a straightforwardly zero-sum rivalry in which one group's gains are another group's losses, (b) a coordination problem whose better equilibrium is real but simply lacks a salient landing point, or (c) a prudent adaptation to deeper constraints that looks wasteful only from the outside. Specify the intervention, comparison, or measurement you would use; state which results would count for classifying the case as a trap and which would count against that classification; and then say what a positive result would still leave unsettled — for instance, whether the word "trap" is doing causal explanatory work or merely redescribing a bad Nash equilibrium, and whether the result shows anything about what a single agent could have done as opposed to what the agents could have done only jointly.
