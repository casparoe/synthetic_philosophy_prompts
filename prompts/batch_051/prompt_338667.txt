Design and describe a runnable classroom activity for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of law (fourteen students, ninety minutes) that makes participants feel the force of self-reference in rule systems rather than hear about it secondhand. The activity should center on a stripped-down, playable version of a Nomic-style game: a game in which changing the rules is itself a move governed by the rules. The aim is for students to run into, through their own choices, the moment at which a proposed rule change applies to the rule that governs rule changes — for example, a proposal to amend the amendment rule itself, to entrench a rule against repeal, or to repeal the entrenchment rule — and to discover that their own procedures do not settle what counts as a valid move in that case. By the end they should have a working feel for three distinctions: first-order rules versus rules about rules; self-amendment versus ordinary amendment; and a rule's content versus its status within the game (in force, entrenched, suspended, repealed). The activity should give participants real choices with small point stakes so that the paradox is something they do, not something they are told.

Include in your design: the complete initial ruleset and payoff structure; a timeline with distinct phases; facilitator moves intended to bring the crisis about without naming it in advance; and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the three distinctions above and onto the broader idea that a rule system can apply its own validity criteria to itself. Also give an honest note on what the classroom game cannot reproduce — for example, that an actual legal system ultimately rests on official practice rather than on any enacted rule, or that in Nomic the self-amendment paradoxes are a feature of the game rather than a defect to be eliminated. Finally, say what predictably goes wrong when the activity is run (players treating the facilitator as an external referee, disputes over vote counts, attempts to exploit point rules without engaging the conceptual issue) and how to handle each failure without defusing the problem.
