In debates about open borders, I keep meeting the analogy that a welfare state is social insurance among citizens: workers pay premiums through taxes, and members may later draw from the shared pool when unemployed, ill, or retired. The claim drawn from it is that admitting many immigrants—especially those whose wages are low or uncertain—is like allowing people to take out an insurance policy after an accident has already happened. Because open borders would select for people who expect to draw out more than they put in, the pool would lose both its solvency and its character as mutual aid among contributors, so open borders and a functioning welfare state cannot be combined. Audit this analogy. Provide the explicit correspondence table (premiums, pool, insurer, policyholder, accident, pre-existing condition); identify where the mapping breaks; separate the breakdowns that actually matter for the anti-open-borders conclusion from the ones that are side-issues; and then commit to a verdict on whether that conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If some other analogy would do the work better, say what it is.
