i host a small philosophy podcast, and next week im talking to John Rawls about the part of A Theory of Justice nobody asks him about: envy and resentment. i've read the relevant stretch enough to know the view, and i want eight to ten questions that go past having him restate it. His line is roughly: resentment is a moral sentiment—it presupposes that somebody's been treated unjustly—while envy isn't a moral feeling at all, just hostility at someone else's having more even when their having more doesn't make you worse off. But then he says envy becomes excusable when the inequalities are so large they wound the self-respect of the least advantaged and it'd be unreasonable to expect the envious person to just get over it, and in a well-ordered society the conditions for that sort of envy are less likely to arise. To me that's a real tension: if the setup is producing excusable envy, why isn't the envy itself a signal that something unjust is going on, even if the envious person never forms a claim of right? I want questions that push exactly there, and that test whether the clean split between moral resentment and non-moral envy survives his own admissions.

Dont ask him to recapitulate the theory. I specifically want at least one question on each of these: (1) whether excusable envy blurs the line between resentment and envy; (2) what "excusable" actually buys—is the envious hostility justified, or merely blameless but still a vice, and what turns on the difference; (3) whether envy can act as an early-warning signal of injustice even when the person feeling it makes no moral claim; and (4) whether a society that kept self-respect high under massive inequalities (say through a meritocratic ideology the poor have internalized) would still be objectionable, or whether the absence of envy would be the thing that's wrong. For each question, write the follow-up you'd use after the answer you're likely to get from him—the follow-up should corner him, not just say "could you elaborate." Make the sequence build, so that by the last couple he can't keep falling back on envy being just a vice that justice doesn't answer to. Eight to ten questions total, answerable from the view as he states it, no page numbers or citations.
