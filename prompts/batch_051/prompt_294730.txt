I'm a law student and I keep going in circles on a rule-of-law puzzle, and I'd rather have someone work through the readings than just say "black box bad." Fictional country for the setup, but go with it.

The constitution has a clause, call it Article 7(2): "No person shall be deprived of liberty or property, or be made subject to any penalty, except in accordance with a rule publicly promulgated in advance and capable of being understood by an ordinary person."

The legislature created a Risk Classification Board with power to keep prisoners past their earliest release date if the Board certifies them a high risk of serious reoffending. The statute says the Board must base that decision on "a validated risk assessment instrument approved by the Minister." The Minister approved a proprietary machine-learning model. Nobody outside the vendor and a few officials knows the inputs, the weighting, or the training data; the training data have since been deleted; the vendor can push updates to the model without notice and the agency wont say what changed. A prisoner is told only: "your score is 7.8, the threshold is 6.0, so you will be detained another two years." When he challenges this, the government answers that Article 7(2) is only about rules, and no rule was applied to him — the model is a technincal instrument, more like a breathalyzer than a regulation, and trade secrets mean it can't be "promulgated" the way a statute is.

So: does Article 7(2) reach him? Walk me through the readings. The plain words — is an opaque score a "rule" in the sense the clause means? The purpose — what exactly is the clause protecting against, and is that the thing happening here? What would the drafters have said if you had described this system to them, and what would a reasonable reader think the clause promises? Then the theory angle: would someone in Fuller's line say this isn't merely a bad rule but not law at all in the relevant sense, because it's not public, not stable, not understandable, and the announced standard (the statute) doesn't match what's actually applied (the model)? Would a Razian object mainly that an unknowable, shifting standard can't guide conduct — or is that weak because the statute at least warns you that a score will be used? Does Waldron add a different complaint, that being governed this way denies the prisoner any chance to answer back to the norm used against him? And if those three strands pull apart, which one should control the case, and what's the actual remedy — disclosure of the model, prospective public validation, striking the scheme down, or something else?
