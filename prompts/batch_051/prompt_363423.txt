I have spent a long time convinced by the parity-based version of the extended mind argument due to Clark and Chalmers. As I understand it, the argument runs roughly as follows: the boundary of the mind should not be drawn at skull and skin; what matters is whether an external resource occupies the same functional role in a cognitive process as an internal one would. If it does, and if it is reliably available and treated as trustworthy in the way biological memory is, then it can count as part of the cognitive process rather than a mere tool. The canonical illustration is Otto, whose notebook does for his dispositional beliefs what Inga's biological memory does for hers.

But the original examples involve dumb, passive stores, and the technological situation has changed in a way that seems to me to cut against the argument rather than merely extend it. My notes and contacts are no longer just a notebook; they are mediated by language-model assistants that summarize, paraphrase, compress, and sometimes confabulate. When I ask such a system what I believe or what I decided, I am not retrieving a sentence I stored; I am reading a plausible reconstruction generated by a system I cannot introspect, whose training data I cannot survey, and whose errors are fluent rather than merely absent. I want to know whether the parity-based argument survives this development, or whether it only worked for passive stores.

Please do the careful version of this. Identify exactly which premise or premises of the parity-based argument the generative, opaque, reconstructive character of these systems puts pressure on. Then say whether the argument as originally stated still licenses counting such a system as part of my memory or cognitive process. If it does not, give the strongest repaired version that includes the case while excluding what everyone would want to exclude: a public library, a search engine I query rarely, another person I ask. If you think the original argument does survive, show precisely why the differences between Otto's notebook and a generative assistant are not differences that matter under the argument's own criteria. I am less interested in a verdict than in the structure of the argument: which premise is load-bearing, and what it still gets us if it is weakened.
