Background: the old nudging debate is whether default-driven choice architecture is harmless helpfulness or sneaky paternalism. This proposal tries to split the difference by letting companies use defaults, but taxing them in proportion to how much those defaults actually move behavior.

The mechanism: any consumer-facing company that rolls out a new default or meaningfully changes an existing one—auto-enrollment, auto-renewing subscriptions, preset tip amounts, menu ordering, privacy settings—must run a three-month randomized trial as it does so. A control group sees the neutral setup with no default (or the old default), and a treatment group sees the new default. The company measures the "nudge gap": the share of the treatment group that ends up in the defaulted option minus the share of the control group that actively picks that same option. The company reports that number itself, then pays a monthly tax equal to the gap times the number of affected consumers times a fixed rate. All the money goes into a national pot that is paid out once a year as a flat rebate to every consumer in the country, whether they were nudged or not. Companies can pick whatever default they want; a bigger measured effect just costs more.

I want you to treat this as a pure mechanism and figure out what it actually does, not whether you like the philosophy underneath. Start with a messy brainstorming pass, then get organized.
