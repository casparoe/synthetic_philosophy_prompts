So I'm teaching a first-year epistemology discussion section next semester — about 16 students, 75 minutes — and I've been told to do the Gettier problem. I dont want to do the usual thing where I define justified true belief, run through the fake-barn and stopped-clock cases, and then watch half the room nod along because they've seen a video essay on it already. I'd rather run the whole session as a structured activity where students have to commit to knowledge attributions before the word Gettier ever comes up, and only later notice that the JTB recipe left something out.

Here's the seed I've been chewing on. Split the class into four panels of four, each acting like an incident review board. Every panel gets the same bundle: an accident report, some witness statements, a maintenance log, and a sealed 'true story of what happened' card held by a facilitator. Two panels get a bundle where the evidence genuinely points at what happened; the other two get a bundle where the panel's best-supported conclusion happens to be true, but for a reason the evidence completely misdescribes — for instance, the maintenance log has a typo that makes a brake line look fine when it was actually fixed the night before by someone who never logged the repair. Each panel has to post a public verdict on whether their investigator 'knew' the cause, and then the room compares cases. I want rules tight enough to run in 75 minutes, a points scheme that doesn't need real money, a debrief that maps what the panels felt back onto why justified true belief isn't sufficient, and an honest note about which feature of real Gettier cases the exercise can't reproduce. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and whether I should withhold the label 'Gettier problem' until the last five minutes.
