I keep getting inconsistent results when I try to apply the standard "sunk costs don't count" line, and I want someone to extract the principle I'm actually operating on, or tell me which of my verdicts to drop.

Verdicts:

- I pay $20 for a movie, 15 minutes in it's clearly terrible. I leave. Making myself finish to get my money's worth feels like a mistake; the $20 is gone.
- I buy a year-long gym membership, discover two months in that I much prefer trail running and dread the gym. I stop going. Obviously right.
- I drive three hours to a trailhead, arrive, and the weather is now dangerous. I turn around. Annoying but obvious; the three hours are gone.

- A friend is in year 5 of a PhD, ABD, hates it, wants to quit for industry. I say he should finish if he can do the dissertation in 8 months without burning out. But my stated reasons are all forward-looking — credential, signal, self-image. The past years themselves I want to say count for nothing.
- That same friend asks about a paper he has 200 hours in that still isnt coming together and whose core argument he now thinks is wrong. I say walk away. But I can't cleanly distinguish this from the PhD case except by telling a story about the size of the future payoff, and that story wasn't about sunk cost at all.
- The one that bugs me most: an investor says a startup can't shut down because they've burned $2M. I want to say that's the fallacy; decide on the forward case. But shutting down now does have real future costs — the founders' fundraising ability, morale, the firm's reputation — and the investor might be confusedly tracking those. Same with "withdrawing would mean the soldiers died for nothing": as stated it's a fallacy, but a country that withdraws after losses has a credibility problem next time.

My half-baked principle: sunk costs matter only through their future effects (reputation, habit, information, self-model), never directly. But that lets me cook up a future-effects story for any staying I already want to do. Can you turn this into a precise principle that fits my verdicts, or say which verdicts it can't fit and must go? And what does the principle say about a case I haven't pre-reasoned my way through, like keeping an expensive but useless gadget around "to get use out of it"?
