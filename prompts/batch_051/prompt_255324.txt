My colleague Varga, a philosopher of language, has made a series of rulings about what a speaker's word refers to. Her track record so far:

1. Oscar on Earth and Twin Oscar on Twin Earth are internally exactly alike, and both use the word "water" for the clear, drinkable liquid in their surroundings. Oscar's liquid is H2O; Twin Oscar's is a liquid indistinguishable from water in ordinary use but chemically different. Varga says Oscar's "water" refers to H2O and Twin Oscar's "water" refers to the chemically different liquid.

2. A man cannot tell elms from beeches, although he has spent his life around both kinds of tree and has always heard each kind called by its correct name. Varga says his word "elm" refers to elms and his word "beech" refers to beeches, not to some undifferentiated tree-kind or to his own indistinct stereotype.

3. A patient with arthritis in his wrists has the false belief that arthritis can occur in the thigh; he learned the word from his doctor while being treated for that disease. When he says "the arthritis has spread to my thigh," Varga says he is talking about arthritis, the joint disease, and that his statement is false rather than true of some private condition.

4. A mineral term was introduced generations ago in a dubbing ceremony for mineral M, but an error in an influential reference manual led the whole community, experts included, to use the term for mineral N; it has been used for N ever since. Varga says a present-day speaker who uses the term refers to N, not M.

Now consider a new case. Lena grew up in an isolated village that is by ordinary standards part of the same linguistic community as the surrounding country: same language, same institutions, mutual intelligibility. In the village, the only clear, drinkable liquid—and the only thing anyone there calls "water"—is a liquid that is indistinguishable from ordinary water in appearance and use but is not H2O. The wider community's chemists use "water" for H2O and would say that the village liquid is not water. Lena has never left the village and is otherwise an ordinary speaker. When Lena says "water is boiling," does she refer to H2O or to the local liquid?

I am less interested in the bare answer than in the reconstruction. What rules about reference fit Varga's four earlier verdicts, and what rules do those verdicts already rule out? What does each surviving rule predict about Lena? What is your best prediction of Varga's ruling, and why? Finally, describe a further case—different from Lena's—that would cleanly settle whichever ambiguity remains.
