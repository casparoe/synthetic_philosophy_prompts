I've always claimed the whole deserving/undeserving poor distinction is a category mistake: benefits should track need and incapacity to work, not moral character or whether someone has contributed. But my gut reactions to concrete cases are making a mess of that priniciple, and I want you to help me find the real principle underneath them, if there is one.

Here are my verdicts. (1) A woman on Universal Credit doesn't declare a few evening bar shifts because declaring them would trigger a two-month sanction and she'd fall into rent arrears. I think that's fine, even though it's fraud. (2) A neighbour claims the higher rate of PIP (the disability benefit) for a bad back and then spends every weekend doing the brickwork on his own extension. I think that's not fine, and I feel something like contempt, not just 'the rules are wrong'. (3) A 19-year-old who has never worked turns down a zero-hours warehouse job the jobcentre found him, so his UC is stopped. I'm not outraged; that seems roughly defensible. (4) A 58-year-old former machinist, made redundant and left with a chronic shoulder injury, is made to attend fortnightly work-search interviews and a confidence-building course. No sanction, but I find it degrading and wrong. (5) A family with two children can't get support for a third because of the two-child limit. I think the limit is wrong: the third child didn't choose anything.

So the same system prompts opposite reactions from me depending on history and attitude, and (1) and (2) are both just lying to the DWP for money, yet they feel completely different. What principle, if any, captures all this? I'd rather have a simpler principle that overturns one or two of my verdicts than a gerrymandered one that saves them all. Tell me which verdicts have to go, and what the repaired principle says about the case I keep going back and forth on: a man who gambled away his redundancy money over a year and now claims Universal Credit. Part of me says his need now is what matters; another part says he should have to work his own way back.

Lead with the best candidate principle in a sentence or two, then run it through the cases. Max 400 words.
