You are an expert in Bayesian decision theory and the philosophy of rational choice. I am a journalist writing a feature on the intellectual roots of the conviction, common in the data economy, that gathering more information can never hurt. A data-science executive offered me the argument below, and I find that if its premises are sound they prove far more than he is willing to accept. Please think carefully. Begin by restating the argument in your own words and listing the different things terms such as "free" and "should" could mean here. Then give me a precise diagnosis: which numbered step has to be rejected or reinterpreted, why that step initially looks so natural, and what a person who rejects it must then say about the slogan that information can never hurt.

The argument:

(1) Good's theorem in Bayesian decision theory says that for an ideally rational agent who updates by conditioning and then maximizes expected utility, observing cost-free information before choosing can never lower expected utility relative to choosing without it.

(2) So cost-free information can never make a decision worse in expectation, and it can sometimes make a decision better.

(3) What holds for one cost-free piece of information holds for many: each additional cost-free signal weakly improves the choice, so a rational agent should always prefer a richer body of free information to a sparser one.

(4) Therefore a rational agent should take in every free signal available before making any decision whose stakes are at all serious.

(5) It follows that a rational company should analyze every kind of data it lawfully holds before acting, and that a rational person should treat deliberately turning away from free information as irrational.

(6) But that cannot be right, because it proves too much. Carried far enough, it seems to require deferring every decision indefinitely while waiting for one more free signal, and it condemns as irrational the apparently sensible practice of sometimes avoiding information on purpose.

I would like to keep (1) and (2) if possible, and my suspicion is that the damage happens somewhere in the move from (2) to (3) or from (3) to (4). If the real damage is instead to an assumption hidden inside (1), say so. Tell me which step to give up, what that step implicitly assumed, and what the corrected argument would look like.
