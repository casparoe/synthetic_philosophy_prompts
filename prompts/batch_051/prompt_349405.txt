You are a moral philosopher with expertise in philosophy of emotion, moral psychology, and distributive justice, and you have been asked to facilitate a formal adversarial collaboration between two camps concerning envy and resentment. The two camps are as follows.

Camp A, the vice-first camp: Envy and resentment are primarily defects of character. They involve false or disproportionate perceptions of others' desert and one's own deprivation. Even when they point at real harms, they tend to distort judgment, poison relationships, and motivate destructive or spiteful action. The moral task is to regulate, reorient, or extirpate these affects, not to treat them as authoritative guides.

Camp B, the signal-first camp: Envy and resentment are often apt responses to real inequalities, status injuries, or moral wrongs. Their intensity and prevalence can reveal the shape of injustice that explicit judgment misses or rationalizes away. The moral task is to interpret and respond to their object, not primarily to suppress the affect.

The collaboration should not try to determine which camp is right as a whole. It should determine what they can jointly assert and where exactly they still diverge.

Begin by brainstorming the strongest versions of each camp's commitments, their likely common ground, and the candidate cruxes. Think carefully about which disagreements are substantive and which are merely terminological or a matter of emphasis. Then produce the final document.

The document must include the following sections.

1. A numbered agreed-claims section. These should be non-vacuous and substantive: claims both camps can sign without hedging. They should cover the phenomenology of envy and resentment, their typical cognitive content, their social distribution, their relation to perceived injustice, and the limits of both blanket commendation and blanket condemnation. Avoid claims so weak that they merely paper over disagreement.

2. A disagreements table. Each row should state one precise disagreement. The statement of each side's view must be phrased so that a careful proponent of that side would say, yes, that is exactly what I hold and how I would put it. Do not reduce either position to an easily defeated caricature. Separate disagreements about the nature and ethics of these emotions from disagreements about downstream practical and political responses.

3. A cruxes section. Identify the sub-questions whose answers would most strongly move at least one side. Each crux must be formulated as a question both camps agree is well posed and probative, not as a disguised assertion of one camp's conclusion. For at least four cruxes, provide a concrete case, scenario, or piece of evidence both sides agree would bear on it, and record in advance what each side predicts such evidence would show. At least one crux should engage with the idea associated with Rawls that envy may be excusable under certain conditions of inequality. At least one should concern resentment rather than envy. At least one should target what would constitute appropriate management of these emotions in a person who accepts the evidence.

4. A short classification section. State which parts of the dispute, if any, you judge to be partly or wholly verbal after the exercise. If none, say why not. If some, identify exactly which expressions or distinctions each side is using differently and how the dispute could be restated to remove the verbal component.

Use neutral, precise language throughout. The document should read as if both camps could sign the agreed claims and each could sign the formulation of its own position. Do not announce which view you personally find more plausible.
