I'm putting together a list of the actual objections to the exit-from-the-state idea — private cities, charter cities, seasteading, the whole competitive-governance family — because I want to defend the view that we should take the thing seriously, and I don't want to discover in the Q&A that I've missed an obvious knock-down point. Here's what I already have, so please don't give me these back: (1) the exit-versus-voice problem: exit only really works for people with money and mobility, and leaning on it quietly weakens voice for everyone left behind in the old jurisdiction; (2) spillovers: pandemics, carbon, nuclear deterrence, and monetary stability don't respect a private city's borders, so exit can't internalize the big stuff and you still need old-fashioned territorial states; (3) the commitment problem: a charter city is only as good as the promise of some host state, so the whole model rests on the very thing its trying to escape; (4) the enclave/neocolonial objection and the whole Honduras-style accountability mess; (5) the mini-sovereign objection: a private operator with a local monopoly is just a smaller state, and resident-customers can end up with thinner rights than citizens, since the charter is ultimately a marketing document; (6) adjudication without teeth: private arbitration handles contract disputes fine but falls apart for violent crime and for enforcing judgments against another jurisdiction; (7) the race to the bottom: rival jurisdictions undercut taxes and regulation, leaving tax havens and pollution havens rather than good governance.

I want one more objection that is not on that list and not a restatement or special case of any of them — not another way of saying exit is costly and not another who-guards-the-guardians point. Ideally something structural about the model itself, not just an implementation hiccup. And tell me honestly whether it's actually any good or just sounds novel.
