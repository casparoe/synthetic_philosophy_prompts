As a journalist who covers AI and the philosophy of mind, I keep returning to a claim that seems to get louder every time a translation system humiliates itself in public: that machine translation, from the old phrase-based systems to today's neural models, does not understand anything in any interesting sense. On this view — I associate it with Hofstadter and others — a translation engine is high-dimensional string correlation wearing a mask of fluency; its gibberish is not a bug but a confession, and its successes are products of pattern completion, not comprehension. I'd like to treat that as a falsifiable thesis about what the system is actually doing, not a slogan. So: what would it take to break the thesis? Which observations about a system's outputs, error patterns, behavior on rare or adversarial constructions, cross-lingual transfer, retraining curves, or probed representations would count as decisive counterevidence; which would be merely awkward; and which are noise a defender could reasonably wave off? I especially want to know what a serious defender of the no-understanding thesis would accept as a genuine concession, as opposed to what its critics claim. Then tell me how exposed the thesis really is — whether it has been padded into unfalsifiability, and whether that padding is a flaw in the claim or a flaw in the word understanding.

Structure your answer as:

- The thesis operationalized (one paragraph): what the no-understanding view predicts a system can and cannot do.
- Decisive counterevidence (one to two paragraphs): results that would force a defender to abandon the thesis.
- Merely awkward evidence (one paragraph): findings that bruise the view without breaking it.
- Concessions a defender must make (one paragraph): what the view itself has to grant.
- How exposed the thesis is (one paragraph): your verdict on whether it risks anything, and whether its insulation is a bug or a feature.
