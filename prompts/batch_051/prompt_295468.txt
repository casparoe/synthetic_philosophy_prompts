I spent the last several months helping care for my father after a stroke, and I have been struck by how much of the moral vocabulary in that world is organized around the word "pity," and by how the same pity can feel both indispensable and corrosive — in me as much as in the people around him. Recently I reread Steinbeck's Of Mice and Men and could not stop reading it as an extended case study of precisely this ambivalence. I want a rigorous philosophical reading of the novella through the debate between Schopenhauer and Nietzsche on pity, from someone who is professionally at home in both nineteenth-century German moral philosophy and literary criticism.

Begin by laying out the rivalry as concretely as you can. For Schopenhauer, pity (Mitleid) is the one genuinely moral motive: to feel another's suffering as one's own, immediately and without ulterior calculation, and the root of justice and selfless love. For Nietzsche, pity is a pathological impulse: it adds the pitier's suffering to the world rather than subtracting the other's, it preserves what ought to decline, it flatters the pitier with a feeling of superiority, and it has a way of turning into a subtle cruelty. Then use those two positions to read the novel. I want it tracked through the details: George's guardianship of Lennie and the dream of the farm; the parallel between Lennie and Candy's dog; the decision George makes at the riverbank; and the ending. For each strand, say what each philosopher would see clearly and what he would be forced to misdescribe or ignore.

Then commit yourself. Does Of Mice and Men ultimately vindicate Schopenhauer's claim that pity is the deepest moral possibility, or Nietzsche's claim that pity is a form of violence done to both parties — or does the novel show that the Schopenhauer–Nietzsche contrast misstates what is actually at stake in George's act? Give a definite, contestable interpretation and argue for it, rather than laying out the options. Close by saying what the novel contributes to the philosophical dispute that abstract argument cannot: how the fact that the pitying has to be done by hands, in a particular place, to a particular person, alters the case.

Start by brainstorming the relevant scenes and the conceptual contrasts together, and think carefully before settling on the reading. A committed and specific interpretation is more useful to me here than an even-handed survey.
