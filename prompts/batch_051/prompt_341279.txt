The home-care cooperative where I work has a charter clause: "Care shall be delivered through a continuing personal relationship between a client and a caregiver who knows the client's history, habits, and ways of communicating; care shall not be reduced to a schedule of tasks performed on a client's body." We are piloting an arrangement in which a social robot performs lifting, bathing, and medication prompting, learns the client's patterns, and converses with them, while a human caregiver who has never met the client monitors several clients remotely and adjusts the robot's routines; clients report preferring this to hurried visits. The drafters plainly assumed that whoever knows the client would also be the one handling the client's body; here those are split, and the only continuous relationship is with a machine. Does the clause nonetheless reach this arrangement? What do its plain words, its evident purpose, what the drafters would likely have said if asked, and a reasonable client's understanding each imply? If the readings diverge, which should govern for an institution that takes the clause to express a care-ethical commitment, and what would that show about what the clause is actually protecting?
