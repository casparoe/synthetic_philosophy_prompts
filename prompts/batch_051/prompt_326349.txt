I've been stuck on Molinism lately — the view that God has "middle knowledge": before deciding which world to create, he knows what every possible free creature would freely do in every possible situation, and he uses that to actualize a world where our free choices still line up with his plan. I always thought the standard objection was that knowing my counterfactual choices in advance kind of makes them not free. But I work with trained models, and a different problem keeps bugging me. When you train an agent — an RL policy, a game NPC, whatever — its behavior in a scenario you never actually ran isn't sitting in the code waiting to be read off. The weights define a function, sure, but whether it picks A or B in a novel case can flip because of training-data order, dropout, random seed, batch composition, all sorts of stuff. In a lot of these systems there's arguably no fact of the matter about what it would choose until you actually instantiate it in that exact situation, and even then different seeds give you different answers. So here's my question. Does middle knowledge survive that? The whole mechanism seems to require a definite fact of the matter about every counterfactual of creaturely freedom. If those counterfactuals are underdetermined — not just unknown to us but genuinely not settled — then which premise in the Molinist argument actually breaks, and can it be patched? Like, is there a weaker version where God knows the full probability distribution over choices instead of the exact choice, and does that still do the job Molinists wanted? Or once you think of free creatures as more like messy trained models than look-up tables, is the whole view dead?
