In a political theory course, the professor summarized Pascal's fragments on justice and force roughly as follows. For Pascal, no merely human account of justice carries authority on its own: what is just is endlessly disputed, while force is obvious and settles the question. Since we cannot make the just prevail, we make the prevailing just, and every actual legal order rests on force before we retrospectively dress that force up as if it had been chosen for its justice. The practical conclusion is that we should obey the laws and customs of our country because they are the established laws and customs, not because we take them to be just; property and hereditary rule are, at bottom, usurpations that time and custom have laundered into legitimacy. In short, the professor said, for Pascal justice is a fiction that the strong invent to make their power look respectable.

This struck me as more cynical than what I know of Pascal's religious commitments, but the professor is the expert. Is this a fair summary? Please separate (a) what in it is accurate, (b) what is a common simplification or a conflation that Pascal's fragments keep apart, and (c) what Pascal would flatly deny. In particular, if the final claim — that justice is a fiction invented by the strong — is too strong, does the practical conclusion about obediently accepting established authority still survive the correction, or does it change character once the theological background is restored?
