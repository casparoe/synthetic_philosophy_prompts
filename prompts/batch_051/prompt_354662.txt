I've been in a small reading group on formal epistemology, and last week we were talking about Aumann-style agreement results and the common prior assumption — the idea that rational differences in belief have to trace back to differences in information, because everyone starts from the same prior probabilities. One of the regulars gave a little argument that the common prior assumption isn't just a modeling choice: he said rationality itself forces it. I'll reconstruct what he said as numbered steps, since I can't shake the feeling it's too strong, but I keep losing the thread.

(1) Suppose two rational agents, Alice and Bob, have different priors about some proposition H. (2) Then there's at least one body of evidence E such that, if both of them conditionalize on E, their posterior probabilities for H still differ. (3) And if there's even one body of evidence that would leave them disagreeing, then no amount of evidence can settle the disagreement — they're not just disagreeing now, they're doomed to disagree no matter what they learn. (4) But a disagreement that no evidence could settle isn't a rational disagreement; it's just a clash of arbitrary starting points with no fact of the matter about whose right. (5) So rational agents can't have different priors, and the common prior assumption is a requirement of rationality.

I suspect step (3) is doing something sneaky with the quantifiers, but I can't articulate it, and I'm also not sure a subjective Bayesian would accept step (4). Is the argument valid? If not, exactly which step or steps fail, and does the conclusion survive if you put the strongest reasonable reading on each premise?
