ok so i've been nursing this idea for a couple years and i want someone to kick its tires before i say it in public. i call it "load-bearing morality." the explanatory half goes like this: in a social world where people truly depend on each other — kin networks, small villages, tight religious communities — certain norms are doing actual structural work. they keep people from shirking, keep obligations enforceable, keep hierarchies stable. in those settings the norms that get moralized tend to be about loyalty, deference, hospitality, purity, honor. in WEIRD settings (western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic), where courts and contracts and police and welfare are already doing a lot of that load-bearing work, people can afford to moralize mostly individual harm and fairness and treat loyalty or purity as taste. a norm like "you don't turn away a cousin who needs a place to stay" is load-bearing in a place with no safety net, and in a city with hotels and tenant law it's just a nice quirk. so far this is descriptive. but i want the stronger claim: a moral judgment is objective only relative to a social structure. "wrong" doesn't mean "violated a universal moral fact"; it means something like "violated a norm that is load-bearing in the structure the action took place in." so a marriage practice could be wrong in one structure and fine in another, each judgment true relative to it's structure — but not free-for-all relativism, because once you fix the structure there are better and worse answers. i think this dissolves the WEIRD problem: the mistake isn't that WEIRD people have parochial norms, it's mistaking norms that are cheap for them for norms that would be cheap anywhere. my questions: is this actually a position or am i just reinventing functionalism with a new word? what would an anthropologist or moral philosopher accept, what would they wince at, and where does the theory break — what does it wrongly predict or count as moral that isn't?
