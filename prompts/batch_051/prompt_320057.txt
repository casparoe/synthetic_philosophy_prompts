I am a retired physical chemist who has only recently begun reading moral philosophy with any seriousness, and I have been drafted onto the commemorations committee of the university where I spent most of my career. The committee must decide what to do about three contested commemorations: a hall named for Christopher Columbus, a portrait of Winston Churchill hanging in the history faculty's senior common room, and a scholarship named for Thomas Jefferson that funds visiting students from the United States. In each case, the principal argument for retention is some version of what I have learned to call the 'man of his time' defence: the figure falls short of our present standards, but it is unreasonable to judge him by standards that were not the operative standards of his own milieu, or by acts that were widely accepted among his contemporaries.

I do not think the defence is empty, but I suspect it is being used as a blanket shield, and I want the committee to have something sharper to work with. Would you draft the working paper I intend to circulate before next month's meeting? It should set out a defensible framework distinguishing the conditions under which the 'man of his time' defence should (a) fully exonerate a commemoration, (b) mitigate but not settle the question, and (c) carry no weight, and it should then apply that framework to the three cases — relying only on widely known facts about each figure — to reach a concrete recommendation in each instance: retain, remove, or retain with modification such as renaming, re-siting, or adding contextual material. Please treat the strongest form of the defence honestly, including the worry that present-day standards are themselves parochial and that removal merely ratifies one generation's moral fashion, but do not let the paper collapse into relativism. It should close with a recommendation the committee could actually vote on.
