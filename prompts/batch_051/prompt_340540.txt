You're a philosophy instructor who writes "which view do you actually hold?" quizzes for undergrads, and I need one for a unit on consequentialism and the place of the virtues and duties. The unit's guiding question is whether virtues and duties are (a) derivative from the good, (b) merely instrumental rules of thumb, or (c) partly independent. I want a 10-question sorting quiz. Before writing the final thing, brainstorm a few distinctions the positions actually turn on — criterion of rightness vs decision procedure, whether virtues are good-making or just useful, that kind of thing — and build the quiz around the ones that do real sorting work. Don't put the brainstorm in the output; just think it through first.

Rules for the quiz:
- Each question is a concrete little case, ordinary life, no trolleys.
- The answer options can't use words like "consequentialism," "virtue ethics," "duty," or any position label.
- Each case should put a virtue or a duty-like commitment on one side and the appeal to overall good on the other, and the options should be about what the person should do or why.
- You must use these three cases somewhere: (1) a nurse who promised an elderly discharged patient she'd visit after her shift, and then a bad bus accident fills the clinic so she could see several badly hurt people in that same time slot; (2) a manager who can give a junior employee painfully blunt feedback, which will likely improve the employee's work and benefit the team, but will wound the employee and may make them want to quit; (3) a person deciding whether to keep a long-standing personal rule against lying when one small lie would spare a friend's feelings and have no other downstream effects.
- After the questions, give a scoring key that maps answer patterns to at least four positions: virtues and duties as mere rules of thumb; virtues and duties as derivative from the good but protected in practice because we're biased, bad at predicting, and weak-willed; virtues as themselves components of the good, not just tools; and at least some virtues or duties as independent sources of reasons. Give each position a plain-English, no-jargon name like "Toolbox consequentialist."
- After the key, do a short diagnosis of the logical space: which combinations of answers your quiz treats as the same position, which combinations look possible but end up unoccupied, and for each unoccupied combination say whether it's unattractive, incoherent, or just unexplored.
- Flag any two positions your quiz can't actually seperate, and say why — do they agree on cases but not on theory, or would you just need a different kind of case?

Make sure the whole thing reads like it's sorting people by what they'd say about cases, not by which label they like.
