I have spent most of my life in the natural sciences, where argument was officially an instrument for discovering which hypothesis could withstand attack. Since retiring I have begun to read philosophy, and I keep noticing a curious asymmetry in our judgments about competitive reasoning. A chess player who trains for years to punish an opponent's smallest lapse is admired; a debater who wins a championship by exploiting an adversary's weakness is applauded; yet a person who carries those same habits into ordinary disagreement — treating the exchange as a contest, conceding nothing, punishing every slip — is dismissed as insufferable and intellectually unserious. The same competitive virtues seem to change their value merely because the setting has become formal. Is there a principle that justifies this asymmetry, or is it an inconsistency we simply live with? If you defend it, say precisely what the formal setting supplies — consent, shared rules, a judge, spectators, the suspension of the truth-aim — and test that account against the case of two people who agree in advance, with a clock and a scoring rule, to argue a question competitively in private. If the formality is what does the work, would making argumentation a sport improve how people reason, or merely license a vice we otherwise recognize?
