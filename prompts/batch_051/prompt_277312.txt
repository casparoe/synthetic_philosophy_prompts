I have been trying to locate the principle behind an asymmetry in our judgements about elections, and I cannot find one that survives much pressure.

If a candidate hands a citizen fifty pounds in exchange for her vote, both parties are treated as corrupt, and the transaction is a criminal offence in most democracies. But if the candidate promises to route a public contract to her employer, or to cut a tax that falls specifically on her industry, we treat that as an ordinary campaign commitment, and the voter who supports him on that basis is simply pursuing her interests. Both look like an attempt to win electoral support by offering an expected benefit, yet our moral and legal reactions diverge sharply.

Is there a principle that generates both judgements, or only a psychological story about why cash feels different? Please find the strongest candidate justification you can, test it against at least one nearby case (a large campaign donation, a promise aimed at a handful of pivotal voters, or a cash payment to cover the cost of travelling to the polling station), and give an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, revised, or abandoned.

Present your answer as a numbered list of exactly five points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
