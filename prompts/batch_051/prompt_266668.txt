I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I've been going back and forth with a lawyer friend about a puzzle in the AI-regulation debate that I'd like to turn into a piece. On one side, the law has no trouble treating corporations as legal persons: they can own property, enter contracts, sue and be sued, and courts in the U.S. have extended at least some free-speech and religious-freedom protections to them, even though nobody thinks a corporation has a mind. When a corporation is prosecuted for a crime that requires a guilty mind, some U.S. courts are even willing to stitch that guilty mind together out of bits of knowledge scattered across different employees, rather than requiring any single person to have had the needed intent. On the other side, proposals to give AI systems even a limited legal personhood meet huge resistance, often on the grounds that an AI isn't the kind of thing that could be a person. A European Parliament committee floated the idea of a special electronic-person status for sophisticated autonomous robots several years ago, and it never went anywhere after the outcry; patent offices have mostly refused to list an AI as an inventor. So there's a weird inversion: the law is comfortable treating a mindless aggregate as a person, and even constructing a guilty mind for it by aggregation, but balks at doing the same for a single system that at least acts autonomously.

I want to work out the philosophy doing the silent work under this pattern before I write anything. What view of legal personhood — of what a person is and what legal personhood is for — does this set of practices actually presuppose? Do corporate personhood and the resistance to AI personhood presuppose the same underlying theory, or are they in tension? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and flagging where the practice is underdetermined so that more than one theory could license it. Then tell me: if we swapped that implicit view for a single consistent one — for instance, a purely functional view on which a person is just a bundle of legal capacities we assign wherever useful, or the opposite view on which personhood requires actual mental capacities like agency or consciousness — what would have to change in how courts treat corporations or how legislatures treat AI? And one last thing: does treating corporations as legal persons actually strenghten the analogy in favor of AI personhood, or does it secretly cut the other way?
