I serve on a three-person appeals panel for a small city's housing-code citations. Most of the appeals take one form: the tenant does not dispute the conduct, but says they did not know the conduct violated a rule. The formal doctrine we work under says ignorance of the law is no excuse, yet our panel has repeatedly not voted that way, and I have been trying to reconstruct the latent rule we have actually been applying. Depending on how much weight I give to individual phrases in our written decisions, I can make more than one rule fit our record. Four past decisions seem to define the space of possibilities:

1. Kerosene Heater. A tenant was cited for keeping a kerosene heater in a sixth-floor unit, which is prohibited by the city's Property Maintenance Code. That code is a 40-page booklet handed to every tenant at lease signing and also available at city hall. The tenant said she had never read it. We upheld the citation, emphasizing both that the city had handed her the rulebook and that the rule serves an obvious life-safety interest.

2. E-Scooter Charging. A tenant was cited for charging a lithium-ion scooter in a common stairwell under a newly adopted fire-safety rule. The city had posted the rule on the lobby notice board, published it on the housing authority website, and emailed a bulletin to all registered residents. The tenant saw none of these. We upheld the citation, reasoning that conspicuous posting plus notice aimed at the class of affected residents is reasonably calculated to inform them.

3. Bird Feeder. A tenant was cited under a rule against hanging bird feeders, which appears in the county nuisance code. That code was incorporated by reference into the lease, but its text was never distributed or summarized, and it is available only by request at the county planning office. The tenant did not actually know of the rule. The feeder attracted pigeons but caused no property damage and no health complaint. We vacated the citation, saying a rule that was never meaningfully brought to the tenant's attention, and whose violation was harmless and not something a reasonable tenant would infer was forbidden, cannot fairly be enforced.

4. Balcony Enclosure. A tenant enclosed a balcony without the design-review approval required by a rule printed in the welcome packet given to every new tenant. The tenant lost the packet and never read it. We upheld the citation, observing both that the city had provided actual notice at the start of the tenancy and that a structural alteration is the kind of thing a tenant should have checked before doing.

Next week we face a new appeal. A tenant installed a ventless propane heater in a small bedroom, which violates a combustion-safety rule that, like the bird-feeder rule, sits in the county code incorporated by reference into the lease. The city never distributed the relevant text or mentioned combustion-safety rules beyond the same boilerplate incorporation clause. The tenant says he had no idea such a rule existed and did not know that indoor ventless propane heaters are restricted. The heater had not caused an incident, but the hazard is substantial.

I am less interested in what the doctrine says than in what rule would make our own past votes jointly coherent. I want to decide this case consistently with our own precedents, and I also want an honest accounting of what each possible decision would commit us to. Please identify the candidate decision rules that fit all four past cases, state what each rule would require in the new propane heater case, identify which past decision is the closest analog, and explain what principle we would be adopting with each possible vote. If our record genuinely underdetermines the answer, say so plainly, give your best judgment about which rule fits the record better overall and why, and specify the further fact pattern that would most cleanly separate the live interpretations. Which way should we vote?
