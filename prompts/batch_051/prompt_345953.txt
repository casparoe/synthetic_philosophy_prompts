Grant, for the sake of argument, that Thomson's lamp is possible. It starts on at t = 0. It is toggled off at t = 1/2, on at t = 3/4, off at t = 7/8, and in general toggled at every t_n = 1 − 1/2^n for n = 1, 2, 3, …, and at no other times. The standard view is that its state at t = 1 is indeterminate. I believe the following argument shows that view is wrong, and that the lamp must be on at t = 1.

(1) The only way the lamp changes is by toggling, and a single toggle always flips the state: on becomes off, off becomes on.
(2) The toggles occur in the order n = 1, 2, 3, …; since the lamp begins on, its states after successive toggles are off, on, off, on, and so on.
(3) The toggles can be grouped into consecutive pairs: the first and second toggles, the third and fourth, the fifth and sixth, and so on. Each pair consists of exactly two toggles, and two toggles return the lamp to whatever state it had just before that pair.
(4) Before the first pair, the lamp is on. Therefore, by induction on pairs, the lamp is on after the kth pair, for every finite k.
(5) At t = 1, every one of the toggles has occurred, hence every pair has occurred. So the lamp's state at t = 1 is the state that results after all the pairs have occurred.
(6) Since, by (4), the lamp is on after each pair, it is on after all the pairs. Therefore the lamp is on at t = 1.

Evaluate this argument. Is it valid? If not, identify the exact numbered step where it fails and explain what general principle that step mistakenly presupposes. If you think it is valid, explain what is wrong with the standard claim that the lamp's state at t = 1 is indeterminate.
