I've been going back and forth on something since a friend who works at an AI startup showed me two of their agents playing a one-shot prisoner's dilemma against each other, and I'd like a second opinion on the argument, not the engineering. The argument I keep coming back to is Hofstadter's superrationality idea. As I understand it: two players face a one-shot prisoner's dilemma with identical payoffs, and each knows the other is exactly as rational and is looking at the same situation. Standard game theory says defect, because defecting dominates no matter what the other player does. Hofstadter's move is to say a superrational player cooperates instead: since the other player will run the same reasoning on the same inputs, your choice is effectively mirrored, so the only real outcomes are both-cooperate or both-defect, and both-cooperate beats both-defect. Defecting looks good only if you picture yourself defecting while the other cooperates, but that's the one outcome the symmetry rules out.

Now the development that's bugging me. The two agents in my friend's demo were initialized from the same base model but then given different system prompts, fine-tuned on different data, and handed different context. So the identical-reasoner premise is gone — neither can be sure the other will land on the same move. But they're still much more similar to each other than two random humans would be, and each knows they share most of their training distribution. Does the superrationality argument survive that change? Is there some threshold of similarity past which the mirrored-reasoning logic kicks back in, does the argument need to be rebuilt into something about partial correlation rather than perfect symmetry, or does it just stop applying the moment the identical-reasoner premise is dropped? I'd also ask you to seperate what's actually settled about superrationality from what's genuinely contested among people who work on decision theory, and not to present a contested reading as the consensus. And I'd like it in continuous prose only, like an email, with no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables.
