I am a PhD student working on the ethics of energy transitions, and I keep running into an asymmetry between the way public debate treats nuclear risk and the way it treats the risk from fossil-fuel generation. After the Fukushima accident, German opinion largely concluded that continuing to run reactors was an unacceptable way to impose risk on the public, and the country accelerated its phase-out; France, with much of the public's acquiescence, kept operating a large nuclear fleet as the backbone of its low-carbon supply. Yet many of the same people who treat a reactor's residual accident risk as something no society may impose treat the particulate pollution from coal- and gas-fired plants as a routine, tolerable cost of keeping the lights on, even though that pollution is associated with substantial premature mortality year after year. One risk is a small probability of a large, visible, catastrophic harm and is treated as a question of prohibition; the other is a near certainty of many small, dispersed harms and is treated as a question of cost-benefit bookkeeping.

That difference may be warranted, or it may be a cognitive bias doing moral work. I want the strongest principle that could justify it. Distinguish it by consent, voluntariness, dread, catastrophic magnitude, the distribution of who bears the risk, the deontic significance of intending or causing a catastrophic harm, or whatever candidate seems strongest; test that principle against nearby cases, such as why an airliner crash is treated as a different kind of event from the far larger number of road deaths; and give an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or abandoned.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
