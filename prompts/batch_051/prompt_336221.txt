I'm a retired atmospheric chemist, and in the last year or so Ive been working my way through some twentieth-century philosophy. Last week my reading group did G. E. Moore's paper Proof of an External World. What stuck with everyone was the famous gesture near the end: Moore holds up his hands and says, in effect, here is one hand, and here is another, and takes that to be a perfectly good proof that an external world exists. One of the guys in my group, a former mathematician, now uses a version of this move to shut down any skeptical worry you throw at him. Brain in a vat? He just holds up his hand and says Moore already settled this. I find that annoying but also strangely hard to answer.

I feel like the line, or gesture, is doing a lot of work that nobody is spelling out. It could be the very modest claim that in ordinary life we all accept that we have hands. It could be the stronger claim that this ordinary acceptance is itself a proof, because the premise is more certain than any skeptical argument's conclusion. It could be a claim about standards of proof, that epistemology should answer to everyday practices rather than replace them. It could be a meta-philosophical claim that common sense outranks philosophical argument. Or it might just be a rhetorical move that licenses dogmatism. I cant tell which of these Moore meant, and I definitely can't tell which of them actually does the work my mathematician friend needs.

What are the distinct precise claims the here-is-a-hand move could be expressing, from the trivial to the radical? Which of those readings actually support dismissing a brain-in-a-vat worry, and which just evade it? Lay them out as a menu I can bring to next week's meeting, with a note on which ones are defensible.
