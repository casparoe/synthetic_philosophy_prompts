Draft the joint statement that a committed optimist about aesthetic testimony and a committed pessimist about it would produce after a good-faith, year-long adversarial collaboration. For this task, treat the positions as stipulated. The optimist holds that testimony can, under favorable conditions, be an adequate basis for justified aesthetic belief—and sometimes aesthetic knowledge—about how a particular work looks, sounds, reads, or is overall, without the recipient having had first-hand experience of that work. The pessimist holds that first-hand experience of the work is required for justified aesthetic judgment, so testimony alone can ground at most a weaker state, such as a justified belief about what the work is likely to be like or about what the testifier judges. Both parties are competent and have shifted on at least some sub-questions during the collaboration; neither is a strawman. The statement is an internal working document, so do not include citations, reading lists, or named-author scorekeeping.

Write the document in this order, with these contents:

1. Agreed claims. A numbered list of at least eight claims that both can sign. Each claim must be substantive: at least one party should have wanted to resist it before the collaboration. Cover at least the ubiquity of testimonial aesthetic belief, the distinctive value of first-hand experience, the conditions under which aesthetic testimony tends to be reliable, and what the debate is not about. Engage with these pressure points somewhere in the document: deferring to reviews and friends' reports; the oddness of asserting an aesthetic judgment one has no independent basis for; cases where testimony gets an aesthetic matter right that the recipient would have gotten wrong; and whether an expert's description can substitute for first-hand encounter.

2. Disagreements table. For each residual disagreement, give: the issue; the optimist's formulation, worded so a committed optimist would sign it; and the pessimist's formulation, worded so a committed pessimist would sign it. Include the central epistemic disagreement and at least two downstream disagreements (for example about blame, assertion, or pedagogy).

3. Cruxes. For each crux—a sub-question such that a settled answer would move at least one side—state why it matters, give one concrete case or body of evidence both sides would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction about it, written in advance.

Be definite throughout: choose each formulation rather than presenting alternatives, and do not hedge with loose option-listing. End by saying which parts of the original dispute the collaboration reduced to merely verbal differences, if any, and which remain substantive.

Open with a one-sentence joint conclusion stating what the collaboration established. Begin directly with that sentence: no preface, no commentary, and no restating of the instructions.
