A few years ago I adopted a rule that has gradually spread from my work into my intellectual life: I do not set quantitative targets for anything I care about, and I refuse to use quantitative metrics even as fallible evidence when I judge something I cannot observe directly—a student's understanding, the quality of someone's work, the health of an organization. My reason is Goodhart's law, which I take to mean that once a measure becomes a target it stops being a good measure, and I now apply this to myself as well: I assume I will specification-game whatever number I am given, skimming to hit a page-count or padding behaviors to raise a satisfaction score. A statistically minded friend says I am overapplying the idea. Goodhart's law, he says, is about what happens when a proxy is placed under optimization pressure, not about using a proxy once as fallible evidence, and by discarding test scores, citations, and retention numbers I am replacing a possibly corrupted measurement with unstructured impressions that are themselves gamed by recency, flattery, and vivid cases. I cannot tell from the inside which is worse. Is my rule defensible as a policy about what evidence to trust? Is there a version that keeps the protection without discarding the information—a test for when a metric remains informative despite having been a target somewhere upstream, or a way to use a metric without making it a target?
