I spent forty years as a bench chemist, so the habit of wanting an operational criterion dies hard. Since retiring, I've been reading about early mathematics — that famous Babylonian tablet, Plimpton 322, with its rows of numbers that look for all the world like Pythagorean triples — and it's pushed me into a position I'd like you to stress-test.

Here's my homemade idea. I call it the Third-Column Criterion: counting becomes mathematics at the point where a number gets written down that no one has counted, and that no one needs to count because its only license is its relation to other numbers. A tally of sheep rests on the sheep — if the tally and the flock disagree, you go out and count again. But one of the columns on that tablet, at least on my untutored reading, seems to be there because of its relation to the other columns, not because a surveyor went out and measured a field that size. The question "how do you know that number is right?" stops getting the answer "look at the world" and starts getting the answer "do the arithmetic." That, I want to say, is the moment arithmetic detaches from inventories and becomes a subject in its own right rather than a skill.

I can already hear some near misses. A merchant who fills an inventory row not by counting the sacks but by multiplying sacks by a standard per-sack weight has also produced a number nobody counted, and it rests on arithmetic — but calling that the birth of mathematics feels grand. A child reciting number words without needing any tokens is doing something even odder. And I realize this may be exactly the sort of thing historians of early mathematics already have a name for, which I haven't read. So be honest: is the criterion coherent? What are the three strongest objections, and is any of them fatal rather than repairable? And if it's repairable, what's the smallest fix? I definately can't see it clearly from inside my own idea.
