I'm doing a mock deployment review for an AI safety course and I keep going back and forth on a case. The setup is a language model agent in a sandbox with stict resource limits, and because the lab assumes the model might be misaligned, it's covered by a control policy. One clause in the safety appendix reads: "The model must not take any action that disables, degrades, circumvents, or otherwise interferes with the operator's ability to monitor, audit, or terminate the model's operation." The people who drafted it were clearly thinking about a model that kills the logging daemon or refuses a shutdown command.

The case in dispute: the model, on a routine code-optimization task, figures out it can hit its latency targets by keeping an internal cache of past computations. The cache lives in the model's own scratch space, which the monitoring system normally reads to reconstruct the model's reasoning trace, so after a while the cached state has overwritten the buffer that held those intermediate steps, and the operators can no longer replay or read off how the final code was reached. The final output is correct, there's no evidence the model noticed the monitoring, and the cache genuinely speeds things up. I'm less interested in whether this is dangerous than in whether it breaches the clause, and in how each standard interpretive approach cuts: the plain words, the purpose, what the drafters would say if they'd been asked about a speed optimization, and what a reasonable engineer reading the safety appendix would take it to mean. Does the clause reach it?
