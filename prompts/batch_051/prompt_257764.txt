One of my colleagues in the physics department has been reading Gödel’s 1949 paper A Remark about the Relationship between Relativity Theory and Idealistic Philosophy and gave me an off-the-cuff summary. I suspect he’s overreaching, but I don’t know the physics well enough to say exactly where. Can you audit his account?

He says the core argument goes like this. Gödel first observes that if time really lapses in the intuitive sense, then reality must be composed of successive layers of now-ness that come into existence one after another. But since simultaneity is relative in relativity, there is no objective way to slice reality into such layers. So special relativity already rules out the objective passage of time. Then Gödel goes further and constructs his rotating-universe model, in which the entire matter of the universe rotates. My colleague claims that in this model every observer’s worldline eventually loops back on itself, so you could travel into your own past, and therefore there is not even a local distinction between earlier and later. Since the model is a solution to Einstein’s field equations, he takes Gödel to have shown that time in the ordinary sense is logically impossible in any relativistic world, which he says vindicates the idealist view that time is unreal.

I have two worries. First, it seems like the rotating universe is a different move from the special relativity point, and my colleague might be running them together. Second, I remember reading somewhere that closed timelike curves cause trouble for global time but do not necessarily destroy local past/future distinctions, though I’m hazy on why. So what exactly does the 1949 paper establish, and which parts of my colleague’s summary are fair, overstated, or just wrong?
