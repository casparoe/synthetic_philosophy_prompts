Last night my friend Dana and I had this long, circular argument about our other friend Sam, who's on the board of a small tenants-rights nonprofit. Sam recused himself from a vote on which caterer to hire because one of the bidders is run by his old roommate; nobody seriously thinks he'd tilt the decision, but he wanted to be careful. Afterwards he mentioned he'd probably still go to the guy's birthday party next month, and Dana jumped on him. Her whole argument was that someone in Sam's position has to be above suspicion, not merely above actual partiality, and she kept waving around the old proverb about Caesar's wife needing to be above suspicion like that ended the matter. I pushed back that there's a difference between being honest and being forced to manage everyone's uncharitable guesses, and anyway a reputation that clean is impossible to maintain and will just favor the people least willing to have an actual life.

I'm still not sure weather she's right, and I want to get clearer on what that proverb even commits you to. Can you lay out the distinct precise claims it could be making—what "above suspicion" actually demands, whose suspicion matters, what kind of evidence it responds to, and whether it's aimed at the person or the institution? I'd like a menu, from the near-trivial readings to the very demanding ones, with a verdict on each: which are obviously true, which are substantive but defensible, which are too strong or outright false, and which reading would actually support Dana's conclusion that Sam should skip the party. And if you can say something about whether being above suspicion is unambiguously good, or whether there are real costs to chasing it, I'd appreciate that too.
