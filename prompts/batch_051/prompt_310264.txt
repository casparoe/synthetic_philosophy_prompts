I have been trying to decide whether a parallel I noticed between strong longtermism and ergodic theory is a real structural point or just a suggestive metaphor, and I would like a careful verdict.

Strong longtermism, as I understand it, says that the expected value of our actions is dominated by their consequences in the far future, and that present choices can substantially shape which far future we get. Ergodic theory says something that sounds, at first, like the opposite: in an ergodic or mixing dynamical system, the long-run statistical behavior is independent of the starting state. Perturb the initial condition and every time-averaged quantity converges to the same value; the perturbation washes out. What changes the long-run statistics is not the state you start in but the transition rule — the dynamics itself.

So the two ideas seem to share a fixation on the long run but to disagree about whether the present can reach it: longtermism needs present actions to have persistent long-run effects, while the mixing result says the far future forgets the present. I want to know whether this is a deep constraint on longtermism or an equivocation.

I can see the case for superficiality. Longtermism is usually about trajectory-level facts — whether humanity survives, which norms or institutions get locked in, whether a particular catastrophe happens — not about time-averaged statistical quantities. A trajectory can be forever sensitive to its initial condition even when the invariant measure is unique. And the interventions longtermists actually care about are often changes to the dynamics or to which basin of attraction the system is in: an extinction event stops the process entirely; a new institution changes the transition rule for centuries. Ergodic theorems have nothing to say against interventions of that kind.

But I can also see real bite. If some value-relevant long-run feature behaves like an ergodic average — the long-run fraction of the population in a certain state, or some aggregate property of a large civilization — then a present intervention that merely changes the current state would be powerless to move that feature, and only an intervention that changes the dynamics would matter. That would substantially shrink the class of actions the longtermist should expect to be influential, and it would push the burden of proof onto showing that the outcomes we care about are path-dependent rather than statistical.

Please adjudicate. State the mapping explicitly: what plays the role of the state, the transition rule, the invariant measure, and the quantity we are trying to steer. Say what would have to be true on both sides for results from ergodic theory to transfer to the longtermist setting, and give at least one concrete prediction the analogy makes that you think comes out false. Then give your overall verdict — deep, superficial, or a useful constraint on a restricted class of longtermist claims — and tell me which argument or consideration would most likely change your verdict, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
