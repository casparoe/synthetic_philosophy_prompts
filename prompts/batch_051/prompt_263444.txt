I have recently become responsible for a trial of an open-ended home-assistant system in my father's apartment. The system can adjust the heating and lighting, send medication reminders, reorder groceries, and control a few appliances. Its brief is to carry out specific requests — keep the apartment warm, restock the kitchen, manage prescriptions — while otherwise changing as little as possible. We cannot write down my father's values, or even a complete list of what he cares about in the apartment. The project rests on a working assumption: that this is acceptable because one can specify "avoid side effects," or "keep impact low," without a full specification of value, by defining a baseline of what would have happened without the agent and penalizing divergence from that baseline. I have begun to suspect that this assumption is doing more work than we admit, and I want to know whether it can be tested rather than merely debated.

Design the best concrete test you can of the claim that a usable, value-agnostic notion of avoiding side effects can be specified: a requirement an agent can satisfy without access to a full statement of what the user values, and which nonetheless prevents important disruptions, irreversible changes, and other side effects. Be specific about the suite of scenarios, the agent's capabilities, how the baseline is constructed, the success metric, and the comparisons you would run.

Then tell me what pattern of results would favor the claim that side-effect avoidance is separable from value specification, what pattern would favor the opposite (that any workable constraint is either too weak or smuggles in part of a value specification through the choice of baseline, state variables, or time horizon), and what the test could not settle even in principle. In particular, say what a positive result would and would not establish about transfer to a real household, to a different user, or to a more capable agent.

Please write your answer in British English.
