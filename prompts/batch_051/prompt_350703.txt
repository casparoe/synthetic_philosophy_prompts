In my legal philosophy study group two people got into a long argument after class about whether it’s fair to bind people to rules they cannot know. Sarah took the position that it usually is fair, because every legal system needs something like the maxim that ignorance of the law is no excuse; otherwise a defendant could just say “I didn’t know” and the whole system falls apart. She added a Hobbes point from Leviathan, saying Hobbes distinguished between ignorance of the law of nature, which excuses no one because reason alone teaches you not to harm others, and ignorance of civil law, which can excuse in some cases when the law hasn’t been sufficiently declared or the person has no reasonable way to know it. Tom jumped on that and said it actually undermines her case: once you admit that some civil-law ignorance excuses, the real question is not whether ignorance excuses but when the state is entitled to expect people to know. Sarah replied that the no-excuse rule still works as a default, because in a basically public legal system people can and should check; if you make exceptions too easy, obligations become optional. Tom said that’s an argument about administrability, not fairness. He gave the example of a criminal prohibition buried in an obscure regulation that is never publicized: convicting someone under it punishes them for failing to do something impossible. Sarah countered that modern law is mostly online, so it’s now more knowable than ever. Tom said availability isn’t knowability—ordinary people don’t read the federal register, and legal complexity can make even diligent people unknowing violators. Sarah answered that no legal system can tailor itself to each person’s educational or linguistic situation, and that doctrines like mens rea and prosecutorial discretion already filter the worst cases. Tom said that’s an admission that the principle alone can’t do the moral work.

I didn’t take a side while they were argueing, but I’ve been turning it over since. Can you adjudicate this? Specifically: who is right about which of the points they actually made, which arguments are stronger or weaker, and what are both of them missing?
