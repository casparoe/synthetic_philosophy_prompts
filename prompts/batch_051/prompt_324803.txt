I'm part of a municipal policy working group that has been asked to stress-test a proposal its author calls "resident-locked futarchy" for our city of roughly 500,000 people. Under the proposal, any capital project over $1.5M automatically triggers two conditional prediction markets: one on the value of a published five-year quality-of-life index (a composite of, say, traffic injuries per capita, housing completions, air quality, and park access) if the project is implemented, and one on the same index if it is not. The project is implemented if and only if the market-implied expected index contingent on implementation exceeds the market-implied expected index contingent on non-implementation by at least five points, measured as a thirty-day volume-weighted average, and total two-sided volume over that window clears a minimum threshold. All positions are locked in escrow until the index is published five years later; there is no early exit. Trading is restricted to residents, property owners, and businesses with at least two years of local economic activity; outside speculators may trade only with three times the usual collateral. The index is set every four years by an independent metric board, but the council can amend it by a two-thirds vote, which also reopens all pending markets. Finally, two percent of projects that fail the market test are selected by lottery and funded anyway from a small reserve, explicitly to generate counterfactual data, with the lottery stratified by policy area.

Treat this as a mechanism rather than a moral position about technocracy or democracy. I need a detailed account of how it would actually behave, not a verdict on whether it is a good idea in the abstract. Work through the likely incentives for each actor: residents and local businesses, project sponsors (contractors, agencies, council members), the metric board, and the council itself. What would each be incentivized to do, including when their interests conflict or align? Where would the system be gamed first, and by whom? Pay special attention to how the randomized implementation of rejected projects would interact with the markets themselves: could it change what prices mean, create arbitrage-like strategies, alter which projects get proposed, or undermine the informational content of the markets? What second-order effects would this have on the city's project pipeline, on public deliberation, and on the index over time? Which failure modes could be fixed without gutting the scheme, and which are intrinsic to the design?

Please answer as an expert mechanism designer and political economist familiar with prediction markets, public choice, and futarchy. Before drafting, think through the mechanism from each actor's point of view, including possible collusion among actors who are nominally on different sides, and flag any ambiguity in the proposal that materially affects the analysis. Where it is gamed first" (roughly 200 words); "4. Interaction with the randomized counterfactual lottery" (roughly 250 words); "5. Second-order effects" (roughly 250 words); "6. Failure modes and whether they are fixable" (roughly 300 words); and "7. Confidence and the point I'm least sure of" (roughly 100 words). In the final section, state how confident you are in the overall analysis and name the single empirical or theoretical point about this mechanism you are least sure of.
