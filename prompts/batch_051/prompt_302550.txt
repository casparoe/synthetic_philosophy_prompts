Design a runnable classroom activity for an upper-level undergraduate seminar in practical rationality. The group has sixteen students and the session lasts seventy-five minutes. The aim is to make participants feel the force of the rational-irrationality problem associated with Derek Parfit's hitchhiker: a driver will give the passenger a lift only if the passenger promises to pay at the destination; the passenger knows that once delivered they will have no self-interested reason to pay; and the driver knows this too, so the lift simply does not happen. The activity should let participants run into this structure themselves, before it is named, through small two-person games with tokens and explicit payoff tables, and should state in advance what participants should come away seeing.

Base the game on a one-shot promise or threat whose backward-induction solution leaves both players worse off than an available deal, and build three phases around it. First, a baseline phase with cheap talk only, in which the announced promise or threat is predicted to unravel. Second, a comparison of commitment devices, with at least one external device that changes the later choice's incentives, such as a forfeitable deposit or an enforceable fine, and at least one felt-but-ineffective device that changes nothing in the payoff table, such as a solemn written vow. Third, an attempt to represent a purely internal disposition to act against one's later best judgement, together with an honest account of why a classroom cannot fully represent it. Specify the roles, the exact payoff matrix for each pair of choices, the sequencing, the anonymity and rematching rules, any small stakes, and the facilitator's administrative script.

Script the debrief so that it maps moments in play onto the distinction between external self-binding and internal self-alteration, and onto the unresolved question whether it can be rational to bring it about that one will later act against what would then be one's own best reasons. The design should also say what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run, for example participants honouring promises out of moral habit rather than payoff, confusion about which devices are enforceable, collusion, or misreading the matrix, and how the facilitator should handle each. End with a clear statement of which feature of rational irrationality the exercise cannot reproduce and what the facilitator must supply in words. Write the answer in British English.
