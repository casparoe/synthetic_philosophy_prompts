Here is an argument I have been working on, and I would like a precise diagnosis rather than a survey of positions. It concerns empty names and truth in fiction. My suspicion is that the argument goes wrong somewhere in the move from what holds inside a story to what holds outside it, but I cannot identify the exact step.

(1) The sentence "Sherlock Holmes is a detective" is true according to the Conan Doyle stories.
(2) If a sentence of subject–predicate form is true according to a story, then according to that story its subject term refers to something.
(3) Therefore, according to the stories, the name "Sherlock Holmes" refers to something.
(4) If, according to the stories, a name refers to something, then that name refers to something.
(5) Therefore the name "Sherlock Holmes" refers to something.
(6) If a name refers to something, then the sentence obtained by putting that name before "exists" is true.
(7) Therefore the sentence "Sherlock Holmes exists" is true.
(8) But the sentence "Sherlock Holmes does not exist" is true, and it is the negation of "Sherlock Holmes exists."
(9) Therefore a sentence and its negation are both true, which is impossible.
(10) Hence the reasoning must contain a fault.

Please assume, for the sake of the exercise, that (1), (2), (6), and (8) are true. Is the argument valid? If it is valid but unsound, which numbered step is the first to be false, and is the fault a false premise or an invalid inference? State the correctly scoped version of that step and explain whether the argument would still go through if the corrected version replaced the original. At the very end of your answer, write \answer{...} containing either "sound" if no step fails, or the number of the first failing step together with a two-word label of the fault in the form \answer{<step number>: <label>}.
