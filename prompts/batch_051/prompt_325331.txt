I have no formal training in philosophy, but I have been thinking about the ethics of having children on my own for a while, and I recently read a magazine profile of David Benatar that summarized his position roughly as follows. According to the profile, Benatar's antinatalism rests on an asymmetry between pain and pleasure. For a person who exists, pain is bad and pleasure is good. For a person who never exists, the pain they would have suffered is absent, and that absence is good even though there is no one there to enjoy it; but the pleasure they would have experienced is also absent, and that absence is not bad at all, because there is no one there to be deprived of it. So never existing, the article said, gets credit for every harm avoided but is not charged for any pleasure missed. Since every human life contains at least some pain, and since no one is deprived by missing the goods of a life they never had, coming into existence is always a net loss compared with never existing. The article then concluded that this is why Benatar thinks it would be better for everyone if no one had children, and that most people's lives are not worth living; they only believe otherwise because of psychological biases that make them overestimate how well their lives are going.

I have been turning this over and I am not sure how much of it to trust. The asymmetry part feels carefully stated, but I keep getting stuck on the jump to saying that most people's lives are not worth living, which sounds much stronger than saying it would have been better if those people had never existed. Could you help me sort out which parts of this summary are accurate, which parts are subtly unfair, and whether that final claim about lives not being worth living actually follows from the asymmetry as described? I would also like to know what a careful defender of Benatar would object to in this presentation.
