So my dad got a diagnosis a few weeks ago — treatable, probably another ten to fifteen good years — and he's refusing. He isn't depressed; he just says things like the story has to end somewhere and he doesn't want to be the last one at the party. I've gone down a rabbit hole on this and it's made me realize 'deathism' mushes together a bunch of positions that pull apart the second you push. I'd like you to write me a "which rejection-of-life-extension view do you actually hold?" quiz — eight questions, meant for my dad, not an academic. Each question should be a concrete offer (a no-side-effect pill, a treatment his wife refuses, a society-wide rollout his grandkids also get, an intervention that slowly changes his temperament, a choose-your-own exit date, something like that) with two or three answer options, phrased so he answers from cases rather than labels. The scoring key should sort answer patterns into leaves: status-quo/loss-aversion, narrative closure, relational solidarity, identity/continuity worry, anti-hubris/naturalness, hidden-costs skepticism (interventions always backfire in unseen ways), adaptive preference ('didn't want it anyway'), and a plain quality-of-life leaf for refusing because living is bad right now rather than because death itself is valued. Build it from the logical space, not the list of names: say which answer combinations are occupied, flag any pairs of leaves your questions can't honestly seperate, and note unoccupied combinations with a one-line guess at why (incoherent, unattractive, or just nobody there). No question should just ask whether he thinks death is natural.
