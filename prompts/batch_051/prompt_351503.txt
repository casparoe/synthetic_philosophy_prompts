I keep running into the same wall whenever I try to think about deplatforming and cancel culture as forms of punishment instead of just as "free speech" or "platform policy" issues. Someone says a ban is just a private company declining to associate, which is obviously not punishment in any interesting sense; someone else says when a hundred thousand people all demand someone be fired it sure acts like a punishment, even if no single one of them has authority to punish. So before I read another take, I want the full spec. Write the desiderata: what does an adequate theory of social sanction as punishment have to get right? By "social sanction" I mean the whole family—deplatforming, firing campaigns, mass shunning, review-bombing, demonetization. The theory's job is to judge whether a given instance was justified or unjust, not to predict when pile-ons happen (that's a different theory).

Lay out the cases it has to handle—a celebrity with millions of followers vs. a nobody quote-tweeted by someone huge; a platform ban vs. an employer firing vs. a crowd; sanctions for what someone said vs. something they did off-platform years ago—and the distinctions it can't afford to collapse: punishment vs. quarantine (we remove you not because you deserve it but because you're contagious, informationally or socially) vs. merely declining to associate vs. expressing disapproval. Notice that a single act might be all four depending on who does it and how many. For each requirement, say why it's a requirement and give the puzzle or case that forces it, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's preferences (the free-association reductionists vs. the everything-is-speech people, say).

Then the part I most want: flag any pairs of requirements you suspect can't be jointly satisfied. My hunch is that the deepest one is the pile-on problem—the way an act changes its moral character purely through coordination, so a repost that's mere protest when one person does it becomes punishment when ten thousand do—and a theory has to either dissolve that or own it. Is that a real desideratum or am I smuggling in a demand for group-agency metaphysics? End by saying which single requirement knocks out the most popular positions people actually defend (you can sketch those positions in your own words). No reading list—I want the criteria and the cases, not citations.
