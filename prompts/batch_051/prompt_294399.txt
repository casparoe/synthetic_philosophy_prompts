A friend and I just spent an evening arguing about whether computers and real-time information could answer the old Hayek/Mises objection to socialist planning — that no central planner could aggregate the dispersed, often tacit knowledge embedded in an economy. We kept returning to Project Cybersyn, so I’d like you to analyze that episode as a case study for the socialist calculation debate.

Cybersyn, built under Salvador Allende in Chile in the early 1970s and designed in part by Stafford Beer, was meant to link state-run enterprises to a central planning apparatus through telex machines. Factories would send daily data on production, raw-material supplies, and absenteeism; software would flag statistical anomalies in near real time; and an operations room would let planners respond to emergencies. It was never fully implemented, and it ended with the 1973 coup. Assume for the sake of argument that it had reached the scale its designers intended.

Analyze Cybersyn through the lens of the debate among Mises, Hayek, and Lange. What would Hayek and Mises have predicted about such a system’s ability to allocate resources across the whole economy, and why? What would Lange have said the system still needed — for example, something like shadow prices or trial-and-error pricing rules — before it could meet the calculation objection? Where does Cybersyn’s actual design answer or fail to answer that objection? Finally, say whether the episode should be counted as evidence against Hayek, against Lange, or as a case the debate’s categories were not yet equipped to classify. Be precise about what Cybersyn was and was not: above all, whether a real-time monitoring-and-control network is relevantly different from a price-discovery mechanism.
