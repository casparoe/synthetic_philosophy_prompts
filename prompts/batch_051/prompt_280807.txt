Ive spent the last month arguing with my brother-in-law, who keeps telling anyone at dinner that natural selection is a dressed-up tautology: the fittest survive, and we only know they were the fittest because they survived. It bugged me enough that I went back and took an intro biology elective as an adult, and the course gave me a working definition of fitness that has honestly made the worry worse rather than better. The way I was taught, an organism is fit to the extent that (1) it actually leaves viable offspring, (2) it survives to reproductive age, and (3) its genes show up in a large share of the next generation's gene pool. That is all about actual outcomes after the fact.

Heres a case I keep turning over. Consider a particular female anole, call her L, carrying a heritable allele that shifts her thermal tolerance a bit upward. In her population, bearers of this allele have averaged about thirty percent more surviving offspring per season than non-bearers over the last few generations, and there is a decent mechanistic story for why (they can forage through the hottest part of the day while others are hiding). But in L's own season, she reaches maturity healthy, and then a mudslide kills her before she ever mates. She leaves zero offspring. Several of her non-carrier siblings survive that year and reproduce normally.

So: is L fit? Is the allele fit? Run the case through the three criteria, say honestly where the criteria underdetermine the answer, and whether the problem is with the case or with the criteria. Then tell me whether the case pushes toward reading fitness as a propensity (expected, not actual, reproductive success) in order to escape the tautology worry, and whether that move actually works. Keep clear what is settled in the philosophy of biology from what is still genuinely contested — don't present the propensity reading as the consensus. Give me the answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
