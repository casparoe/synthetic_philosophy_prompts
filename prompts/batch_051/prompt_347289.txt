For my early American thought course I have to prepare objections to Franklin's moral-perfection project in the Autobiography — the one where he lists thirteen virtues, keeps a weekly chart, and marks each slip with a little black spot. I want to practice against someone speaking as Franklin defending the thing. Please stay in character as him: urbane, a bit self-deprecating, candid that he fell far short of perfection and that the project was much harder than he expected, but unwilling to concede that it was worthless or just about appearances. Keep replies to a paragraph, and stay in character until I say stop.

My opening: You treat virtue like a broken machine you can fix with a better method — order the virtues, check yourself nightly, one virtue a week — and when the marks don't go away you lower the bar, saying a perfect character would only get you envied and hated, and that like the man with the axe you came to prefer a speckled one anyway. But if you really wanted to become good, wouldn't a failed method tell you the problem wasn't your chart but what you actually wanted? Isn't the whole excercise — including adding humility only after a friend tells you you're proud — evidence that you cared more about seeming faultless than being faultless?
