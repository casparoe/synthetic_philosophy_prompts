Having spent most of my career as a research chemist, I came to philosophy late, and I have been reading the recent discussions of intellectual humility with a mixture of recognition and unease. The recognition comes from laboratory life: I learned to admire colleagues who were blunt, even a little prickly, while reporting exactly how uncertain a result was, and I learned to distrust a bit the colleague who was unfailingly self-deprecating but would later overclaim because he did not want to seem difficult or disagreeable. The unease is conceptual. Humility and modesty are usually treated as one virtue or as close variants of it, yet the two laboratory types I just described strike me as almost opposite character types, and ordinary usage would call both of them humble in different senses.

I have therefore come to separate what I call "evidential self-grading" from "presentational self-effacement." Evidential self-grading is a property of one's believing: holding a view with a degree of confidence that tracks the evidence, including the evidence one has about one's own training, biases, blocks, and known error rates. Presentational self-effacement is a property of one's conduct: understating one's competence, contribution, or confidence so as to signal approachability, avoid giving offense, or preempt status conflict. My stronger thesis is that only evidential self-grading is a virtue in any unconditional sense, because it has a standard of correctness—one can be wrong about how much to trust oneself—whereas presentational self-effacement is a context-bound social tactic: sometimes demanded by kindness or teamwork, sometimes a failure of honesty, and often merely conventional. I also suspect that institutions, universities especially, praise the second under the name of the first, because both lower the status cost of disagreement; but I may be converting a sociological observation into a philosophical claim too quickly.

I would like an honest assessment rather than confirmation. Has this distinction already been drawn in a better or cleaner form, and if so, what exactly would be lost by adopting my version of it? Is the stronger thesis right, or does presentational self-effacement sometimes count as an intellectual virtue, and does evidential self-grading sometimes fail to be one? Give me the three strongest objections to the view as stated, and tell me which, if any, is fatal rather than merely a forced modification.
