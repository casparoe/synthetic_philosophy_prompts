I've been arguing with a friend about what grown children actually owe their parents, and it's turned into one of those conversations where we're both confident about individual cases but can't articulate the principle underneath. I've come across a view—sometimes called the friendship model of filial obligation—that says the whole question is framed wrong, or at least that the answer isn't "repayment": what adult children owe their parents is supposed to work like what close friends owe each other, arising from the ongoing relationship itself rather than from some ledger of past sacrifices. I find that attractive, but it seems to make the word "filial" do almost no work, and I'm not sure it handles the ugly cases.

So I want a workable defintion of filial obligation—or of "what an adult child owes a parent," if you prefer that framing—that gets these cases right. (a) A warm, supportive parent who scrimped to send you to college now has dementia and needs help you could easily give. (b) A parent who was abusive, disappeared for twenty years, and now shows up demanding money. (c) A parent who paid for everything and was materially generous but emotionally cold and self-absorbed, now old and alone and needing help moving into assisted living. (d) A close friend and a parent each need the same kind of help at the same time, and you can only do one. (e) A parent who gave up a career to raise you now expects you to support them indefinitely, even though that means never saving for your own kids. (f) A neglectful but not abusive parent, never really present, now destitute and ill. I'd count (a) as clearly owed and (b) as clearly not, but I'm honestly unsure about (c)–(f), and I don't fully trust my instincts on some of them.
