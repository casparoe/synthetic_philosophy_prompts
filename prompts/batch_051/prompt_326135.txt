I have been reading outside any formal study about Ian Hacking's distinction between indifferent kinds and interactive kinds, and about the looping effects he thinks arise in the classification of people, and I would like to be sure I grasp the mechanism rather than just the slogan. As I understand it, an indifferent kind is something like a quark, which is unaffected by how it is classified, whereas an interactive kind is a classification of people, where those classified can learn of the classification and change their behaviour in response. A looping effect, on that picture, is a two-way causal process: the classification shapes the people, and the changed people in turn prompt revisions of the classification. Could you take me through that process in some detail, using one worked psychiatric example such as dissociative identity disorder or borderline personality disorder? In particular, I would like you to: (a) set out the stages of the loop and indicate where expert knowledge, institutions, and the classified people themselves each enter it; (b) explain what distinguishes this from the more familiar idea of a self-fulfilling prophecy, and from the possibility that the people themselves do not change but merely come to be described under a new label; and (c) say what looping effects, taken seriously, imply for the question whether psychiatric categories are discovered or made, and where the idea itself begins to break down if one presses it too far.
