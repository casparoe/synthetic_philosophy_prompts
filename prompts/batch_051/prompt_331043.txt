I'm a PhD student in AI safety and a supervisor of mine floated something during an argument about safety cases that I keep going back and forth on. Suppose a major lab adopts the following internal rule for deployment: a safety case can only cite worst-case guarentees as positive evidence for deploying a new system. By that I mean formal proofs that a harmful output cannot occur over an explicit class of inputs, or under an explicit adversary and budget. Average-case empirical evidence — red-team success rates, refusal rates on a benchmark, the fraction of unsafe completions under some prompt distribution — can only be used to block deployment, never to support it. So a 0.1% unsafe-completion rate counts against the system, but a 99.9% safe rate counts for nothing.

Treat it as a mechanism rather than as a moral claim. I'd like an analysis of how it would actually play out: what it incentivises among safety researchers, capability researchers, and management; how a team under delivery pressure would game it (narrow input classes, proving things about a censored or guard-railed version of the model, picking an adversary model that's too weak); whether it selects for systems that are robust or for systems that happen to admit tractable proofs; what happens to empirical red-teaming when it can only ever stop a deployment and never justify one; and what second-order effects you'd expect once the rule has been in place for a couple of years. Also say which failure modes could be fixed by tightening the rule and which would force you to abandon the worst-case-only idea altogether. Please answer in British English. And be explicit about confidence: say how confident you are in the main predictions and single out the one point you're least sure of.
