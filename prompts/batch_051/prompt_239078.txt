I spent my career as a field biologist (ecology, with systematics always hovering around the edges) and I've only recently started reading philosophy of biology. Before I go further into the species-concept literature, I want the specification first. What would an adequate species concept actually have to get right? Give me the requirements list: the cases it has to handle, the distinctions it can't collapse, and the results from biology it can't contradict, with the reason each one is a requirement. Then flag any pair of requirements you suspect can't be jointly satisfied, and tell me which single requirement does the most work in knocking out the usual candidates.

The cases I care about: a ring species, where populations at the two ends meet but don't interbreed while every adjacent step along the way does; asexual lineages (rotifer-style all-female ones); bacteria and archaea where genes move sideways, so the old common-gene-pool picture breaks down; fertile hybrids that keep backcrossing; chronospecies in the fossil record, where a lineage changes gradually and somebody still has to draw a line in time; and partially isolated populations caught in the middle of speciating, with one-way gene flow and isolation that may only arise after the split has already started. That last bit matters to me, and I want it on the list explicitly: reproductive isolation is often a late symptom, not the thing that inititates divergence, so a concept built entirely on isolation seems to be tracking a downstream sign rather than the process itself.

The list should also keep seperate some distinctions I don't want collapsed: the rank (species as a category) versus the particular species; what a species is versus the operational signs we use to recognize one; and discovering where the boundaries are versus deciding on a criterion in the first place. It should be honest about what the concept is for, too. For me the job is to let a working systematist and an ecologist mean the same thing by the same name, and to track something real in the evolutionary process rather than just a filing convention. If a concept does one of those jobs but not the other, say so instead of pretending the list is job-neutral.

A concept also can't contradict what we know from population genetics: gene histories can come apart from population histories, gene flow between good species is common and sometimes adaptive, and a big chunk of the tree of life doesn't have tidy vertical descent at the base. Which requirements do you think are non-negotiable and which are just local preferences? Is there any place where the requirements force a tradeoff, like between handling ring species coherently and treating asexual lineages as species? Then, in a few sentences, how the usual contenders — a reproductive-isolation view, a morphological one, and a monophyly-based one — fare against the list. Please answer in American English.
