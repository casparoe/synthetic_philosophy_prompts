I follow a policy about other people's aesthetic judgments that I keep almost finding exceptions to, and I want to know whether the policy itself is defensible. When a friend or critic with good taste tells me that a film is emotionally shallow, that a novel is masterfully plotted, or that an album rewards repeated listening, I treat those claims as a reason to spend my time accordingly — I'll choose what to watch or read next partly on their word. I'll also pass along their factual reports about the work (the twist, the structure, the conditions of its making) as if I had verified them myself. But I won't adopt the evaluative verdict: after three readers I trust tell me a novel is brilliant, I still won't say the novel is brilliant, I won't include it in a list of books I think are great, and if someone asks me in conversation whether it's good, I hedge with "people I respect say so" rather than giving an answer of my own.

The asymmetry showed up last week. My partner asked whether a book I'd been recommending to everyone was actually any good, and I realized I'd been recommending it on borrowed authority while refusing, on principle, to say the thing that would make the recommendation honest: that I think it's good. I had no similar scruples about repeating the plot, which I also only knew second-hand. I could tell a story that makes this principled — maybe aesthetic judgments are the kind of judgment one has to make for oneself, whereas plot facts are reportable — but I'm not sure that distinction carries much weight, since historical and scientific facts are also the kind of thing one can in principle check for oneself, and I defer about those all the time. And I'm already half-deferring in my choices: if their verdict controls what I pick up, how different is that from taking their verdict as evidence about the work's quality?

So: is this policy defensible as it stands, a bias dressed up as a rule, or something I should restate? Under what conditions — about the people, the disagreement, the kind of aesthetic property, the stakes of the conversation — is it rational to use aesthetic testimony to guide decisions but not to ground the corresponding judgment? And is there a cleaner version of the policy that keeps whatever is good in it without the arbitrariness? I care more about whether the boundary does real work than about what the boundary is called.
