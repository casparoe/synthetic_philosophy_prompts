I've been trying to work out a broadly Hofstadterian view of the self — that a self is a pattern rather than a thing — and I've built an argument in its defense that now looks as though it proves too much. It runs like this.

(1) What makes a person the same self over time is the survival of a self-referential pattern: memories, dispositions, habits of thought, the running inner story — not the survival of any particular physical substrate.

(2) If being the same self just is the survival of that pattern, then any physical system that runs enough of the pattern is, to that degree, the same self. The view should not care whether the brain carrying the pattern is "mine" or "someone else's."

(3) "Enough of the pattern" has no sharp cutoff. Pattern similarity comes in degrees, and those degrees run continuously from a full continuation down to a faint echo: a close friend who has absorbed my mannerisms and can finish my sentences; a colleague who has internalized my views well enough to argue them on my behalf; a reader who studies a detailed diary of mine and then takes up my unfinished project.

(4) So, from (2) and (3), I am weakly present — to some small but nonzero degree — in each of those: in the friend, in the colleague, in the diary's reader, in anything that shares enough of my pattern.

(5) But identity is all-or-nothing and one-to-one. A self cannot be smeared across many minds at once; I cannot be more-or-less identical to someone.

(6) That cannot be the whole story. Since I refuse to give up (5) — I cannot make sense of ordinary practical life if "me" has no determinate boundaries — at least one of (1)–(3) must go: maybe (1) is false because selves need more than pattern survival; maybe (2) is false because running the same pattern is not being the same self; maybe (3) is false because there is a sharp line after all.

Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? I find (1) and (3) very hard to give up, and I worry that rejecting (2) collapses back into the view that only the original physical continuant counts — which is exactly what I was trying to escape. But if the argument is sound, I no longer know what it means to say that I survive at all. I want to know which step fails, and what I am really paying when I reject it.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — which step or premise gives way, and what giving it up costs — and then give your reasoning. No preamble and no restating of the question.
