The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem is usually summarized as follows: with three or more alternatives, any rule for aggregating unrestricted ordinal preferences that leaves every alternative a possible winner and makes sincere voting always a voter's best strategy must be dictatorial. The following five responses are sometimes offered as escapes or partial fixes: (1) breaking each multi-option decision into a sequence of yes/no votes; (2) restricting the admissible preference profiles to those single-peaked along one shared dimension; (3) randomizing, for instance by drawing a voter at random and adopting that voter's top choice; (4) switching to approval voting, where each voter approves a subset of the alternatives; and (5) requiring public deliberation and recorded votes so that insincere voting carries reputational costs. Rank these five responses from best to worst at genuinely removing the strategic-voting problem rather than merely shifting it into the agenda, the domain assumptions, the ballot format, or social pressure. Present your ranking as exactly five numbered items, one per response, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
