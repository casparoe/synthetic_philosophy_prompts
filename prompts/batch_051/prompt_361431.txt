I've been arguing with a friend in my animal-ethics reading group about whether insect welfare deserves a place near the top of the list of causes for people who care about reducing suffering. My friend takes the usual skeptical line: we simply do not know whether insects can suffer, and until we do, worrying about their welfare is speculation. I tried to turn the familiar scale argument into a clean, numbered argument to show that uncertainty is not a reason to set the issue aside, but now I suspect I have hidden a sleight of hand somewhere and cannot find it. Here is the version I wrote out after our discussion:

(1) At any given time there are on the order of 10^18 insects alive on Earth, and far more if one counts the many short generations that pass each year. (2) However uncertain one is about insect sentience, it would be dogmatic to assign a probability of exactly zero; a reasonable lower bound is something like 1% (or at least some small but non-negligible chance) to the hypothesis that insects have valenced experience and can suffer. (3) If insects are sentient, then a great many of them plausibly suffer intensely over their short lives — from predation, parasitism, starvation, desiccation, and the like — since most insects die violently or by attrition long before they reproduce. (4) Combining (1) and (2): at even a 1% chance of sentience, the expected number of sentient, suffering insect lives is about 10^16; if the 1% attaches to insects as a class rather than insect-by-insect, the expected value is the same, though the structure is different — a 1% chance of 10^18 suffering lives. (5) An expected-scale catastrophe of that size, whether it is a near-certain 10^16 or a 1% chance of 10^18, is exactly the kind of consideration that should reorder our priorities; refusing to act because the matter is only probabilistic would be like refusing to maintain a safety system because the disaster is only 1% likely. (6) Therefore, even if one believes insect sentience is somewhat unlikely, one should treat insect suffering as a major or dominant moral priority rather than parking it as speculative.
