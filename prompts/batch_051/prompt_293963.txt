I'm drafting a position paper on whether psychopaths know that what they do is wrong, and I've ended up with a set of commitments that I need stress-tested before I defend them as a package. Here they are:

1. Psychopaths are not morally ignorant in the ordinary sense. They can state the moral rules and classify a wide range of actions as right or wrong about as accurately as non-psychopaths. Their well-documented deficit looks affective — callousness, absence of guilt and empathy — rather than a failure to know the rules.

2. Moral knowledge does not require moral motivation. A person can know she shouldn't lie and lie anyway; akrasia is real, and it doesn't strip her of the knowledge that the act was wrong.

3. I treat moral knowledge as factual knowledge. Knowing that an act is wrong is a matter of getting a moral fact right, and I don't see why it should be more motivationally loaded than knowing that a bridge is unsafe.

4. Yet I can't bring myself to say that the psychopath who coldly and correctly calls a murder wrong knows that it is wrong. His accuracy feels like that of someone who can grade essays in a language he doesn't speak: the answers are right, but something essential to understanding is missing, and I wouldn't defer to him for moral insight.

5. The only way I know to make 4 stick is to say the missing thing is affective. Empathy and guilt aren't just motivational add-ons; they are part of what it takes to appreciate the force of a moral rule, so the psychopath's classifications are hollow: he can cite the rules but cannot feel their demand.

I'd like an audit rather than a lecture. Which of these are actually inconsistent with one another, which only appear to be, and how severe is each tension? For each real tension, tell me what would have to give and what the cheapest repair costs the rest of the view. Rank the tensions by severity, and tell me which single revision would do the most work.
