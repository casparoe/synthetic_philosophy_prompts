I am a PhD student working on practical reason and the structure of hard choices, and I would value a candid assessment of a distinction that has become central to my dissertation. I have been separating what I call metric incommensurability from deliberative incommensurability. Two options are metrically incommensurable, as I use the term, when there is no common cardinal measure in which their value can be represented without distortion — no shared unit, no stable exchange rate, and no numerical score that tracks what matters about both. Two options are deliberatively incommensurable when, even under full information and unlimited reflection, there is no rationally determinate answer to whether one is better than, worse than, or exactly as good as the other; the options are neither equally weighted nor ordered but left genuinely unordered by the space of reasons.

My concern is that discussions of hard choices slide too easily from the first claim to the second. It is plausible that friendship and money, or two careers, cannot be placed on a single scale. But I have come to suspect that nearly every alleged case of deliberative incommensurability is in fact a case of what I have begun calling "specification deficit." The alternatives are described at a level of abstraction at which the ranking-relevant features have been left out. Consider a choice between taking a job in one city and staying near an ailing parent, framed as career versus family. Framed that way, it looks hard. But once the options are specified the way the actual agent would specify them — the salary, the teaching and commuting load, the parent's needs, the texture of the relationship, the agent's tolerance for guilt and for long flights, what she would do with her evenings in each place — an ordinal ranking usually seems to appear. Full specification does not yield a cardinal common measure; it yields something weaker, a contextually grounded comparative judgment. My thesis is therefore that metric incommensurability is ubiquitous, while deliberative incommensurability among fully specified options is at most extremely rare, and that what makes hard choices feel hard is most often our refusal or inability to finish specifying the alternatives.

I would be grateful for an honest assessment. Is this a sound distinction, or does it collapse into a more familiar notion such as parity or incomparability? Am I recapitulating a known position — for instance, some form of the view that apparent incomparability is an artifact of incomplete descriptions — without seeing it? And where exactly does the thesis break? My own anxieties are that specification has no principled stopping point and that "fully specified" may quietly import the very comparative standards the view was supposed to earn, but I would rather have the case made sharply than keep circling through it.
