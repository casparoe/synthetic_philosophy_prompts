I'm currently in a seminar on the formal structure of value, and I'm writing my term paper on a question that keeps bothering me: how should distinct value-bearing considerations combine into a single overall verdict? We've contrasted three families of views. On an additive view, each unit of well-being simply sums across individuals, so a large enough total can in principle outweigh a single concentrated loss. On a multiplicative view, the value of a state is the product of factors—for example, hedonic quality scaled by consciousness or some other amplifier—so if either factor is near zero, the whole product collapses. On a lexical view, one type of consideration outranks another entirely: no amount of ordinary welfare can compensate for a violation of it, the way no number of B's can equal an A on a graded scale.

I want to bring this framework to Ursula K. Le Guin's short story "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" (1973). The story describes a city whose evident happiness depends on the abject misery of a single child kept in a locked room, and it dramatizes how the citizens respond once they learn this: most accept the bargain and get on with their lives, while a small number walk away into the unknown. The story is usually taught as a blunt rejection of adding costs and benefits across persons, but I would like a more careful reading than that. Where does each of the three structures—additive, multiplicative, lexical—get something right about the story's implicit moral arithmetic, and where does each misdescribe what Le Guin is actually doing? Is the text best read as asserting a lexical constraint against this kind of exploitation, as dramatizing the strange behavior of a multiplicative value function when one factor is driven near zero, or as something that resists clean formalization in any of these terms?

I would especially appreciate close attention to the texture of the story: the narrator's ironic tone while describing the festival, the moment when citizens are told how their happiness is sustained, and the deliberately unexplained choice of the ones who leave. My paper needs to end by saying what the story adds to the value-theoretic debate that a pure argument about aggregation would miss. I'm less interested in a summary of the plot than in an argument that does justice to both the formal models and the literary details, including the places where the story refuses to cooperate with the models.
