I have been trying to sort out my views about anachronism in the history of mathematics and science — when it is legitimate to describe past work using concepts the historical figures did not have, and when that distorts them. I have collected my verdicts on a set of cases, and I want to know whether there is one principle behind them, or whether some of my verdicts have to go. Please commit to a definite account rather than laying out the options.

Here are the cases as I find myself judging them.

(1) Eudoxus on proportion. When a mathematician says that Book V of Euclid's Elements, in its theory of ratios of magnitudes, essentially constructs something very like the real numbers, or anticipates the nineteenth-century treatment of irrationals, I think that is basically fair. It identifies the same dividing structure between the commensurable and incommensurable cases, and I would not want that observation banned from the history of mathematics. I object only when the writer then talks as if Eudoxus were a nineteenth-century analyst.

(2) Aristarchus and heliocentrism. When a textbook says Aristarchus of Samos anticipated Copernicus because he put the Sun at the center, I think that is wrong. A bare heliocentric proposal without a worked-out planetary theory, a dynamics, or a sustained research tradition is not the thing Copernicus did, and calling it an anticipation trades on a similarity that matters less than the differences.

(3) Newton's fluxions. When a modern presentation glosses Newton's fluxions as just limits, or rewrites his proofs in epsilon-delta form and calls the result Newton's proof, I find that misleading. It imports a later standard of rigor, and a later ontology of static quantities, into a theory about quantities generated continuously in time. But I have no problem with using modern calculus silently to check that Newton's results are correct; that strikes me as good scholarly hygiene, not anachronism in any objectionable sense.

(4) Archimedes' Method. When historians say that Archimedes, in the Method, balances a plane figure against known figures by treating it as though it were made of weighted lines and summing them, and that he is thereby using something recognizably like integration, I am inclined to accept it. It is not Cauchy's or Riemann's integral, but it solves the two standard problems we now call integration by a procedure that looks to me like a genuine ancestor rather than a chance resemblance.

I am aware that (3) and (4) look inconsistent: I resist saying Newton's fluxions are limits, but I do not resist saying Archimedes' quadratures are integration. I cannot currently say why the two should come apart, and that is one of the things I want the principle to do — or to tell me that one of these verdicts must be revised.

(5) Priestley and phlogiston. When a chemistry text says Priestley discovered oxygen but did not know it, I think that is false: one cannot discover a substance under a description one explicitly rejects. But I find the statement that phlogiston theory was, in effect, tracking the opposite of oxidation acceptable, and even explanatory — it captures why the theory was not simply a mistake. These two sub-verdicts feel hard to reconcile, since both speak about the same episode in later chemical vocabulary.

(6) Medieval impetus and inertia. When a writer says the medieval impetus theory, which held that a projectile carries an impressed force that keeps it moving, anticipated the principle of inertia, I think that is wrong. But I also think it is correct, and not damagingly anachronistic, to say that the theory's difficulties — especially what happens as the impetus is used up — helped make inertia thinkable. So retrospective description of a theory's problem situation is fine for me, while retrospective attribution of a later concept is not.

What I want from you: a precise principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible. For each clause of the principle, say which case or cases motivate it. Then apply the principle to two or three cases I have not listed (for example, whether saying the Greeks lacked the concept of zero is anachronistic, or whether Darwin's pangenesis — his hypothesis that every part of the body throws off tiny hereditary particles — anticipated genes), and state clearly what it predicts. Finally, and this is the part I most need, say which of my verdicts cannot be kept under the principle you endorse, and defend overturning exactly those rather than saving everything with a complicated patchwork. I would rather have a simpler principle that forces me to revise one or two verdicts than an epicycle-laden one that preserves them all.

Please take a definite position: pick one principle and argue for it, rather than leaving me with a menu of possibilities. End your answer with a final line beginning exactly "Verdict:" and then a one-sentence statement of the principle you recommend, as precisely as one sentence allows.
