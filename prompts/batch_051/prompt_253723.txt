I'm working on a long magazine piece about why physicists keep reaching for aesthetic words — "elegant," "natural," "beautiful" — when they talk about which theories to take seriously. One string theorist I interviewed kept saying that if a theory is ugly, that's a reason to bet against it even before any new data comes in; a philosopher of science I talked to the next week said that's basically astrology with better math. I honestly don't know where I stand, and I'm supposed to have an opinion by Friday. So: give me your credence in the following claim — when two theories in fundamental physics fit all the current data equally well, the more elegant one is more likely to be true (or at least closer to the truth), not just nicer to calculate with. For calibration, I'm at about 0.65 on that proposition; my editor, who trained as a physicist, is at about 0.15.

But don't just give me a number. Decompose it. I can see at least these moving parts: (i) whether "elegance" actually picks out a single stable property rather than a loose family of post-hoc hunches (symmetry, unification, fewer free parameters, mathematical naturalness); (ii) the actual historical record — among candidate theories that were genuinly on the table at the time, did the elegant ones get vindicated more often than the ugly ones, once you correct for the fact that physics mostly remembers its elegant survivors; (iii) whether there's any reason in principle elegance and truth should track each other, or whether it's all selection bias in what gets pursued, funded, and published; and (iv) whether elegance adds evidence on top of empirical fit or is just a tie-breaker for cases where data can't decide. Assign a rough probability to each, show how they combine into your overall credence, and tell me which one your final number is most sensitive to. Also say where you'd locate the disagreement between me at 0.65 and my editor at 0.15 — which of those moving parts are we probably actually disagreeing about? And one more thing: I need this for a piece, so keep clear about what's genuinely settled among people who work on this and what's still contested — please don't make a contested view sound like the consensus. If there's a particular piece of evidence or argument that would move your number the most, say what it is.
