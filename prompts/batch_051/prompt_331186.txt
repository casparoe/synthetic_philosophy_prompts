I'm a PhD student in cognitive science, working on computational approaches to mental representation. A postdoc in my department sent me the following paragraph from a review he's drafting, and asked what I thought. I'm not sure wether it's fair to computationalism, so I'd like a proper accuracy audit.

He writes:

'Computationalism is the view that the brain is literally a digital computer running a programme, and mental states are just states of that programme. Turing's imitation game is designed to show that if a machine produces the right verbal outputs, it thinks. The game deliberately brackets the body—skin, voice, hair length and so on—because Turing thought physical stuff is irrelevant to mentality. As he puts it in Computing Machinery and Intelligence, "The new problem has the advantage of drawing a fairly sharp line between the physical and the intellectual capacities of a man." So for a computationalist, a silicon machine and a brain are in exactly the same position provided they run the same programme. One obvious problem, which Turing himself admits, is that the nervous system is not a discrete-state machine. If the brain isn't a digital computer, then computationalism in its interesting form is false, which is why most people now retreat to a vague non-digital sense of "computation".'

I want to know which bits of this are accurate, which bits are subtle overstatements or conflations, and what Turing would object to. In particular, I can't tell whether the quote about a sharp line between physical and intellectual capacities actually supports the metaphysical claim that physical stuff is irrelevant, or whether Turing is making a much more modest methodological point. And does the nervous system point really damage computationalism, or is it an objection Turing already answers?

Please seperate what is right, what is a common simplification, and what is just wrong, and flag any places where the paragraph slides between different senses of 'computer' or 'computation'. Also, since I want to know how much to trust the overall verdict: say what would change your mind—which argument or consideration would lead you to a different assessment, and where your reading of Turing is most likely to fail.
