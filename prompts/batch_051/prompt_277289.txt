Take the absent-minded driver problem in its standard form. A driver plans to exit at the second of two successive highway exits. Taking the first exit yields payoff 0, taking the second yields payoff 4, and missing both yields payoff 1. The driver is absent-minded: whenever he reaches an exit, he cannot remember whether he has already passed the first one. The puzzle lies in the tension between the ex ante optimal plan—exit with probability one-third at each exit—and the reasoning available to the driver once he is actually at an exit, given that he cannot distinguish the two decision points.

Now modify the case as a counterfactual. Suppose the driver can write himself a note before starting. At each exit, the note is legible with a known probability; if legible, it tells him whether he is at the first or second exit, and if illegible, he faces exactly the original problem. He chooses the note’s content, but he cannot condition his later action on anything other than what the note says and whether it is legible. In addition, the driver has access to a commitment device: he can program the car to take the first exit, take the second exit, or randomize with a fixed probability, but overriding the device at an exit imposes a small cost.

Give a reasoned prediction of how these changes alter the decision-theoretic analysis and the driver’s best choice. Specifically: Does the availability of fallible external memory dissolve the dynamic inconsistency, or merely relocate it? Does the commitment device reduce the problem to an ordinary ex ante choice, or does the possibility of a costly override keep the interesting structure intact? Which features of the original absent-minded driver problem are robust to these modifications, and which are artifacts of perfect indistinguishability? Be explicit about where your argument is confident and where it depends on background commitments, such as whether the driver reasons as an evidential, causal, or functional decision theorist.
