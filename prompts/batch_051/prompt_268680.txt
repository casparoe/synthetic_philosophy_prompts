For several years I have been developing a view I call the unfinishedness criterion for judging whether a future would actually be good to live in. The central claim is that a life is good to live in proportion to how much engaging difficulty it contains, where difficulty counts as engaging only if it matters to you, you can act on it, and it cannot be fully and permanently solved. Solved problems go inert and stop drawing anything out of you; untouchable problems produce only passivity; the valuable middle is unfinished business. On this criterion, most utopias fail not because they are too comfortable but because they are too finished: they abolish scarcity, conflict, risk, and the genuine possibility of failure, and in doing so they remove the very surface a person needs in order to grip anything at all. A good future would deliberately maintain a renewable stock of unfinished business — mild scarcity, friction, reversible losses, goals that outrun you — while eliminating only the kinds of difficulty that produce helplessness rather than engagement. The view implies, for example, that a post-scarcity world that instantly satisfies every want would be bland rather than good, and that a materially rougher world stocked with solvable-but-unfinishable problems would be better to live in; it also implies that someone who has fully mastered a craft is living a worse life than a beginner still failing half the time, which I find half right and half disturbing.

I would like an honest assessment. Is this a coherent criterion, or have I merely redescribed a familiar position under a new name? What does it get right, where does it break, and what sort of life does it rank too high or too low?
