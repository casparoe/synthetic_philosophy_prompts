I'm taking a course on formal philosophy of science and we've just hit the problem of verisimilitude, and I'm already irritated at how quickly everyone jumps to their favourite model before saying what a model is even for. The starting point is Popper's idea that science can progress even though its theories are strictly false: Newtonian mechanics is false, but it is intuitively less wrong than a theory that gets all the same things right and then adds a load of false claims about planetary orbits; and if general relativity later turns out false too, we would still want to say it comes closer to the truth than Newton's theory does. On Popper's own definition, in terms of true consequences and false consequences, the thing famously falls apart — for two false theories the truth-content side and the falsity-content side pull in opposite directions, so the comparison collapses. Other approaches get into trouble over irrelevent additions, over what happens when you redescribe the same theory in different vocabulary, and over how much formal machinery (distances, similarity metrics) is actually needed.

So before I read another technical proposal, I want the spec. Write me the list of desiderata an adequate account of verisimilitude has to satisfy, with the case or consideration behind each. For each one, say whether it is non-negotiable or whether it is really just a preference of one camp, and flag any subsets of the list you think cannot be jointly satisfied — I have a hunch that 'non-trivial discrimination among false theories' and 'translation-invariance' are in tension for any purely logical account, but I want to know whether you agree and how deep the problem goes. Then, briefly, run a content-based account, a possible-worlds similarity account, and a relevant-consequences account through the list, and tell me which single desideratum knocks out the most candidates. Keep the scoring short; the criteria are the part I actually need. And if you could write the answer in British English, that would be ideal.
