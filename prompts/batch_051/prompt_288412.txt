I've been helping my niece with her spelling and it's gotten me stuck on something. She's a strong reader, but she keeps writting 'siz' for 'size' and 'nite' for 'night', and when I correct her she says she's just writing what she hears — which is hard to argue with. A friend who grew up in Spain told me Spanish spelling is basically just writing what you hear, and English spelling barely even tries. But then I noticed English spelling does something stranger than just being inconsistent: it will ignore the actual sounds to keep a word looking like its relatives. The 'g' in 'sign' is silent, but we keep it because it shows up in 'signature'; the 'b' in 'bomb' is silent, but it reappears in 'bombard'; 'electric' and 'electricity' share a 'c' even though one has a /k/ and the other an /s/. The plural marker is spelled the same whether it's pronounced /s/ in 'cats' or /z/ in 'dogs'. So English writing seems to be encoding something that isn't in the speech signal at all — morphemes, word families, something underneath the pronounciation. That looks like a quiet commitment to a view of what a writing system is for, not just noise, but I honestly can't tell whether it's a theory or just historical inertia.
