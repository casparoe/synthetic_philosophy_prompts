Hey — I teach an upper-level seminar on practical rationality (18 students, one 75-minute meeting), and I want to run a session on the dollar auction and escalation of commitment. The thing is, the students can all describe the phenomenon fine when they read about it; I want them to feel it happen to them. What I have in mind is actually running a dollar auction in class: I auction off a real five-dollar bill, with the rule that both the highest bidder and the second-highest bidder pay what they bid, but only the highest bidder gets the bill. The students shouldn't be told what concept this is about until afterwards — no lecture up front, no name for the thing.

Could you turn that into a proper runnable design? I need rules (increment size, whether bids must rise by fixed steps or any amount, what happens on a tie, a hard cap on losses so nobody is out more than, say, $3–4), a materials list, a timeline for the 75 minutes, and a small-stakes payoff scheme that keeps it real without hurting anyone. Also a debrief that maps what happens in the auction onto escalation of commitment and sunk costs: the moment the two top bidders are trapped, the moment bidding shifts from trying to win to trying to avoid a loss, the point where a simple rule would have said "stop." And tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it — people refusing to bid at all, someone opening at twenty dollars as a joke, two students colluding to split the payoff — and how to handle each without deflating the exercise.

Finally, be honest about what the classroom version can't capture. Real-world escalation has days or months between decisions, stakes that feel existential, and a growing pile of self-justifications that build up over time; a 75-minute public auction is going to be to compressed and too social to reproduce all that. I'd rather hear exactly what the facilitator has to supply than have the activity oversell itself.
