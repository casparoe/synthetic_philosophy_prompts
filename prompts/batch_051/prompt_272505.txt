I'm a retired chemist who's recently got into philosophy, and I've been reading around the Mīmāṃsā school and getting myself into a proper muddle. As I understand it, they claim the Veda is *apauruṣeya*, authorless — never composed by any person or god — and they treat this as making it *more* reliable, not less. The move seems to be that any authored report can be spoiled by its author's ignorance, bias, or lying, whereas an authorless text has no single point where the error could have occured. They also seem to accept something like *svataḥ prāmāṇya*: a report counts as valid by default until a specific defect is found, so you don't need a known author to vouch for it.

I'd like a clean working definition out of this, setting the theology aside. Could you put on your epistemologist-of-testimony hat and give me a definition of "testimony" — or, if you think "trustworthy source" is the better term, say so — that handles the cases below? Please start by brainstorming a few candidate definitions before picking one, then run it through each case and show me where it holds up and where it creaks. I don't need essays on each case, just enough to see the joints.

Cases:
(a) A friend tells me the next train platform after glancing at the departures board.
(b) A stranger at the station tells me the same thing from memory.
(c) An unsigned notice taped to the station door saying the lifts will be out of service next Tuesday.
(d) A lab notebook left on a bench, full of careful measurements, no name and no date.
(e) The Veda on the Mīmāṃsā picture — eternal, authorless, preserved by a meticulous oral tradition, but with no one to cross-examine.
(f) A sat-nav route recalculated by software.

My raw instincts are that (a) is clearly in and (f) clearly out, and I flip-flop on the rest; correct me if those instincts are part of the problem. I'm especially curious whether the Mīmāṃsā instinct — that losing the author can be an epistemic advantage — can be made sensible without going mystical, so give (c), (d) and (e) special attention.
