Carroll's Tortoise is standardly read as showing that the force of an inference cannot be supplied by adding a further premise. If the Tortoise accepts A and accepts A→B but still refuses B, offering him the further proposition (A∧(A→B))→B does no work: he now accepts three propositions rather than two, and has still been given no license to move from them to B. The usual moral is that rules of inference are not premises: a rule is something one applies to propositions, not another proposition one accepts.

Now consider a development that appears to erase that line. In a proof assistant based on the Curry–Howard correspondence, propositions are types, proofs are terms, and the modus ponens step is itself represented inside the object language by a function that, given a proof of P→Q and a proof of P, returns a proof of Q. The transition the Tortoise could never be supplied with is now an object with a name, a type, and a computational behavior; it can be passed around, referenced, and applied. Does Carroll's argument still go through, or has the premise/rule distinction collapsed into the single category of terms that can be both accepted and executed? If the regress is dissolved, say exactly which feature of the proof assistant dissolves it; if it merely relocates, say where it now bites — in the type-checking judgment, in the reduction relation, or in the meta-language in which one explains why executing a term is the right thing for an inquirer to do.
