I've been trying to figure out what a truth commission actually assumes, and I keep going in circles. Take the South African Truth and Reconciliation Commission after apartheid: the deal was that people who'd committed politically motivated crimes could get amnesty, but only if they came forward and gave full, public testimony about what they did. Victims and families got a public record and sometimes the chance to confront the perpetrator, but most of those people never went to prison. The same country's ordinary courts would punish a confessed murderer, yet a state hit-squad member could walk away if he told the whole story in public. I can't tell what view of justice, or punishment, or reconciliation that setup makes sense under. Is the idea that truth-telling itself does the moral work — that public acknowledgment is a form of accountability rather than a replacement for it? Is the transition to democracy valuable enough to buy with impunity? Or is it just a pragmatic confession bargain dressed up in ceremony? What I want unpacked is this: reconstruct the implicit theory behind the amnesty-for-full-disclosure arrangement, and say which rival views (retributivism, deterrence, a duty to prosecute) it quietly rules out and which it's actually compatible with. A hardline retributivist might still accept the same institution as a sad compromise, so I want you to seperate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Also, does the full-disclosure condition presuppose a different picture of truth and memory than the amnesty side presupposes about punishment, and do those two parts even hang together? Then say what would have to change about the design if you rebuilt it on a different assumption — for instance, that the point is maximizing the accuracy of the historical record, or deterring future perpetrators, or handing control to victims. And where is the whole thing philosophically underdetermined, such that several incompatible theories would still license exactly the same commission?
