Since retiring from a career in the natural sciences, I have been attending a reading group on Bayesian epistemology. At our most recent meeting, two members fell into a long argument about what the Bayesian convergence theorems actually establish regarding the "washing out" of prior probabilities. The session's reading had defended the familiar claim that, in the long run, shared evidence overwhelms differences in starting priors. The exchange afterward, as I recorded it in my notes with the moves numbered, went roughly as follows.

1. Marian defended the reading. The standard results, she said, show that two Bayesians who share a likelihood and whose priors assign positive probability to the same possible states of the world will have posterior distributions that grow arbitrarily close as evidence accumulates. If the model is correctly specified, she added, the posterior also concentrates on the true hypothesis. She concluded that the subjective element in prior choice, though real, is less serious than critics of Bayesianism maintain.

2. Paul answered that she was overreading the mathematics. The classic merging result requires the two priors to be mutually absolutely continuous: neither agent may assign zero probability to a hypothesis the other treats as possible. If the parties disagree about the model class, or if one assigns zero prior probability to the hypothesis that turns out true, the theorem's conditions fail and their posteriors need never merge. The result, he said, does not reconcile inquirers with genuinely different theories; it only reconciles inquirers whose disagreements are already confined to a shared framework.

3. Marian replied that Paul was leaning on boundary cases. In ordinary scientific controversy, she argued, disputants do share a model class and have smooth priors, so the theorem applies. She conceded that the result is asymptotic, but said this is no objection: no inferential procedure can promise agreement after any fixed finite quantity of data. What matters for the philosophical claim, she said, is the limiting behavior, and in the limit the data dominate.

4. Paul said this asymptotic reading smuggles the conclusion in, because the convergence is not uniform. For any preassigned finite sample size, one can find priors satisfying the theorem's hypotheses whose posteriors will with high probability still be far apart at that sample size; the time needed to become close depends on the chosen priors, and no bound holds uniformly over the class of admissible priors. Since every actual inference is finite-sample, "eventually" is compatible with the prior mattering enormously in every case that could actually confront us. He added that merging of opinions is not convergence to the truth: two inquirers can converge on the same false hypothesis if both priors concentrate inside a misspecified model. To get truth, one needs a further substantive condition, roughly that the true distribution receives positive prior weight in the relevant sense.

5. Marian answered that Paul was attacking a straw man. The washing-out claim, she said, was never about dogmatic or grossly misspecified agents; it is about non-dogmatic inquirers who share a model — the only setting in which convergence could be expected at all. In that setting, differences of prior opinion are transient rather than permanent. The fact that a theorem cannot certify that you share a model with your opponent is, she said, simply fallibilism, not a defect in the convergence results.

6. Paul replied that this concession gives away the main point. Once "reasonable prior" is defined to mean "prior that already agrees with its rival about what is possible and about the likelihood," the theorem only smooths over disagreements already confined to a common framework — much weaker than the usual slogan that priors wash out. And the positive-weight condition is more delicate than it looks: in nonparametric settings a prior can have the true density in its topological support and still lead to an inconsistent posterior, because convergence to truth requires the prior to charge neighborhoods of the truth in the Kullback–Leibler sense. Far from showing that priors do not matter, he concluded, the convergence theory shows exactly how much, and under what locally shared assumptions, priors stop mattering.

At that point the session ended. I came away convinced that part of the dispute is about which theorem is being invoked and part is about what the slogan "priors wash out" must mean if it is to answer the traditional worry about arbitrary priors, but I could not produce a precise breakdown.

Please adjudicate the exchange turn by turn. For each numbered move, say which claims are mathematically or philosophically sound, which are overstated, and where the two parties are talking past one another. In doing so, distinguish the merging-of-opinions result from the convergence-to-truth result and state their presuppositions; say whether either result licenses the conclusion that prior probabilities are epistemically unimportant; and give an overall verdict on who is more right and what each should concede.
