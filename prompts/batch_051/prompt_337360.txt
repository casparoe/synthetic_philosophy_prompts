I keep going in circles on whether Putnam's Twin Earth argument survives large language models, and I want someone to actually work it through with me rather than give the standard first-year summary. The usual setup: Oscar on Earth and Twin Oscar are identical down to the last neuron, but one uses water to talk about H2O and the other to talk about XYZ, so the reference differs even though what's inside their heads is the same. Putnam's punchline was that meaning aint in the head. That much I know.

Here's the wrench. We can now train a language model on nothing but text, no body, no perceptual contact with the wet stuff at all, and it uses the word water in ways that track our usage well. People sometimes take this as evidence that Putnam's inference was too quick: if a purely internal system can get that far, maybe the Twin Earth setup only showed something narrow about duplicate pairs, not about internalism in general. The obvious reply is that the model is just borrowing reference from its training corpus, the way a person who has never seen water can still mean H2O when they defer to chemists. I want you to sort out whether the argument survives this development, needs a patch, or is dead, and to say exactly which premise takes the hit. To make it sharper, think about a Twin Earth for models themselves: two identical architectures trained on Earth-produced text versus Twin-Earth-produced text. If their water tokens end up doing different semantic work even though the weights are identical, does that let an externalist restate Putnam's point without any biological duplicates at all? If it doesn't, why not?
