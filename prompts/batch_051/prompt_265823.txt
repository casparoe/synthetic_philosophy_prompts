I've been following the consequentialist cluelessness debate for a while now and I can't figure out why it doesn't move. One side says: take any ordinary choice—this charity or that one, this job or that one—and the long-run causal ripples are so huge and unpredictable that consequentialism simply can't tell you which act is right, so it fails as a decision procedure. The other side says that's just being bewitched by a standard of "knowing" the outcome nobody actually needs; you put weak probabilities over a wide spread of futures, do expected value with a sensible prior, and act. Both sides accept the same underlying fact that we're deeply ignorant about long-run consequences. Both agree some options are obviously worse than others. And still nobody changes their mind.

My working theory is that the two sides are answering different questions under one heading: one side is asking whether consequentialism can tell us, with justification, what to do; the other is asking whether we can reasonably act as consequentialists under deep uncertainty. On that reading it's not really a disagreement about epistemology—it's a disagreement about what a decision procedure has to deliver. But maybe I'm wrong and theres a genuine formal or empirical question buried in it, like how fast our probability distributions flatten out over long time horizons, or whether the tails dominate expected-value calculations.

Diagnose the disagreement. Is it verbal, substantive-but-hidden, or a clash of starting intuitions about what counts as "telling us what to do"? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress—a formal result about priors, a cleaner distinction between first-order and second-order uncertainty, a change of question—or should we expect a permanent stalemate because the two sides have different success conditions? I want the diagnosis, not a survey of the positions.
