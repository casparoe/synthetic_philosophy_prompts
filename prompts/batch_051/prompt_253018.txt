The student wrote:

The claim is that a true identity statement involving proper names, such as the claim that Hesperus is Phosphorus, is necessary but a posteriori. Kripke's central idea is that proper names are rigid designators: a name refers to the same individual in every possible world in which that individual exists. Because Hesperus and Phosphorus both rigidly designate Venus, the identity statement is true in every possible world, and that is what necessity amounts to. Yet the statement is not knowable a priori, since it was an empirical astronomical discovery that the evening star and the morning star are the same body; no amount of reflection on the meanings of the words would have revealed it. So we have a necessary truth that is known only a posteriori. This breaks the traditional pairing according to which all a priori truths are necessary and all a posteriori truths are contingent; Kripke shows the two distinctions cut across each other, so a priority and necessity have nothing to do with one another. The same structure applies to natural kind identities such as the claim that water is H2O: the identity is necessary because water is that compound in every possible world, but chemists discovered it empirically. The necessity in both cases rests on the fact that the two terms are synonymous, or at least have the same descriptive content; once one sees that the two terms pick out the same thing, the sentence is true in virtue of meaning alone. An objector might say that Hesperus could have turned out not to be Phosphorus, but Kripke replies that talk of what could have turned out tracks only our epistemic situation, not the metaphysical modal facts.
