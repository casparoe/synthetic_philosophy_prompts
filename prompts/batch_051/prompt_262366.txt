I am a second-year philosophy undergraduate currently taking a course on contemporary ethical theory, and I also help run a philosophy forum for students from other degree subjects — typically twelve to fifteen people, no background in academic ethics, meeting for one hour after dinner. In a fortnight I am meant to lead a session on the debate between moral generalism and particularism: very roughly, whether a feature that counts as a reason for an action in one case must count the same way wherever it appears, or whether its moral relevance can shift from case to case and must be judged in the whole context. I do not want to open with a lecture, and I would rather the group encounter the problem inside an activity and only learn the labels — holism of reasons, generalism, particularism — during the debrief.

Please design a runnable activity that makes them feel, first, that one and the same feature (for instance, that an action causes pain, or that it involves a lie) can count in favour of an act in one context and against it in another, and, second, that this context-sensitivity puts real pressure on the idea that morality proceeds by applying principles rather than by judging whole cases. Give me the setup, rules, and materials, with no real-money stakes; a timetable for the hour; and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the particularist challenge and the standard generalist replies, including what it costs each side to accommodate those moments. I would also like an explicit note on which part of the debate the exercise cannot reproduce and therefore has to be asserted by the facilitator, together with the most likely failure mode the first time I run it and how to handle it if it occurs — my worry is that participants will simply conclude that it depends on context and treat that as trivially compatible with having principles, so the design itself should force them past that. Please write the whole answer in British English.
