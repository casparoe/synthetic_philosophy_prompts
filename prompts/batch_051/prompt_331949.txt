An adversarial collaboration has been convened between two longstanding opponents over the status of patterns and the three stances. One side — call it pattern realism — holds that the patterns disclosed from the design stance and the intentional stance are real in the only sense that matters: they are objective enough, multiply realizable, noise-tolerant, and indispensable for compressing and predicting a world too complex to track in the full vocabulary of fundamental physics. The other side — call it physical-stance reductionism — holds that only regularities that can be stated and projected from the physical stance are genuinely real, and that design- and intentional-stance descriptions, however predictively useful, are instruments whose terms (goals, beliefs, functions) do not refer to anything over and above the physical facts. After a year of structured exchange, they have committed to producing a joint statement both can sign.

Draft that statement. To fix the stances: treat the physical stance as the strategy of predicting a system from its physical constitution and the laws that govern it, the design stance as the strategy of predicting it by ascribing functions, design, or purposes, and the intentional stance as the strategy of predicting it by treating it as a rational agent with beliefs, desires, and goals. The statement should contain:

1. A numbered agreed-claims section. These claims must be substantive commitments, not vacuous platitudes: they should say what all three stances are, what it takes for a regularity to count as a pattern, what compression and noise-tolerance do and do not establish, and what predictive success from a stance-relative description can and cannot show about what exists.

2. A disagreements table, with one row per live point of contest. For each row, give the precise formulation the pattern realist would insist on and the precise formulation the reductionist would insist on, each written so that its own side would sign it without wincing and the other side would accept it as a fair specification of the opposing position.

3. A cruxes section identifying the sub-questions that genuinely separate the camps — for example, whether the predictive success of intentional-stance descriptions could be recovered, without loss, in a sufficiently detailed physical-stance description; whether the noise-tolerance of a pattern is a fact about the world or a fact about the observer's interests and tolerances; and whether the direction of dependence runs from real patterns to the usefulness of the stances, or from the usefulness of the stances to our calling patterns real. Flag any point of apparent disagreement that turned out, in the collaboration, to be merely verbal, and say which side's vocabulary caused the confusion.

4. A tests-and-predictions section. For each genuine crux, state the concrete case, argument, or choice of description that both sides agree bears on it — draw on cases such as the chess-playing program, the thermostat, a center of gravity, a gene 'for' a trait, and the prediction of an agent's movements from attributed beliefs and desires — and record, for each side, what it predicts or what concession it has pre-committed to making if the crux breaks against it.

The resulting document should be the one a well-run adversarial collaboration on this topic would actually produce: formulations that are neither equivocal nor slanted, residual disagreement made exact rather than softened, and no bibliographic apparatus. The value is in the joint text itself, not in adjudicating the debate.
