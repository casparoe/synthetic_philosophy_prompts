When I read about the distant past, I follow a rule of thumb: I will accept as evidence only material produced around the time of the events – inscriptions, documents, coins, archaeological remains. Anything written afterwards, even by a careful ancient author a generation or two later, I treat as interpretation rather than as evidence, unless it can be confirmed by one of those contemporary sources. I apply this to Thucydides as much as to much later writers: where he reports events he did not witness and offers no contemporary document, I hold his narrative at arm's length. The rule has saved me from treating legend as history, but I worry that it may be a bias against testimony dressed up as a method. Is this policy defensible as a way of forming beliefs about the remote past, or am I simply demanding a kind of evidence that for most of antiquity will never exist? What sorts of historical knowledge does it silently rule out, and is there a more principled version that keeps its benefits without discarding later narratives altogether?
