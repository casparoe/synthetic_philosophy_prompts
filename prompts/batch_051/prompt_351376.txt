I am teaching a graduate seminar on rationality and the passions, and we have spent several weeks on the question of whether loss aversion and the endowment effect are cognitive errors to be debiased or, instead, reasonable ways of valuing positions one has already built one's life around. I would like the next assignment to be a case analysis rather than a survey, so the students are forced to do the mapping themselves.

Take the sulfur dioxide emissions market created by the 1990 amendments to the United States Clean Air Act as the episode. You may assume the salient history as follows: Congress capped total annual sulfur dioxide emissions from electric utilities and created a system of tradable allowances, each permitting the holder to emit one ton; the initial allocation gave the great majority of allowances to existing plants free of charge, proportional to their historical fuel use, rather than auctioning them. Many economists at the time favored auctions on efficiency and revenue grounds, and the allowances promptly acquired a substantial market value, while defenders of free allocation replied that it was necessary to secure passage and to cushion the transition for incumbent plants and coal-dependent regions. Analyze this episode through the lens of the endowment effect and loss aversion, understood as rival candidates—bias on the one hand, defensible preference on the other—for explaining both the political decision and the economic behavior that followed.

The analysis should run in both directions. In the first direction, apply the theory to the episode: identify who holds the endowment, what the relevant reference point is, what counts as gains and losses for utilities, ratepayers, workers in coal-dependent regions, and legislators, and what the theory implies each of those actors would do and should have done depending on whether one adopts the debiasing reading or the legitimacy reading. In the second direction, use the episode to test the theory: say which features of the grandfathering episode the endowment-effect interpretation genuinely explains, which are better explained by stranded investment, reliance on the old regulatory understanding, or ordinary distributive politics, and where the theory falls silent. In particular, address the standard economic point that, in a well-functioning market, the initial distribution of tradable allowances should not affect the final pattern of abatement, and say whether that point dissolves the bias-versus-preference question or merely relocates it. End with a considered judgment about what the episode shows about the underlying dispute: does it support treating the endowment effect as a bias, as a legitimate preference, or as a phenomenon whose correct classification depends on factors the theory itself does not supply?
