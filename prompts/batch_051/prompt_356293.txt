I spent my working life as a research physicist, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of mind and artificial intelligence in earnest. I have arrived at a set of views about the frame problem, and I would like an audit of whether they form a coherent package before I present them to a seminar.

My present commitments are these. (1) Intelligent behavior is computation over structured representations: a mind is, in the end, a physical system that manipulates symbols according to rules, and I take any account that denies this to be leaving the hard part unexplained. (2) The deep difficulty for such a system, the frame problem, is not making inferences but controlling relevance: from an indefinitely large store of standing information and its indefinitely many consequences, selecting the few items that bear on the present task while leaving the rest untouched by an update. (3) Relevance is holistic and interest-relative. Whether a fact matters depends on the agent's current goals, background knowledge, and situation all at once, and there is no small, formal criterion that tracks relevance, no finite set of context-independent frame axioms that would do the job. (4) The frame problem is therefore real, not an artifact of an obsolete style of modeling, and it is unsolved for any system whose competence consists in applying explicit rules to explicit stored beliefs. (5) At the same time, I believe that current large language models, which are given neither a declarative knowledge base nor explicit rules of inference, are in practice surprisingly good at ordinary judgments of relevance.

Please go through these commitments and tell me which ones genuinely pull against each other, which only appear to, and how severe each real tension is. I would like a ranked audit: say what would have to be revised or given up to restore consistency, and identify the single change that would make the package cohere most cheaply.
