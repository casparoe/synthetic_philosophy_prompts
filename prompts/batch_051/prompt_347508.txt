A familiar skeptical argument says that reports of eerie coincidences are exactly what chance predicts: the mind counts the hits — the friend who calls just as you think of her — and forgets the near-misses, and the birthday paradox shows that with enough opportunities even a very unlikely match becomes nearly certain. So, the argument concludes, a coincidence anecdote barely raises the probability of any non-chance explanation. But the argument was formed when the pool of opportunities lived mostly in fallible, salience-filtered memory. Now a large and growing share of a person's days is automatically timestamped and archived: calls, messages, email, photo streams, location traces, browsing histories, even dream journals. Does the coincidence debunking survive this change? Identify which premise is under pressure, whether the argument needs repair or is dead, and what a repaired version would claim about anecdotal evidence in a world of logged lives.
