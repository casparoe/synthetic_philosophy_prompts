I keep going in circles with a friend about the St. Petersburg paradox, and the recurring problem is that everyone's solution quietly changes which version of the game they're trying to resolve. He says it's just risk aversion and calls it a day; someone else says a finite bankroll makes the paradox disappear; a third person says the real issue only shows up under repeated play; I've noticed that log utility gives a finite price but seems to still let you pay a crazy amount for some truncated version. Before I read another so-called resolution, I want the full spec of what a resolution has to accomplish.

Write the desiderata that a theory of how to evaluate a risky gamble with heavy-tailed or unbounded payoffs must satisfy. Include the cases it has to get right: the original game with an infinite expected value, a finite/truncated version, a gamble with finite mean but infinite variance, a gamble where the expectation doesn't converge at all, one-shot vs. iterated play, and a player who is already rich vs. one for whom the stakes are material. Include the distinctions it can't collapse—e.g., between an infinite expected value and an undefined one, and between a normative account of how to choose and a descriptive account of how people actually choose. Also include the facts from ordinary finite-stakes gambling that it can't contradict, like stochastic dominance and the standard treatment of small finite gambles. For each desideratum, give the case that motivates it and say whether it's nonnegotiable or merely costly to drop. Then flag which single requirement does the most work in excluding the standard resolutions, and briefly score expected-value reasoning, log or bounded utility, finite-bankroll truncation, and cumulative prospect theory / rank-dependent weighting against the list. A table is fine.
