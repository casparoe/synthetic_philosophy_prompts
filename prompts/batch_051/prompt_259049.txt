i keep going back and forth on this. the factored-cognition picture of deliberation says there's nothing spooky about a judgment being made by one mind: any question can be broken into subquestions, each handled by a copy of me (or a cheap automatic stand-in for that copy) that only sees its little piece, and then the pieces get reassembled into an answer. the HCH setup—one human who can consult arbitrarily many copies of themselves, recursively, no single copy ever holding the whole problem in their head—is the clean idealization of this. i find the theory genuinely compelling: it's simple, it scales, it gives a story about how a pile of limited parts can be smarter than any one of them, and it dissolves the mysterianism about a self that deliberates. but i can't shake the intuition that when i actually deliberate about something hard—whether to change careers, or what's fair in a messy family dispute—there is a single subject holding the whole question, and the final verdict is mine in a way that a distributed computation's output isn't. maybe the intuition is just a user-illusion, like the little cursor on a screen. or maybe the theory is mistaking an idealization of deliberation for the thing itself. i'm not asking you to pick a side. tell me: what would it take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what would it take for the theory to be the thing that gets amended or abandoned, and is there a reading on which 'a single subject weighs the whole' and 'the deliberation is factored' are both true?
