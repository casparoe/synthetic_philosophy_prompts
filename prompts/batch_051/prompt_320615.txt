I'm on the membership comittee of a small Reformed church, and we're stuck on a situation that none of our materials from 1978 anticipated. The membership covenant every member signs says, and it hasn't been touched since it was written: "Members shall obey every command of God as set forth in Holy Scripture, and in all matters of faith and life shall conform to the counsel of the elders." A longtime member has recently started reporting that God is speaking to her directly — not audibly, she says, but in unmistakable impressions she has been recieving and confirming through circumstances. The specific command she now reports is that she should empty her savings and give them to a refugee resettlement nonprofit that the elders voted months ago not to support, and that she should do this even if the elders object, because a command of God outranks the counsel of men.

The drafters were plainly imagining commands like the Ten Commandments, not a member claiming fresh, private, extra-scriptural commands that cut against the session. But she is as sincere as anyone I've ever met, and she signed the same covenant we all did.

So here's what I need. Walking through the standard approaches — plain text, the purpose of the clause, what the 1978 drafters would have said if we could have asked them, and what a reasonable member reading the covenant today would take it to mean — is she violating it or following it? Which approach should govern if they diverge? And the philosophical thing that's been keeping me up: as soon as we try to decide whether this counts as a command of God, we seem to land right on the Euthyphro dilemma — do we obey because God commanded it, or do we first judge whether this is the sort of thing God would command before treating it as a command at all? Is there a reading of the covenant that doesn't collapse onto one of those two horns?
