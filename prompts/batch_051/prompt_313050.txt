Suppose Abelard were asked to assess the way modern criminal justice treats attempts, and to do so using the ethics for which he is remembered. His position is usually reconstructed as follows: sin lies in consent alone, not in the inclination that precedes it or the deed that follows it. The outward act adds nothing to guilt; an impulse not yet consented to is not yet sin; and a deed performed without the relevant consent is not sin either, whatever harm it causes. The deed matters only as an occasion for or indicator of consent.

Modern criminal law looks almost designed to contradict this. Completed offenses are punished more severely than attempts; attempt liability requires conduct, or at least a substantial step; and some offenses dispense with proof of culpable intention altogether.

This is a prediction exercise: work out, from Abelard's commitments, what he would probably think of these arrangements. Do not stop at "he would object." Address these questions specifically. (1) Does punishing attempts less than completed crimes commit the law to the view that the completed act adds moral wrong beyond the attempt, and would Abelard accept that? (2) What would he make of the requirement that there be an act at all, if the moral fact is already settled by consent? (3) What would he say about a completed harmful act done without the relevant consent — for instance, taking another's property while reasonably believing it is one's own — and what does that imply for strict-liability offenses? (4) If the deed is morally inert except as evidence of consent, is Abelard entitled to the evidential use of conduct that modern attempt law relies on? Then indicate which of your inferences from his texts you would assert with the most and the least confidence, and where a defender of modern criminal law could make the strongest reply.
