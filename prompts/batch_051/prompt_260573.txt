I keep noticing an asymmetry in how we judge ordinary trade and principled compromise. If two people with different tastes each give up something they value less for something they value more, we call it mutually beneficial exchange, and we do not think either has done anything worse than improve their position by their own standards. But if two people with different political or moral convictions make a structurally identical exchange — an environmentalist and a developer, say, agree that one will drop her opposition to a housing plan she cares little about while the other drops his opposition to a wetland rule he cares little about, each gaining what they value more — we, and often the parties themselves, reach for words like sellout, betrayal, and treating principles as if they had prices. Is there a principle that generates both the approval of the trade and the suspicion of the compromise, or is the asymmetry a mere inconsistency? I want the strongest candidate principle you can construct, tested against nearby cases: a legislative logroll, a charity accepting a donation from a source it condemns, a state swapping a human-rights concession for a security gain. Then give me an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up.
