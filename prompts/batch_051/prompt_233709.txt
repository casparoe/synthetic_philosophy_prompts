One of the members of the metaphysics reading group I attend has been arguing that the debate about fundamentality is largely settled by the image that gave the area its vocabulary: reality, he claims, is like a building. The fundamental facts are the foundation; everything derivative is an upper floor; and the supporting relation is metaphysical grounding, understood as the non-causal dependence by which one fact obtains in virtue of another. Since a building can stand only if its load is ultimately taken up by something that does not itself need further structural support, he concludes that every chain of ground must terminate in something ungrounded — that is, that grounding is well-founded. On his view, any metaphysics on which grounding could descend without end, or on which every object decomposes into proper parts all the way down with no bottom level, is as absurd as a tower floating unsupported. He therefore takes well-foundedness to be not one metaphysical hypothesis among others but a requirement built into the concept of grounding, and he treats views such as metaphysical infinitism (every grounded fact has a further ground) and gunky ontology (every object has proper parts, so there are no mereological atoms) as not live options.

I suspect the building is doing more work than it is licensed to do. Please audit the analogy for exactly this argumentative use. First, provide a correspondence table: what plays the role of the foundation, the upper floors, the load, the soil beneath, the architect, and any other elements worth including; then say where the mapping breaks. I would like the disanalogies separated into those that matter for the conclusion and those that do not. For example, a building's foundation is a spatial part of the building, located below the rest and made of the same material as it, whereas fundamental facts are not below anything and an upper floor is not a less real part of reality; grounding is an explanatory relation among facts, not a physical prop. Indicate which of these differences actually undermine the inference to well-foundedness, and which are merely incidental. Finally, judge whether the conclusion — that grounding chains must terminate and that gunk is impossible — survives once the picture is set aside, and propose a better analogy for fundamentality if one is available.
