I'm an advanced undergraduate in a small upper-level seminar on Scanlon-style contractualism, and we've spent most of the term on the aggregation problem: when, if ever, do many people's lesser interests outweigh one person's more serious interest? The professor teaches almost entirely through cases, ruling firmly on each and making us extract whatever principle is doing the work. By now the class keeps a running list of her settled rulings, and three of them look as though they ought to settle everything:

(1) Rescue. One stranger is drowning on one side of a river and five strangers on the other; only one side can be reached in time. She says: save the five.
(2) Broadcast. A technician is pinned by equipment in a transmitter room during the final fifteen minutes of a World Cup match, in severe but stable pain that will not worsen if he waits. Freeing him immediately requires cutting power, which would black out the broadcast for millions of viewers. She says: cut the power; the viewers' aggregate enjoyment does not justify leaving him in severe pain.
(3) Clinic. A small clinic has a single surgical team free today. One patient needs surgery for a fractured femur now; without it, she will have a permanent limp. Thirty other patients need treatment for acute migraines; without it, each faces a full day of severe pain. She says: operate on the femur patient.

Now the professor has posed, but not yet answered, a new case. A different clinic has one operating room available for a single slot. One patient can have the use of her dominant hand saved; otherwise the loss of use is permanent. Five other patients can each have the use of one finger saved; otherwise each permanently loses that finger's use. The alternatives are exclusive: it is either one hand or five fingers, and all six patients are strangers with no relevant differences in age, health, or urgency beyond the injuries described.

I want to predict her verdict. Walk me through the candidate rules that fit the three recorded rulings, what each rule predicts for the hand-or-fingers case, which of the prior rulings is doing the most work in her overall pattern (and whether it only seems to settle the new case), and your best guess about how she would rule, with the reason it fits her track record better than the alternatives. Then give one further case you would put to her next that would most cleanly distinguish the leading rules, and say what each leading rule predicts about it. No readings or citations; I only need the case-by-case reasoning.

End with a final line of the form \answer{predicted verdict; best-fitting rule}, using short labels—for example, \answer{five fingers; same-kind aggregation}.
