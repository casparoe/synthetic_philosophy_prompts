In a paper I am drafting on causation in the law, I keep returning to a blunt asymmetry in ordinary causal talk. Suppose a sluice is open, the river is rising fast, and a town downstream will flood unless the sluice is closed within the next hour. Two people separately notice this, and each can close the sluice by pulling a lever in a few seconds and at no risk. One is the keeper, employed by the river board to close it in exactly these conditions; the other is a hiker who happens to be passing. Both do nothing, and the town floods. Competent speakers say without strain that the keeper's omission caused the deaths; they strain to say the hiker's omission caused them, and many refuse outright. Yet the counterfactual structure is identical: if either had pulled the lever, the deaths would not have occurred. The only apparent difference between the two cases is positional. The law tends to reproduce the same verdict by pointing to the keeper's duty.

I want a principled account of whether this asymmetry can be justified as a matter of causation, rather than as moral judgment leaking into causal description. Is there a defensible theory of causation by omission that places the keeper's inaction in the causal history of the deaths while excluding the hiker's? Or does the appearance of a causal difference simply track a prior distinction between breach of duty and failure of good-heartedness? If the latter, should we accept that causal language is morally loaded here, or should we reform the language and say the hiker's omission caused the deaths exactly as much as the keeper's? The strongest answer will articulate the best available rationale for the asymmetry, test it against deliberately varied cases—an off-duty keeper, a hiker who has publicly promised to watch the sluice that afternoon, a keeper who has been hired but has not yet begun his shift—and close with a definite verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, revised, or abandoned. Please answer in British English.
