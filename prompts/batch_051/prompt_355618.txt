In my philosophy class, two classmates argued after our discussion of forgiveness, and I could not tell who was right at each step. Here is roughly how the exchange went.

(1) Ana said forgiveness can never be obligatory, because it is essentially a gift: if a person could be required to forgive, the act would stop being forgiveness and become a policy of excusing. (2) Ben replied that this is too absolute for minor wrongs — when someone has sincerely apologized, repaired the harm, and changed their behavior, continuing to withhold forgiveness looks like a vice, so forgiving after full repentance seems not just optional but owed. (3) Ana answered that "looks like a vice" and "is owed" are different: a person who refuses forgiveness in such a case may be cold or spiteful, but we can criticize them as ungenerous without saying they failed a duty, otherwise we erase the difference between the decent minimum and what is only admirable. (4) Ben then asked how her view handles unconditional forgiveness, the kind given to someone who has not repented at all. If people are praised for that, he said, forgiveness cannot really be conditioned on the wrongdoer's response. (5) Ana replied that unconditional forgiveness is either impossible or collapses into deciding the wrong no longer matters: to forgive, you must still judge the deed to have been wrong, but an unrepentant wrongdoer has given you nothing that could justify releasing them. (6) Ben answered that she was confusing the wrongdoer's deservingness with the victim's letting go of resentment; a victim can release resentment even when the wrongdoer does not deserve release, because carrying resentment hurts the victim.

Please go through their exchange point by point, say who is right about each specific claim, and identify anything important that both of them are missing.
