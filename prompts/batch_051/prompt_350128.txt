I'm compiling a list of arguments for the conclusion that genuine moral dilemmas are impossible, where a genuine dilemma is not just a hard or tragic choice but a situation in which two incompatible courses of action are each all-things-considered obligatory, so that whatever the agent does she violates some requirement that remains in force. Here is what I have so far.

1. The agglomeration argument. If the agent all-things-considered ought to do A and all-things-considered ought to do B, then by agglomeration she ought to do both A and B. In a dilemma, A and B cannot both be done. Ought implies can rules out obligations to do the impossible. Therefore at least one of the original oughts was not, after all, an all-things-considered obligation, and the conflict was only apparent.

2. The deontic-logic argument. On the standard semantics for the logic of obligation, obligation and permissibility are related so that if an action is obligatory, refraining from that action is not obligatory. Conflicting requirements are thereby excluded not as a substantive moral discovery but as a matter of the logic of requirement itself. Reports of genuine dilemmas are therefore pressure on the formalization only if we have independent reason to prefer a weaker deontic logic, which the skeptic of dilemmas denies.

3. The Ross-style argument from prima facie duties. Conflict is real and ubiquitous at the level of prima facie duties, which can pull in opposite directions. But a genuine dilemma would have to be a conflict among all-things-considered duties. Deliberation weighs the competing prima facie duties and issues in at most one all-things-considered duty in any given situation. What gets reported as a genuine dilemma is a case in which the prima facie duties are very closely balanced or the correct ranking is obscure, not a case in which two final duties coexist.

4. The action-guidance argument. Part of the point of moral requirements is that they guide action. Two incompatible all-things-considered requirements cannot jointly guide: the agent cannot act on both, and a theory that issues both gives her no answer to the practical question of what to do. Since the inability to settle the practical question is a failure at the very function that talk of obligation serves, a correct account of moral requirement will be structured so as never to generate such pairs.

5. The remainder argument. The residual guilt, regret, or felt need to make amends in supposed dilemma cases is the strongest phenomenological evidence for dilemmas, but it can be fully explained without positing a second, continuing obligation. Regret is an apt response to an unchosen good that was lost or to one's causal role in its loss. These remainders track harms and values, not necessarily wrongs, so the distinctive evidence for dilemmas can be explained away without admitting a violated obligation.

6. The reasons-first argument. An action is all-things-considered obligatory when the balance of reasons decisively favors it. Two incompatible actions cannot each be decisively favored by the balance of reasons, since the balance of reasons is a single ranking of the available options. Difficult choices are therefore better explained by incommensurable values, incomplete information, or very close reasons than by the coexistence of two final obligations.

I suspect this list already contains the standard moves, so I am asking you to go beyond it. Give me one more argument for the conclusion that genuine moral dilemmas are impossible that is genuinely distinct from these six. By distinct I mean: not a rewording of any entry, not a special case or application of one of them, and not a recombination of two or more of them. In particular, do not give an argument that rests on the agglomeration principle, on ought-implies-can, or on the formal semantics of deontic logic, since those moves are already in the list. The argument can be one you recall from the literature, or an original construction if you cannot recall a further argument, but it must be one you are willing to defend. After stating it, assess it honestly: say exactly what work it does that the six arguments above do not do, whether it actually establishes the impossibility of genuine dilemmas or only something weaker, and the strongest reply a defender of genuine dilemmas could make to it.
