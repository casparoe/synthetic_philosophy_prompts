I'm drafting a short position memo on algorithmic pricing and competition law, and before I circulate it I want a consistency check on the following five claims. (1) Collusion, as competition law should understand it, requires communication, agreement, or at least intent to coordinate, so purely tacit parallelism should remain legal. (2) I nonetheless expect that a market served by independent reinforcement-learning pricing algorithms, left running long enough, will often settle at supracompetitive prices even though no firm programmed or intended that outcome. (3) I think that algorithmic price drift is not legal collusion in the sense of (1), but it is still a serious policy problem regulators should try to prevent. (4) I think the best prevention tool is transparency: firms should be required to disclose their pricing algorithms so regulators can detect coordinated behavior. (5) I think competition policy should ultimately be evaluated by consumer-welfare outcomes rather than by the mental states or internal processes of firms or their software. Please audit this set: identify which pairs of commitments are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, rank the genuine tensions by severity, say what the cheapest revision would be for each, and state which single change would do the most to make the whole set cohere.
