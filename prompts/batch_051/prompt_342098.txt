I am a third-year undergraduate in a course on impartiality and moral reasoning, and we have spent the past two weeks on the golden rule: the positive form, on which I should treat others as I would want to be treated; the negative form, on which I should not do to others what I would not want done to me; and a family of attempts to repair the rule when it misfires—when others' preferences differ from mine, when the relationship is asymmetric in power, when the issue turns on an omission, or when doing what someone wants conflicts with treating them with respect. I write for our department's student philosophy blog, and I want to publish a diagnostic quiz that sorts respondents into the version of the golden rule their case judgments actually commit them to, whether or not they recognize the label. I would like you to produce the quiz and its scoring key for me.

The quiz should contain eight to ten multiple-choice questions. Each question should open with a short, ordinary scenario and offer two or three responses, each phrased as a concrete thing to do; neither the question nor the responses should name any philosophical position or use theory vocabulary such as projection, universalizability, preference, autonomy, or side constraints. The questions must be answerable by someone who has never encountered these distinctions, and no question should ask respondents which formulation sounds most attractive or whether the golden rule is correct. The scoring key should map patterns of answers onto at least these positions: a literal directive version, on which the agent's own preferences are the standard; a negative-only version, on which the rule only forbids; an imaginative role-reversal version, on which you ask what you would want if you had the other person's situation and history; an autonomy-respecting version, on which you defer to what the other person actually wants within firm moral limits; a universalizability reading, on which the rule functions as a rough test of acting only on policies you could will everyone to follow; and a deflationary position, on which no single formulation survives the cases and the rule should be retired. If you add leaves beyond these, each should represent a stable combination of answers rather than merely a familiar label.

Please include at least the following cases, chosen to stress different joints in the logical space: someone who genuinely wants to be publicly insulted as part of a comedy roast; a person in distress who refuses help that the respondent would certainly want in their place; a bystander case that turns on whether staying passive counts as a violation; a supervisor deciding how much of a hard truth to tell someone who has explicitly asked not to know; a case about holding someone accountable for wrongdoing; and a paternalistic case involving a child or a temporarily impaired adult. At least one question should force a choice between deferring to the other person's stated wishes and doing what you would want if you were in their circumstances, since that is the split I suspect most people have not noticed in themselves. In addition to the quiz and key, provide a short map of the logical space: which questions are meant to separate which positions, which answer combinations no leaf occupies, and for each empty combination whether it is empty because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any two positions your questions cannot separate and state what additional case would be needed to pull them apart. The intended readers are undergraduates with some ethics coursework but no special background in these distinctions, so the scenarios should be familiar and the responses natural. Keep the whole deliverable to roughly 1,000 words, and make the key simple enough that a reader could score it by hand in under two minutes.
