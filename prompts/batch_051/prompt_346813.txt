I have noticed a parallel that I keep turning over, and I would like a careful expert in both the theory of AI training and moral philosophy to tell me whether it is deep or merely verbal.

In the supervision of AI systems, there is a live dispute between process-based and outcome-based training signals. Outcome-based methods assign credit or blame on the basis of the result actually produced, even where the very same line of reasoning would have produced a different result had the sampled continuation, the environment, or the evaluator's random seed differed. Process-based methods instead reward the quality of the reasoning or decision procedure itself, irrespective of how matters happen to fall out. This looks to me structurally identical to two familiar distinctions in ethics and criminal law: the distinction between assessing an agent's deliberation or intentions and assessing the consequences of what she does, and the law's distinction between attempts and completed offences. Outcome-based supervision appears to reproduce the problem of resultant moral luck, while process-based supervision looks like an engineering answer to the control principle — that an agent may properly be assessed only for what lies within its control.

Is this a genuine identity of structure, or am I pattern-matching on the words 'luck' and 'control'? Please state the mapping as precisely as you can, say what would have to be true on both sides for results to transfer, and identify at least one prediction the parallel makes that turns out false or strained. Where transfer is legitimate, tell me which ideas from the moral luck literature should actually change how a machine-learning researcher designs supervision, and which should not. Start by brainstorming candidate mappings before settling on one, and think carefully about the respects in which the two sides fail to line up. Please write the answer in British English.
