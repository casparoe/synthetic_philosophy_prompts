Three weeks from now I present a paper at a mainline divinity school's systematic theology and philosophy colloquium defending a specific reading of the *teleological suspension of the ethical* in *Fear and Trembling*. The thesis I defend: the suspension is not best understood as a valid moral obligation being overridden for religious reasons, but as the suspension of the universal's standing as the final tribunal before which action must be justified. Abraham's trial, on this reading, does not show that he is licensed to violate the ethical; it shows that a normative relation — the individual's absolute responsibility to God — cannot be made intelligible to, or judged by, the ethical universal. The book is not claiming that faith trumps morality, but that the universal is not the highest court.

The argument I give runs in three moves. First, I argue that de Silentio's contrast between Abraham and the tragic hero is built to make exactly this point: the tragic hero remains inside the ethical because what he does can still be expressed and judged in universal terms, whereas Abraham cannot be mediated. So the relevant question is not whether Abraham has a permission the universal grants, but whether the universal can frame every normative relation at all. Second, I argue that the *teleological* in *teleological suspension* is load-bearing: the suspension is for the sake of a higher telos, and that is what keeps it from collapsing into aesthetic caprice or demonic defiance — but it also means the defensibility of the whole notion hangs on whether such a telos can be real. Third, I argue that the discussion of Abraham's silence in the book is not rhetorical decoration: it is the phenomenology of an agent whose justification cannot be shared, and the book treats that incommunicability as a cost to be insisted on, not a defect to be papered over.

The audience is not the one I would have picked. The regulars include two Kantian moral philosophers who will hear any suspension of universalizability as special pleading, a Hegelian social ethicist who was trained to read the book as an individualist protest against *Sittlichkeit* and considers that protest philosophically regressive, a Lutheran systematic theologian shaped by Barth who is sympathetic to something like divine command but suspects my account makes God a bounded competitor with the ethical rather than its ground, and a Levinas-influenced ethicist who thinks Abraham's silence toward Isaac is the scandal of the book and will not let me aestheticize it. There are also several philosophically trained agnostics who will push for crisp definitions of *the universal*, the telos, and the exact normative status of Abraham's act.
