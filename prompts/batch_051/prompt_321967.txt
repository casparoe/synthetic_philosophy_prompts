You are a philosopher of social science who works on social ontology, the metaphysics of kinds, and the way social categories are made and debated. I want to work out what I actually think about whether generational categories are real, not by reading more theory but by reverse-engineering the principle behind my own verdicts on cases. Please think carefully and, before you settle on an answer, brainstorm at least two or three candidate principles: at least one that would save nearly all my verdicts at the cost of being complicated, and at least one that would be simple but would force me to overturn something.

My verdicts, in the order they come to me, are these. The Baby Boomers feel unambiguously real: there was a visible postwar surge in birth rates, a shared cultural ascendancy, and the cohort was treated as a generation almost from the start. The Millennials also feel real, but I am less sure why, since the usual boundary years for them look like the invention of survey houses rather than any natural break; still, they do seem to share formative experiences, above all the arrival of the internet at a particular point in their lives and the 2008 crash at a particular career stage. Generation X I go back and forth on: the label began as media and popular culture, and the cohort is largely defined by falling between two bigger groups, yet by now it does seem to pick out a real cultural shape; I lean toward saying it is real only retrospectively, though I am not sure what that means. The cuspers trouble me most: people born in the late 1970s and early 1980s, sometimes called Xennials, had an unusually distinctive experience, an analog childhood and a digital adulthood, remembering life before the internet with eyes old enough to notice the change. My gut says they are real, perhaps more real than Generation X, even though almost no institution counts them as a generation. But when I consider Generation Jones, the supposed younger half of the Boomers, I want to say it is not real; it feels like a marketing label bolted on after the fact, even though people who fall there genuinely identify with it, and early Boomers and late Boomers really did grow up in different political climates. The Lost Generation of the 1920s also feels real to me, but real in the way a self-conscious literary circle is real, not in the way a demographic cohort is real. And I suspect Gen Z is real but is currently being overused as a synonym for teenagers, and that its boundaries will look different once its members are forty.

I can already see the strain in these verdicts. Xennials earn their reality by having a sharp shared experience and almost nothing else; Generation Jones loses its reality despite having both a shared experience and people who identify with it. Millennials keep their reality despite boundaries that look arbitrary, while the arbitrariness of Generation Jones's boundaries is one reason I want to dismiss it. Is there one principle about what makes a generation real that captures most or all of these verdicts? If there is, state it precisely, say which of my cases motivates each clause of it, and say what it predicts about the cohort now being called Generation Alpha, whatever its uncertain boundaries are, and about a person born a year to one side of a conventional boundary who recognizes themselves in neither side. If there is not, tell me which of my verdicts the best available principle forces me to drop, and why it forces those rather than the others.

Please answer in continuous prose, as if you were writing me a careful email or letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
