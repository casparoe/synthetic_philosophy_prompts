I am a retired neuroscientist and former clinician, now reading philosophy of emotion, and next month I will present to my former hospital's clinical ethics seminar. My thesis is that when a terminal diagnosis makes intense grief over an anticipated loss highly probable, it is ethically permissible—and in some cases advisable—to offer a safe, voluntary pharmacological attenuation of the felt component of grief, provided the patient retains accurate beliefs about the loss, can still act on what matters, and gives informed consent. My argument, in outline, is that grief's value is instrumental: it orients attention, communicates need, and motivates parting actions and care. Each of those functions can often be served with a duller felt component; what is thereby removed is hedonic, and while that loss counts against intervention, it does not by itself make it wrong. The audience will be palliative care physicians, bereavement counselors, hospital chaplains, and one philosopher of emotion. Please prepare my question period: the objections this audience will actually raise, in their own vocabulary, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is weakest—and include the question I should most hope nobody asks. Please write the answer in American English.
