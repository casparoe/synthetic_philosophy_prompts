People used to talk as though there's a hard constraint on making sense of a black-box system: the thing doing the interpreting has to be more transparent than the thing it's interpreting. Otherwise you've just traded one mystery for another—you'd have to interpret the interpreter, and then the interpreter's interpreter, and the chain never bottoms out. On this view, AI can't really be what interprets AI; at most you can use tools, but the actual understanding has to come from a human or from a formal model small enough to hold in your head.

But modern interpretability does almost exactly what this argument says can't work. Sparse autoencoders are trained on a model's activations to pull out features, and people now routinely use LLMs to draft labels and explanations for what those features or individual neurons seem to do. The interpreter in that setup is itself a big, opaque network—often bigger and less understood then the thing it's studying. And it sort of works? It produces features that look genuinely meaningful, circuits, steering vectors that do roughly what the label says they'll do.

So I'm trying to figure out whether the old argument survives this development. Is the claim that the interpreter must be more legible than the target simply false, or just too strong, with some repaired version that still says something real? Or is the argument basically right, and the apparent success is a sign that 'understanding a network' means something different than people think? I care about what happens to the argument, not about whether current interpretability methods are good.
