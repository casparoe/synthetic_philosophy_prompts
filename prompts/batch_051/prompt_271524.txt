Longtime LessWrong reader here, so I've internalized the reflex that "you can't pull yourself up by your own bootstraps" is a conversation-ender about self-trust, but I've never actually seen it disambiguated. There's a thread on epistemology of calibration where someone argues: you can't establish that vision is reliable by going out and looking at more stuff, because that's using vision to check vision; and you can't point to humanity's millenia of successful navigation, because your only evidence that the navigation went well is itself perceptual. He finishes every reply with the bootstrap line and a shrug, like that settles it. I'm pretty sure the sentence is hiding several different objections: bare premise-circularity, rule-circularity, using a source before it's been independently credentialed, using a source as its own only test, maybe even the demand that a system never update its own priors on the basis of its own outputs. Some of those are genuinely fatal, some are just how every instrument gets calibrated, and one or two seem false. Please lay out the distinct precise principles that one-liner could be expressing, from the trivial to the radical, say which are defensible and which aren't, and then tell me which readings would actually support refusing to trust perception (or ordinary reasoning) from its track record, and which wouldn't. I also want to know whether the strongest anti-bootstrapping reading quietly rules out the easy knowledge we clearly have from our senses, or just blocks one argument for it. And since I'd rather not turn into the guy with a wrong theory and a clean argument, tell me where your verdict is most likely to fail: what argument or example would make you change your mind about the reading you end up endorsing.
