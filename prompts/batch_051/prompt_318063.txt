I teach philosophy of science, and over the years I've settled into a rule for reading empirical papers, and now I'm not sure whether it's a decent heuristic or just a bias with tenure. The rule: whenever a paper reports a pooled effect and someone points out that the effect flips inside subgroups — a Simpson's-paradox reversal, an interaction, whatever you want to call it — I stick with the pooled estimate and treat the subgroup flip as probably dredged up. My reasoning is that the pooled estimate uses all the data, subgroup cuts are underpowered and easy to fish, and if the subgroups were the real story the authors should have pre-registered them. I've seen to many seminar audiences get led down a garden path by an unreported subgroup split to take those reversals at face value. But last week a statistician friend pushed back hard. She said this is exactly the disposition Simpson's paradox punishes: the pooled estimate can be the confounded one, and the subgroup pattern can be the causally relevant one. She brought up the Berkeley graduate admissions case, where the aggregate looked like sex discrimination and the department-by-department data didn't. I don't think she's confused, and I know her field has reasons to take the within-group structure seriously; I also suspect her profession has an incentive to make every headline effect disappear into a subtlety.

So the question: is my habit — prefer the pooled estimate unless the subgroup analysis is pre-registered — defensible as a general policy for someone in my position (a philosopher, not a statistician, reading applied work on a deadline), or is it a bias dressed up as a rule? If it isn't defensible as stated, is there a repaired version that keeps the protection against post-hoc subgroup fishing without blinding me to real Simpson reversals? And I'd like you to say what would change your mind about the verdict — which argument or feature of the case would push you the other way, or where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
