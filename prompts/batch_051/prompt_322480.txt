I'm putting together a seminar on fakes and forgeries, and the standard line — a forgery is wrong because it deceives the buyer — looks to quick to me, since the value of an original seems to matter even when no one is deceived. I want to use Borges's "Pierre Menard, Author of the Quixote" as the test case. In the story, Menard doesn't copy an existing work and pass it off as someone else's; he sets out to write, under his own name, a few chapters that wind up coinciding word for word with chapters of Cervantes's Don Quixote, by reconstructing the conditions under which the original was produced. The narrator then says the identical words are different, and richer, when read as Menard's. I'd like a reading of the story through the philosophy of originals and forgeries. Is Menard's text a forgery, an original, or neither, once you take provenance, achievement, and intention as candidate sources of an original's value? Does the story undercut the idea that an original's value lies in its authentic history, or does it sharpen that idea by showing that a thing's history can change what the thing is while the surface stays fixed? And where does the fable cheat or stack the deck — for example, by running together the meaning of a literary text with the value of a material object? I want the reading to take both the story and the philosophy seriously, not just use Borges as an illustration.
