I'm running a small reading group on AI alignment — about 14 people, grad students plus a couple of working engineers — and we have 75 minutes to spend on why the AI-safety-via-debate idea isnt the free lunch it sounds like. In the abstract the proposal feels airtight: two AI systems argue opposite sides of a factual question, a human judge picks the better argument, and the systems get trained so the judge keeps picking the side thats actually true. The objection everyone reaches for is that the judge might not understand the topic. What they don't feel viscerally is the obfuscated-arguments problem: a debater who is smarter than the judge, or just willing to be sneaky, can pile on jargon, fake precision, and a buried error deep enough that the judge ends up more confident in the wrong answer, and the procedure gives no way to tell whether the judge is being informed or snowed.

I want a runnable classroom game that makes that failure happen to them instead of being explained to them. The asymmetry should come from the rules, the incentives, or the way points are scored, not from me debriefing heroically afterwards. What I'm picturing: rotating triads (two sides, one judge), a few simple logic or probability puzzles with a clean right answer and a tempting wrong answer, and a secret role card that tells one side to argue honestly and the other to defend the wrong answer using only stated premises and misleading emphasis — no flat-out lying. Include a points scheme where the obfuscator can spend points to buy extra moves that add no information but cost the judge effort to check, so verbosity becomes a weapon rather than a bug. Give me the roles, materials (cards, timers, score sheets), a timeline for the 75 minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the real proposal — judge confidence, the gap between sounding rigorous and being right, why a training signal based on judge agreement might actively select for obfuscation.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'm expecting someone to win by sheer volume and then the class to decide the game was rigged), and be honest about which part of the real proposal a one-off classroom exercise cant reproduce — for instance, whatever it would be like for the obfuscator to be genuinely more capable than the judge in every domain at once. And if you can, tack on two or three worked example puzzles with their honest and obfuscating one-page briefs so I can just print them.
