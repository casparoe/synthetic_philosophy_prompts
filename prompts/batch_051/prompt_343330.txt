I've wandered into the following cluster of views and I can't tell whether it's coherent. Here it is. (1) The rightness of an action is settled by an impartial criterion: an act is right just when an informed, impartial observer would endorse it, and the agent's own attachments carry no independent justificatory weight except insofar as impartial considerations license them. (2) Williams's example of the husband who rescues his drowning wife moves me: the husband who pulls her from the water without calculating, and who can only say that she is his wife, has not acted worse than one who first judges that this is a case in which it is permissible to save one's wife; if anything he is the better agent, and the further justificatory thought is one thought too many. (3) I deny that a criterion of rightness is refuted merely by being self-effacing — a standard can be true even if it tells agents not to try to apply it in the heat of deliberation. (4) But I also can't shake the conviction that a normative theory whose best agents would, by its own lights, be worse for trying to apply it is defective in a way that counts against the theory, rather than being a mere fact about our cognitive limitations. (5) I reassure myself with the thought that the husband can offer a retrospective impartial vindication — for instance, the impartial optimality of a rule permitting limited partiality toward intimates — and that this retrospective availability of an impartial justification is enough to make the act right, even though no such justification figured in his deliberation.

Do these five commitments form a consistent package? I want an audit, not a lecture. Trace which pairs put genuine pressure on each other and which are only apparently in tension; rank the genuine tensions by severity; and for each, say what would have to give and what it costs to give it.
