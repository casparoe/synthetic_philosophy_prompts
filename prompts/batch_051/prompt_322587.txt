I can't stop turning over a parallel, and I want to know if it's real or just cute. The classic sphex story: a wasp stings a cricket to paralyze it, drags it to the mouth of her burrow, leaves it there, goes inside to check the burrow, comes back out, and then pulls the cricket in. If you nudge the cricket an inch away while she's inside, she won't just pull it in from the new spot; she hauls it back to the threshold and runs the whole burrow-check again. Move it again and she loops, over and over, without noticing the check already passed. Fine. But last week I fixed a bug in our order-processing script that did almost exactly the same thing: every time the reply-status row was NULL it re-sent the customer email, because the script didn't "remember" the email had already gone out, it just re-ran the check and re-triggered the action. I fixed it by adding a flag, and afterward I had the uncomfortable thought that a lot of what I do on autopilot--checking the same pocket for my keys, re-reading a sent message, running a morning checklist in the same order even when it makes no sense--is just a taller stack of the same loops, with more flags and interrupts. But maybe the ability to notice the loop and step outside it isn't just "one more interrupt"; maybe that's exactly the thing free will is supposed to name. So is the parallel between the wasp's rigidity and my script (and my habits) deep or superficial? What would have to be true of both sides for the comparison to actually transfer, and what does it predict that turns out false once you pin it down? I'm especially curious whether "having a representation of why the check exists" is doing real philosophical work here, or whether I'm just flattering myself.
