I'm auditing a metaethics seminar and one of the grad students keeps boiling expressivism down to the line "saying 'stealing is wrong' is just booing stealing." Last week he used that line to shut down a question I actually wanted answered. Someone asked how, on an expressivist picture, a simple argument like this could still be valid:

(P1) If stealing is wrong, then getting your little brother to steal is wrong.
(P2) Stealing is wrong.
(C) So getting your little brother to steal is wrong.

He replied that moral sentences aren't truth-apt, so moral "arguments" aren't real arguments and we shouldn't expect them to obey logic. I'm sure that little slogan is doing alot of work, but I don't know exactly what it claims.

Please act as a professional metaethicist and think this through carefully. Start by brainstorming a range of distinct, precise claims the one-liner could be expressing — from the anodyne (uttering the sentence conventionally signals disapproval, or expresses a motive the speaker has) to the radical (moral sentences have no truth conditions at all, or there are no moral facts to report). For each reading, tell me (a) whether it's true, false, or merely controversial, and (b) whether it actually licenses his move from "not truth-apt" to "moral arguments can't be valid." Then run each reading through the Frege-Geach problem: say which readings can explain how the same moral sentence keeps its contribution when embedded in a conditional or under negation, and which readings quietly give out at that point. I don't want the one right reading — I want a menu of readings with verdicts, and a clear statement of which ones would actually support his dismissal. Because right now I suspect the slogan is doing more in the room than its entitled to.
