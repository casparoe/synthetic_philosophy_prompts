I have spent a considerable amount of time reading the moral debate over the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, and it has an unusual shape. Among historically literate participants, a great deal of the record is common ground: the condition of Japan's leadership by the summer of 1945, the existence of a planned invasion and its anticipated costs, the standing alternatives of blockade and continued firebombing, the timing of the Soviet entry into the Pacific War, and the sequence from Hiroshima to Nagasaki to surrender. The disagreement is not primarily about these facts but about what they license. One side treats the bombs as a tragic but defensible act of necessity: the war was ended sooner and with fewer net deaths than the realistic alternatives, and a state is entitled to choose the least bad among genuinely awful options. The other side treats them as a moral failure rather than a forced choice: the bombs deliberately targeted civilian populations, the second bomb was dropped three days after the first and before the Japanese leadership had time to fully reassess its position, and the range of alternatives was wider than the necessity framing lets on.

What puzzles me is less the disagreement itself than its stability. I have seen the dispute persist even when the historical facts are stipulated and the contested counterfactuals are held fixed for the sake of argument; the participants then relocate the disagreement to a point of principle rather than changing their minds. My working hypothesis is that the surface historical debate is downstream of a pair of unannounced differences: one about the temporal standpoint from which an act of war should be judged, ex ante as a choice under uncertainty versus ex post with the benefit of what we now know, and one about the baseline against which lives saved and lives lost are being counted. But I can imagine several other diagnoses: that the term justified is being used equivocally, that the hidden disagreement is specifically about whether the second bomb requires an independent justification rather than inheriting the first's, or that the two sides hold bedrock intuitions about the moral weight of enemy civilian lives that no historical finding can move.

I am asking for a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than a verdict on the bombings. What kind of disagreement is this: a verbal one, a hidden factual or counterfactual one, a difference in baselines, a difference in moral theory, or a difference in which cases are being treated as the disputed item? Which of these does the actual argument give the strongest evidence for? And given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress: a distinction that sharpens the dispute, a finding that would settle part of it, a more precise question, or an argument for why the disagreement should be expected to remain permanently unresolved. Please answer in American English.
