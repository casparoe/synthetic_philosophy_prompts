I've come around to a view about love that I used to find to romantic, and now I think is right: love isn't a response to value that's already there in the beloved. When I love someone, I'm not primarily detecting their wonderful qualities and getting pulled in by them; the loving itself confers the value, or at least makes their qualities count in a way they wouldn't otherwise. Call it the bestowal view, as against the appraisal view, on which love is a response to antecedently existing value, the way admiration is a response to excellence.

What I'd like you to do is tell me what would count against the bestowal view. Not just the obvious "so love is arbitrary?" objection, but the full map of its exposure: which intuitions, possible cases, and results in nearby areas (philosophy of emotion, the ethics of partiality, moral psychology) does it stand or fall with? Things I keep chewing on: we can usually say what we love in someone, and are expected to when a friend asks "what do you see in him?"; people sometimes stop loving when the beloved changes, and sometimes don't stop loving when the beloved gets much worse; falling in love feels like something that happens to you rather than something you do; parental love seems to show up before there's much of a self to appraise. For each of these, say whether a committed bestowal theorist would concede that it counts against the view, or whether they'd dismiss it as already presupposing the appraisal view.

I'd also like to know whether the view has been quietly insulated against refutation — for example by saying the bestowal is always guided by latent appraisals, or by treating every quality the lover cites as something love made salient rather than found — and if it has, whether that's a problem for a view of this kind, or whether demanding falsifiability here is just the wrong demand. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be honest about whether the strongest objections just are the appraisal view sneaking back in. I'd personally find it most persuasive if you could point to a kind of case the bestowal view cannot describe at all, rather than one it merely describes awkwardly.

When you're done, list the three defeaters you'd weight most heavily in order, and on the very last line put the top one in \answer{...}, in six words or fewer.
