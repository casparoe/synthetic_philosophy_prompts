I keep going back and forth on this, but right now I basically hold that pre-AGI bargains—deals made before AGI exists about post-AGI outcomes—are close to worthless. The core of the view is that no pre-AGI party has any way to enforce a bargain against an agent that is, by the time performance is due, much smarter and more powerful than them, and there's no third party around to enforce it either. So these bargains are ceremonial at best, or cheap signalling.

I want a serious map of what would count against this. What would have to be true for a pre-AGI bargain to actually have teeth? For each failure point in my view—enforceability, incentives of the post-AGI party, stability across time, whatever else you see—say how it could fail, what the world would look like if it did, and whether that escape route is live or just logically possible. I can imagine candidate mechanisms like escrow or collateral that some third party really controls, cryptographic commitment devices, constitutional or treaty arrangements with built-in enforcement, the AGI caring about its reputation as a counterparty, or pre-commitments working as Schelling points that make later coordination easier. Which of these are real and which are cope? Sort the defeaters from decisive to merely awkward.

Also go after whether I've insulated the view. Is this claim at risk of being unfalsifiable—like, every time a pre-AGI bargain seems to work I can say it was never actually binding, and every time it fails I can say the setup was bad? If I have insulated it, is that a problem for a view of this kind, or is that just the nature of a claim about a one-off event like AGI? And are there actual existing cases—constitutional provisions, treaty regimes, long-shot corporate commitments, intergenerational deals—that already show pre-power-shift bargains sometimes do hold?
