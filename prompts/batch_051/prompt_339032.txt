Here's a case that's been bugging me. The standard argument for expected utility theory says a rational agent should maximize expected value, which implies there's a well-defined price you should be willing to pay for any gamble. But take the Pasadena game: a fair coin is flipped until it comes up heads, and if the first heads is on flip n you receive (−1)^(n−1) · 2ⁿ/n dollars (assume your utility is linear in money). The expected payoff works out to the alternating harmonic series, which converges to ln 2 when you add the terms in their natural order — but only conditionally, so reordering the terms lets you make the sum come out to any number at all, or diverge to ±∞. So the expected value isn't really well-defined. Does the argument for expected utility maximization survive this, or does it need repair? I want to know exactly which premise the game puts pressure on, whether the right response is to say the game has no well-defined fair price while expected utility theory stays fine for everything else, or to say the theory was only ever an idealization and doesn't have to cover pathological gambles. Also tell me where your own analysis is most likely to fail, and what argument would change your mind about whether this is a genuine problem for expected utility theory rather than a curiosity.
