I've been trying to pin down a working definition of the line Keynes was getting at when he insisted that not all probabilities are numerically measurable — that is, the line between cases where a numerical probability is genuinely warranted and cases where putting a number on it is just spurious precision. My gut verdicts are these:

(1) A fair roulette wheel, betting on red: 18/38 is warranted, no caveats.
(2) A biased coin with unknown bias, before any flips: 1/2 feels warranted by symmetry, though I'm less comfortable than in the roulette case.
(3) The same coin after 200 flips with 117 heads: roughly 0.585 is warranted, and I'm more comfortable with it then before.
(4) A particular unstable atom decaying in the next hour, given a well measured half-life: the probability is warranted.
(5) A 30% chance of rain tomorrow from a model well calibrated on 10,000 similar days: warranted. The same 30% from a stranger at a bus stop with no track record: not warranted, even though the number is the same.
(6) The chance that a particular country's government falls this year: no precise number is warranted, whatever the pundits say.
(7) The chance that Goldbach's conjecture is true: I don't know what 70% would even mean.

Is there one principled distinction behind all this, or am I really tracking two separate ones — one about when probabilities are meaningful at all, and one about how much weight or confidence should attach to a number? Give me the working definition (or definitions), run them against these cases, say which of my verdicts they can't accommodate, and tell me what they predict about the case I keep flip-flopping on: a venture capitalist saying there's a 27% chance a particular first-time founder's startup becomes a unicorn.
