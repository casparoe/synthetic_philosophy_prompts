I recently joined the ethics committee of a teaching hospital as a non-clinician, and I have noticed a pattern in my own reasoning that I want to understand. When a physician tells me something empirical—say, that a drug has a given probability of restoring a patient's consciousness—I update my beliefs and move on without hesitation. But when the committee's ethicist tells me something moral—that continuing ventilation would be wrong, or that a proposed triage rule is unfair—I feel that I ought to reach the conclusion myself, even if I end up agreeing. I am trying to determine whether this asymmetry is defensible or just moral vanity.

Please explain the strongest version of the view that moral testimony is importantly different from non-moral testimony. Do not settle for the platitude that one should not outsource one's conscience. Drill down into the question of what exactly goes wrong when I treat another person's moral verdict as a proxy for my own. Is the defect fundamentally epistemic—for instance, that moral testimony cannot transmit moral knowledge or understanding the way medical testimony transmits factual knowledge—or is it a separate moral or practical failing, such as a failure of autonomy or an objectionable form of deference? In the course of your answer, confront the following difficulty: even to select and trust a moral testifier—to judge that someone is likely to be right rather than self-interested, dogmatic, or unreliable—I seem to need to exercise my own moral judgment. Explain how a defender of the asymmetry can answer this selection problem without either giving up on the possibility of ordinary moral judgment or conceding that moral testimony is no more problematic than testimony about medicine. Finally, say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would be most likely to lead you to reverse your verdict, and where in your reasoning you are least confident.
