I am taking a course on value theory, and we recently read Aristotle’s Nicomachean Ethics, book 1, chapter 5. The passage that set off our discussion was this one:

The Life of Money-making is a constrained kind of life, and clearly wealth is not the Good we are in search of, for it is only good as being useful, a means to something else.

Someone in my discussion group then offered the following argument, which seems to prove too much, but I cannot locate exactly where it slips.

(1) If wealth is only good as useful, then the goodness of any amount of wealth is exhausted by the goodness of the things it buys or brings about.
(2) Abundance, or prosperity, is just a matter of having more of this merely instrumental good.
(3) So abundance cannot have a kind of value that smaller holdings of wealth lack; at most, it is a more effective instrument for obtaining the same good things.
(4) Therefore, prosperity has no moral worth beyond what it buys.
(5) But then a society that is generally prosperous is not better in any respect than a society that is equally just, free, and happy but much poorer—which seems implausible.

Where does the argument go wrong? I would like to keep Aristotle’s claim about wealth if I can, but I want to know which premise or step is weakest and what a defender of Aristotle would have to give up by rejecting it.
