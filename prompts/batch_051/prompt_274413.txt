You are a philosopher of science who works on the rationality of scientific change, and who knows both Thomas Kuhn's The Structure of Scientific Revolutions and Karl Popper's falsificationism in detail. Think carefully, and begin by identifying the two claims and the apparent conflict before you evaluate candidate reconciliations.

In Thomas Kuhn's The Structure of Scientific Revolutions, there are two well-known stretches of argument that seem to pull in opposite directions. In the chapters on the nature and resolution of revolutions, Kuhn argues that the shift from one paradigm to another cannot be made a step at a time, forced by logic and neutral experience; it is more like a gestalt switch, and the transfer of allegiance from one paradigm to another is a conversion experience that cannot be forced. He also says that choosing between competing paradigms is like choosing between incompatible modes of community life. But in his account of crisis and of how new paradigms come to be accepted, he says scientists do not renounce an old paradigm lightly: they need an alternative that seems to resolve some outstanding and generally recognized problem in a way the old paradigm cannot, and over time a new paradigm earns allegiance because it solves puzzles and opens up new research. Later, in the chapter on progress through revolutions, he even suggests that the community's eventual verdict is reasonable because the new paradigm has demonstrated problem-solving advantages.

On their face, those two descriptions conflict: the first says revolutionary theory-choice is not settled by proof, neutral rules, or stepwise argument; the second says it is constrained by recognizable problem-solving achievements and can be retrospectively well-founded. Are these claims genuinely inconsistent? Construct the strongest readings on which they are compatible. Consider at least: the distinction between what moves an individual scientist at the moment of crisis and what explains the community's verdict over time; the difference between being rationally justified and being psychologically or causally explained; the possibility that Kuhn is describing different stages of the same process rather than one act of decision; and the possibility that Kuhn thinks good reasons operate only after a new paradigm has already been built up, not before it exists. Then assess each reconciliation honestly: which reading fits the text best, and which one requires special pleading or dissolves Kuhn's claims into triviality? Finally, bring Popper into the verdict: would Popper regard Kuhn's account of conversion as the unacceptable abandonment of rational criticism, or can Kuhn's two claims be read as a rival account of scientific rationality rather than a denial of it?
