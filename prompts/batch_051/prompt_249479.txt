I'm a retired physical chemist who's only recently started reading philosophy of mathematics, so apologies if this is a known debate. I've noticed I have a standing epistemic policy I never consciously chose. If someone proves "there exists an x such that P(x)" by assuming "for all x, not P(x)" and deriving a contradiction, I'll accept that the universe cant be without such an x, and I'll even use the result downstream in a calculation. But I wont say I know there exists an x in the sense I mean when I say I know there's a molecule with a certain geometry because I've synthesized it, or computed a structure that had it as a solution. The policy only bites for infinite or nonconstructive existence claims; for finite decidable cases I'm perfectly happy with excluded middle and proof by contradiction. My philosopher friends say this is incoherent: if the axioms are true and the logic is valid, the existence claim follows, full stop. Others say I've just re-invented Brouwer-ish constructivism and should admit it's a whole metaphysics rather than a policy. What I want to know is whether the habit is defensible as an epistemic policy, or just a residue of lab life that I should drop. And if it is defensible, is there a modest restatement something like "nonconstructive existence proofs warrant only consistency claims, not the kind of existence I use in explanations" that keeps the caution without me having to go all the way to intuitionism?
