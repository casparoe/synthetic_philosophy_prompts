I'm in a philosophy of math course and we've been reading Lakatos's Proofs and Refutations, and something keeps bugging me. Math journals wouldn't publish something as a theorem just because it's checked out for millions of examples, and a single counterexample to a proof gets treated as a refutation (or at least a hole that needs patching). So the officialy story seems to be: mathematics isn't empirical, and only proof counts. But the actual practice looks a lot messier: the four-color theorem was accepted largely because a computer exhaustively checked a huge number of cases, some proofs are so long that almost nobody has read them end to end, and people doing "experimental mathematics" use simulations to find patterns that get treated as real mathematical evidence even though they aren't proof. What view of mathematical knowledge does this mixed practice actually presuppose? Does it commit mathematics to being quasi-empirical in Lakatos's sense, or is proof a special category that can survive all this? And if mathematics really is quasi-empirical, what would have to change about how papers are refereed or about why the four-color theorem counts? Also, please tell me where the shakiest part of your reasoning is, and what consideration would make you flip your answer.
