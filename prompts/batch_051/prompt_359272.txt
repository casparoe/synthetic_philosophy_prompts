I've been trying to pin down when I count two forms as the same word and when I count them as two different words, and I can't find a rule that fits my own gut. Here are my verdicts.

cat/cats: same word, different forms. run/ran: same word. go/went: same word, even though they share no sounds. mouse/mice: same word. So sameness clearly isn't a matter of letters or sounds being alike.

But cook in 'she cooks' and cook in 'the cook left' feel like two different words that happen to be the same string. Open in 'open the door' and open in 'the door is open' feel like two different words too. Whereas red in 'a red apple' and red in 'the red of the apple' feels more like one word being used in two roles. I'll admit the cook pair is doing my head in.

Run/runner: different words; runner isn't a form of run. But strong/stronger: same word, stronger is still a form of strong, even though the -er ending looks the same as the one in runner. leaf/leaves is the same word; leaflet is a different word. happy/unhappy feels like a different word even though it's just a prefix. good/better is the same word despite sharing nothing; good/goodness are different. strong/strength are different.

Is there one criterion that gets all of these (or nearly all), and which of my verdicts does it force me to drop? Concretely: why do cat, went, mice, better and stronger count as forms of a single word while runner, leaflet, unhappy, goodness and strength count as seperate words? And what should I say about actress (is that a form of actor or a separate word?), or building in 'the building was tall' versus building in 'they were building a wall'? Also, am I wrong to treat cook the verb and cook the noun as two words but red the adjective and red the noun as one?
