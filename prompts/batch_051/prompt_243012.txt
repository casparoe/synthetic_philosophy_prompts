At a party last weekend I got dragged into a long argument between two friends, Sam and Maya, about whether a world with more people is better, all else equal. They eventually made me promise to get a third opinion. So here's the question: please adjudicate their exchange, move by move. Here's how the core of it went:

(1) Sam: Compare two futures that are identical except that in one a cheap infertility cure leads to four billion extra people by 2100, all living lives as good as everyone else's. That future is better. It has more wellbeing in it. More lives worth living, all else equal, is good—that's the kernel of truth in pronatalism.

(2) Maya: Better for whom? Every person in the bigger future is exactly as well off as they'd be in the smaller one, and the extra people aren't deprived of anything in the smaller world, because they never exist. A betterness with no beneficiary is just a number, not an ethical fact.

(3) Sam: That test can't handle the decisions that actually matter. Climate policy, immigration policy, family policy—they change which people get born. If outcomes can be better or worse only for the same particular people, then almost no major policy is better or worse than another. That's too high a price.

(4) Maya: You're overreading my view. I don't require the same people in both outcomes. I can compare how well each actual person's life goes in each outcome and require that at least one actual person is better off and none worse. In your population-boom case, that test says the two futures are equally good, because no actual person is better off. My view isn't paralyzed; it just doesn't count extra lives as automatically good.

(5) Sam: But now consider a public-health program that causes an extra million children to be born and live decent lives, without making anyone else worse off. On my view, clearly good. On yours it flunks too: no actual person is better off than in the no-program world, and the extra people can't be "helped" because they never existed there. So you have to call a harmless program that creates good lives morally on a par with doing nothing. That's a cost you should feel, not just dodge.

(6) Maya: That's a real cost, but yours is worse. Your view has to say a world with an astronomical number of people whose lives are just barely worth living—no pain, but nothing much else—is better than a world with a modest population living excellent lives, if only the number is big enough. You can't just acecpt that as a weird implication; it means your view treats quality as fully exchangeable for quantity.

(7) Sam: I do accept it, but it only sounds bad because the scenario is built to sound bad. A life that's barely worth living is still worth living, so each extra one is a genuine good to the person living it. What you're expressing is mostly distaste for a crowded, mediocre world, not an argument.

They went around in circles after that, but the above is the core. Please go through it move by move: for each move, say whether it's clearly right, clearly wrong, or somewhere in between and why. Then say who is more right overall, and what both of them are missing.
