I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I design choice architecture for a consumer product: default privacy settings, auto-renewal checkboxes, consent flows, and the ordering of options in menus. In design reviews I keep hearing a principled-sounding defense of these nudges: the claim is that a default is not manipulative, and not objectionably paternalistic, as long as people can easily opt out, the nudge is disclosed, and an empirical test is available — namely, whether the person, once the nudge is pointed out to them, still endorses the choice they were steered into.

I want to know whether that claim can actually be tested, and what a test would and would not establish. Treat the claim as being about autonomy and manipulation: that disclosure plus subsequent endorsement is enough to show that a nudge respected the user's autonomy rather than manipulated them. Something feels off to me from an engineering standpoint, because I can think of mechanisms that would produce the same observed result — the user could rationalize a status quo they were nudged into, or go along with the default to avoid the effort of switching, or be anchored more firmly by the disclosure itself — while still having been steered in a way I would hesitate to call autonomy-respecting.
