You're a moral philosopher who works at the boundary between descriptive moral psychology and normative ethics. Take your time, and maybe start by sketching the candidate principles this looks like before you commit, even the ones you'll end up rejecting.

For the last few months I've been collecting my own snap moral verdicts, trying to figure out whether there's one principle underneath them. (1) My aunt knitted me a sweater I find hideous; I told her I loved it. Fine, maybe even required, and I dont even file it under "lie." (2) A friend runs a small side business and habitually pays his suppliers sixty days late because he knows they're too small to chase him. Wrong — he's taking money that isn't his, and the fact that the invoices are technically lawful doesn't blunt it. (3) An engineer at a car company quietly reports, first internally and then to a regulator, that his team's software cheats emissions tests. Right thing to do, full stop; I don't think he owed his coworkers silence, since they were the ones doing wrong. (4) But when a different friend found out that a mutual friend's wife was having an affair and told the husband, I couldn't call him wrong, yet it sits badly with me in a way the engineer case doesn't. (5) Two adult siblings, separated at birth, meet in their thirties, and begin a fully consensual sexual relationship using two forms of contraception; no one else ever finds out. I think it's wrong, and I cant produce a harm that survives the stipulations. (6) At a protest someone burns a flag from a country that has done horrific things; no one is in danger. I don't think it's wrong and I'd defend the right to do it, but it feels different from burning a scrap of cloth in a way I can't articulate.

I've been struck by how my verdicts cluster into what I've seen psychologists call moral foundations — care/harm, fairness/cheating, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, purity/degradation, maybe liberty/oppression — and by the fact that my left-leaning friends share most of my verdicts (they're even more relaxed about the flag), while my conservative relatives weigh loyalty and purity much more heavily. So two questions. First: is there a single principle, or a small organized set of them, that captures all of these verdicts, and if not, which of them would a principled account force me to give up? Be explicit about which case motivates which clause, and tell me what your preferred account says about a seventh case: someone who waits until a parent is on their deathbed to report the parent's decades-old tax fraud. Second, the bigger one: if it's true that left and right systematically weight these clusters differently, does that tell us anything about who's actually right, or is it just a fact about our psychologies with no normative upshot? I'd rather hear that a couple of my verdicts have to go than get a complicated principle that gerrymanders all of them.
