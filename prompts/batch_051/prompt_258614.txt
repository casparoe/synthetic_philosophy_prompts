When I interpret objects deposited in the prehistoric graves at my site, I follow a strict rule. Before I may call anything a religious or metaphysical object, I must try to place it in one of three prior categories, in this order: accidental inclusion, meaning something worn, carried, or lying about that entered the grave without any intent to equip the dead; social display, meaning something intended for the living—status, mourning, kinship, or completed custom—whose explanation need not ascribe to anyone a belief about death; or practical provisioning, meaning things supplied for the dead as if they would go on needing them, but under a description that commits no one to postmortem survival, a soul, or spirits. Only when I can point to a concrete feature that rules out all three categories may I use the residual category of objects that encoded metaphysical belief. The rule is deliberately asymmetric: one ad hoc patch to a mundane story beats a clean supernatural one. Last season it made me read a pot full of red ochre placed at a woman's feet as practical provisioning, and a dog buried with a stone wedged in its jaws as accidental. My colleagues say that under this rule we could not recognize a religious object if we were to find one, because every object can be given some low-cost mundane story. Is my rule defensible? If not, what would a sounder version look like—one that lets us tell the difference between "we found no evidence of religious belief" and "our method is calibrated not to notice it"?
