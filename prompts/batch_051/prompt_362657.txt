I have internalized the standard rationalist-blog line that pure time preference is a mistake. The usual thought, which I associate with Sidgwick, is that a benefit's date is like its spatial coordinates: not itself a welfare property, and therefore not a rational ground for weighting it differently. Someone who discounts future welfare simply because it is future seems to be committing the temporal analogue of egoism. But when I tried to write out the strongest version of the contrary view, I produced the following argument, and I want it checked.

(1) A unit of welfare has the same intrinsic value no matter when it occurs; the "when" is an indexical feature of the event, not a feature of the welfare itself.
(2) Nonetheless, practical reasoning is always conducted from a particular temporal location. An agent cannot choose from the standpoint of eternity; she chooses from now, with the future available to her only as a set of possible outcomes of present actions.
(3) It is therefore rationally permissible for an agent to give structural priority to present and near-term concerns—not because present welfare is intrinsically more valuable, but because those concerns are the only ones her present actions can directly engage or reliably influence.
(4) If such present-priority is rationally permissible for an individual, then discounting future welfare at a modest positive rate is not automatically a bias. It can be the shadow of situated agency rather than a failure of impartiality.
(5) The same situatedness applies to any generation in a transgenerational policy problem. No generation can act from the standpoint of all times at once; each generation's present is the locus of its own practical reasoning.
(6) So a planner who applies no pure time discount across generations is not being impartial but is instead pretending to occupy a standpoint no agent can occupy. Therefore, a positive pure rate of time preference is rationally defensible in intergenerational choices.

I half-suspect the weak point is a slide between "I can affect or predict it" and "I may care less about it," but I cannot make the diagnosis precise. Is the argument valid? If not, which numbered step is the first failure, and is the failure an equivocation, a scope shift, a suppressed premise, or something else? If it is valid, does it establish only something much weaker than a positive pure time preference?
