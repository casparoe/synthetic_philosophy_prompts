I write alternate-history novels, mostly what-ifs about nineteenth-century technology and empire, and after the last one I realized I've been helping myself to a bunch of half-remembered ideas about how much history can actually bend. Before I build a whole plot on them I want a consistency check. Here's roughly where I've landed:

1. Path dependence is real and sometimes decisive: some standards and institutions got locked in by small early accidents and increasing returns rather than because they were better — QWERTY, VHS, that sort of thing — and once they're locked in they can persist for generations even when almost everyone can see a better option.
2. But I also think the lock-in talk gets oversold. Over long enough stretches, market and political selection grinds down most inefficient arrangements; most things that stick are sticking because they're good enough with real switching costs, not because people are caught in a mistake they can't undo.
3. As a working method I believe in the minimal-rewrite counterfactual: you change one small, well-attested event, hold the big structural background fixed, and let the ripples run. But I also think an alt-history stops being informative if divergence snowballs without limit, so the story shouldn't have the world become unrecognizable from a single nudge.
4. I think the huge macro outcomes — agriculture, states, fossil-fuel industrialization, the broad shape of modernity — are overdetermined by material conditions. Even fairly large changes to particular events or particular people wouldn't move there timing or location by much.
5. Yet I don't want path dependence to be an excuse machine. I think later actors are never really trapped: the path persists because their ordinary choices keep reproducing it, and at some point enough of them could coordinate to break it if they wanted to, so responsibility for keeping an arrangement alive belongs to the present, not to the original accident.

Do these hang together? I honestly cant tell where the real strain is; some of it feels like it should be compatible and some of it doesn't, but I don't want to guess. Go through the set and give me an audit rather than a lecture: which pairs are genuinely in tension, which only look like they are, rank the real ones by severity, and say what the cheapest fix is for each. If there's a single revision that would clean up the most, say what it is and what it costs me as a novelist.
