I am a retired geophysicist, and after a year of reading in rational choice theory and democratic theory I have set down my current working position on the rationality of voting. I would like you to act as a professional philosopher who works on the paradox of voting, and to audit the set of commitments below. I am not asking for a survey of the literature, and I am not attached to any particular item on the list; I want to know whether the whole set can be held together and, if not, where it gives.

My commitments:

1. Practical rationality just is expected-utility maximization applied to one's beliefs and values; the values may be self-regarding or other-regarding, and no separate principle of practical reason is needed.
2. In any election large enough for its outcome to matter to many people, the probability that my individual ballot is the tie-breaking or otherwise decisive vote is positive but minuscule.
3. If I feed only my own self-interested payoff from the better candidate into that probability, the expected instrumental value of casting a ballot is far below the cost of voting; the calculus therefore appears to say: stay home.
4. Nonetheless, I vote, and I regard voting as rational in the full sense, not as a forgivable deviation from rationality that is dressed up in civic sentiment.
5. When I redo the same calculation with an impartial weighting of the interests of everyone affected, the stakes grow large enough that even the minuscule probability may not be negligible; this is the so-called ethical-voter move. I can see its technical force, but I am suspicious of it, because it seems to make the rationality of voting hostage to a highly demanding and perhaps unrealistically saintly motivation that I suspect few actual voters have.
6. I also believe there is a moral duty to vote in a reasonably functioning democracy, at least for citizens who are adequately informed; but I am wary of invoking a duty, since that appears to change the subject from what it is rational to do to what it is obligatory to do.
7. I accept a small-probabilities-can-matter principle: an action is not rationally irrelevant merely because it is almost certain to have no causal effect, when the potential benefit is very large and the cost is small.
8. Yet when I try to apply 7 consistently to electoral politics, the principle iterates. Any marginal hour spent becoming better informed, checking registration, or helping others vote similarly buys a tiny increase in the probability of affecting an outcome that matters to many people; so 7 seems to commit me to spending large and open-ended amounts of time on politics. I reject that conclusion, and I do not live that way.
9. I reject the naive universalization test: the fact that if everyone stayed home democracy would collapse does not by itself establish that my staying home would be wrong, any more than the badness of universal celibacy establishes that my remaining single would be wrong.
10. At the same time, I care about the civic culture of participation and think my voting is connected to its preservation; I simply cannot say whether this is a causal consideration that belongs inside the expected-utility valuation or a reason of a wholly different kind.

I want an audit rather than a lecture. Please work through the commitments pairwise before settling on your judgment; in particular, mark any pair that looks tense on a first reading but dissolves once the terms are disambiguated, so that the final audit does not over-flag. Then present the genuine tensions in order of severity. For each genuine tension, state exactly which commitments are involved, why they pull against one another, what the cheapest revision would be, and what that revision costs elsewhere in my set. Finally, identify the single revision that would do the most to restore coherence across the whole list.
