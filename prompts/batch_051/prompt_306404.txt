ok so in philosophy class we're doing Ryle's knowing-how vs knowing-that thing, and my teacher wrote four criteria on the board for "S knows how to X":

1. S can actually do X, not just describe X
2. S can adapt the doing when the situation changes — its not just a memorized routine
3. S doesn't have to run through instructions or rules in their head while doing it
4. S gets better at X with practice

(she also said know-how is hard to fully put into words, but I left that one out cause it felt circular.)

Here's the case I can't stop thinking about. Imagine a language model AI that has read pretty much everything ever written about swimming. It can give you perfect stroke-by-stroke instructions, diagnose exactly whats wrong with your kick just from your description, write a training plan for a specific person, and come up with good advice for weird situations like riptides or swimming in a river current. But it's never been in water and it has no body.

Does it know how to swim? Go through each of the four criteria and say whether the AI meets it, partly meets it, or fails it. The thing I'm actually stuck on is whether this case shows the criteria are right or shows they're missing something. Like, the AI has the whole "information side" better than any human coach, but it fails the "performance side." If you end up saying no, is that just because of criterion 1, and is criterion 1 secretly doing all the work? If you end up saying yes, which criteria do you have to stretch, and is that cheating?

Is that enough to call it know-how, or am I confusing "automatic" with "has no idea what it's doing"?
