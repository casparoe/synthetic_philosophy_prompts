Okay here's where my head's at with tipping, queueing, jaywalking, and all the little scripts that get called conventions, and I want someone to make sense of it instead of just saying "when in Rome." I'll give my gut calls on six cases; you tell me what principle, if any, they add up to.

1. A visitor from a country where there's no tipping comes to New York and eats at a diner. I think they still have to tip, and tip properly, because the server's livelihood depends on it. Refusing because "it's not my culture" would be stiffing a worker, not just being different.

2. The same visitor is later in Kyoto and wants to leave a generous tip for a cab driver who went out of his way. I tell them not to, because in much of Japan tipping isn't the custom and can read as not trusting the person to do their job properly. I don't think that's stingy; I think in that context the tip is the wrong move even though it's well-intentioned.

3. Queueing at a London post office. A visitor from a crowd-the-counter culture has to join the back of the line, and I'd say cutting in would wrong the particular people in front of them, full stop, even if the visitor themselves wouldn't have minded if someone had jumped ahed of them back home.

4. Crossing an empty street against the red in Berlin at 5 a.m. A German friend tuts; I go anyway. I don't think I did anything wrong, and I don't think a visitor who does it does wrong either, even though the locals wait. It doesn't feel like queue-jumping at all to me.

5. Standing on the left side of a Tube escalator in London while everyone walks up the right. I think it's genuinely inconsiderate, but I can't bring myself to call it a moral wrong; or if it is one, it's much smaller than queue-jumping.

6. Being asked to take your shoes off at the door when you visit someone's home in Japan, and not doing it. I think that's clearly wrong, and "I'm a guest, my culture wears shoes indoors" is no excuse.

So my calls pull in different directions: sometimes a visitor must adopt the local script, sometimes they must give up their own homescript, sometimes the local script is optional, and one pair among these really bugs me because queueing binds but crossing against the light doesn't, and I can't say cleanly why. What principle captures my verdicts? State it precisely, and for each clause tell me which case forced it. Then run it on cases I haven't given you: haggling hard in a fixed-price culture where it's seen as aggressive; standing silent in a lift; a tourist destination where the staff have started expecting tips because tourists tip but locals don't; and a local custom of honking once before reversing out of a driveway. Say where your principle disagrees with one of my six calls, which one you'd overturn first if they can't all be kept, and whether the queueing/jaywalking pair is really a problem or a clue. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and don't restate the question.
