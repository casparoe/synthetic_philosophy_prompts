For an upper-level undergraduate seminar in political philosophy (about 16 students, one 75-minute session), design a structured role-play or game that makes participants feel the difference between two claims that are often run together in debates about open borders and the right to exclude: that a group may choose its own members in the way that friends or a club may, and that a political community may exclude outsiders from the only territory available to them. Participants should come away seeing that the second claim cannot simply be read off the first, because the freedom to choose one's associates is not the same as the power to leave someone with no comparable option. The activity should be built so that participants discover this gap themselves, in what happens, rather than having it explained to them afterward.

The design you propose should include rules and materials; a timeline for the 75 minutes; a scheme of stakes, points, or consequences that does not rely on real money; and a debrief. The central device I have in mind, though you should flesh it out, is to run the same admissions decision in two conditions. In the first condition, rejected applicants can form a new association with equivalent resources, so exclusion costs them only the loss of this particular group. In the second, all resources and territory are already claimed by existing associations, so a rejected applicant has nowhere comparable to go unless some association admits them. The two conditions should be identical except for this one feature, and the same stated justifications for refusal should be available in both, so that participants can feel why a reason for exclusion that seems harmless in the first condition changes its weight in the second.

The debrief should map specific moments in the activity onto the philosophical structure: freedom of association as a candidate ground for the right to exclude; the objection that a state is not a club because membership is largely unchosen and territory is monopolized; and the distinction between having a right not to associate with a particular person and having a right to exclude that person from all comparable options. The debrief should also identify what predictably goes wrong the first time the activity is run — for example, participants drifting into a general debate about immigration policy rather than staying inside the role, or making generous decisions because the stakes feel artificial — and say how the facilitator should handle those failures. Finally, be explicit about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and what the facilitator will have to supply by direct statement.

Please write the answer in American English. Also, say how confident you are in the design and in the philosophical mapping, and single out the point you are least sure of.
