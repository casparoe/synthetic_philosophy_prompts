I'm a software engineer, and at work we use a planning tool that asks engineers to enter probabilities for milestones ("migration done by end of Q3"), then a forecasting layer turns those numbers into some kind of confidence range for the whole rollout. A while back two teammates, Sam and Marcus, got into it over whether Marcus's numbers were actually meaningful, and I've been chewing on the argument ever since.

Sam had noticed that Marcus put 0.6 on "migration lands by end of Q3," 0.5 on "migration lands by end of Q4, conditional on it missing Q3," and 0.65 on "migration lands by end of Q4" without qualification. Sam said the first two force the third to be 0.8, so the three together are incoherent; he called it a Dutch book and walked us through how, if Marcus would actually trade at those prices, someone could buy and sell a handful of bets with him that leave him with less money no matter which timeline actually happens. Marcus said that's a neat trick, but it depends on him having offered all three at once, which he never did; the 0.65 was his real feeling about Q4, while the 0.5 conditional was an offhand answer to a weird counterfactual, and the tool made him squeeze both into numbers. Sam shot back that a probability can't be "real" in one sentence and "offhand" in another; either the number is your degree of belief or it isn't. Marcus said that's exactly the point: he's not a market maker, the tool asked for rough forecasts, and he wouldn't have taken the combined package even if he'd have taken each bet separately, because seeing all three at once changes what he's willing to sign. Sam said no, if each individual trade is acceptable at your own odds, and the package is just those trades added together, refusing the package is just refusing to look at the contradiction. Marcus then said Sam was smuggling in an extra assumption: that money in the timeline where the migration misses Q3 is worth the same as money in the timeline where it hits, and it isn't, because his year-end, stress, and backup plans differ between those branches. Sam said Dutch book arguments don't need money to be a perfect utility function when the stakes are this small.

I don't have enough philosophy or probability theory to sort out which moves in there where legitimate and which were cop-outs. Could you go through the exchange and rule on each specific claim: is Sam right that Marcus's numbers need fixing, or is Marcus right that the Dutch book only shows something about a hypothetical package he never accepted? Who is more right overall, and is there something important they're both missing?
