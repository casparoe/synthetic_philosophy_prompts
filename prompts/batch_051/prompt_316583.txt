I have been making my way through the vagueness literature on my own, and I have come to accept epistemicism: the view, associated with Timothy Williamson, that vague predicates such as "heap" and "bald" nonetheless have sharp cutoffs, that classical logic and bivalence are not threatened by vagueness, and that what we call borderline cases are cases of irremediable ignorance rather than semantic indeterminacy. On this picture the major premise of the sorites is simply false — there is, for any heapable series, a last grain whose addition makes a heap — but the location of that cutoff is unknowable in principle, not merely unknown in practice. What attracts me is that the view treats vagueness as a fact about our cognitive limitations rather than a fact about the world's or language's lack of definiteness, so it asks for no revision of logic or compositional semantics.

I want a map of what would count against this position. What evidence, arguments, intuitions, possible cases, or formal results should move an epistemicist, and which should they regard as expected costs? Among the candidates I can already see: the brute intuition that a sharp boundary is not merely unknowable but absent — that there is no last grain because "heap" simply does not settle the question; the problem of higher-order vagueness, and whether the view can draw a boundary between the determinate and the indeterminate cases without either importing a rival semantics or inviting an infinite ascent; the apparent tension between sharp, speaker-independent cutoffs and the thought that meaning is fixed by use or by a linguistic community; and results about margin-for-error principles (roughly, that knowledge requires not being too close to a case where one would be wrong) and the unavailability of luminous conditions (roughly, that no mental condition is such that being in it guarantees knowing one is in it), which seem to support epistemicism's negative program but may also threaten its positive claim that the boundary is there at all. I would also like to know what epistemicism predicts that its rivals — especially supervaluationism — do not, and whether any of those predictions constitute a genuine test rather than a restatement of the view.

Please sort the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about which of them a committed epistemicist would concede as real exposure once the obvious replies are exhausted. Finally, tell me whether the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability by making the cutoff unknowable in principle, and whether that insulation is a vice in a theory of this kind or the honest consequence of taking knowledge seriously. What would most impress me is not a list of standard objections but an argument that the view is internally unstable, or a demonstration that the standard objections all quietly presuppose the rival picture they are trying to defend.
