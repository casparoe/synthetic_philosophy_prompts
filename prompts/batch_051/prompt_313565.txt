I am a high school junior sitting in on a college philosophy of science course, and for my term project I took up the question of whether elegance in science—the symmetry, compactness, and visual simplicity of an equation or model—is evidence for a theory's truth or merely an aesthetic preference. A teaching assistant gave me an argument that seems airtight while I read it, but its conclusion unsettles me, because the reasoning appears to prove far more than the target. I have tried and failed to find where it gives way, and I would like a precise diagnosis rather than a one-line answer.

The argument runs as follows. (1) If a feature of a theory counts as evidence for the theory's truth, it must reflect something about the world the theory describes, not something about how we choose to present or represent the theory. (2) Elegance is a feature of presentation: whether a set of equations looks symmetric, compact, or tidy depends on the variables, coordinate systems, and notational conventions we adopt. (3) Any body of data can be fitted by many empirically equivalent formalisms, some elegant and some deliberately cumbersome, and a theory can often be made more or less elegant without changing a single observable consequence. (4) Therefore elegance can vary independently of how the world is. (5) Therefore elegance cannot be evidence for truth. (6) What worries me is that premise (1), once accepted, seems to generalize: simplicity, uniformity, even the assumption that the future will resemble the past all appear to be facts about how we organize or generalize observations, not facts read directly off nature. (7) But ordinary induction—such as concluding from samples that all copper conducts electricity—draws its force from just such simplicity and uniformity assumptions. If they cannot be evidence, ordinary induction has no evidential force, which is absurd. (8) So somewhere the chain must break, even though each step looked forced on me while I went through it.

Where does the argument first go wrong, and what does giving up that step cost? I am most reluctant to abandon (5), because I do not want aesthetic taste to masquerade as evidence, but I suspect saving it may force me into an uncomfortable position about induction. I would be grateful for a careful account of which premise or step is the weak one and what the standard options have to pay to reject it.
