I've been turning over a parallel between AI alignment and economics that I can't decide about, and I'd like an honest verdict on whether it's deep or superficial.

The setup on one side: the orthogonality thesis says an agent's intelligence and its terminal goals are independent in principle — you can imagine a system that is superhumanly good at achieving almost any objective. Instrumental convergence then says that, despite this, a few intermediate goals show up for nearly all of them: self-preservation, acquiring resources, preserving its own goal content, improving its capabilities. So the ends are arbitrary, but the means are boringly predictable.

The other side: I've always thought of money as the cleanest example of a purely instrumental good. Different people want wildly different things out of life, yet almost all of them convergse on wanting more money — not because they value money for its own sake, usually, but because money converts into almost anything else. Diversity of ends, convergence on the same means. Is that basically the same structure as instrumental convergence?

But I worry I'm just pattern-matching on the word 'means.' The obvious difference is that money is a social institution: its universal instrumentality depends on a legal system, shared expectations, and the ability to exchange it. The convergent subgoals of an AI are supposed to fall out of the structure of optimization itself, with no institution required. So my question is twofold. First, is there a genuine structural identity here, or just a loose analogy? Second, does the money case actually earn its keep — for instance, by predicting where instrumental convergence might fail? Money stops being instrumentally valuable when it can't be spent: hyperinflation, capital controls, non-market goods. Is there an analog for why a highly capable agent might not converge on self-preservation or resource acquisition? Or does the disanalogy about institutions swamp everything? Answer in British English, please.
