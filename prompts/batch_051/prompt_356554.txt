I would like a single number — your credence, between 0 and 1 — for the following proposition, together with the decomposition that produces it.

Proposition: There is at least one defensible reconstruction of the core Eleatic argument which is sound by contemporary standards, and whose conclusion is the strict Eleatic thesis: the ordinary world of many distinct things that come into being, change, and pass away does not exist as such; reality is a single, unchanging unity, and the plural, changing world of experience is strictly false rather than merely an imprecise description of that unity.

By "defensible by contemporary standards" I mean: the argument's premises can be stated in a way a careful present-day metaphysician or logician would recognise as intelligible, and each premise has survived — or would survive — the standard objections raised since Plato and Aristotle. I am not asking whether Parmenides himself had exactly these resources.

Decompose your credence into at least four sub-questions, for example:

1. The semantic premise. How likely is it that some version of the claim that what is not cannot be meaningfully thought or said — suitably regimented so that it is not refuted by the familiar point that the word 'is' functions both predicatively and existentially — is true, and does genuine work in ruling out coming-to-be and differentiation as unreal?

2. The methodological premise. How likely is it that a maximally general, largely a priori argument about being may legitimately override the apparent deliverances of the senses? Assign a probability to the principle that a valid argument from full generalities can show the manifest image false.

3. The contemporary physics term. How likely is it that the actual character of time — say, the B-theory of time or the block-universe reading of relativity — provides independent support for the strict Eleatic conclusion of unchanging oneness? Price in the difference between the view that time's passage is an illusion (which still permits a plurality of static things) and the far stronger Parmenidean claim that plurality itself is unreal.

4. The diagnostic term. How likely is it that the Eleatic argument mainly succeeds as a diagnosis — that ordinary ways of talking about change and difference are more confused than we ordinarily suppose — and that this diagnostic success transfers into warrant for the strict thesis, rather than merely showing that our concepts need repair while the world of many changing things stays fundamentally as it seems?

5. The reconstruction term. How likely is it that the strongest modern reconstruction actually goes through, granting for the sake of argument that some premise set and some formalisation are available? This term asks whether the argument is valid and jointly acceptable, independent of whether the historically intended argument is.

For each sub-question, give your rough probability and a sentence or two of justification. Then show how the terms combine, and state clearly whether you treat them as independent or whether your overall number is dominated by one of them. Finally, identify the sub-question to which your final credence is most sensitive — the one where revising your probability by a little would move the bottom line most — and say what kind of development (a formal result, a philosophical argument, a scientific finding) would most shift it. Please commit to a number rather than hiding behind the fact that much depends on how the reconstruction is formulated.
