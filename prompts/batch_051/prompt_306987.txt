Okay so heres the situation. A few months ago I inherited a modest amount of money and I've been trying to decide whether to give a big chunk of it to longtermist causes, the kind that say the far future swamps the present because there could be trillions of trillions of future beings. I went down the expected-value rabbit hole and now I cant shake the thought that if there's even a 1-in-10,000 chance my money nudges the far future in a good direction, the math says I should basically ignore the present. That feels insane, but I cant find the actual flaw.

Then I came across the simulation argument — roughly, the claim that if our kind of civilization tends to reach a point where it can run lots of ancestor simulations, then we are probably in one of those simulations rather than in the base reality. And if we're simulated, the "far future" I'm trying to influence might get paused, restarted, or just not matter in the way the fanatic calculation assumes. That took some of the air out, but I'm worried I only like it because it gives me an excuse to keep the money.

Act as a professional philosopher who works on decision theory and population ethics. Think carefully and don't just give me the standard intro-course answer. First, brainstorm two or three more "dampeners" for far-future fanaticism — considerations that would deflate the expected-value case for letting the far future dominate. Then rank all of them, including the ones I list below, by how much they should actually reduce someone's practical commitment to far-future causes. Give each item a score out of 10 (10 = it completely defuses the fanaticism, 0 = it does no real work) and one sentence of reasoning for each.

My list:

1. The simulation argument (we're probably simulated, so the deep future we might influence is not the base-level deep future).
2. Unreliable tiny probabilities (below some threshold, subjective probabilities are just noise, so multiplying them by astronomical stakes is Pascal's-mugging territory).
3. Cluelessness (we can't even predict the sign of our long-run effects, so expected value is massively indeterminate).
4. Moral uncertainty about population ethics (whether far-future beings count the way total utilitarianism says depends on unresolved questions, and under other views the astronomical sums don't arise).
5. Convergent long-run outcomes (if advanced civilizations tend to end up in the same broad states no matter what we do now, our marginal leverage on the far future is much smaller than the fanatic thinks).
