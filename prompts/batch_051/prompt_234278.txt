I have been reading in the history-from-below tradition alongside a handful of microhistories, and I keep running into what looks like a contradiction between the program and its most admired specimens. The program, as I understand it, sets out to recover the lives of ordinary people — peasants, artisans, servants, the unnamed mass — rather than kings, ministers, and generals. Yet the studies everyone points to are about people who were anything but ordinary: a sixteenth-century miller tried for outlandish cosmological heresies, a man who passed himself off as a villager's long-absent husband, a Pyrenean village investigated for heretical beliefs. These figures appear in the record only because courts and inquisitors noticed them; they are deviants and troublemakers, not representative samples of the peasantry's everyday life.

If the archive is systematically biased toward conflict, crime, and power, it seems the method should compensate: microhistorians ought to select more typical subjects and show how to correct for the unrepresentativeness of their sources. So my question is what methodological resources microhistory has for making good on its claim to represent ordinary people, and how a working microhistorian should choose subjects to offset the bias toward the exceptional. I would like a committed response — take a definite position on what the method should do, with reasons, even if that means challenging the way the question is framed, and do not just lay out the available options.
