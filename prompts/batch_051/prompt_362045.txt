I have spent the past two years caring for my mother since her dementia reached the point where she no longer recognizes me. What unsettled me is not the loss of her memory but the fact that it has not taken her with it. She cannot say what she did this morning and cannot produce more than a sentence of autobiography, yet everyone who knew her before agrees that she is still recognizably the same person: the same wary way of entering a room, the same rhythm of jokes, the same automatic kindness toward children, the same irritability when interrupted. This has made me suspect that personal identity rests less on memory or life-narrative than I had been taught, and more on something like a persistent style of being that memory merely helps maintain.

I have been trying to give that suspicion a precise form, and I want it checked. I call it the attractor account of the self. The idea is this: conceive of a person as a point moving through a very high-dimensional space whose dimensions are dispositions, attention habits, emotional reflexes, automatic interpretations, and ways of responding to other people. A self, on this account, is a stable attractor in that space — a basin that behavior keeps falling back into despite perturbations. Memory and narrative are real causes, but their importance is derivative: they are among the forces that keep the system in the basin. Losing memory therefore damages the self only to the degree that it lets the basin collapse or lets the trajectory wander into another basin. My mother, on this view, is still herself because her old attractor is still pulling her behavior back into its characteristic shape every few minutes, even without a story to glue the episodes together. The central claim is that continuity of personhood across time just is continuity of such an attractor, and that this continuity matters more than continuity of memory, narrative, or bodily identity.

I am not sure whether I have invented something or merely rediscovered a view under a fashionable name. It sounds half like a bundle theory and half like the no-self tradition's claim that the self is a useful construction, but it also sounds as if it is smuggling in a hidden soul made of habits. Is the account coherent? Is the attractor doing real work beyond the everyday ideas of habit and character, or is it just those ideas renamed? Does it actually improve on the familiar problems of psychological-continuity views, such as what to say about duplication, radical forgetting, or someone whose basin changes slowly over time until it has become another person's? And am I right to think the no-self tradition would be roughly friendly to this picture, or does it cut the other way? Give me an honest assessment, including the strongest version of what I am reaching for.

Throughout, please distinguish what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who have thought carefully about these questions, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
