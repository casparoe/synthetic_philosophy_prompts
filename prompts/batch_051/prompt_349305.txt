I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I've ended up in an argument I can't resolve. A colleague claims that people who call the ranking of historical atrocities obscene are quietly committed to the very thing they condemn. Laid out, his argument is this:

(1) Everyone who rejects the ranking of historical evils still makes comparative judgments: they will grant that a genocide that kills millions is worse than the killing of one person, and they normally expect more resources to be spent commemorating the former than the latter.

(2) To grant that one event is worse than another is to grant that the two stand in a relation of overall comparative severity.

(3) 'Worse than' is transitive, and over any finite collection of events such a relation can be extended into a complete ordering.

(4) Therefore anyone who accepts even a single comparative judgment of this kind is committed to the in-principle legitimacy of a complete ranked list of historical atrocities.

(5) But that conclusion seems false, and more than false: it seems morally mistaken, as though each atrocity could be given a single leaderboard position, flattening exactly what makes each distinctive.

I want to reject (5), but I cannot tell which of the earlier moves forces me to accept it. Which step should be given up, why does that step look so innocent, and what does rejecting it cost for the ordinary comparative judgments in (1)?
