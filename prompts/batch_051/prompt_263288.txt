Write me a parody in the form of an internal company memo, about 600 words, from the new Head of People at a startup, announcing a mandatory "Total Ownership" program. It should stay deadpan and HR-cheerful the whole way through — no winking at the reader. The philosophical engine is Sartre on radical freedom and bad faith. The memo should insist that every employee is completely free, and therefore wholly responsible for their motivation, output, mood, career, and even whether the company survives; appeals to background, burnout, pay, conditions, childhood, or "the market" should all be treated as excuses. But the memo itself must be one sustained act of bad faith: the author treats the company's situation, the policy, and his own role as fixed facts that simply had to be, while demanding that employees be pure freedom, and he identifies with the role of Head of People exactly when that lets him off the hook. Work in, without ever naming Sartre: (1) a self-review form praised as total honesty, but which requires employees to identify with their job title and past metrics — sincerity as another flight from freedom; (2) a description of the "authentic professional" that is basically the waiter making himself into a waiter-thing; (3) the claim that an employee's choice of attitude chooses a way of being for everyone in the firm, so the firm has a legitimate stake in their inner life. The vocabulary should occasionally brush against anguish and abandonment, but always in corporate register. Finish with an HR policy that reads like a straight-faced reductio of take-responsibility culture, without saying what it's a reductio of. Structure the response as two clearly seperated sections: "The Memo" (550–650 words) and "What the memo is doing" (150–250 words). In the second section, explain the main Sartrean moves and which details were deliberate.
