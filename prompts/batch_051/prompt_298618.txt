Lately I've been trying to work out when deferring to someone else's judgment is a virtue and when it's a failure, and I've found that my own reactions to cases keep pointing in different directions. I have no background in this beyond ordinary life and a long-running disagreement with my sister, who is in the military and thinks I'm much too suspicious of authority. I'd like you to help me find the rule behind my reactions, test it, and tell me where I'm being inconsistent.

Here are my verdicts. (1) A nurse preparing a medication notices that a dose is far higher than anything she has given before. She calls the prescribing doctor, who has thirty years of experience and a spotless record, and he explains that this patient metabolizes the drug unusually quickly and the dose is deliberate. She gives the medication. I think she did nothing wrong; checking and then trusting him seems exactly right. (2) A soldier is ordered to guard a supply tent all night inside a well-lit base camp. He privately thinks the guard duty is pointless, but he does it without complaint because the order came through his normal chain of command. I think that is fine; no unit can function if everyone re-litigates every assignment. (3) A lab assistant in a memory study is told by the researcher running it to keep administering increasingly strong electric shocks to another participant, who cries out and asks to stop. The researcher tells him the study cannot be stopped and that he must continue, and the assistant keeps going. I think the assistant was wrong to continue, no matter how insistent the researcher was. (4) A junior accountant is handed a financial statement by a partner with fifteen years of error-free work, and the partner assures her it is accurate. She signs off without reviewing it herself. I think that was wrong; the problem is not that she trusted him, but that she never looked. (5) A jury foreperson has real doubts about the prosecution's story, but the other eleven jurors, including two lawyers, are unanimous, so he votes guilty. I think that was wrong; there is a kind of personal responsibility in a verdict that you cannot hand to other people. (6) A seven-year-old is told by her mother not to cross the street even though she can see no cars coming, and she obeys. I think that is perfectly fine, even wise, even though the mother is not an expert in any special sense. (7) An office worker joins in her manager's teasing of a colleague because the manager started it and everyone else is laughing, even though she privately thinks it is cruel. I think that was wrong, even though refusing would have cost her socially.

I can feel that something like "defer to people who know more than you, but never outsource the things you yourself can directly see to be wrong" is close, but it falls apart on the details. Cases (2) and (6) are not about expertise at all, and I still approve of them. I cannot cleanly separate (1) from (4) except by saying that the accountant failed to do her own job first, which may be a separate principle about due diligence rather than about deference. Is there one principle that captures all of these without becoming a patchwork of exceptions? If a simpler principle would force me to overturn one or two of my verdicts, tell me which verdicts and why. And I would like that principle to settle a case I keep going back and forth on: a welfare office clerk processes a claim even though she believes the applicant has been misclassified by a new rule, because her supervisor instructs her to apply the rule as written and the rule is indeed on the books. She has double-checked the file and the regulation and she still thinks the applicant is being misclassified. Is she behaving virtuously by deferring to her role and her supervisor, or is she doing the same thing as the lab assistant?
