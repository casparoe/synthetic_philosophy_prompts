I'm on my college's academic integrity board, and we're considering a proposal to replace our standard confidential sanction for first-offense plagiarism with a restorative process where the student has to sit in a circle with the professor and two or three classmates they affected, hear directly how their cheating made others feel, and read a short statement of what they did and why it was wrong. The advocates say this works because guilt tells you the act was wrong but shame tells you you've fallen short of who you ought to be, and the latter is what actually prevents repeat offenses. One of our members then read aloud this from Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics Book IV chapter 9: "Shame is a mark of a base man, and springs from a character capable of doing a shameful act. And it is absurd that, because a man is of such a nature that he is ashamed if he does a shameful act, he should therefore think himself virtuous, since actions to cause shame must be voluntary, but a virtuous man will never voluntarily do a base action." Another member shot back that Aristotle is exactly the sort of thing you'd expect from a culture which chose guilt over shame, and our institution can choose differently. We need a decision memo. Draft the integrity board's recommendation memo: should we approve the restorative shame-based process, reject it, or approve with conditions? Please give an actionable bottom line and reasons, and treat the strongest argument on the losing side seriously. No headings, bullet points, bold, or tables; just continuous prose like an email.
