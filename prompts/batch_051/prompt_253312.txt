I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm several weeks into reporting a feature on a puzzle I can't shake: how can a sound—something with no beliefs, desires, or history—be sad? I keep noticing that people use the phrase "sad music" with total confidence, yet the researchers I've interviewed mean very different things by it. Some locate the sadness in acoustic features such as slow tempo, minor mode, falling pitch contours, and low energy. Others locate it in the listener's emotional response, or in a kind of imaginary persona we hear behind the music. I need a working definition I can actually use to sort examples as I write, not a survey of the field.

Here are eight cases I've been using to test people's intuitions:

1. A slow, legato cello line in a minor key, descending in pitch, played softly; practically everyone I ask calls it mournful.
2. An upbeat major-key jingle for a breakfast cereal, bouncing rhythm, no lyrics.
3. A field recording of a person sobbing, presented as an audio track in a sound-art installation.
4. An uptempo pop song in a major key whose lyrics describe the end of a relationship in flat, hopeless detail.
5. An electronic piece built from the standard "sad" recipe—minor mode, slow tempo, descending contours, soft dynamics—whose synthetic timbres make many listeners call it cold or eerie instead.
6. A bird's dawn song, which ornithologists describe as a territorial display but which some people find mournful.
7. A completely static sine tone, no melody or harmony, displayed in a gallery under the title "Grief."
8. An improvised instrumental passage in a mode that is heard as sorrowful in one musical culture and celebratory in another.

I'm confident about the first two: (1) is sad music and (2) isn't. I have leanings about (4) and (8) but couldn't defend them. The others I genuinely don't know how to classify.

Please draft the short memo I'll keep next to my keyboard. Your task is to propose a working definition of "sad music" that gets these cases right, run the definition through all eight, identify where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and either revise it or defend the surprising verdicts. The definition should say what sort of property sadness is here—a property of the sound, of the listener's response, of the work's expressive character, or something else—rather than merely listing acoustic cues.

Structure the memo in these sections, in this order:

1. Proposed definition — one or two sentences, plus a brief note on the kind of definition you are giving. About 150 words.
2. Case-by-case verdicts — run the definition through all eight cases, with a reason for each verdict; about 400 words. A compact table is fine provided each row gives a reason, not just a label.
3. Where the definition strains — the cases that most resist the definition and what that reveals about the boundary of the term; about 200 words.
4. Revised definition or defense — either revise the definition in light of the hard cases or explain why the original definition's surprising verdicts should stand; about 150 words.

End with a single sentence stating which of your eight verdicts is the least secure.

You are a philosopher of music and emotion who also follows the empirical psychology of music perception. Before writing, think through at least two candidate definitions and test each against the hardest cases; discard any candidate that only handles the easy ones. Do not include that brainstorming in the memo itself. I don't need citations or a reading list; the cases are the test.
