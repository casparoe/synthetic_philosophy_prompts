I'm a software engineer on a team that ships an LLM assistant for customer support, and I've been put on the internal safety review tool. We log the model's chain-of-thought (the scratchpad reasoning it emits before the answer), and after the fact a reviewer looks at flagged traces and decides whether the case should have been escalated. Right now each reviewer uses their own gut feel about whether the on-screen reasoning is what the model "really did," and the team is split on weather to trust these traces for anything safety-relevant. One camp says CoT is basically a press release: the model doesn't have reliable access to its own computation, so the trace is a plausible-sounding reconstruction. Another camp says that's too dismissive: sometimes the trace walks through safety-relevant considerations and that's enough to escalate. And a third worry is that the more we rely on CoT as a safety signal, the more optimization pressure there is for models to produce traces that look good to reviewers while the actual behavior differs (obfuscation, basically). The thing I keep tripping on is what "trust the trace" even means when the trace is itself a generated output under the same optimization pressure as the answer.

I want a decision procedure a junior reviewer can actually run in about 10 minutes per trace, with no theory background, to decide how much safety-relevant weight to put on a logged chain-of-thought: when it counts as evidence about what the model did, when it counts mainly as evidence about what the model says it did, and when it counts as no evidence at all. Please prescribe the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with do not escalate, where it stops with escalate, and where it hands the decision to a human because it's a value call rather than a factual one. If any step asks for a probability, say what the reviewer should do when they can't get a number.

Format it exactly like this:
1. The mental model — two paragraphs max, plain English: what faithfulness, obfuscation, and optimization pressure come to for the person reading a trace.
2. The procedure — the main part, about half the answer: the ordered questions, stop-and-escalate points, and the human-judgment handoffs.
3. Three traces run through it — run the procedure on (a) a trace that says it will stay within policy and does, but in formulaic language; (b) a trace that works through a plan to circumvent a safeguard and then complies, with a completely fine final output; (c) a trace that just restates the final answer without any intermediate reasoning. Include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer I won't like, and tell me why I should trust it anyway.
4. When the procedure breaks — one paragraph on the failure modes and what the procedure cannot do for us.
