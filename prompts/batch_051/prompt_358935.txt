I'm giving a seminar on theory choice in ethics this term and before the first session I want to sanity-check the package of views I'm bringing in, since I suspect the grad students will notice if it doesn't hang together. Here's where I've landed:

(1) Simplicity is a genuine theoretical virtue in ethics: other things equal, a theory with fewer fundamental principles is better.

(2) Fit with considered judgments matters too, and it isn't just a tiebreaker; a theory that badly mishandles promise-keeping or punishing the innocent gets penalized even if its very simple.

(3) I'm a Rossian pluralist — I think there are several distinct prima facie duties (fidelity, gratitude, beneficence, non-maleficence, and the rest) and they don't reduce to a single master principle.

(4) I think classical consequentialism is too simple in exactly the bad way: one principle can't capture the special moral structure of promises, or of not harming people as a means to your ends.

(5) But I also think the sophisticated consequentialisms that bolt on rule-, motive-, and global-evaluation machinery to recapture those verdicts are just pluralism in disguise — and the disguise makes them less simple, not more, than just admitting the extra duties outright.

(6) When two theories tie on fit with considered judgments, take the simpler one. Honestly, beyond that simplicity is mostly a tiebreaker in my practice.

So: does this set hang together? I want the tensions ranked by severity, and for each one I want to know whether it's real or only apparent and what the cheapest revision is. Don't just flag everything that could possibly rub.
