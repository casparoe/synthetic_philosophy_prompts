Ever since a friend showed me the Boltzmann brain idea last year, I've been kind of unsettled by it, and I've ended up with a personal rule about which theories I'm willing to take seriously. The rule is roughly this: if a theory implies that, if it were true, I would probabaly be a Boltzmann brain—or more generally that my current mental life is probably just a random fluctuation with no reliable connection to the world it seems to track—then I don't treat that implication as evidence either way; I just put the whole theory in a box and stop updating on it. My reasoning is that the theory sawes off the branch it's sitting on. If most observers like me are Boltzmann brains, then my memories, my sense of having carefully evaluated the arguments, and even the cosmological reasoning that led me to the theory are all probably noise, so the conclusion can't be trusted. My friend says this is just me refusing to follow arguments where they lead, because the universe doesn't owe me reliable faculties. He also says the same branch-sawing argument would have let a caveman dismiss real cosmology, since a caveman's brain wasn't built to do cosmology either. But that feels different to me, because the caveman's conclusion wasn't undermining the very reasoning he used to reach it.

I genuinely can't tell whether this is a defensible epistemic policy or just a bias dressed up as a rule. Can someone pick it apart: when is it legitimate to set aside a theory as self-undermining, when does that move become question-begging, and is there a cleaned-up version of my rule that keeps whatever is good in it while avoiding the bad parts? Also, does it matter that I'm not claiming the theory is false, only that I can't use my own judgment to assess it if it's true?
