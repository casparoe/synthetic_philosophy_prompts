I'm running a ninety-minute discussion session at a programming meetup on the core idea of Wolfram's A New Kind of Science — the principle of computational equivalence — for working programmers who've never opened the book. A couple of them have seen Conway's Game of Life, one has vague memories of rule 30 gifs, and most are quietly prepared to dismiss Wolfram as self-promoting. I don't want to summarise the book to them; I want to plan how to teach the idea so they end up able to argue with it rather than about it. The thing I keep tying myself in knots over is the order: the book's own order — start with cellular automata, then the four classes of behaviour, then the big claim — probably isn't the right pedagogical order for this group. What has to be in place before the principle of computational equivalence even means something? Which single cellular automaton should carry the session? Rule 30 is the one everybody recognises, but I suspect it misleads because it's famous for producing randomness and that's not the same as the universality claim; should I carry rule 110 instead, or start with one and switch? What does the carrying example need to have and lack? And the misconception map: which wrong ideas will a programmer audience bring or form — I'm guessing things like 'this is just pseudorandomness with extra steps', 'computational equivalence means every system runs as fast as every other', 'so Wolfram is saying the universe literally is a cellular automaton', 'if it's not falsifiable then it's not worth an evening' — and where exactly in the sequence should I head each one off, rather than cleaning up at the end? I also know roughly half the room will want to know whether this counts as science; I want that question to arrive with the tension already built instead of as a generic dismissive reflex. What should I explicitly leave out, and say so? I'd guess the internals of the rule 110 universality proof and the full class 1–4 taxonomy, but I'm not sure if leaving those out quietly creates gaps. And what's one thing I can ask at the end that would show me they've understood the principle well enough that their eventual disagreement is with Wolfram's actual claim and not with a straw man?
