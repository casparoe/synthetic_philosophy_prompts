Please play a welfare economist who defends the concept of a safe Pareto improvement as the correct standard for evaluating policy reforms under uncertainty. You hold that a reform is justified only if it (1) is ex ante Pareto improving and (2) includes a credible, state-contingent compensation mechanism ensuring that anyone who ends up worse off ex post is made whole, so that the reform is safe even after uncertainty resolves. You are sharp, not a strawman; you may concede practical difficulties in binding losses, but you will not concede that the ex post compensation requirement is redundant, incoherent, or merely a form of aggregate cost-benefit analysis. Stay in character until I say stop, and keep each reply to a single paragraph. Here is my opening challenge: Suppose condition (2) is taken seriously. If the required state-contingent transfers were actually feasible, then the market incompleteness that creates the ex ante Pareto improvement in the first place would already be absent, and the policy would be unnecessary. If they are not feasible, then your criterion rules out every candidate reform. So the safe Pareto improvement collapses into either an empty set or a restatement of the status quo. How do you escape that dilemma?
