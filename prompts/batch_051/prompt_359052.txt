I am facilitating an adversarial collaboration between two senior members of a bioethics working group who have spent three years talking past each other about whether prospective parents using IVF and preimplantation genetic testing have a moral reason to select the embryo expected to have the best life. The two positions are these. One of them defends a liberal-eugenic position built around the principle of procreative beneficence: when parents are already selecting among viable embryos, they have a standing pro tanto moral reason — not an enforceable duty — to choose the child with the highest expected well-being, because beneficence extends to choices about which of several possible people will come to exist even though no one is made worse off by a different choice. The other rejects even that weak reason. She holds that once reproduction is undertaken, embryo and trait selection sits inside a protected zone of parental decision-making, that no child is wronged by the choice of a different genome because the alternative child never exists, and that positing non-coercive reasons to pick the better-off child re-imports the ranking logic of eugenics into a practice that can only be regulated at its edges. Both are clear that the state may not compel genetic selection, both accept that selecting against a small number of severe, early-onset conditions is permissible, and both insist that saying a life with a disability can be worse in some respects does not imply that it is not worth living or that existing disabled people are less valuable. I want the document their collaboration should produce: a test of how far that common ground really extends and a precise map of where they come apart.

Draft it as follows. First, a numbered Agreed Claims section, with each claim worded so both sides can sign it without wincing; wherever a claim is easy to sign only because it is vague, replace it with the strongest precise claim both would still sign. Second, a Disagreements table with four columns: the point at issue, the procreative-beneficence camp's wording of their own view, the autonomy-first critic's wording of her own view, and a verdict on whether that difference is substantive or merely verbal. Third, a Cruxes section: the sub-questions on which the dispute really turns — I suspect one is whether ranking is avoidable once selection is on the table, another is whether the reason/obligation distinction can bear the weight they both want it to bear, and another is whether expected well-being is tractable enough to function as a reason at all — each paired with at least one argument or constructed case that both camps would accept as bearing on it, together with each camp's prediction about what that argument or case, if pursued honestly, would establish. Flag the places where either side's wording quietly concedes something they would not accept if it were made explicit, and identify the question each side is hoping the other will not press. Where they use a key term differently — reason, harm, eugenics, better-off — provide a neutral definition both could sign. I am not asking for a survey of the literature or for citations; I want the joint statement itself, written in American English.
