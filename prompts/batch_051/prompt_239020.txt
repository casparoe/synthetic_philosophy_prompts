Consider a jurisdiction that abolishes fixed statutes of limitations and replaces them with a single continuous time-discount rule. Each year a standing Time Discount Commission surveys a random sample of two thousand citizens. For each offense category, panelists read vignettes holding the offense and the offender's culpability fixed while varying the time since the crime across one, five, twenty, and fifty years; the vignettes stipulate that evidence is perfectly preserved and the offender has otherwise lived a blameless life. Panelists assign a deserved sentence in months, and the commission fits the mean responses to S(T) = S_0 exp(−dT), publishing the resulting annual discount rate d for each category. At sentencing, the judge starts from the ordinary guideline sentence and multiplies it by exp(−dT), where T runs from the offense to conviction, so the sentence decays continuously with elapsed time. If the defendant caused or substantially exploited the delay—concealment, threats, destruction of evidence—the discount is suspended for the portion of the delay so caused. Once the discounted sentence falls below one month, prosecution is barred; there is no other limitation period.

Treat this as a mechanism rather than a moral position. I care less about whether the discount rate is substantively correct than about what the system would actually do. Analyze the incentive landscape: what does it incentivize among victims, prosecutors, police, defendants, and the survey respondents themselves, once their answers set binding sentences? How would it be gamed? Pay particular attention to offense categories whose social blame rises or falls over time, to partial rather than total defendant-caused delay, and to the fact that the survey imagines preserved evidence while real cases rarely have it. Where does the rule break, and which failure modes are fixable by amending the mechanism rather than abandoning it?
