I ask this as a retired field biologist who has recently been reading the free-will literature. The wasp made famous by the term "sphexish" is used to mark a sharp line between mechanical routine and flexible agency, and I want to test where that line actually lies.

Counterfactual. A digger wasp stings and paralyzes her prey, drags it to the burrow entrance, leaves it at the threshold, goes inside for a brief inspection, and then returns to pull it in. If the prey is moved a few centimetres while she is inside, she does not simply finish the job; she drags it back to the threshold and runs the inspection again. With repeated displacements the sequence repeats indefinitely, even though all the individual routines needed to complete the task are present and functional. Now suppose one lineage acquires a single additional control rule: when she re-encounters prey that has moved farther than a threshold distance from the entrance since her last inspection, she suppresses the inspection subroutine and drags the prey directly into the burrow. Nothing else in her nervous system changes—no general learning capacity, no new memory trace, no representation of the nest interior; just that one gating rule.
