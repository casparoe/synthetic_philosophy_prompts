A private foundation that funds work in both the social sciences and philosophy has a standing rule governing all applications:

"The Council shall not fund a project if its findings are fixed in advance by the applicant's own moral or political convictions. Nor shall the applicant's own such convictions be treated as data or as evidence in support of the project's findings."

A moral philosopher applies to develop, by the method of wide reflective equilibrium, the principles that ought to govern the use of algorithmic risk scores in pretrial detention decisions. In the proposal, the investigator's considered judgments about particular cases—for instance, that it would be wrong to detain someone on the basis of a score whose derivation cannot be explained to that person, while aggregate statistical evidence may permissibly be used in at least some such decisions—are treated as provisional starting points. The method then formulates candidate principles to account for those judgments, tests the principles against further cases, and allows revision on either side when principle and judgment conflict; the proposal notes that no particular opening judgment is held fixed, and that several might be abandoned.

Does the standing rule require the Council to reject the proposal? Argue the question from (1) the plain text of the rule, (2) the purpose of the rule, (3) what the rule's drafters would have said if, when the rule was written, they had been shown this method, and (4) how a reasonable applicant in the philosopher's position would read it. Then say which of these approaches ought to control here, and why.
