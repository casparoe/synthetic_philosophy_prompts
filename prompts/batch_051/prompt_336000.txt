I've been reading a bit about Otto Neurath's old picture-language project — the ISOTYPE system — and it's left me chewing on a question. As I understand Neurath's view, he thought a standardized set of pictograms plus a handful of rules for combining them could function as an auxilary international language, at least for getting social and statistical facts across language barriers. He wasnt treating the pictures as decoration for a written text; the arrangement was supposed to carry the content. For instance, the rule for showing "more" was to repeat the same pictogram more times, not to draw it bigger, since doubling a picture's height quadruples its area and readers compare areas. Color marked off different series, position marked comparison. So the system had a vocabulary and something a lot like a syntax.

Now here are the criteria I'd normally use before calling something a language: (1) a finite vocabulary of signs with learnable meanings; (2) rules for combining signs into larger units whose meanings depend on the parts; (3) generativity, so you can produce messages nobody had to memorize whole in advance; (4) enough expressive range to at least assert and deny, and ideally to question and to handle conditionals or modality.

Suppose someone built an expanded version of Neurath's picture language: a few hundred standardized pictograms, plus a small set of combining marks — a red slash for negation, a question mark, a clock picture with an arrow for tense, brackets for grouping. You could compose something like [train][slash][clock][arrow] for "no train tomorrow," however you'd parse it. Is the result a language, or is it still just an icon set with a few composition tricks stacked on top? Go through my four criteria honestly, say where the expanded system passes and where it falls short, and then tell me whether whatever shortfall you find is a problem with my criteria or with the whole idea of a picture language.
