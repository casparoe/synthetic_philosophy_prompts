For a graduate seminar of about twenty students in economics and philosophy of probability, I want a 75-minute in-class activity that makes participants feel the difference between an ensemble average and a time average in a way that they can later connect to Ole Peters's ergodicity economics. The central idea I want them to internalize is that a gamble or investment can look attractive when averaged across many parallel copies of an agent at one time, yet be ruinous for a single agent who repeats it over many periods, and that whether the relevant average is the ensemble or time average depends on the dynamic structure of the process.

Please design the activity so that participants run into this problem themselves rather than being told about it. I would like them to make repeated choices under uncertainty, record their own wealth over many rounds, and then compare their individual paths with the cross-sectional average of everyone in the room. A concrete suggestion is to have them repeatedly stake their entire current wealth on a multiplicative gamble, such as one that multiplies their wealth by 1.5 on heads and by 0.6 on tails, but I am open to a better design if it makes the phenomenon more visceral. Include clear rules, materials, a points-based payoff scheme that does not require real money, and a timeline for the 75 minutes. The payoff scheme should make the distinction salient without requiring risk-preference assumptions up front.

Then provide a debrief that maps what happened in the room onto the distinction between the ensemble-average growth rate and the time-average growth rate of a single path. The debrief should explain why the time-average behavior is better captured by the logarithm of wealth in a multiplicative setting, and it should connect this to Peters's claim that many economic problems have been incorrectly framed as if the ensemble average were the relevant one. Use the students' own generated data as much as possible, and end by stating precisely what would need to be true for the ensemble average to be the right guide: something like a single agent being able to diversify across many parallel worlds while also being able to rebalance resources across them.

Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this. For example, students may attribute the result to the particular payoffs, to one lucky participant distorting the room's average, or to the artificial assumption of staking all wealth each round. Give me concrete ways to handle each issue during the debrief without lecturing over the students' own discovery. Also be honest about what the activity cannot reproduce: especially the long-run behavior of an actual stationary non-ergodic process, the mathematical definition of ergodicity itself, and the practical question of how much an agent should care about time-average effects when they do not face full ruin.
