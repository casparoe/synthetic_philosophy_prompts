In my school's philosophy club, we spent two meetings on the question of whether a conscious decision can really be the cause of the bodily movement that follows it. One of the older students laid out a version of what he called the causal exclusion argument, and I still cannot decide whether the argument is drawing a profound conclusion or quietly assuming something. Here is how he presented it.

(1) When I decide to raise my arm and then raise it, the movement of my arm is a physical event with a physical cause in my brain and nervous system. (2) Whenever a physical event like an arm movement has a cause at all, the physical events leading up to it are sufficient on their own to bring it about, so the movement already has a complete physical cause. (3) If a physical event already has a complete physical cause, then nothing distinct from that cause can also be a cause of that same event, except in cases of overdetermination, where two independent causes each would have been enough by itself. (4) My decision is either identical with some event in that physical chain, or it is distinct from every event in the chain. (5) If it is distinct, then either my decision overdetermines the arm movement or it does not cause the movement; but ordinary arm raisings are not like a victim struck by two assassins at the same instant, in which each shot alone would have been fatal. (6) Therefore, if decisions are not identical with events in the physical chain, they do not cause our movements, which contradicts what we ordinarily believe about acting for reasons.

I suspect that the inference from (2) and (3) to (5) is doing most of the work, but I am not sure whether that means the argument is invalid or just that one of the premises is the place a defender of mental causation should attack. Is the argument valid as written? If it is not, exactly which step fails and why? If it is valid, which premise should be rejected first, and what happens to the argument once that premise is rejected?
