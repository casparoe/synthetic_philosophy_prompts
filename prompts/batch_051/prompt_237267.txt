You know Fearon's rationalist explanations for war—the puzzle that rational states should be able to reach ex ante bargains that avoid costly conflict, and his three mechanisms for why they don't: private information with incentives to misrepresent, commitment problems, and issue indivisibility. I want to apply that to the current US–China competition over AI as if it were a war, or at least a war-shaped bargaining failure. Not the military side exactly; the thing I'm interested in is the AI race itself, roughly from 2018 onward: export controls on advanced chips, the semiconductor supply chain, mutual suspicion about AGI, the AI supremacy rhetoric. I know the basics; no need to summarize the CHIPS Act.

Here's the task. First, do a careful mapping: who are the relevant players—are they unitary states, or does Fearon's framework break because the real actors are firms like TSMC, Nvidia, and OpenAI on one side and the CCP and Chinese firms on the other? What is the issue over which they're bargaining, or failing to bargain? Is there a war outcome, and what is it: actual kinetic conflict, total decoupling, or an all-out race to AGI without safeguards? What would the bargained settlement look like—some kind of arms control for compute, a treaty, or a mutual restraint norm?

Then run his three mechanisms on the case. For private information: what do the US and China privately know about their AI capabilities, their timelines, their willingness to accept risk, and why would they have incentives to misrepresent it? For commitment problems: if the US agreed to lift export controls, what prevents it from reimposing them once China's AI industry becomes dependent; if China promised not to use AGI for military coercion, what prevents it from doing so once it has the capability? Does the rapid shift in underlying power from AI make a commitment problem more severe than Fearon's shifting power examples because the shift is not just in military power but in economic and epistemic power? For indivisibility: is access to leading-edge chips or AGI-like capabilities indivisible in a way that prevents compromise, or is that actually more divisible than territory?

Then I want the residue report: where does Fearon's framework bend or break. His model assumes war is a costly lottery between states, but an AI race has externalities for all of humanity—an AI catastrophe could harm everyone, not just the loser. His actors don't have a single utility function; firms' incentives diverge from states'. His war isn't necessarily fought on a battlefield, and the costs might come later, like a slow-motion failure rather than a conventional war. Is this case a genuine instance of rationalist explanations for war, a metaphor, or an anomaly that shows Fearon's framework doesn't travel to post-military security competitions? And in the end: does running Fearon on the AI race make you more or less worried that US–China AI competition ends in somethign war-like?
