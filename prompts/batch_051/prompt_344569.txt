A friend and I got into a long argument the other night about whether it's ever okay to set aside positions for particular groups, and we kept going in circles. Every time one of us brought up a concrete system, the official defense of it seemed to be doing different things. In the US, at least the way universities defended race-conscious admissions until quite recently, the rationale was diversity — the educational value of a classroom with lots of different backgrounds — and people went out of their way to insist it was not a quota. In India, reservations for scheduled castes and tribes in government jobs and universities are set up as percentages and defended mainly as reparation for centuries of caste exclusion and as representation for groups that were locked out. In Norway, the law that required public limited companies to put at least forty percent women on their boards has been defended partly in terms of fairness but also a lot in terms of breaking up an old boys' club and using talent better, which sounds almost like an efficiency argument. It struck me that the mechanism — reserving or setting aside a share of positions — stays roughly the same, but the justification seems to bend toward whichever kind of unfairness a given society already accepts as real: diversity here, reparation there, efficiency somewhere else.

I'm trying to figure out whether that pattern has a name. I don't mean affirmitive action itself; I mean the way a policy's rationale gets fitted to the local theory of fairness until it sounds obvious, so the same mechanism can be sold three different ways in three different places. If there is an established term or literature for this, what exactly does it pick out, and how is it different from the boring claim that a policy can have more than one justification at once? If there isn't a term, what would a good one have to cover, and what cases would it exclude? I'm less interested in the label than in getting the edges of the phenomenon right.
