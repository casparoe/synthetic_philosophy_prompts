We've been going through possible worlds semantics in my epistemic logic group, and we hit an argument that seems to prove I already believe every consequence of arithmetic, including ones I can't even parse. I can't find the exit. Here it is:

(1) On the standard possible-worlds model, S believes p iff p is true in every world S regards as possible (every world S hasn't ruled out).
(2) Suppose S believes p and p entails q. Then every world S regards as possible is a p-world, so it's a q-world. So S believes q.
(3) So the model makes belief closed under entailment, whether we want it to be or not.
(4) I believe the usual axioms of arithmetic: the successor facts, induction, the basic behavior of addition and multiplication.
(5) Those axioms entail a huge pile of arithmetic I've never worked through: theorems about primes, divisibility, whether equations have whole-number solutions, you name it.
(6) By closure, I believe every one of those consequences.
(7) But I don't. Some of them I couldn't even state right now, let alone believe, and there are some I'd get wrong if you put a gun to my head.
(8) So either the possible-worlds picture is the wrong model of belief, or I do believe all of that without having any idea that I do — which sure feels like a trick with words.

I'd like to keep the possible-worlds model if I can, becuase it's basically our only clean semantics for belief ascriptions, but I also don't want to water down "believes" into "would assent after an ideal proof search." Which premise do I reject, and what does that rejection screw up downstream?
