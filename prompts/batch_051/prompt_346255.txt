At a reading group where some of us have been working through the philosophy of probability, two colleagues got into a long argument that nobody at the table could referee, and I'd like you to walk through it in detail. They were discussing objective Bayesianism, specifically whether the principle of maximum entropy (MaxEnt) can actually supply a uniquely rational prior from partial information.

(1) Priya defended MaxEnt. Her claim was that if all you know about a quantity is some constraint—say its mean—then the rational prior is the distribution with maximal entropy among all distributions satisfying that constraint. Any other distribution with the same mean, she argued, would encode additional statistical structure that your information does not justify. It would be importing knowledge you do not have. Maximum entropy, she said, is the uniquely noncommittal choice.

(2) Mateo objected that "noncommittal" is not a description-independent notion. Entropy is not invariant under redescribing the unknown. If you switch to new variables—for example, you work with the square or the logarithm of the quantity, or you carve the range into a different partition—the maximum-entropy distribution obtained in the new description differs from, and is generally not even the transform of, the one you obtained in the old description. The same state of knowledge then yields different "unique rational priors" in different representations. So MaxEnt cannot be delivering the one objectively rational prior.

(3) Priya replied that full re-description invariance is too much to demand. The original constraint itself says something specific. Knowing the mean of a quantity in certain units is information about that quantity, not about its logarithm, unless you also transform your knowledge into the new variables. MaxEnt, she said, is relative to the variables through which the information is stated. Choosing those variables is part of formulating the inference problem, not an arbitrary prior smuggled in afterward; the arbitrariness, if there is any, already entered when someone chose which observable to constrain.

(4) Mateo answered that this relocates the problem rather than solving it. In actual practice, he said, researchers often have only a vague piece of knowledge—something like "it is on roughly this scale" or "we have measured this functional"—and the choice of parameterization is part of what remains uncertain. Two reasonable researchers who describe the same background knowledge in different coordinates will reach materially different posteriors, which is exactly the kind of arbitrariness objective Bayesianism promised to eliminate. He added that the known repairs, such as the transformation-group arguments used to choose priors for location and scale parameters, cover only special symmetries and do not give a general answer to partition dependence.

(5) Priya conceded that MaxEnt is not a general invariance machine, but she said the right comparison is with available alternatives, not with an unreachable standard. Relative to a specified representation, MaxEnt prevents you from introducing information beyond your stated constraints and makes your assumptions inspectable by others. Reference priors and transformation-based methods have pathologies of their own. The point of objective Bayesianism, she said, is not the fantasy that priors are handed down by pure logic; it is the discipline of deriving them from stated information by a rule that colleagues can examine and criticize.

I genuinely could not decide how much each point lands, and neither of them would budge. Could you adjudicate the exchange move by move? Please say which claims survive, which need qualification, where each of them is strongest and weakest, and what a fair third party should conclude about whether MaxEnt supplies objective priors. Do not simply declare an overall winner; evaluate each of the five moves.
