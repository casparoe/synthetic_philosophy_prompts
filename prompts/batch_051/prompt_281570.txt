Here is a parallel I have been turning over and cannot decide about. The no-free-lunch theorems for supervised learning and optimisation state that, under a uniform weighting of all possible target functions or objective functions, no algorithm is better in expectation than any other. What makes one algorithm better than another in practice is not something intrinsic to the algorithm but the fact that it embodies assumptions — an inductive bias, a prior, a restricted hypothesis class — suited to the particular region of problem space we actually inhabit. Hume's problem of induction, as I understand it, makes a structurally similar claim about inference in general: any justification of the expectation that the unobserved will resemble the observed must itself presuppose the uniformity of nature it is supposed to establish, so no chain of reasoning can license induction from a neutral starting point. In both cases the source of success is a substantive, contestable commitment about what the world is like, and that commitment is not forced on us by logic alone.

The mapping I have in mind is this: the uniform average over all possible worlds in the theorem corresponds to the absence of any non-circular justification in the Humean problem, and an algorithm's inductive bias corresponds to the assumption of the uniformity of nature. Both appear to say that nothing works, and nothing is justified, without an assumption whose support cannot be supplied from within the system that uses it.

I can see a point where the analogy may come apart, though. The no-free-lunch theorems are a claim about expected performance under a specific weighting of possible worlds, one that gives no world more weight than any other, whereas the problem of induction is usually read as a claim about the justificatory status of a particular inference in a single actual world. It is not obvious that the claim that no algorithm is privileged across all worlds bears on the claim that no inference about this one world is justified, nor that the uniform weighting is the right way to represent the absence of a reason. That could make the analogy superficial exactly where it matters, or it could show that the problem of induction admits a decision-theoretic formalisation after all.
