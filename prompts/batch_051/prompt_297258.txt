I work at a climate-risk modeling startup. We build a set of coupled models — weather, hydrology, crop yield, and a thin economic layer — and sell the outputs to insurers and grain buyers. Every few weeks someone proposes adding fine-grained detail to one of the models: sub-daily rainfall, field-level crop varieties, individual grower decisions, insect counts per square meter. Right now the decisions come down to whichever manager argues loudest, and nobody can say in writing why we left something out. I want to stop doing that.

A physicist friend once sketched the idea that physics treats different scales as sort of autonomous: you can get big-scale predictions right without resolving the small scales; small-scale details usually just shift a few parameters rather than changing the form of the model; and physicists distinguish 'relevant' from 'irrelevant' parameters to know whether a tiny detail gets washed out or amplified as you zoom out. He called it effective field theory. I don't need the physics — I need the decision procedure that falls out of it. Please write a checklist my team can run on any proposal to add fine-grained detail to an existing model. The questions should go in order, each should say what counts as a good-enough answer to move on, it should say where the procedure stops with a no, and it should flag the points where we are making a judgment call rather than a technical determination. We can spend maybe an afternoon on a given proposal; the people running the checklist are modelers with master's-level quantitative training but no background in this stuff; and a nontechnical product manager signs off at the end, so the final step should produce a plain-language paragraph she can read.

Then run the procedure on two actual proposals: (a) adding explicit plant-to-plant competition to a regional yield model where yields currently come from a calibrated response function, and (b) adding daily soil-moisture dynamics to a seasonal runoff model that currently parameterizes soil moisture at the monthly scale. Also invent a third proposal where you think the procedure would tell us to include detail we would rather skip, and say why. End with an honest paragraph on what this procedure cannot do for us — the kind of proposal it will handle badly, and where following it would still leave us stuck.
