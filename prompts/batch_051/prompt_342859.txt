I have been arguing with friends about whether two communities with incompatible convictions can genuinely make a trade that leaves both better off without either side renouncing its principles, and I want to push the question out of the abstract. Consider the following scheme.

A municipality is divided between two groups over a recurring public practice: the playing of amplified music in the central square on a day that one group regards as a day of quiet, while the other group treats the freedom to play music on that day as part of ordinary civic life. The council refuses to settle the matter by majority vote, on the grounds that this lets whichever side is larger impose its calendar on the other at no cost to itself. Instead, each year the council fixes a total budget of permitted amplification—say, one hundred hours—deliberately set between the two groups' preferred levels. It issues one hundred one-hour tokens and distributes them by lottery, giving every adult resident an equal expected number. Tokens may be freely bought, sold, inherited, or permanently retired. A token surrendered to the council authorizes one hour of amplified music; a token retired without use simply reduces that year's total. The council enforces the rule that no amplification may occur without surrendering a token, but takes no further position on whether the practice is good or bad.

Walk through how this arrangement would actually behave. Who ends up buying and retiring tokens, and who ends up using them? Compare the equilibrium—in both distributional and welfare terms—to majority rule, to a uniform ban, and to an ordinary permit fee. Then consider the expressive effects. Does a musician's paying objectors for their tokens turn an assertion of majority culture into a consensual transaction, or does it convert the objectors' conviction into a price and thereby erode the very value the scheme was meant to protect? Finally, identify where the scheme gets gamed first: strategic registration of residents to capture tokens, narrow or broad definitions of the regulated activity, token brokers and outside buyers, a majority buying tokens to retire them as a de facto ban, a minority selling tokens while publicly denouncing the practice, and so on. Which of these failure modes can be fixed by adjusting the mechanism, and which suggest that the analogy between moral conflict and tradable pollution permits is doing too much work?
