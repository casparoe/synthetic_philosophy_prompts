I've been going back and forth on Wang Yangming's unity of knowledge and action, and I keep flip-flopping between thinking it's obviously right and obviously a word game. The view, as I understand it: genuine knowledge isn't a matter of being able to state the right principles; it's realized in conduct. If you say you know that helping your parents is right but don't do it, you don't fully know it — you only have something like a slogan, or a piece of secondhand opinion. What I like about this is that it dissolves a bunch of moral self-deception at one stroke: the cheater who "knows" cheating is wrong and keeps cheating isn't somehow knowing and failing to act; he doesn't know in the sense that matters. It also makes moral knowledge look less like a private mental state and more like a skill or a way of being, which feels true.

But my everyday experience pulls the other way. I know cardio would be good for me, I can give you the reasons, I endorse them when I'm calm, and at 6am I still dont go. That feels like a textbook case of weakness of will — the knowledge is there and the action doesn't follow. If the only way to save Wang Yangming is to say "then you didn't really know," I'm worried the view has become unfalsifiable, or has just redefined knowing so that it includes acting and then called that a discovery.

I don't want to be told which side wins. Give me the strongest version of both: first, how far can the identity be pushed before it collapses into a tautology, and what independent content it might still have; second, how much of ordinary akrasia survives if we accept the identity, and whether the "you didn't really know" move is a debunking of the intuition or a refusal to listen to it. Then tell me what would have to be true for my intuition to be the thing that goes, what would have to be true for the identity to be weakened or abandoned, and whether there's a reading of "know" on which both can be kept. Keep it under 400 words, and lead with your bottom line.
