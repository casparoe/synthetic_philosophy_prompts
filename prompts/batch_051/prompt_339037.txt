Below are a final-examination question and a student's answer from an upper-level philosophy course, in a unit on conceptual engineering and ameliorative analysis. Grade the answer as the examiner would: assign a single numeric mark out of 20, with no range and no statement that another mark would be equally defensible, and then give the point-by-point comments that justify that mark. The comments should assess both the student's accuracy about the material and the internal coherence of the answer, and they should make clear why the specific mark follows.

Question. The course distinguished three ways of approaching a concept such as woman. Project A asks what we already mean by the word. Project B asks what the concept picks out, or what its real nature is. Project C asks what concept we should use, given our purposes; this is the ameliorative or engineering project. Sally Haslanger's candidate definition of woman is, roughly, that a person is a woman just in case she is systematically subordinated along some dimension, and is marked for that subordination by observed or imagined bodily features presumed to indicate a female reproductive role. Explain the difference between Project A and Project C, using Haslanger's proposal about gender as the example. Then evaluate the objection that Haslanger changes the subject because her candidate definition does not capture what ordinary speakers mean by woman. Does the objection succeed when the project is explicitly ameliorative? Address both the meaning-targeting form and the extension-targeting form of the objection.

Student's answer. Haslanger is clearly doing Project C rather than Project A. Her proposal is a recommendation, not a report of ordinary usage, so it cannot be refuted merely by showing that her definition diverges from what speakers would say. The extension-targeting objection therefore misses its target: noting that the definition leaves out women who are not subordinated would matter only if she were claiming to report the preexisting extension, which she is not. The meaning-targeting objection likewise fails, because changing the subject is a criticism only of a project whose aim was to stay on the original subject. An ameliorative proposal should be judged by whether it serves its purpose—here, whether it makes gender hierarchy easier to see and criticize—not by fidelity to ordinary linguistic intuitions. Still, the proposal does have to count as a definition of the same word. If a revised concept preserves the connotation of woman, in particular the connection with female biology that Haslanger retains through the marking condition, then it is a definition of the same concept no matter how much the extension changes. And in the end, an ameliorative analysis succeeds only when the new definition turns out, on reflection, to state what the existing concept of woman really was all along, just more explicitly.
