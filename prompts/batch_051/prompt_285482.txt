I have been trying to make sense of the idea that one false theory can be closer to the truth than another, and I have collected some verdicts about small artificial cases that I would like you to unify.

Take the truth that a package arrived on Tuesday, by courier, at the front door. I judge that a theory saying it arrived on Tuesday, by courier, at the back door is exactly as close to the truth as one saying it arrived on Tuesday, by regular mail, at the front door: each gets two of three independent facts right and misses one. My working rule was therefore that closeness to truth is a matter of counting how many independent components a theory gets right. But my next two verdicts seem to break that rule.

If I take the first theory and add a true claim that has nothing to do with the delivery, such as that copper conducts electricity, I do not think the resulting package of claims is any closer to the truth about the delivery than the original was. Now consider the truth that the butler committed the murder in the kitchen. I judge that the claim that the butler committed the murder in the garden is closer to the truth than the claim that the gardener committed the murder in the kitchen, even though each is again wrong about exactly one component: my verdict seems to weight the identity of the culprit more heavily than the location, and I do not know how to justify that weighting without making truthlikeness objectionably interest-relative.

What account of closeness to truth, if any, captures all three verdicts? State your proposed principle precisely enough to apply to new cases, and say which of my verdicts it must reject if none captures all of them. Then test your account on a case I have not listed: a theory that gets an underlying general law exactly right while misdescribing each of its observed instances, versus one that gets every observed instance right while misstating the law. Please write your answer in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
