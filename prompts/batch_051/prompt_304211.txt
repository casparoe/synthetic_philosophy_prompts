I teach epistemology, and I am working through what a defensible account of testimonial justification should say — in particular, whether a hearer is ever entitled to believe another person's say-so as such, or whether any entitlement must be earned from the hearer's background evidence about the speaker, the topic, and the stakes. Before I commit to a position, I want to know whether my own verdicts about particular cases hang together. Here they are.

(1) At the airport, a stranger ahead of me in line says my flight has been moved from B12 to B4. I have no special reason to think he knows the situation and no reason to think he is trying to mislead me. I walk to B4 without checking the departures board. I do not think this is credulous.

(2) A friend with an excellent record of accurate observation tells me she saw a mountain lion in a suburban park where no confirmed sighting has been reported in fifty years. I accept that she saw something she genuinely took to be a large cat, and I do not lower my general estimate of her reliability. But I will not tell neighbors that there is a mountain lion in the park until there is a photograph, tracks, or a second sighting.

(3) A patient reports crushing chest pain and every test comes back normal. I think the physician should treat the report as true for the purposes of further investigation and care, and should not require an objective finding before taking it seriously.

(4) In a fraud trial, the defendant, confronted with his signature on the relevant transfer documents, testifies that he did not know the transfers were fraudulent. I think the jury would be right if this denial barely moved their confidence in his knowledge, even though he is the only direct witness to his own state of mind at the time.

(5) Two equally credentialed and equally scrupulous climate economists give sharply contradictory estimates of the net cost of a proposed policy. I do not adopt either estimate; I suspend judgment and treat the disagreement as removing any default entitlement I might otherwise have felt toward either expert's word.

(6) A used-car salesman tells me a car has never been in an accident. Although I can settle the matter only by paying an independent mechanic, I would not buy the car on his word. I treat his bare assertion as nearly weightless until it is checked.

(7) A ten-year-old tells his teacher that a caregiver hit him the previous evening. The teacher is in no better position than I am to verify the report, and the report could be false. Nevertheless, I think the teacher should act on it as true at once — reporting it through the proper channels — rather than wait for corroboration.

My question is whether there is a single, precisely stated principle about when and how far a hearer should believe a speaker that delivers all seven verdicts. If there is, I want the principle stated exactly, with each of its clauses motivated by the case or cases that force it; its verdict on one further case; and an honest statement of any of my seven verdicts it cannot accommodate without special pleading. The further case is this: an anonymous review on a booking site says a budget hotel has bedbugs, while a friend who stayed there six months earlier says he saw no sign of them and found the hotel clean. What should I believe, and with what confidence?

If no single non-gerrymandered principle captures all seven, tell me which of my verdicts I would have to revise, why those, and what the most defensible repaired set of verdicts and principle looks like. Where the principle you offer necessarily leaves room for judgment rather than yielding a mechanical answer, mark the place explicitly.
