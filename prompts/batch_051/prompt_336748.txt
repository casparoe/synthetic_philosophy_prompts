I am a novelist at work on a historical novel set among American abolitionists in the decades before the Civil War. Frederick Douglass appears in the book, and I want to render his position on the Constitution fairly rather than lean on a stock image of him. In the research I came across this paragraph in a popular survey of the period, and I do not want to build a characterization on it until I know how much of it to trust:

"Douglass's position on the Constitution was, in the end, a matter of tactics. As a young man he followed Garrison in treating the document as a pro-slavery compact that no honest abolitionist could support, and he held that it should be condemned rather than used. Then, in the early 1850s, Douglass reversed himself: having come to believe that the movement could accomplish more by voting and working within the political system, he began insisting that the Constitution, correctly read, never sanctioned slavery. The reversal was a practical concession to political reality, not a discovery about the text itself."

I am inclined to distrust the words "tactics" and "practical concession," but I am not historian enough to separate a fair compression from a subtle distortion. I would like an audit of this account: which claims about Douglass are accurate, which are defensible simplifications, and which are quietly wrong, with the reasoning a careful scholar would give. Please also be explicit about your confidence in each verdict and about where the genuine scholarly disagreement lies rather than where the paragraph is merely simplifying. And if the request itself is badly framed — for instance, if it is misleading to speak of Douglass as having one settled "view" across these years — tell me that plainly instead of pretending the paragraph can be sorted neatly into true and false lines.
