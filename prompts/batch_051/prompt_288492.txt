In the foundations seminar I am taking, we have been using a four-part test for deciding when a mathematical totality has been treated as an actual infinity rather than a merely potential one. The test runs: (1) the totality is presented as a single completed object that can be named, quantified over, and treated as a member of further collections, rather than only as a rule for producing more items; (2) membership in the totality is closed and determinate, not relativized to a stage or to how far a generation procedure has been run; (3) unqualified universal and existential statements over the totality are accepted as having determinate truth values in virtue of the whole domain; and (4) the totality is assigned a determinate cardinality and compared in size with other completed totalities.

Now consider the collection of all finite binary strings, S. Every member of S is finite: 0, 1, 00, 01, and so on, each one a string a finite machine could print and stop on. There is a perfectly decidable rule of membership, no largest string, and no finite list of strings that contains the whole collection. Formal language theory treats S as a single object: a language is a subset of S, S is said to be countably infinite, results are stated and proved under full universal quantification over finite strings, and the set of all languages over S is proved uncountable. But a potentialist can re-read almost all of this as talk about an open-ended ability to produce finite strings, refusing only the step where S itself is reified, assigned a cardinal number, and made a completed domain over which classical quantifiers are interpreted.

Go through the four criteria one at a time and say how S fares on each, being explicit about where a criterion is satisfied only because practitioners talk a certain way rather than because they have taken on the ontological commitment the criterion is meant to track. Then give an overall verdict: do the standard uses of S commit one to an actual infinity, or is S merely potentially infinite but dressed in set-theoretic language? Where my four-part test underdetermines the answer, say whether the defect lies in the test, in the case, or in the way the practice is usually described. Finally, tell me what would change your verdict — which argument or consideration would flip it from actual to merely potential or the other way around — and say where your reasoning is least confident.
