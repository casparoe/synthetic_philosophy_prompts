I organize a monthly discussion group for working engineers and data scientists in a rationalist-adjacent community; they are sharp and used to argument, but none of them has done philosophy of perception. Next month I am leading a ninety-minute session on whether photographs are transparent, in the specific sense philosophers argue about: whether looking at a photograph of a thing counts as literally seeing that thing, the way a mirror or telescope is normally treated as letting you see the thing, as opposed to seeing a representation of it. I am not asking you to explain the debate to me. I want you to build the session plan. What distinctions and concepts have to be introduced, in what order, so that the transparency thesis arrives as a live and intelligible option rather than a strange assertion; that is, what depends on what? Give me the single carrying example the whole discussion should orbit, and say what the example must have and must not have to do the work. Then map the misconceptions this group will predictably bring or form along the way: I expect at least a reflex that photographs are mechanical recordings and therefore somehow objective, and a confusion of transparency with accuracy or realism. Tell me exactly where in the sequence to head each one off. Tell me what to explicitly set aside so the session does not collapse into general epistemology of perception, and give me the one final question I can pose to test whether they understood the transparency claim rather than just rephrased it.
