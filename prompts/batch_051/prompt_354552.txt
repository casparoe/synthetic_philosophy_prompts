A city's municipal archive will operate under the following rules. No record deposited by a resident may ever be destroyed or altered. Any resident may deposit one self-authored account per year, no longer than one page, about any event or condition in the city's public life; deposits enter a public chronological ledger in the order in which they are received. A deposit becomes searchable and receives cataloguing metadata only if it gathers twenty-five co-signatures from other residents within ninety days of deposit. In addition, the archival staff may promote at most five unindexed deposits per month to indexed status, chosen solely on their own judgment of historical significance; all other deposits remain in the ledger, unindexed, in submission order. If a deposit is challenged as false or defamatory, it is not removed: a redacted copy takes its place in the public ledger while the unredacted original is sealed for seventy-five years. Treat this system as a mechanism for manufacturing a historical record, not as a debate about what deserves to be preserved. Analyze how it shapes who gets recorded and who becomes a silence in the sources: the incentives it creates for residents, organized groups, and the archivists themselves; where it will be gamed first; what the searchable archive ends up mirroring; and what a historian working fifty years later would conclude about the city from it.
