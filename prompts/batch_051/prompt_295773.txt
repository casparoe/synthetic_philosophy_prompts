I teach a third-year course called Philosophy for Programmers—about 24 students, mixed CS and philosophy majors, one 90-minute session. I want an activity that makes them actually feel why mutation is hard to think about, rather than me just asserting it. The confusion I want to surface: they tend to treat a variable as though it is the object, so after `y = x` they expect a copy, and then mutating `y` surprises them. And if you mutate an object in place and ask "is it still the same object?" they either say obviously yes or obviously no, without noticing there's two different readings of "same"—same identity vs. same contents.

Design a runnable classroom game or simulation—with concrete rules, roles, materials I can actually get (sticky notes, cards, whiteboard, whatever), a payoff scheme if it helps but no real money, and a timeline for the 90 minutes—where the participants themselves run into aliasing and in-place mutation and have to make a call about whether something is "the same thing." The point should hit them before any lecture; I'll do the naming afterwards. The takeaway I'm aiming for: after the debrief they can say in their own words why "x changed" and "the object x points to changed" are seperate claims, why a variable name is a route to an object rather than the object itself, and why equality (== vs .equals, or identity vs. value) becomes a slippery live question once mutation shows up.

Then give me a debrief that maps specific moments from the activity onto those distinctions—binding vs. object identity, aliasing, value equality vs. identity equality, persistence through change (the old Theseus's ship problem restated for mutable data).
