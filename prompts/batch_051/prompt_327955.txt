I host a small philosophy of mind podcast, and next week's guest is a philosopher known for defending heterophenomenology as the right method for a science of consciousness. On his view, the science of consciousness should collect subjects' first-person reports, but treat those reports as beliefs about how things seem to the subject and as texts to be interpreted, while remaining neutral about whether the items represented in those reports—qualia, a self, the contents of the subject's heterophenomenological world—correspond to anything real. Draft eight to ten interview questions, counting each primary question and each follow-up separately, that go beyond having the guest restate the method. I want at least one primary question, with a follow-up, on each of the following four pressure points; for each, first give the reply the guest is most likely to give, then give the follow-up that presses on the weakest point in that reply: (1) the objection that heterophenomenology is behaviorism in thin disguise, because it only ever works with publicly observable verbal behavior and never recovers the first-person ontology of experience; (2) the method's privileged reliance on language, and whether it has anything to say about preverbal infants, nonhuman animals, or subjects who cannot report; (3) whether its policy of bracketing the real existence of reported experiences leaves it unable in principle to distinguish a conscious subject from a hypothetical reporter who behaves identically but has no experience at all; and (4) how it handles subjects whose reports are confabulated, delusional, or otherwise unreliable, and whether the need to interpret reports by attributing beliefs and rationality to subjects brings back exactly the theoretical commitments the method was meant to avoid. The remaining questions should pursue adjacent tensions in the view, not generic biography or inspiration.
