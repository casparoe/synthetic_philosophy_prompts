I am a PhD student in moral philosophy working on the mechanisms behind large-scale changes in moral norms. I want you to compare Bernard Williams's treatment of how ethical confidence can be lost with Kwame Anthony Appiah's honor-based account of moral revolutions. On Williams's picture, the modern morality system—with its emphasis on obligation, blame, and universalizability—is a historically contingent formation, and our confidence in its central concepts might someday be lost in much the way earlier societies lost confidence in slavery or dueling; ethical change, on this view, is not primarily a matter of a better argument defeating a worse one. Appiah, by contrast, argues that moral revolutions such as the end of dueling, footbinding, and British slavery occurred when the practices became attached to dishonor, so that participants could no longer maintain their self-respect and social standing through them. I want you to evaluate whether Williams's notion of lost confidence supplies a deeper explanation of the phenomenon Appiah identifies, or whether Appiah's account shows that Williams's picture is too retrospective and too thin about the actual social psychology of change. Which of the two better captures what is going on in the agents themselves when a once-accepted practice ceases to feel like something one can decently engage in? In your response, lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning; do not include any preamble or restate the question.
