I have been collecting my own reactions to political situations in which someone says that the people have willed something, and I find that I have firm verdicts about the cases but no principle that yields all of them. I would like you to work out whether there is a single principle behind my verdicts, and if there is not, to tell me which of my verdicts it would be most rational for me to abandon. My tentative view is something like this: the will of the people is not a polling fact or a sum of individual preferences; it is the output of a recognized, binding procedure—an election or a referendum—in which a question is settled after open contestation and after the losers have had a real chance to win. But I also think no procedure can constitute a rights-destroying majority as the people's will, because a measure that strips a group of equal standing no longer treats them as part of the people whose will is being expressed.

Here are my reactions.

1. A country held a binding referendum, with high turnout, on leaving a regional trade-and-movement bloc. The leave side won 52–48, in a campaign in which both sides said in advance that the result would settle the question. Within two years, as the costs became visible, public opinion moved to roughly 54% for staying. I believe the government was right to treat the original result as decisive rather than pausing or re-running the vote in light of the later polls.

2. A country held a referendum, honestly administered and with similar turnout, on whether members of a long-established religious minority may hold national public office. It passed 52–48. I believe the result should not be implemented, and I think I would hold that line even if it had passed with 70%.

3. Reliable polling has shown 65–70% support for a decade for decriminalizing minor drug possession, but the two dominant parties both oppose the policy and have kept it off every ballot. I think the public has a legitimate grievance, and the parties are wrong not to enact it; but I do not think the unmet polling majority is itself "the will of the people," because the question has never been put through an election or referendum in which the policy was actually at stake.

4. A party fought an election on a fully specified manifesto whose centerpiece was a sharp increase in a broad-based tax, and won a landslide. Polls taken after the election show a majority opposed to the tax. I believe the government has a mandate to pass the tax anyway, because the tax was the advertised question and the electorate answered it.

5. A president, elected on a platform that never mentioned the idea, signs an executive order expanding a border wall program at great expense and says he is doing what the people demand. Post-signing polls show 55% opposed and 38% in favor. I believe this is not the will of the people; it is a claim about the people with no procedure behind it.

6. A constitutional court invalidates a counterterrorism law, passed by a large parliamentary majority and supported by 70% of respondents in polls, on the ground that it permits indefinite detention without charge. I do not think the court is thwarting the people's will. I think the majority may be large, but it cannot will the elimination of the conditions under which it counts as a majority of a self-governing people.

7. A well-disciplined movement with support consistently around 30% stages prolonged highway and port blockades for an emergency climate measure, saying it represents "the people" against corrupt elites. I think this is false: the blockades may be a legitimate protest tactic, and the cause may be right, but the movement does not thereby express the people's will.

8. The case I cannot settle is this. A randomly selected citizens' assembly of 150 people, chosen by lot, spends six weeks hearing evidence and deliberating, and votes 62–38 to adopt a system of proportional representation. Public support in polls is only about 45%, with a large bloc undecided, and the issue was never central in any election. Sometimes I think the assembly's considered vote is a better expression of what the people would will under decent conditions than any poll or election would be; sometimes I think the assembly was never authorized to decide, and so its vote is not the people's will at all.

I notice that cases 1 and 2 look superficially inconsistent—same margin, opposite conclusions about whether to implement—and cases 3 and 4 look like mirror images of each other: a persistent majority that has never won a procedure, and a procedure that has won despite a contrary polling majority. I need to know what feature is doing the work.

Please give me the most principled account you can that captures my settled verdicts, or as many of them as possible. State the principle precisely, tie each clause to the case that forces it, test my tentative principle against the cases, and repair it where it fails. Then say explicitly which of my settled verdicts the repaired principle cannot accommodate. Finally, apply the account both to the assembly case (8) and to a further case I have not yet formed a view on: a petition signed by a majority of registered voters within a week of a viral online campaign, demanding the immediate repeal of a recently enacted climate law.
