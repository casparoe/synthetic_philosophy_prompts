For this assignment, consider the familiar use of the grandfather paradox as a reductio against backwards time travel. The standard version runs as follows: if backwards time travel were possible, a traveler could kill her own grandfather before one of her parents was conceived, making her existence and her nonexistence both obtain in a single history. That argument appears to depend on there being one fixed past in which the relevant events either happen or do not. Now suppose the correct background physics is a no-collapse quantum theory with branching histories, so that an intervention in the past realizes a different branch of the total state rather than altering a unique history from which the traveler came. Does the grandfather paradox still refute backwards time travel? If it does not, identify exactly which premise gives way and whether any weaker impossibility claim survives; if it does, explain why branching makes no philosophical difference. Please answer in American English, and be careful to separate what is settled in the literature from what is genuinely contested among specialists, rather than presenting one contested interpretation as the consensus.
