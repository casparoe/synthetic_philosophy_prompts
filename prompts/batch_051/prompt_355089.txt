You are a poet who also has a working command of Sartre's early existentialism, and you are composing a poem for a joint philosophy and poetry seminar on the thesis that existence precedes essence. Think through the poem's design before you write it; treat the formal constraints below as philosophical material, not as decoration.

Write a Petrarchan sonnet in the voice of the paper-knife from Sartre's lecture Existentialism Is a Humanism — the object whose maker conceives its design and purpose before it is produced, so that its essence arrives before its existence. Write the sonnet in iambic pentameter, fourteen lines, with an octave rhyming ABBAABBA and a sestet on a CDECDE or CDCDCD pattern, and place the volta at line 9. Treat the sonnet form itself as a predetermined essence imposed on the poem: the pattern is fixed before the poem exists, in something like the way the paper-knife's nature is fixed before the knife exists.

In the octave, let the paper-knife describe its condition with precision and a controlled pathos: its meaning is assigned rather than achieved; it has never had to choose what it is; its purpose was settled without it. Let it register, perhaps with envy or longing, the contrast with a human being, who exists first and must define itself through its choices. Avoid turning the octave into a lecture; let the knife's situation show through image, apostrophe, and tone.

At the volta, pivot away from this comparatively simple contrast and into the deeper difficulty. The knife, or the poem itself, should realize that the very longing for a self-chosen existence can only be expressed here under a form that was fixed in advance — that the poem, like the knife, cannot speak except as the kind of thing it was already made to be. Let this self-awareness at line 9 enact the standard charge of incoherence against the Sartrean thesis: that the claim that there is no human nature, and that existence precedes essence, seems itself to state something essential about human beings — namely, that they are the kind of being burdened with radical self-definition, always already in the position of having to choose themselves. The sestet should make that bind felt and performed rather than merely asserted, with the fixed rhyme scheme doing some of the work of confinement. The closing couplet should leave the paradox open rather than dissolving it into consolation or resolution.

A reader who knows Sartre should recognize both the paper-knife example and the objection being staged, and should find the poem's use of the form exact rather than decorative; a reader who does not know Sartre should still be able to follow the poem and feel the trap it describes.
