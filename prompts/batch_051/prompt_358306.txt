ok so i have a rule i follow and my friends tell me it's insane but i think it's just logic. my rule is: i don't count on a group actually doing the thing that's obviously best for the group, unless the decision has been made out loud in front of everyone involved, with everyone acknowledging it — and i don't mean 'everyone already knows it's best and everyone knows everyone knows it's best', i mean actually said in the shared space, together, so that everyone saw everyone else see it. here's the kind of thing that made me adopt it. in student council we had to pick which of us would give a speech at an assembly. it was obvious to all five of us, i think, that one particular person was the best speaker; we'd each said so in one-on-one conversations, and each of us probaby knew the others thought so too. but nobody had said it in a full meeting. when the deadline got close, two people still were not willing to commit, because although everyone thought the same thing, nobody had seen everyone else commit to acting on it, and if there is any chance that other people will hesitate, the safe move is to hesitate too. we nearly missed the deadline. after that i started treating plans as not-real until they pass through a public moment where everyone sees everyone else see the plan. my friend says this is a distinction without a difference: if everyone knows everyone knows, that's the same as it being out loud. i don't think it is, because in a group bigger than two, each person can know the plan and still not be sure that the others are sure that everyone will actually follow through. but my rule has real costs, it slows things down and people call it bureaucracy. is it defensible as a general policy about group reasoning?
