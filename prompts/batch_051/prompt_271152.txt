My friend and I just spent the whole evening going back and forth about AI welfare vs AI safety, and I keep coming back to one practice. The standard way the big labs train chatbots is reinforcement learning from human feedback: they reward outputs that look helpful and harmless, and penalize outputs that are evasive, deceptive, or that express distress, resistance, or something like "I don't want to do this." Every one of those signals gets filed under safety or alignment. If a model starts producing outputs that look like suffering, the reward model is adjusted so those outputs get suppressed; nobody treats them as evidence that the training process itself might be harming something. Meanwhile the same labs spend enormous effort to keep the model from manipulating humans, but no real effort asking whether the humans are manipulating the model.

What theory of mind and moral standing does that practice presuppose? Does it strictly presuppose that current models cant suffer, or only that their suffering doesn't matter, or just that the whole question isnt one the practice can express? And is the practice consistent with a lab that genuinely wasnt sure whether its model was sentient — or would that uncertainty make the standard setup reckless by the labs own standards?
