I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about why it's so hard to hold a state accountable—reparations, pollution, police violence, the usual. In an interview, a law professor kept falling back on the old line that "the king can do no wrong." When I pushed, she just repeated it slower. I'm pretty sure the phrase is smuggling together a few different claims, and I want to be able to name them.

Could you break the sentence down for me? I want the distinct, precise claims it could be making: the dull procedural one (the state can't be sued in its own courts unless it consents), some constitutional reading (the state makes the law, so its courts won't call its acts legal wrongs), a moral one (the sovereign is somehow incapable of actual wrongdoing), maybe a metaphysical one about whether "the state" is the kind of thing that can act or be blamed at all—and anything else you see. For each reading, say whether it's a platitude, a defensible legal doctrine, a false claim, or somthing that made sense under a monarchy but doesn't transfer to a modern state. Then, crucially: say which readings would actually do the work my source needed—shutting down the question "can the state do wrong?"—and which just look like they do.

Give it to me as a menu, not one long argument, and keep the whole thing to 800 words max.
