I'm a journalist covering legal affairs and I've ended up teaching a one-off evening class on statutory interpretation for journalism postgrads. I set this take-home question, but I'm not sure wether I can mark it reliably because my own instincts are a bit rusty. The question was:

"Explain the core difference between textualism and purposivism as approaches to statutory interpretation. Then use a hypothetical statute of your choosing to assess whether a judge who describes herself as a purposivist is entitled to depart from the text when the text's plain meaning leads to a result the legislature probably did not want. Be precise about what 'the text' includes in each approach."

One student handed in this answer:

"Textualism and purposivism are rival theories about how judges should read statutes. Textualists believe the meaning of a statute is fixed at the time of enactment and is determined by the ordinary public meaning of its words. They reject legislative history out of principle, because it is unenacted and often unreliable. Purposivists believe the judge's job is to give effect to the purpose the legislature was trying to achieve, and so they read the text in light of that purpose, using the statute's title, the problem it addresses, and legislative materials such as Hansard.

Take the Dog Fouling (Public Spaces) Act 2025, a fictitious statute that makes it an offence for a person to bring a dog into an enclosed children's play area. A blind woman enters with her guide dog. The plain meaning of 'dog' includes a guide dog, so a textualist would have to convict her, which is absurd. A purposivist would see that the Act's purpose is to stop dogs fouling play areas, and would hold that Parliament did not intend to criminalise guide dogs, because guide dogs are highly trained, their owners depend on them, and they cannot simply be left outside the gate. Purposivism is therefore clearly better, because it stops the law producing silly results.

Textualism is sometimes defended on the ground that it constrains judges, but that is overstated. The plain meaning of 'dog' is not plain once you look at context, and textualists also use canons of construction, which let them pick whichever answer they like. Purposivism is more honest because it admits judges are doing policy when they interpret statutes, and it lets them reach the just result. The main criticism of purposivism—that it invites judges to legislate—fails because judges are not legislators and can only use purpose to resolve genuinely ambiguous text."
