I am giving a paper next month at a law school's criminal law theory colloquium, defending the following thesis: the paradox of blackmail dissolves once we analyze blackmail as an extortionate proposal rather than as the conjunction of a permissible disclosure and a permissible request for money. My central claim is that a conditional proposal can be coercive even when the act threatened is one the speaker has a right to perform. On my account, the coercive force of a proposal depends on the speaker's intention to extract a transfer by presenting as a threat an outcome whose practical salience the speaker has deliberately manufactured, together with the fact that the addressee's baseline choice set is made worse by the proposal itself; it does not require the threatened act to be independently wrongful. Blackmail, on this view, is wrong not because disclosure would be wrong or because selling silence is wrong, but because the blackmailer deliberately frames a truthful disclosure as the price of nonpayment, thereby extracting a payment the victim would not otherwise make. I distinguish blackmail from ordinary hard bargaining and from legitimate payments to suppress unflattering information by looking at the structure of the proposal: in blackmail, the speaker creates the choice between paying and suffering the disclosure, whereas in an ordinary offer there is no engineered fallback consisting in the speaker's own threatened action.

The room will be roughly half law-and-economics scholars and half moral philosophers who work on coercion and consent, with a few criminal law doctrinalists. The law-and-economics group tends to treat the fact that the blackmailer had a right to disclose as close to a knockdown point, and they think of blackmail primarily in terms of incentives and the efficient pricing of secrecy. The philosophers are sympathetic to the conclusion that blackmail is coercive but will press hard on the baseline problem: why should the victim's situation before the proposal, rather than the situation after disclosure, fix the baseline once we grant that the blackmailer was entitled to reveal the information? The doctrinalists will want to know whether my account tracks the structure of actual blackmail statutes and whether it can distinguish blackmail from settlement bargaining, prepublication review agreements, and other reputation-management transactions the law permits.

Write my Q&A prep sheet: the eight or nine objections I should actually expect from this specific audience, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way this audience would phrase it, with a note on which faction is most likely to raise it, a two-minute reply, and an honest note on where that reply is softest. Tell me what I should concede up front, and include the question I most hope nobody asks, phrased as it would actually be asked, with a brief explanation of why it would cause more trouble than it first appears.
