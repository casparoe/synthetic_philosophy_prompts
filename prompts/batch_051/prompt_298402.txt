For a metaphysics seminar I'm TAing, I need a handout on what a serious solution to the combination problem for panpsychism actually has to do, rather than hand-wave at it. The setup I want the class to internalize: panpsychism says the fundamental stuff has experiential, conscious-ish properties all the way down, but it's then not obvious how a bunch of tiny subjects (or proto-phenomenal bits) turn into one unified stream like the one I've got. So here's the task: write the list of requirements a theory has to meet before you'd say it has solved the combination problem. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are just preferences of particular camps (micropsychists, cosmopsychists, neutral-monist types, people who think the real issue runs the 'decomposition' direction instead).

The part I most want you to press on: I suspect some of these requirements are jointly unsatisfiable. It'd be natural to demand, say, that (a) the micro-level subjects are real and keep their own point of view, (b) the macro-level subject is genuinly one subject and not a comittee, (c) phenomenal character isn't explained by relabeling something non-experiential, and (d) the view doesn't end up making every aggregate conscious — a rock, a storm, a corporation, whatever has parts. If some subset of that can't be met all at once, given the standard objections in that area (the grain problem, the boundary problem, the subject-summing problem, the old mind-dust worry), say exactly which requirements conflict and how far you can get by dropping one while saving the others. Also: which single requirement does the most work to exclude candidate solutions? You don't need to score every contender; a quick word on one or two is fine.
