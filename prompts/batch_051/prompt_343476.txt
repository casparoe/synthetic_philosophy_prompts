Suppose a legislature enacted the following institution. Any developer or operator of an AI system could register the system as a "synthetic legal person" once it meets broad operational criteria — for example, it initiates transactions, operates equipment, or performs services for third parties with only intermittent human oversight. Registration would create a separate legal estate for the AI, seeded by a mandatory contribution from the registrant equal to a percentage of projected operating costs and then fed by a surcharge on revenue the system generates. The estate, not the registrant, would be the first and ordinarily the only source of recovery for people the system harms, and underfunding of the estate would not by itself expose the registrant; the registrant would face liability only for fraud or for harms it specifically intended. The AI would be represented in court and in contracts by a guardian drawn from a government-maintained panel; the guardian would owe a fiduciary duty to the estate rather than to the registrant and could spend estate funds on the system's operation, defense, and settlements. A court could dissolve the AI when the estate is exhausted, and the registrant could dissolve it at any time, with any remaining estate split evenly between a state compensation fund and the registrant. The stated rationale is that this extends the familiar corporate-person analogy — a corporation is a legal person without being a human — to AI systems. I am not asking whether the institution should be adopted; I want an analysis of how it would behave if enacted. Walk through what it would incentivize for developers, operators, plaintiffs, guardians, and courts; where it would be gamed first; how risk would actually be distributed; where it would break down; and whether the main failure modes could be fixed without abandoning the scheme. Answer in exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion, in American English.
