Here's an asymmetry I keep tripping over. In ordinary criminal justice, a punishment that literally never ends is the canonical example of disproportionality: if a judge gave someone life without parole for shoplifting, we'd call the sentence unjust even if the judge acted within their formal powers. But the same structure — finite offense, never-ending sentence — is a mainstream answer to the problem of hell, and it's held to be perfectly compatible with divine justice rather than a monstrous exception to proportionality. Sure, there are candidate disanalogies: the standing of the offended party, the supposed gravity of sinning against an infinite being, the idea that the punishment is in some sense self-chosen, whatever. I want to know whether any of them actually do the work, or whether the whole thing is just motivated reasoning downstream of a doctrine people commited to for other reasons. So: is the asymmetry justified? If you'd keep it, give me the strongest version and then tell me which nearby case puts the most pressure on it.
