Act as a professional philosopher whose specialty is the metaphysics and semantics of vagueness, and who can state both epistemicism and supervaluationism in their strongest, most charitable forms. Think carefully before you write, and begin with some private brainstorming: first list, for each camp, the five or six commitments it would least want to see misstated, then use that list to discipline every formulation in the joint statement.

Two research programs have agreed to participate in an adversarial collaboration about vagueness. The first position, epistemicism, holds that vague predicates have sharp boundaries that are unknowable to us, whether in practice or in principle: in a sorites series there really is a last heap and a first non-heap, and our inability to locate the cut-off is an epistemic limitation rather than a semantic absence. The second position, supervaluationism, holds that the meaning of a vague predicate underdetermines its extension: meaning fixes a range of admissible precisifications, and a sentence is true simpliciter just in case it comes out true on every admissible precisification, false simpliciter just in case it comes out false on every one, and otherwise neither true nor false — so that borderline predications are truth-valueless.

Draft the document this collaboration would produce, in the following form. First, a numbered list of substantive agreed claims about the phenomena of vagueness — about the sorites, about what a theory of vagueness must explain, and, importantly, about the difference between the law of excluded middle and bivalence, which both parties can sign without hedging, though for different reasons. Second, a disagreements table in which each row states the exact point of divergence in wording each side would accept for its own side: the epistemicist's entry must be one an epistemicist would sign unforced, and likewise for the supervaluationist; include at least one row about the nature and source of the boundary, one about the status of our ignorance of it, and one about what borderline cases are. Third, a statement of the genuine cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side, with, for each crux, a consideration or test both sides agree would bear on it, and each side's prediction recorded in advance.

In constructing the cruxes, do not remain at the survey level. Think hard about higher-order vagueness; about whether the claim that meaning is determined by use discriminates between the two positions or only appears to; about whether the epistemicist should say the unknowability of the cut-off is a necessary limitation on finite minds or a contingent limitation of our cognitive situation; and about whether supervaluationism can give a satisfying account of what we are claiming to be ignorant of when we say "there is a cut-off but I do not know where it is." Include at least one proposed diagnostic case that the two sides have not endlessly rehearsed — for example, a forced binary classification of a finely graded series repeated under varying stakes by multiple competent speakers — and state, in advance, what pattern of responses each side would count as evidence against its own view.

Finally, say explicitly which residual disagreements are substantive and which, once the wording is made precise, you judge to be merely verbal. Name the point, if there is one, on which the two sides are closer than either side's public rhetoric admits. The output should be a constructive text that both parties could put their names to, with the remaining disagreement made exact — not a verdict on which theory is true.
