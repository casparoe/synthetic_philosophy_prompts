I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, writing a feature on private claims to the orbital commons. In the Second Treatise of Government (chapter V, section 27), Locke says that one who mixes his labour with something taken from the common state of nature makes it his property, but only "at least where there is enough, and as good, left in common for others." Suppose a satellite operator has obtained, through international coordination, the exclusive right to the last prime geostationary slot above the equator whose footprint covers a large, populous region; no remaining unassigned slot can serve that region with comparable coverage or reliability. The operator has invested heavily in building and launching a satellite for the slot and has offered the two neighbouring states inexpensive access to its capacity, though they would prefer to operate their own satellites there. Locke's examples of acorns, land, and water did not contemplate a finite, non-replaceable location that is useful only through advanced technology. Does the proviso permit this appropriation? Work through the bare text, the purpose of the clause and the harm it aims to prevent, what Locke would most plausibly say if he were told how geostationary slots work, and what a reasonable reader takes the phrase to require; then say where these readings diverge and which one should govern.
