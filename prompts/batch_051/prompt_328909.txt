I've been teaching Dennett's three stances in an intro philosophy module, and in a separate argument with a friend about whether an LLM "lied" to me I couldn't stop seeing the same structure. The physical stance maps to the weights-and-forward-pass level of AI description: high-dimensional matrices, attention heads, token logits. The design stance maps to the training story: next-token prediction on a huge corpus, plus RLHF (training on human preference rankings) to be helpful and harmless. The intentional stance maps to the everyday discourse that treats the model as an agent that "knows", "wants to answer", "believes the user is asking about X", or "is being evasive".

I'm not sure whether this is a real structural match or just relabeling. Dennett's two big claims, as I understand them, are roughly: (1) the intentional stance is justified by its predictive purchase, not by discovering internal representations of beliefs; and (2) any system whose behavior you can predict this way is, to that extent, an intentional system, whatever its parts do. If that's right, then asking whether an LLM literally has beliefs is the wrong question: it has beliefs when, and to the extent that, taking the stance pays. But that seems to make belief-attribution depend partly on us and on what we find useful, which my friend rejects. My friend says Dennett is only entitled to the stance if the system is rationally coherent enough that beliefs and desires earn their keep, and LLMs fail because they are next-token predictors that contradict themselves, don't update the way agents do, and can be prompted into saying they believe things they obviously don't.

Where does the parallel actually break? Is Dennett's idea of a "real pattern" doing the relevant work here, and if so, is "the model wants to answer" a real pattern or just a handy fiction? And is my friend adding a rationality condition Dennett didn't add, or did Dennett smuggle it in? Please end your answer with a one-sentence summary as the last line.
