Last evening a friend and I had a long, unresolved argument about whether a company can itself be morally blamed — genuinely blamed, as distinct from being fined, sued, or regulated — when serious harm flows from its ordinary procedures, such as a bank whose incentive structure leads branch staff to systematically mis-sell products even though no single employee planned the whole pattern. My friend tried to settle the argument with one sentence: "Corporations are people, so the company itself is the proper object of blame." I have heard that slogan more often in political arguments about corporate rights, and it seemed to me that he was borrowing its force for a different job. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims that "corporations are people" might be used to express in the context of the question whether a company can be blamed? For each candidate claim, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false; indicate which reading people who repeat the slogan usually mean; and then say which of these readings, if any, would actually support blaming the company itself rather than only the individuals inside it — that is, whether his one-sentence argument went through.
