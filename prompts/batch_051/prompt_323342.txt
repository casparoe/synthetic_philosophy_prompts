I run a small film club, and next week we're discussing John Ford's The Man Who Shot Liberty Valance. I don't want the evening to become just another argument about whether it's a good Western. Lately I've been turning over a problem the film seems built around: when does a story a community keeps telling about its beginnings become history, and what is lost or gained when the story and the facts come apart? In the film, a man's entire public life is founded on a shooting that did not happen the way everyone believes; the man who actually fired the shot stays silent, and an editor later makes a deliberate choice to keep the printed record false rather than correct the legend. I'd like a reading of the film through that problem, using the old debates over whether there is a real historical core behind the Trojan War, King Arthur, and the Exodus as points of comparison rather than as subjects in themselves — I don't need a survey of the scholarship. I'm after a two-way reading: where does the question illuminate the film's scenes and choices, and where does the film resist any simple answer — for instance, does it treat a founding legend as merely useful falsehood, or does it suggest the legend carries a truth the written record cannot? And, to be honest about it, where does the analogy between the film and those ancient traditions break down, and what does the film add to the problem that arguing over Troy or Arthur or the Exodus alone does not? Please stay grounded in specific scenes rather than quoting dialogue, since none of us will have the script in front of us.
