I have been reading about higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia, and I keep coming back to one concrete case: the decision to launch the space shuttle Challenger in January 1986. The shape of the case, as I understand it, is this. On the night before the launch, engineers from the contractor responsible for the solid rocket boosters reasoned from data on O-ring erosion in prior flights that the unusually cold temperatures forecast for the morning made the seals likely to fail, and they recommended delaying the launch. Over the course of a tense teleconference, under pressure from NASA managers and from their own management, the contractor withdrew that recommendation and instead endorsed the launch; the shuttle launched the next morning and broke apart seventy-three seconds later when an O-ring seal failed. I am not asking for a retelling of the historical narrative, and I am not asking about the engineering as such.

What I want is a disciplined epistemological analysis of the decision through the lens of the literature on higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia. Take the engineers' initial no-launch recommendation as a first-order conclusion drawn from concrete data, and treat the surrounding pressures — the insistence that they prove failure rather than merely show risk, the shifting of the burden of proof, the organizational incentives to keep the launch schedule, the suspicion that their caution was ordinary engineering conservatism — as higher-order evidence about, or at least purported higher-order evidence about, the reliability of that conclusion. Then work through the case in both directions. First, by the lights of the main positions in the higher-order-evidence debate, were the engineers rationally permitted, required, or forbidden to revise their no-launch conclusion when they did? Second, was the eventual state — endorsing the launch while presumably still believing that the evidence did not support that endorsement — an instance of epistemic akrasia, and if so, does the irrationality belong to the engineers, to the managers, or to the decision structure as a whole? Third, say which features of the episode the framework explains cleanly — for example, the slide from being unable to prove failure to treating the system as safe enough — and which features it distorts or simply cannot see, such as the fact that the relevant epistemic agent is partly a group with authority gradients and career incentives.

Finally, tell me what the case does to the theory. Does it make the ban on epistemic akrasia more credible, or does it reveal that the ban is tailored to individual reasoners assessing their own evidence in isolation, and that it has little traction once we move into institutional settings where evidence, incentives, and authority cannot be pried apart?
