In his essay *Anarchical Fallacies*, Jeremy Bentham examines the French Declaration of the Rights of Man clause by clause, arguing that a right has determinate content only when one can point to the enacted law that imposes the corresponding duty and to the public force that enforces that duty. In one summary passage he distinguishes rights derived from real law, which are themselves real, from rights derived from imagined law, which are imaginary; he then dismisses natural rights as incoherent, and natural rights described as imprescriptible as something worse still.

Suppose a common-law jurisdiction decided to operationalise that passage. A new parliamentary standing order provides that no bill may use the word *right* unless the accompanying explanatory memorandum identifies, for every such use: the specific duty-bearer or class of duty-bearers; the conduct that would constitute a breach; the body that would hear a complaint; and the remedy available. Rights language in a statute is not given interpretive weight unless these four items are present, and a court may not cite a treaty or human-rights instrument as the source of a domestic right unless the same test is met.

Analyse this mechanism as a mechanism, not as a moral stance. What would it incentivise among legislators, judges, executive agencies, and litigants? How could the system be gamed while preserving the official form of the four-part test? Which kinds of rights claim—procedural, privacy-related, environmental, cultural, economic—would become easier or harder to enact and enforce, and why? Which effects would appear quickly, and which would take decades to show? Finally, what would twenty years of operating this rule teach a jurisdiction about Bentham’s underlying principle, and which empirical outcomes would most undermine it?
