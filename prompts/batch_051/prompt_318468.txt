I'm a PhD student in political theory working on how mass culture converts political defeat into moral endurance—the machinery of lost-cause narratives—and I want to think through the American case closest to home, Gone with the Wind, before I compare it with the very different uses of Masada or Dunkirk. My working framework is that a lost-cause myth does several distinguishable things at once: it reframes the defeated cause as the worthier one; it recasts defeat itself as proof of moral purity rather than of error; it relocates legitimacy from military and political outcomes to memory, commemoration, and private virtue; and it quietly converts the loss into a practical program for the future while obscuring what was actually at stake—here, slavery and its aftermath. Use this framework to read the film or novel, noting where the two diverge if it matters: where the work exemplifies those moves, where it complicates or partly resists them, and where an honest critic should admit the framework distorts the work. I'm especially interested in whether Scarlett's vexed relation to the Cause—her impatience with it, her practical survivalism, and the ending's turn back to Tara—undercuts the myth from the inside, or is ultimately absorbed by the film's own lost-cause frame. I'd also like a closing thought on what this work shows about lost-cause narrative that a purely theoretical account might miss. If the framing itself seems mistaken—if lost cause is not the best lens on this work, or the framework I've given misdescribes how such myths operate—say so plainly rather than forcing the fit.
