So my cognitive science professor have been giving one-word verdicts on whether some capacity counts as a module, and I've been writing them down. So far she's said: color perception, modular. Face recognition, modular. Low-level motion detection, modular. Parsing the syntax of a sentence you hear, modular. Figuring out whether a political argument commits a fallacy by thinking it through step by step, not modular. Judging whether two analogies have the same structure after reflecting on them, not modular. She insists she means module in a strong sense — domain-specific, informationally encapsulated, mandatory, fast, with a relatively fixed neural implementation — not just "a dedicated brain area." Next week's case is the ability to immediately spot that someone in a social exchange took a benefit without paying their share, the kind of cheater-detection thing where people are much better at it when the puzzle is about free-riders than when the same logic is dressed up in abstract letters and numbers. What's she going to say? I care less about the bare answer than about the rule behind her list. Is she running something like Fodor's input-system view, or massive modularity, or a deflationary "it just has to be automatic and specialized" criterion? Show me which of those rules fit the verdicts so far, what each one predicts for cheater detection, your best guess about her call, and one more case you'd ask her about to settle it.
