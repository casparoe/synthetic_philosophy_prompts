I work in risk at a mid-size insurer, and I'm the one who has to approve or reject "lottery-style" policies: tiny, tiny probability of an enormous payout in exchange for a modest premium. Brokers keep bringing us deals that are basically St. Petersburg games — flip a coin until it lands heads and pay the holder 2^n dollars, so the naive expected value is infinite — and the arithmetic says we should either demand an infinite premium or walk away, which isn't a usable answer when I have to make these calls every few weeks. I need a reusable checklist for whether to write such a policy, at what premium, and how to handle the tail (caps, reinsurance, reserves, treating near-zero probabilities as zero). It has to be usable by my non-philosophy colleagues in under an hour, it can't just say "use a utility function" because we can't measure the counterparty's utility, and it should honestly flag where judgment enters. Ground it in what the St. Petersburg paradox actually shows and doesn't show, and run three worked cases through it: at least one where the procedure tells us to walk away from a deal that looks like profit, and one where it tells us to accept a deal the sales team will hate. Also separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among economists and philosophers who work on this, and don't present a contested resolution as the consensus.
