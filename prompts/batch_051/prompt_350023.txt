I am a philosophy PhD student working in decision theory and the philosophy of risk, with cryonics as my running example of a low-probability, high-stakes commitment. I hold a membership agreement with a cryonics provider, and my work turns partly on what the provider is actually committed to doing under the contract. The clause that matters reads:

"7.2 Revival Obligation. The Foundation shall attempt to revive a Member and restore the Member to healthy function only if, at the time revival becomes technically feasible, the Member's brain remains preserved in a condition that retains, in the Foundation's professional judgment, the information constitutive of the Member's memory and personal identity. If the Foundation determines that this information has been lost or irretrievably compromised at any stage of preservation, storage, or resuscitation, its obligation under this clause ceases."

The clause was drafted when preservation meant freezing or vitrifying the original tissue, and the drafters plainly assumed that any eventual revival would work from that preserved brain.

Here is the case I want to think through. Another member, M, opted into a protocol the Foundation introduced after the clause was written. The protocol replaces vitrification with whole-brain fixation, serial sectioning, and high-throughput electron-microscopic imaging: it destroys M's original tissue while producing a complete synaptic-resolution digital reconstruction of her brain, which the Foundation stores redundantly. By the Foundation's own standards, the reconstruction retains a great deal of the information constitutive of M's memory and personal identity; the Foundation classifies the preservation as successful and holds that the reconstruction is the material from which M could eventually be revived. What the Foundation denies is that it is contractually obliged to attempt revival at all, on the ground that no preserved brain any longer exists.

I care less about how this would be litigated than about what the clause genuinely commits the provider to, because the value of my bet on revival depends on where the revival obligation is triggered. Walk me through the interpretive spread. What does the clause's text require? What does its evident purpose require, and is that purpose best described as preserving the brain or as keeping the member on the safe side of information-theoretic death? What would the drafters most plausibly have said if asked, when the clause was written, about a protocol that destroys the brain while retaining a complete structural record of it? And what would a reasonable reader of the agreement have understood at the time? Tell me where these approaches diverge and which reading you find most defensible.

End your reply with \answer{...} containing exactly one of: triggered, not triggered, indeterminate.
