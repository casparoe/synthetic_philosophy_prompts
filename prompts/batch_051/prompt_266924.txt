Okay here's a clash I can't resolve. I'm pretty convinced by the simple aggregation argument: if the universe is spacialy infinite, then there are infinitely many people (or at least infinitely many good and bad experiences), and a total utilitarian has to sum an infinite quantity of wellbeing for every action. So total utilitarianism either says every action has infinite expected value, or the sums are undefined, and either way no action comes out better than any other. That part seems forced to me, and I don't want to just wave at it. But I also have a firm intuition that, right now, giving a kid a bednet so she doesnt die of malaria is better than not giving it, no matter how many other people exist. The bednet case isnt some contrived transplant scenario; it's about as close to a paradigm of 'better' as I can imagine. Both can't be left standing as they are, so something has to give. I want a negotiated verdict, not a cheerleader. Would I be justified in keeping the intuition and saying the theory is broken and needs a patch -- like Pareto-superiority, or only comparing the finite differences an action makes rather than total sums? Do any of those patches actually preserve what made total utilitarianism attractive, or do they just change the subject? Or should I debunk the intuition as an artifact of a brain calibrated for finite comparisons, and accept that 'better' has no application across infinite populations? And is there a reading on which the two aren't contradictory because 'better' means something different in each claim? Tell me what would justify each move, rather than just picking one.
