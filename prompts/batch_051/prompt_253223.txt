I've been reading about Bayesian confirmation theory and something struck me when I dipped into some evolutionary biology for a different project. The old-evidence problem says roughly this: if a piece of evidence was already known before a theory was formulated, then on a standard Bayesian account it can't raise the probability of the theory for anyone at the time, because it's probability was already 1. Yet we all agree that the precession of Mercury's perihelion counts as evidence for general relativity even though it was known long before Einstein, and likewise for many other old observations. The evolutionary concept of exaptation looks structurally similar to me: a trait is present before a new function for it is identified, so that function can't be cited in the trait's initial appearance, yet the trait's existence can still count as support for claims about what the function is. In both cases there's a kind of retroactive confirmation: the thing exists before the hypothesis or function is on the table, but becomes evidence or adaptation only after the fact. I'm trying to work out whether this is a deep structural identity or just the word 'before' doing all the work. What would have to be true of both sides for results to transfer? And can you name a case where one side's reasoning clearly shouldn't transfer to the other, i.e. a place where the parallel predicts something false? Where you are giving me a verdict, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
