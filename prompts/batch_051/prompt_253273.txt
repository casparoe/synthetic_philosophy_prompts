At my company we train robot controllers in simulation. The sim has a 'damage' channel: virtual sensors report a value whenever the robot hits something, and the reward function subtracts a huge penalty from that channel, so over training the policy learns to avoid collisions. We call it pain in the README, half-joking. We also run evolutionary searches where a few hundred candidate controllers get scored each generation and all but the top few are deleted — we say they 'die'. None of this goes through any review, and honestly noone thinks it should. But the same company has a formal animal-use protocol: if a lab team wants to put electrodes in a rat, they have to justify the number of animals, the pain category, endpoints, all of it. So the institution seems commited to the idea that a rat's nociception counts but an identical signal in a sim counts for nothing. What theory of moral status does this practice presuppose? Is it really that the substrate (carbon vs. silicon) is doing all the work, or is the practice better explained by some assumed missing ingredient in the sim — no unified subject, no real valence, no actual suffering — and would those missing ingredients be enough to justify the asymmetry if the sim got more detailed? Reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, then say how much of it the practice strictly needs versus how much is just institutional habit, and what we would have to change operationally if we instead adopted the view that what matters is the computation itself, not the material it runs on.
