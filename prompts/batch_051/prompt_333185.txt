Consider a variant of Thomson's lamp in which the original two-position switch is replaced by a continuously adjustable dimmer: the lamp's brightness can take any real value from 0 to 1, and the setting is held fixed between operations. The lamp starts at brightness 1 at t=0. For each n ≥ 1, at t = 1 − 2^-n the dial is turned down to brightness 2^-n. Unlike in Thomson's original case, the sequence of brightnesses at the successive stages now converges, to 0, so there is a natural candidate answer: at t=1 the lamp is fully off. Does this variant dissolve the original puzzle, or does a version of the original difficulty survive? Take a definite position on what the variant shows about whether Thomson's problem was generated by the failure of the on/off sequence to converge or by the very idea of completing infinitely many distinct operations in a finite interval.
