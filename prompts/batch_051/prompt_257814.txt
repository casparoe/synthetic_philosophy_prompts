I’m a third-year cognitive science student, and this semester I’m taking a course on predictive processing and the free energy principle (FEP). We’ve spent several weeks on the idea that the brain is a Bayesian inference engine: it builds a hierarchical generative model of the causes of its sensory input and continuously minimizes the mismatch between what the model predicts and what actually arrives, with variational free energy serving as an upper bound on surprise. On this picture, perception is the brain’s best guess rather than a transparent readout of the world, and action is active inference — organisms act on the world to bring their inputs in line with their model, rather than merely updating their beliefs to match the inputs.

For a class assignment, I want to try something more ambitious than a summary paper. Please write a deadpan satirical corporate onboarding memo, around 600 to 700 words, in the voice of the fictional Chief Inference Officer of a startup called Helmholtz Labs that has adopted the free energy principle as its official management philosophy; title it something like “Minimizing Surprisal in the Workplace.” The humor has to come from getting the theoretical machinery right while applying it absurdly to stand-ups, performance reviews, and quarterly planning. Work in, naturally, concepts such as the generative model as an employee’s read on the world, prediction error, precision weighting, active inference (you don’t just update your model — you act on the world to make your inputs fit), expected free energy and its balance between epistemic and pragmatic value, and Markov blankets as the boundary between a team and its environment. The memo should be structured around the dark room problem: a skeptical new hire has written to HR asking whether, if the company really is serious about minimizing free energy, the best strategy isn’t simply to sit in a dim cubicle and do nothing. The memo’s reply should mirror the actual theoretical response — priors shaped by evolution and homeostasis, and minimization of expected free energy over the long run, which calls for seeking information and acting rather than retreating to a literal dark room.

The satirical target is the productivity-and-self-help genre that borrows scientific jargon to sound rigorous, and more specifically the glib over-application of the free energy principle as if a descriptive formalism were a life philosophy or a management system. Keep the irony sustained, deadpan, and internally consistent: the author never gets the theory wrong, never breaks character, and never winks. The piece should still be legible to someone who hasn’t taken the course, but a specialist should recognize that every joke is built on a fair reading of the science. Please write in American English.
