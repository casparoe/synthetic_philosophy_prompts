Write a dialogue between two members of Benjamin Franklin's own circle — an enthusiastic defender of Franklin's Plan for Attaining Moral Perfection and a biblically minded objector who thinks the whole enterprise mistakes the nature of human corruption. Set the scene at a meeting of Franklin's Junto in Philadelphia. The defender should argue that Franklin's thirteen virtues, his little book with its ledger of daily faults, and his counsel to master one virtue at a time constitute a realistic, genuinely useful method of moral improvement: self-examination, habit formation, and public accountability are the best available tools for becoming a better person. The objector should press the point that Franklin's own ledger entries are the strongest evidence against the method: when a friend told Franklin he was generally thought proud, Franklin added humility to the list, yet he later confessed that he had made little progress in the substance of that virtue though he had gained something of its appearance. The objector should argue that the project mysteriously transforms even the pursuit of humility into a new occasion for pride and self-congratulation, and that Franklin's anecdote of the man who gave up grinding his ax and said he preferred a speckled ax is not a harmless modesty but a rationalization for accepting a merely cosmetic virtue. Have the defender respond to this at length, including the claim that Franklin never promised perfection but only improvement, and that the appearance of humility has social value even when the interior reality lags. The two should engage each other's actual arguments, with real back-and-forth; neither should be a strawman. Conclude the dialogue with the defender asking the objector what concrete alternative to Franklin's method he would propose for a young tradesman who wants to become better, and the objector answering.
