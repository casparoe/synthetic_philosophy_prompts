I am a novelist, writing an essay for a literary-philosophical magazine, and I want to approach Flaubert's Madame Bovary through a particular philosophical question: is novelty a component of well-being, or merely an instrument that serves pleasure and desire-satisfaction? I would like a philosophical reading of the novel, not a general literary introduction.

The problem as I see it is this. A deflationary view says that variety, adventure and novelty are good only because they refresh attention, hold off hedonic adaptation, or create occasions for pleasure; a life that repeated the same pleasant episodes would not on that account be any worse. Against this, I want to press the thought that novelty matters in its own right, and that the fear of a boring life is tracking something real rather than a merely aesthetic or romantic preference.

I have been using a distinction of my own. Felt novelty is the texture of an experience: the freshness of a new place, a new lover, a new possession. Trajectory novelty is the sense that one's life is moving toward something new rather than cycling through the same season. Emma Bovary seems to me rich in felt novelty — the ball at La Vaubyessard, the early phases of the affairs with Rodolphe and with Léon, each new purchase — yet starved of trajectory novelty, and each felt novelty decays back into provincial sameness. My question is how Flaubert's irony bears on this distinction. Does the novel treat Emma's craving for novelty as a genuine response to a real deficit in her life, or as an appetite manufactured by the sentimental novels of her youth and by the images of Paris and luxury she has absorbed, so that the craving is itself part of the disease? And does the novel, taken as a whole, contribute anything to the philosophical debate that the arguments alone cannot — perhaps something about how the desire for novelty is formed by stories about what an intense life looks like, or about why felt novelty without trajectory novelty collapses so quickly?

Give me a reading that takes both the novel and the philosophy seriously, that points to particular episodes as evidence without quoting, that is willing to say where the novel resists a clean verdict on whether novelty is a constituent of well-being, and that ends by naming what the novel adds to the philosophical discussion that a paper would not. Please write in British English, and end with a one-sentence summary of your reading as the final line, beginning "Summary:".
