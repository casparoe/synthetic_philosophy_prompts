I serve on the board of our county library system. We have been offered a large donation: several shelves of books and DVDs arguing for the New Chronology, the claim that the standard timeline of ancient and medieval history is a deliberate fabrication, that nearly all events said to have occurred before roughly AD 1000 either happened much later or are disguised duplicates of medieval events, and that ancient Egypt, Greece, Rome, and the biblical kingdoms as commonly taught are largely inventions or misdatings assembled by early modern scholars for political and religious reasons. The donor wants the collection shelved in the regular history section as the other side of what he insists is a live scholarly debate.

The staff is split. Some want to decline the gift outright; they say the material is pseudohistory, that placing it beside standard histories would mislead patrons, and that accepting it at all lends it a legitimacy it has not earned. Others say a public library is not in the truth business, that our role is to provide access and let patrons judge for themselves, and that refusing the gift would turn librarians into censors. A middle option has emerged: accept the donation but keep it out of the standard history shelves, in a clearly labeled section for contested or revisionist claims, with a brief note that the collection contradicts the overwhelming consensus of professional historians.

We have to choose among declining the gift, accepting and interfiling it with the regular histories, and accepting it with the separate labeled treatment. I honestly do not know which is right. What I keep circling is a distinction I cannot make crisp: when does an unorthodox claim deserve to be treated as a live rival to an established view, and when is it so far outside the practices of a field that a public institution is entitled to treat it as something other than history? And what do we owe the patron who cannot tell the difference?

Draft the decision memo I will bring to the board. Make it exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no concluding sign-off. The five items should include a clear bottom-line recommendation and an honest statement of the strongest argument against that recommendation; beyond that, use the items for your reasons and any conditions.
