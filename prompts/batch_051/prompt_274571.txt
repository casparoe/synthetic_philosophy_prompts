I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm doing a piece on the small but intense world of insect welfare advocacy. One of my sources, an ex-engineer who now runs a tiny nonprofit, walked me through a version of the scale argument that I haven't been able to shake, and I want to know whether it's sound before I repeat it in print. As he laid it out:

(1) There's at least a 1% chance that insects (or most of them) have valenced experience — that it feels like something to be them and that things can go badly for them.
(2) There are on the order of a quintillion insects alive at any moment, and ordinary human activity — agriculture, pesticides, outdoor lighting — affects a huge fraction of them.
(3) So the expected number of insect lives we might be worsening is a 1%-of-a-quintillion number: quadrillions of expected suffering beings, which dwarfs everything we do to vertebrates, humans included.
(4) If the expected amount of suffering in one domain dwarfs that in another, and the cost of acting on it is comparable, we ought to prioritize the first domain.
(5) Therefore even someone who is 99% sure insects feel nothing should, to be consistent, spend their career and donations on insect welfare rather than on human health or farm-animal welfare.

But the same reasoning seems to generalize in ways even my source wouldn't accept. If a one-in-a-hundred chance of insect sentience flips our priorities, why doesn't a one-in-a-thousand chance that plants, bacteria, or trillions of future digital minds have valenced experience flip them again, those populations being bigger or longer-lasting? At the limit, you'd spend your life trying to make vast numbers of probably-unfeeling things marginally better while visible human suffering went unaddressed. My source waved this off as Pascals mugging, not the scale argument, but I couldn't see the principled difference.

So where, if anywhere, does the argument go wrong? I'd honestly like the diagnosis — which step fails, or which premise is least costly to give up — and what I'd have to swallow if I kept the conclusion. I'm not willing to walk away thinking child vaccination is a second-order cause, but maybe that's just my priors talking. I don't want my piece to either launder a bad argument or dismiss a serious one out of snobbery.
