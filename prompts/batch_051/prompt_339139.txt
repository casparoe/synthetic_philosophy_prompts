I'm a TA for a medieval philosophy survey, and I'm grading a short-answer question on Aquinas's doctrine of divine simplicity. The students were given this passage from Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae* I, q. 3, a. 1, reply to the first objection:

> Holy Writ puts before us spiritual and divine things under the comparison of corporeal things. Hence, when it attributes to God the three dimensions under the comparison of corporeal quantity, it implies His virtual quantity; thus, by depth, it signifies His power of knowing hidden things; by height, the transcendence of His excelling power; by length, the duration of His existence; by breadth, His act of love for all.

The exam question was: Using this passage, explain why, for Aquinas, Scripture's attribution of corporeal dimensions to God does not undermine divine simplicity. What rule of interpretation does he apply, and does that rule tell us anything positive about God?

Here is the student's answer:

> Aquinas is arguing that because God is simple, He cannot have literal bodily dimensions. The passage tells us that scriptural corporeal terms are not literal descriptions; they are comparisons or metaphors through which we understand divine things in a bodily way. So when Scripture says God is deeper than hell, this doesn't threaten simplicity because no actual depth is being ascribed. The interpretative rule is that all such terms are used metaphorically, and their only role is to avoid contradiction with God's simplicity. I also think the specific meanings are helpful: depth means God's hidden knowledge, height His excelling power, length His duration, breadth His love. But if breadth really means an act of love, then God has an act distinct from His essence, and if love is an act, it must be an accident, which would make God composite. So the passage ultimately shows that these metaphors don't positively describe God at all; they just shield the doctrine.
