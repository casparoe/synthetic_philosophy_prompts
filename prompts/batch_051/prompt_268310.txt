I've just come from a long argument with a friend about drug policy: he favors full legalization of essentially everything, I'm more persuaded by Portugal-style decriminalization combined with treatment and regulation, and neither of us can settle whether strict prohibition of alcohol or opioids is ever defensible in principle. We kept circling around a passage from John Stuart Mill's *On Liberty*, Chapter V ("Applications"), and I'd like help understanding exactly what it commits Mill to. The passage is this:

> On the other hand, there are questions relating to interference with trade, which are essentially questions of liberty; such as the Maine Law, already touched upon; the prohibition of the importation of opium into China; the restriction of the sale of poisons; all cases, in short, where the object of the interference is to make it impossible or difficult to obtain a particular commodity. These interferences are objectionable, not as infringements on the liberty of the producer or seller, but on that of the buyer.

Could you help me parse the argument here? Specifically, when Mill says these interferences are objectionable "not as infringements on the liberty of the producer or seller, but on that of the buyer," is he claiming that *any* restriction making a drug harder to obtain is an infringement of the buyer's liberty, or only restrictions of a certain kind? And how does this fit with his earlier statement of the two maxims—that the individual is not accountable to society for actions that concern only himself, while society may punish actions prejudicial to others? I want to understand whether Mill's view here rules out prohibiting the purchase of intoxicating drugs for one's own use, and whether the fact that he groups opium importation with the restriction of poisons tells us anything about how he would draw the line today.

We also got stuck on the next paragraph, where Mill allows that the "preventive function of government" is legitimate in taking precautions against crime before it has been committed. If almost any use of a drug could be represented as increasing the facilities for some form of delinquency, how can Mill consistently reject restrictions on the sale of intoxicants without collapsing into the paternalism he elsewhere rejects? More concretely: would this passage push Mill toward full legalization of drugs, or could he consistently support decriminalization of possession combined with restrictions on commercial sale? I'd like you to walk me through the steps of his reasoning rather than just summarizing his overall position.
