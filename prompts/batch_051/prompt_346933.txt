I've been reporting on a running debate in philosophy of medicine between naturalists and normativists about what a disease is, and it's starting to feel less abstract than I thought. The naturalists say you can define disease in value-free biological terms: a condition is a disease when some part or process isn't doing the job that natural selection shaped it for, and the failure is statistically unusual for the person's reference class. The normativists say that's hopeless, because deciding what counts as a function, what the right reference class is, and how rare is rare all smuggle in judgments about which lives are good or normal. I keep wondering what would happen if a country actually tried to run its healthcare system on the naturalist definition.

Here's the counterfactual I want you to reason through. In 2026 a wealthy country—call it Valmont—writes into law that public insurance may only cover "disease," defined as: a part or process failing to perform the function evolution shaped it for, plus performing statistically below the species norm for its age and sex. Anything that can't clear both bars is legally a variation: no coverage, no sick leave, no disability benefit, no workplace accommodation. I don't need a yes or no about whether its a good idea. I need a prediction, thirty to fifty years out, with the mechanisms showing: Which conditions get pushed out of medicine first, and which ones get redefined as biological dysfunctions so they can stay in? What do patient groups, drug companies, and health agencies do in response? Does the law actually make medicine more objective, or do values re-enter through the back door via choice of reference class, function, and statistical threshold? And be explicit about which predictions you're confident in, and which depend on assumptions that could easily go the other way.
