As a reporter who covers science and ideas, I keep a notebook of verdicts about when a partially ordered set has to have a maximal element, and I want the principle behind them rather than a list of cases. A finite poset always has a maximal element; the natural numbers in there usual order never do. For the linearly independent subsets of an infinite-dimensional vector space, ordered by inclusion, I'm convinced every linearly independent set extends to a maximal one—a basis. For the countable subsets of the reals, ordered by inclusion, I'm convinced there is no maximal element, since you can always throw in one more real. My working principle was: a poset has a maximal element exactly when every chain in it has an upper bound. That handles the first four nicely—finite chains top out; the naturals themselves form the unbounded chain N; a union of a chain of linearly independent sets is linearly independent; and in the countable-subsets poset I can build an increasing chain indexed by the first uncountable ordinal whose union is uncountable, so it has no upper bound inside the poset. But now take the proper subspaces of an infinite-dimensional vector space, ordered by inclusion: I can build a chain of proper subspaces whose union is the whole space (so no upper bound among proper subspaces), yet I'm still convinced maximal proper subspaces exist—delete one vector from a basis. So the 'only if' half of my principle looks dead. What's the real principle, which verdicts does it force me to revise, and what does it say about a case I haven't listed, like the open interval (0,1) under the usual order?
