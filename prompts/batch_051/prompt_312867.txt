My friend has very strong views about this sort of thing, and I've been collecting her verdicts:

- a flawless modern copy of a Vermeer, indistinguishable from the original even to specialists: negligible value, and she says she'd never hang it;
- a Rembrandt that gets reattributed to a pupil: the value collapses overnight, even though the canvas itself hasn't changed;
- a 19th-century fake of an ancient Greek bronze, exposed as fake after she'd bought it as real: she still values it, but now as a document of 19th-century taste rather than as ancient art;
- a Greek vase reassembled from original sherds with about a third modern reconstruction: nearly full value, and she calls it 'the real thing';
- a Stradivarius whose top, back and neck have all been replaced over the centuries, so only the scroll and ribs are original: she insists it's still the same instrument;
- a posthumous Rodin bronze cast from the artist's own plaster, authorised by the estate: she treats it as a genuine Rodin, though slightly below a lifetime cast.

The new case is this: an artist leaves behind a fully worked-out design for a large sculpture, down to measurements and materials, but dies before making it. Her estate fabricates the piece exactly to the design and presents it as her final work.

I'm less interested in the verdict itself then in the rule she's running. Predict how she'd probably respond, and why, lay out the two or three rules that fit all six verdicts equally well, say what each predicts about the new case, and name the further case you'd need to settle which rule she's actually using. Please write your answer in American English.
