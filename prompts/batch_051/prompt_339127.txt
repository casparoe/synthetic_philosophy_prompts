I've been teaching the centipede game in a course on strategic reasoning, and I built a version of the backward-induction argument that now strikes me as proving too much, because it concludes that two perfectly rational players would stop the game on the very first move and leave most of the available payoff untouched. Here is the game and the argument.

Two players, A and B, alternate moves. On each move a player can either Take or Pass. Taking ends the game with the payoffs listed below; passing hands the move to the other player. If B passes on the fourth move, the game ends with the final payoff. The terminal payoffs, in order of the moves, are:

A takes on move 1: (4, 1)
B takes on move 2: (2, 8)
A takes on move 3: (16, 4)
B takes on move 4: (8, 32)
B passes on move 4: (64, 16)

Now the argument:

(1) If move 4 is reached, B takes, since 32 is more than 16.
(2) So if move 3 is reached, A, knowing B is rational, can see that passing yields 8 (because B takes on move 4) while taking yields 16; a rational A therefore takes on move 3.
(3) So if move 2 is reached, B, knowing A is rational, can see that passing yields 4 (because A takes on move 3) while taking yields 8; a rational B therefore takes on move 2.
(4) So at move 1, A, knowing B is rational, can see that passing yields 2 (because B takes on move 2) while taking yields 4; a rational A therefore takes on move 1.
(5) Therefore, if both players are rational and this is common knowledge between them, the game ends on move 1 with payoffs (4, 1), even though both players would be better off if play somehow reached (64, 16), and even though experimental subjects often pass on early moves.

That conclusion seems to me too strong. I would most like to keep the claim that a rational player maximizes expected payoff given their beliefs, but I am prepared to give up whichever other step the argument requires. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? Which step is weakest, why did it look natural, and what exactly does rejecting it cost?
