I'm the research director at a small legislative policy outfit. We review submissions on draft bills—family law, work, education, health—on a rolling basis, and a depressing share of them try to settle things by an appeal to "nature": something should pass because it's "natural," or should be blocked because it's "unnatural," or we should keep the status quo because that's "how nature made us." My staff are mostly lawyers and social scientists with no philosophy background. They don't have time for a seminar on Mill, but I want them to stop treating "natural" as if it ends the argument.

Write me a short, reusable procedure—a checklist or flowchart in words—that a staffer with no philosophy background could run on any argument from nature in about 20 minutes. Draw it out of what Mill is actually doing in The Subjection of Women, especially the first chapter, when he deflates the appeal to nature: he seperates the descriptive claim from the normative conclusion, points out that "unnatural" usually just tracks what's unfamiliar or contrary to custom, and observes that every form of domination has looked natural to the people it benefits. I want the procedure to make those moves concrete, not just name-drop them.

The procedure should: list the questions in order; say what counts as a passing answer to each; say where the procedure stops with "under-specified, send it back" versus "this argument is dead"; and flag the points where the staffer is making a judgment call, not applying a rule. Keep it usable fast.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) a submission arguing paid parental leave should apply only to mothers because mothers are naturally the caregivers; (2) a submission arguing that a school curriculum should teach that competition is natural and cooperation is artificial; and (3) one of your choosing—make it a case where the procedure gives an answer that would annoy someone who had been using "natural" as a synonym for "good." I want to see the procedure embarrass positions I'm sympathetic to as well, so don't soften it.

Close with a note on what the procedure can't do, where the remaining question is a values call that no amount of Mill will settle. And keep straight what's solidly established in his critique versus what's still genuinely contested among people who know this stuff—my staff shouldn't go around treating one reading of Mill as the consensus.
