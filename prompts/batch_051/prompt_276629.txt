I have a standing rule about how much conviction I allow myself in mathematics, and I would like to know whether the rule is defensible. The rule is that I count a theorem as proved only when its justification is surveyable: when the decisive work is done by a chain of steps that a competent mathematician can follow and check, even if the chain is very long, even if it is distributed across many people, and even if no single person has in fact read all of it. What I do not grant the status of a proof is a result whose decisive step is a computation too large for any human being to inspect in a lifetime; there I treat the result the way I treat a reliable instrument rather than a thing I know. That is the attitude I took toward the original computer-assisted proof of the four-colour theorem and toward the original computer proof of the Kepler conjecture. I relied on both results, and I expected them to be correct, but they did not produce in me the conviction that follows from a surveyable proof.

The situation that has unsettled me is this. I have been asked to judge a theorem whose proof is machine-checked: a small proof-checking kernel, simple enough for me to survey, verifies a certificate that records an exhaustive search over far more cases than any person could ever inspect. The kernel certifies that the certificate is a correct derivation, but I can no more survey the derivation itself than I could survey the original case analysis. My rule seems to say that I should still withhold full conviction, because the decisive certificate is unsurveyable; but the kernel is surveyable, and re-running the check is cheap and does not depend on the people who first ran it. I find myself hesitating.

Is my policy rationally defensible, or am I confusing the fact that no human has read it with the claim that it has not been properly checked? If it is not defensible as it stands, what is the defensible version of it — for example, one that tracks independent reimplementation of the computation, the smallness and correctness of the trusted kernel, or the presence of a separate argument that the algorithm terminates and classifies every case correctly — and which of these substitutes keeps what the rule is really protecting while avoiding the part I should abandon?
