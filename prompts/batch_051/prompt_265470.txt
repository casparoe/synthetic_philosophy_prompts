I've been reading Margalit and Rieff on whether there's a duty to remember, and I'm trying to settle what I actually think by going case by case. My rough prinicple is: remember victims and the wrongs done to them, forget grievances, and never commemorate falsely. Then my own verdicts keep breaking it.

My grandmother's older sister was killed in a massacre before my grandmother was born. My grandmother kept her name alive and said the family owed it to her parents. I think she was right: the family had a duty to keep that memory, even though nobody alive had ever known her.

A town holds an annual commemoration of a nineteenth-century uprising, celebrating a heroic victory and never mentioning that their side massacred a neighboring village during it. I think the ceremony as it stands is wrong. Not because they remember, but because they remember falsely: tell the whole story or stop holding the ceremony.

A civil war ends with a peace deal that includes amnesty and an agreed public silence about atrocities for a generation. I think that can be the right call, even though victims' families get no public reckoning and perpetrators who should be remembered with shame are not remembered at all.

A survivor of a massacre never tells her children what happened to her, saying she wants the memory to die with her. I think she's within her rights, and probably right to do it, even though the wrong done to her passes out of family memory.

A friend's mother was killed in a mass shooting, and every anniversary the media call him for a quote. He's asked them to stop, saying he'd rather not mark the day; they keep calling in the name of "honoring victims." I think the journalists are wrong and his wish not to remember publicly wins.

The national archive preserves records of mass graves that almost no one consults. They're stored but not displayed. I think keeping them available is a duty even in a society that has chosen not to build ceremonies around them—but I can't say who the duty is owed to.

My grandfather fought in a war and late in life learned his own side had committed atrocities. He spent years trying to get his town to commemorate the victims; the town refused and called it disloyal. I think he was right and the town was wrong, even though it was their own crimes they wanted to let drop.

Is there one principle capturing all of this, or do I need two or three? The asymmetry that keeps surfacing is between forgetting by victims or their families and forgetting by perpetrators or their communities, and between private forgetting and public silence. Patch my cruder principle into something precise, or tell me which verdicts it forces me to give up. Then apply it to two cases I haven't settled: a city removing a statue of someone who did genuine good and genuine evil, and the last fluent speaker of a dying language who chooses not to record its songs because she wants them to die with her.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then walk me through the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating the question.
