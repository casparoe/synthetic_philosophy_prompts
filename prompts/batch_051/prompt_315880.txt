You are a professional philosopher specialising in moral psychology and the ethics of interpersonal attitudes, with particular expertise on respect, recognition, and what we owe one another beyond outward action. I would like you to think carefully, and to begin by brainstorming several candidate principles and stress-testing them against the cases below before committing to an answer. Please write your answer in British English.

The question I am working on is this: what do we owe one another in attitude, as distinct from in action? I have collected my own verdicts on a series of concrete cases, and they seem to pull in opposite directions. I would like you to find the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that best captures my pattern of verdicts, state it precisely, tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, and then apply it to cases I have not considered.

Here are the cases.

1. After a delayed flight, a friend drove across the city at midnight to collect me from the airport. I believe I owe him something more than a thank-you note, a drink, and a returned favour. I owe him genuine gratitude as an attitude: if I performed all the outward grateful actions while feeling nothing, I would still have failed in something I owed him. And yet I cannot produce gratitude to order, which leaves me believing I owe an attitude I cannot reliably summon.

2. A junior researcher's contribution to a long-running project was quietly absorbed into her seniors' work for years. Eventually the written record was corrected. I believe the correction was not enough: the group owed her recognition, in the sense of actually coming to see the contribution as hers and giving it the attention it warranted. Strangely, I find this owed-attitude judgement less puzzling than the gratitude one, though I cannot yet say why.

3. I hold a neighbour in genuine contempt for things he has done. Still, I believe I owe him a default of respectful treatment: not making him a standing joke, not denying him the ordinary courtesies I would show any stranger. But I do not believe I owe him the attitude of respect. Feeling esteem for him, or treating his judgement as a serious consideration, would strike me as false rather than morally required.

4. A man wronged a friend of mine and later delivered a long, polished apology. My friend says he owed her remorse, not just the words. I disagree: I do not think a feeling of remorse is something anyone can owe a victim as a debt, and I do not think the victim is entitled to demand it. What he owed was repair, candour, and a credible intention to change. The absence of felt remorse is, on my view, a defect in him, but not a further thing he owes her.

5. A relative treated me badly for years, has since apologised, and has plainly changed. I think I owe him a fair hearing and civil treatment now, in the full practical sense of something due to him. But I do not think I owe him forgiveness. Forgiveness strikes me as a gift rather than a debt, no matter how complete the apology.

6. At a friend's mother's funeral, I did not think I owed my friend grief; I barely knew her mother, and the idea that grief could be owed seems absurd. But I did think I owed my friend something more than attending and saying the right words: something like a direct attention to the loss itself, rather than merely a competent performance of sympathy. Whether that is best classified as an attitude or an action is exactly the kind of question I hope a principle might settle.

7. A rival's work is plainly better than mine, and my resentment has been quietly distorting how I read it. I think I owe it to her to correct that distortion, working on my resentment until I can assess the work on its merits. But I do not know whether, once I see the work clearly, I owe her the admiration that would naturally follow. The fair appraisal seems owed; the felt admiration seems not; and yet the two are difficult to prise apart.

My own tentative theory, which I would like you to test and repair rather than accept uncritically, is this: what we strictly owe is always a performance or a course of action, because only actions are under the will, whereas the matching inner attitude is never itself owed. This fits cases 3 through 6 tolerably well. It does not fit cases 1 and 2, where the inner attitude itself feels owed. So I need one of two things: a principle that explains why gratitude and recognition are owed as attitudes while remorse, forgiveness, grief, and esteem are not; or an argument that I should abandon the verdicts in cases 1 and 2 and accept a narrower view of what can be owed.

Please give me the principle or principles you think best unify these verdicts. State each candidate precisely, show how it handles each of the seven cases, identify which of my verdicts it saves and which it reinterprets or overturns, and apply the resulting account to two further cases I have not listed: one in a workplace and one in a family. I am especially interested in whether a single distinction can separate the attitudes I take to be owed from those I take not to be owed.
