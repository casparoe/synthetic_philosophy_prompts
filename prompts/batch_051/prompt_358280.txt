I've been going back and forth with a coworker about this old essay by C. S. Peirce, "The Fixation of Belief." I'm a software engineer and don't have any formal philosophy background, so I'm probably mangling the terminology, but my friend keeps saying inquiry is basically debugging. His analogy goes: a belief is like a system running in production; doubt is a bug report; and the four methods Peirce lists for fixing belief (tenacity, authority, a priori, science) are just different strategies for handling the ticket. Tenacity is closing the ticket without looking, authority is asking the most senior dev and shipping whatever they say, a priori is refactoring until the code feels elegant according to internal consistency, and science is the only one that runs the test suite against an external server. He uses this to defend Peirce's claim that the scientific method is special because it's the only one that exposes your beliefs to something outside them.

But Peirce says early on:

The object of reasoning is to find out, from the consideration of what we already know, something else which we do not know.

So I'm trying to figure out how far the debugging analogy actually holds. Could you give me the correspondence table — what maps to the code, the tests, the production environment, the bug report, the patch — and then the places where it breaks. In particular, does the analogy quietly import the idea that there's a spec or a correct output out there independently of us, and is that what Peirce's "external permanency" is supposed to be? Or does it break because bug fixes are answerable to a pre-existing intended behavior, whereas in real inquiry we're also changing the spec as we go? Tell me which disanalogies matter for the conclusion and whether my friend's defense actually survives once those are on the table.
