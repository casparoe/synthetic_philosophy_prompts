Spent last night arguing with a friend about effective altruism, and I can't stop turning over one asymmetry. When a charity does something countable—bednets distributed, people dewormed, cash sent—its numbers get treated as proof of impact, even though those numbers also rest on modelling and on counterfactuals almost nobody checks. But when a charity works on a policy win or movement-building that could plausibly do far more, the fact that it can't produce a clean number gets treated as proof that its probably less effective, not just harder to evaluate. That looks like punishing the second group for uncertainty we never ask the first group to face. Is there anything that actually justifies that asymmetry, or is it just a bias for what's easy to audit dressed up as epistemic caution? Give me the strongest defence of the asymmetry you can construct, test it against a couple of awkward cases, and then say straight whether you'd keep it. Oh, and could you write the answer in British English.
