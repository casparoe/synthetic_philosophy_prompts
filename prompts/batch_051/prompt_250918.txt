I am putting together a small anthology of poems to introduce formal ideas to a decision-theory reading group, and I would like a villanelle about Pascal's mugging. For the poem's purposes, here is the scenario. A stranger stops you and offers a bargain: for a modest sum he will deliver an enormous reward, while if you refuse he will deliver an enormous harm. You judge the probability that he can make good on any of this to be vanishingly small, but not zero. A decision-maker who maximizes expected utility and whose utility scale has no upper bound will then find that any finite demand is worth paying, because the promised stakes can always be raised until they dwarf the cost; and after each payment the stranger can raise the stakes and run the identical calculation again, so there is no point at which the calculation itself supplies a reason to stop. The trouble is not this or that demand but the combination of unbounded utility, nonzero probability, and expected-utility maximization.

Write a strict villanelle — nineteen lines, five tercets and a closing quatrain, rhyming ABA in the tercets and ABAA in the quatrain, with the first line returning at lines 6, 12, and 18 and the third at lines 9, 15, and 19 — and make the form do the conceptual work. The two refrains should be fixed lines stating, respectively, the stranger's promise and the victim's justification: perhaps one refrain asserts that the reward is beyond all accounting, and the other that the chance is small but not zero. In the middle tercets, vary the scale of the stakes — pocket change, a wallet, a year, a world, an afterlife — while the refrains return unchanged, so the reader feels the escalation occurring around a calculation that never alters. In the closing quatrain, bring the two refrains together so that the repetition turns on the reader: by then it should be clear that the mugger is almost incidental and that the real target is the decision rule's inability to find a stopping point. The poem should be accurate enough that a decision theorist would recognize the structure of the paradox, not merely a spooky street encounter.
