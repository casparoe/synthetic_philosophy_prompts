I work as a software engineer on the ranking team of a video-sharing platform. Our team's job is to keep people watching, and we run a continuous program of A/B tests whose success criteria are almost entirely behavioral: click-through rate, watch time, sessions per day, thirty-day retention, and similar engagement metrics. Last quarter we shipped a variant that lifted average watch time by several percent, mostly by serving what I privately think of as the algorithmic equivalent of junk food — low-effort, high-arousal, not the kind of content anyone would later say they had wanted to spend their evening on. The decision is treated as settled: users voted with their thumbs, and our job is to give them more of what they in fact choose.

A colleague of mine who reads philosophy objected that we are quietly committing ourselves to a position on what a person's preferences really are. On our metrics, a user's preferences just are whatever their behavior reveals under the current interface — the position he called revealed preference — autoplay and defaults and all. He contrasted this with an idea he called coherent extrapolated volition, a kind of idealized preference: roughly, the preferences you would arrive at if you knew more, thought faster, were not being pushed around by the interface, and were more the person you wish you were.

Please spell out the philosophical view that engagement-optimized recommendation presupposes about preference and value. I want to know which parts of that view our practice strictly requires and where the practice is actually compatible with several different views. Then tell me what, concretely, would have to change in our metrics, our experiment design, and the system itself if we tried to optimize for something like coherent extrapolated volition instead of revealed preference. Finally, give me an honest assessment of whether the idealized-preference idea is well-defined enough to be an optimization target, or whether it merely moves the problem somewhere else.
