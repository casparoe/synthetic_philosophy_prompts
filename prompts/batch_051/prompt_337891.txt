I've been going back and forth with a flatmate who's convinced that the profit motive is the root cause of existential AI risk. Her version of the argument is roughly: firms racing for market share have every reason to cut safety corners, deploy systems before they're understood, and treat rare catastrophic outcomes as someone else's problem, so if you took the profit motive out of the picture — say, a single publicly funded, well-run lab, or a genuinely non-commercial research programme — the race would slow down and the acute risk would mostly go away. I've always found that fairly persuasive, but I don't think its obviously true anymore once you look at the last few years. A large share of the most capable models are now released as open weights by companies that don't charge for them directly, which seems to undercut the "they're just chasing revenue" story. Some of the most aggressive open-release behaviour looks driven by status, ideology, geopolitical competition, or sheer hobbyist enthusiasm, and the proliferation of dangerous capabilities doesn't noticeably slow down. If anything, once weights are out, you can't pull them back, so the tail risk may be worse precisely in the cases where profit isn't doing the causal work.

So what I'm trying to figure out is what actually happens to the argument. Can the profit-motive account be repaired — for instance, by saying open-weight release is still ultimately a commercial strategy to commoditise rivals or win ecosystem share — or does the new landscape show that profit was never doing the real work, and that the deeper driver is competitive dynamics, prestige, or something like a multipolar trap that would survive non-capitalist or non-profit regimes? I'm not asking for policy recommendations; I'm asking whether the argument survives, needs repair, or is dead, and which premise the development bears on. Also, whatever verdict you land on, I'd like you to say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would push you the other way, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
