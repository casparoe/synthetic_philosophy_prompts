I keep getting pulled into arguments about how weird coincidences are, and I want to actually pin down what the math does and doesn't show before someone at work drags out the birthday problem again. Classic version: 23 people in a room, over 50% chance two share a birthday, because there are 253 pair comparisons and we underweight that. Fine. The variant that actually bugs me is this: in real life there's no fixed room. Over a few years you interact with hundreds of thousands of people, and after the fact you notice the person behind you in line has your same birthday and grew up in the same county. You didn't prespecify that pair or those two features; the pair got flagged because it already matched, and the features got picked for the same reason (it could have been last name, first car, elementary school, whatever). Work through how the birthday-paradox analysis changes when the comparison set is retrospective and the features are effectively selected after seeing the data. Where exactly does the usual '23 is enough' reasoning break, and what would it actually take for a retrospective coincidence like that to be evidence of a real common cause rather than a selection or memory effect? Keep the settled probability stuff clearly separated from the what-should-I-conclude part; on the latter, I want to know where people who actually study this genuinely disagree, not a single answer dressed up as consensus.
