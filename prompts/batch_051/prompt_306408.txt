I’m a reinforcement learning engineer with solid working knowledge of POMDPs, belief-state updates, value iteration, Q-learning, and policy-gradient methods, but I have no formal decision-theory background. Last night I had a long argument with a friend who works in philosophy about the absent-minded driver problem. The version he used was this: a driver plans a route with two consecutive exits. Taking the first exit gives payoff 0; taking the second gives payoff 4; driving past both gives payoff 1. The driver is absent-minded and cannot tell, when at an exit, whether it is the first or the second, so the same behavioral plan must be used at each exit. My friend insisted that this toy case exposes a real gap between optimality at the planning stage and optimality at the moment of action, and that this gap matters for understanding commitment, self-locating beliefs, and consistent choice. He also said standard RL language tends to suppress the gap because it treats policies as objects evaluated from a single privileged perspective.

Act as an expert philosopher of decision theory who is also fluent in reinforcement learning. Restate my friend’s position—and the core puzzle—in the vocabulary I actually use: environments, observations, histories, belief states, policies, value functions, Q-values, on-policy versus off-policy evaluation, and the POMDP formulation of partial observability. Do not simply map each term to a term; tell me what translates cleanly, what gets distorted, and what has no RL counterpart at all. Then give me a residue report: is the thing that fails to survive in my vocabulary a real feature my framework is missing, or is it an artifact of the philosophical framing?
