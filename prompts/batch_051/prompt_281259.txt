I recently took on the revision of the case-based scenarios used in a clinical ethics training program, and I have watched otherwise thoughtful people reverse their verdicts on the same vignette when the order of cases changed, when the harm was described more vividly, or when the wording shifted between "allowing" and "doing." That experience has made an old methodological debate feel urgent rather than academic. When philosophers use thought experiments, are they producing evidence, illustrating a conclusion they already accept, or simply pumping an intuition?

I want an essay that takes a definite position on what becomes of the evidential role of thought experiments in philosophy if we accept, as an empirical starting point, that intuitive responses to many canonical cases—trolley cases, transplant cases, the violinist, and others whose force depends on felt reactions—are unstable across ordering, framing, and mood, and that this instability is found among trained philosophers as well as novices. The evidentialist view has typically depended on something like this premise: a carefully constructed case can confirm or disconfirm a philosophical claim because competent judges will reliably see the same thing in it. The development I want you to confront is that this reliability premise now looks false, or at least badly in need of qualification.

Does the evidentialist defense of thought experiments survive that development, or must it be downgraded—say, to the claim that a thought experiment merely illustrates a theory, or that it works as an intuition pump that persuades without adding justification? If it survives, identify exactly which premise carries the weight in the repaired version and state the repair precisely. If it does not, say what thought experiments are still good for and whether that remaining use is philosophically substantive. The essay should not end on a non-committal note: reach and justify a verdict. Throughout, keep separate what is reasonably settled in this debate from what remains genuinely contested among informed participants, and do not present a contested position as the consensus.
