I've been developing a distinction I call "surplus compliance" and I want it stress-tested. In Republic Book II, Glaucon argues that people are just only because they care about their reputation with each other, and that if a just person and an unjust person were given the ring of Gyges—made invisible and wholly unaccountable—they would act the same. (I know he doesn't put it in those exact words, but that's the idea I'm engaging with.) My notion is that even if the ring thought experiment works for the big dramatic cases, most people show surplus compliance: they tell the truth when lying would be undetectable, hand back extra change unprompted, or refuse to cheat at a single-player game where no one will ever know. I want to say that answering "why be moral?" doesn't require producing a reason that beats the ring; it requires explaining how surplus compliance is generated, maintained, and destroyed, because the real phenomenon of morality is this excess over what self-interest plus fear of detection would predict. But I can already hear the objection that I've swapped the normative question for a psychological or social-scientific one, and that Glaucon can accept all the surplus compliance data and still ask why he, here and now, shouldn't put the ring on. So: is this a genuine distinction or am I relabeling something like internalized norms or moral identity? Does it already have a name? Give me the three strongest objections and tell me whether any of them is fatal rather than just uncomfortable.
