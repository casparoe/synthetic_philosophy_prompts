Two friends of mine recently got into an argument about something I had said regarding a decision I'm facing. I'd mentioned that I was trying to decide whether to leave my current job, and that I'd assigned rough probabilities to my own possible choices—40% that I stay, 35% that I accept the new offer, 25% that I go freelance—so that I could sketch a decision tree. They then debated whether such numbers can be genuine probabilities, and I just listened.

Mia said they cannot. For a normal event, a probability says something about how things might come out independently of the forecast. But my choice, she argued, is not like that: it is something I am about to settle, not a fact already fixed in the world that I am trying to estimate. Assigning odds to it amounts to pretending I am a bystander to my own will.

Dev replied that people predict their own behavior all the time—whether they will vote, exercise, or keep a resolution—and are often reasonably well calibrated. A self-assigned probability, he said, is just a summary of evidence about one's own dispositions. It is a prediction, not an abdication of agency.

Mia answered that those familiar cases concern habits and lapses, not a choice one is presently making. In the middle of deliberation, she insisted, there is no independent settling event against which the probability could turn out to be right or wrong. The number merely reports how torn one feels.

Dev said that was too restrictive: a person can step back and take a third-person view of herself as a creature with predictable dispositions even while deliberating. On that view, a low self-assigned probability of following through is not fatalism but information about where one is likely to land, and it can be acted on.

Mia concluded that this move changes the subject. "What I will do" is a fact about the world that can perhaps be estimated, but "what to do" is a practical question, and a decision tree built on estimates of the former does not answer the latter. Dev countered that if Mia were right, it would be illegitimate for a therapist, a manager, or a policy analyst ever to assign a probability to any person's future choice, which seems absurd.
