In a seminar on the epistemology of legal proof, I argued that the same testimonial evidence should put a juror in the same epistemic position whether she is deciding a libel suit or a capital murder case: if her evidence is strong enough for her to know the defendant is liable in one, it is strong enough for her to know he is guilty in the other. A classmate dismissed this out of hand, insisting that "whether you know depends on what is at stake" and treating the slogan as self-evident. On his view, the same testimony could amount to knowledge when the worst possible outcome is an award of damages, but not when the worst possible outcome is execution.

I can hear several different claims inside that one sentence, and they seem to range from an unremarkable point about caution to a substantive claim about what knowledge itself is. Help me separate them before I respond to him. Treat the slogan as a piece of language needing disambiguation, and lay out the precise principles it could be asserting. In particular, distinguish readings on which it is a maxim of practical caution about how much evidence one should gather before acting, a claim about the word 'knows' (that its extension shifts with conversational stakes), a claim about knowledge itself (that the threshold or relation varies with what the subject has at stake), or a claim about which alternatives a knower must be able to rule out. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false. Then say which of these readings, if any, would actually support his claim about the two juries, and which version of the slogan I could accept while still rejecting that conclusion.
