Consider an asymmetry in ordinary judgement. A nation that acquired its flag, its anthem, and several of its seemingly ancient public ceremonies during the nineteenth century is seldom thought to be fraudulent on that account; the ceremonies come to be treated as part of the national past, and demonstrating their recent manufacture is felt by many to be an exercise in cynicism rather than truthfulness. A company that does the analogous thing — inventing a founding story, a heritage logo, or a timeless craft tradition while quietly omitting the year of its incorporation — is generally judged to be misleading its customers, even where no individual purchase is affected. In both cases a community has manufactured continuity with a past it did not have. What, if anything, justifies treating the national case so differently from the commercial case? Identify the strongest candidate justifications — that a nation is a people rather than a firm, that the interests at stake are identity and belonging rather than profit, that collective self-fashioning differs from corporate deception, or that the passage of time turns invention into inheritance — and test each against nearby cases such as a recently founded state, a long-lived family business, a football club's invented tradition, or a national pageant exposed as a nineteenth-century contrivance. Conclude by stating whether the asymmetry should be retained, revised, or abandoned, and why. Please answer in British English.
