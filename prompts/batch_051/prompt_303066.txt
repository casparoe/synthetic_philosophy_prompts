I'm a PhD student in clinical psychology working on the boundary between productive reflection and rumination, and I would like a consistency audit of an epistemic habit I have drifted into. My standing view is that introspective self-explanation is largely unreliable: claims of the form I did X because I felt Y are post-hoc and motivated, so I discount them in myself, in research participants, and in friends, preferring behavioral, situational, and physiological indicators wherever they are available. But I rely heavily on introspection for exactly one task: deciding whether my current self-examination is productive reflection or whether it has slipped into rumination. For example, after a bad lab meeting, I ask myself whether I am generating new interpretations or merely rehearsing the same scene, and whether the activity feels compelling but circular; if it feels circular and affectively flat, I treat that as evidence of rumination and make myself stop. The exception seems defensible to me on the grounds that I am using introspection for metacognitive regulation rather than for causal explanation of my behavior, but I cannot tell whether that distinction is principled or gerrymandered. If introspective reports of causes and states are untrustworthy, why should my introspective judgment that I am currently ruminating be any better? Does the same motivated reasoning contaminate both, and does a fully consistent version of my policy lead to a regress of second-order monitoring? I would like to know whether the policy is defensible as a policy, what its main failure modes are, and what the most defensible restatement would be that keeps the practical benefit without the inconsistency.
