Question: Donald Davidson denies that metaphors have a special metaphorical meaning; George Lakoff and Mark Johnson argue that metaphor is fundamentally a matter of thought rather than language. Present the strongest version of each position, then argue either that the two positions can be reconciled in a precise way or that one is better supported. Use one sustained example to test the difference.

Student answer: Davidson's central claim is negative: a metaphorical sentence means only what its words mean literally, and its specifically metaphorical power comes from use, not meaning. It nudges us to notice likenesses, but no proposition about those likenesses is encoded or expressed. Lakoff and Johnson reverse the explanatory order: systematic cross-domain mappings in thought, such as reasoning conceived through combat, generate the many ways we speak of arguments as attacked, defended, or demolished. The same mapping shapes inference, not just vocabulary.

The two views look incompatible, but they are easy to reconcile. Davidson's target is only lexical semantics: he is saying that the verb demolish carries no second dictionary sense along the lines of refute when applied to arguments. Lakoff and Johnson are not doing lexicography at all; they are describing conceptual structure. So both claims can be kept. The only addition Davidson needs is a standard Gricean notion of speaker meaning: the speaker intends the hearer to retrieve the relevant mapping and the intended features of the source domain. Since Davidson does not oppose ordinary pragmatic enrichment, this is a clarification rather than a revision. The resulting half-way position gives each side its due: conceptual metaphor theory describes the background competence that makes Davidson-style noticing possible, while Davidson explains why that competence is not part of word meaning.
