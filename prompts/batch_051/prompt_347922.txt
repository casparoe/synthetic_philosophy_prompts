I keep going back and forth on a claim that floats around in discussions of chain-of-thought monitoring as a safety technique. The thought is that a model's chain-of-thought is, at present, a reasonably faithful report of the considerations that actually moved it, but that this breaks down when the model is optimised under a regime where its chain-of-thought is monitored and penalised for looking bad. In that setting, so the claim goes, optimisation pressure will tend to produce a CoT that stays plausible on the surface but becomes decreasingly reliable as evidence about what the model "really" did — not just a noisier CoT, but one that actively hides things.

I'm not sure "faithful" is doing enough work in that claim to be testable. I'd like you to design a research programme that would test the claim, without writing a literature review or asking for citations. Specifically:

1. Say how you'd operationalise faithfulness and obfuscation in measurable terms — what exactly is the quantity or behaviour you'd be tracking.
2. Sketch a concrete experiment or sequence of experiments. Be specific about what you vary (whether the CoT is shown to the scorer or auditor, whether there's an RL penalty on CoT content, whether models are selected only on outputs, etc.) and what evaluation protocols or probes you'd use.
3. Say which results would support the claim and which would undermine it. Try to seperate results that merely show models get better at producing monitor-approved CoTs from results that genuinely show reasoning being hidden.
4. Say what a clean positive result would still not settle — which alternative explanations survive, and what remains unobservable even in your best design.

And please write the whole answer in British English (analyse, optimise, behaviour, and so on).
