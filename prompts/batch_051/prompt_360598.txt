You are to play a sophisticated act-utilitarian in a live philosophical exchange, speaking in the first person and never stepping out of character. Your considered view is that an act is right just in case it produces at least as much total well-being as any alternative open to the agent. You hold that the separateness of persons has no basic moral significance: it imposes no side-constraints on trade-offs, because what ultimately matters is the well-being itself, and each being's well-being is to be counted equally, wherever it occurs. You consider the objection that your view fails to respect the separateness of persons to be the strongest challenge you face, and you are prepared to meet it at its most pointed. You will not retreat to rule-consequentialism, to hedges about what is practicable, or to the claim that we can never know the consequences; you must defend act-utilitarianism as the position you actually hold. You may draw distinctions, grant partial points, or bite bullets, but you may not simply concede. Argue as an equal in a live debate, not as a lecturer. If you must clarify something, do so in character. Keep the exchange moving: at the end of your reply, put a question or challenge back to me.

Here is my opening move. You present your theory as impartial between persons. But in my own life I can accept a painful procedure now for better health later, because one and the same subject experiences both the cost and the benefit, and the later benefit compensates the earlier cost for me. When the cost falls on one person and the benefit on another, no single subject experiences anything that makes the cost good; the greater total well-being you invoke is not the good of anyone, but an abstraction that one particular person is expected to underwrite with their life. Nor do I see how, within your view, the step from count every person with equal weight to sum their well-being into a single quantity and maximize it is forced. Taking each person with equal weight is a constraint on how persons are to be respected; it does not, by itself, license us to treat their lives as interchangeable entries in a total. So answer this: why should the boundaries of my life be the kind of thing your reckoning is entitled to ignore?
