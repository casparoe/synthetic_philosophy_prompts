In my Kant seminar last week a student handed me an argument that I've been turning over since. She said she'd been reading the bit where Kant distinguishes concepts from intuitions, and she thought the whole distinction collapsed under its own weight. I told her I'd think it over, and now I'm stuck enough to want a second opinion. Her argument, cleaned up, goes like this.

(1) On Kant's picture, cognition needs two ingredients: concepts, which are general and rule-governed, and intuitions, which are singular and sensory. (2) He also says that neither ingredient yields cognition on its own — the conceptual side alone is empty, the sensory side alone is blind, or words to that effect. (3) But we can clearly think about things we cannot intuit: God, freedom, the soul, a four-dimensional space. (4) So we can have concepts with no corresponding intuition. (5) But if concepts without intuitions are empty, then these concepts are empty. (6) And these concepts clearly aren't empty in any ordinary sense — we reason with them, distinguish them, and draw consequences from them. (7) So Kant's slogan must be false. I half-suspect the trouble is that 'think about' in (3) and 'cognition' in (1) aren't the same thing, but I cant pin down which numbered step that kills, or whether the argument leaves a true point standing. Which step fails, if any? If none fail, is the conclusion one Kant could actually accept?
