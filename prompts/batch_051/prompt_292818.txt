In a course on the history of the Romance languages, my instructor keeps meeting the question "when did Latin become Italian?" with the line, "A language is a dialect with an army and navy." His gloss, as I understand it, is that the linguistic record contains no moment at which the speech of the peninsula stopped being Latin and started being Italian; the distinction is drawn by political and institutional history rather than by anything in the grammar, so the question as usually put is ill-posed. I find the move plausible but suspiciously strong, because that one sentence could be read in several quite different ways. It might mean only that speech communities draw the boundary, or that no structural property of the varieties draws it, or that official backing is what turns a variety into a named language, or that labels such as "Latin" and "Italian" track states and institutions rather than properties of the language system, or even that the transition occurred precisely when some political body began treating the vernacular as a language. Lay out for me the distinct precise theses this sentence could be used to assert. For each, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or a claim that is false or overstated; then tell me whether it would actually support the conclusion that "when did Latin become Italian?" has no non-political answer, or whether it supports something more modest, such as that the question needs a stipulated criterion or splits into many questions each with its own date. I want a menu of readings with verdicts rather than one chosen interpretation with the rest dismissed. Please answer in continuous prose, as if writing a letter, with no headings, bullet points or numbered lists, no bold text, and no tables.
