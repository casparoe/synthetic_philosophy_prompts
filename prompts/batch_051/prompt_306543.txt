I've got two judgments that keep sitting uneasily together, and I'd like your best shot at a principle that saves them both.

First, if I had to choose between one person being tortured for a year and a trillion people each getting a brief dust speck in the eye, I'd choose the dust specks. And not just for a trillion—make it a trillion trillion. It feels like no amount of tiny eye-irritations adds up to a reason to torture one person. I take that as a serious strike against the idea that harms just aggregate.

Second, I'd approve a road project that saves ten million commuters two minutes each per day but is projected, on the same traffic models used to justify it, to cause about three additional serious injuries each year. Here I aggregate without any hesitation: three severe harms a year, against millions of two-minute savings, feels obviously worth it.

The problem is that the road case looks like a less favorable version of what I'm refusing in the torture case. In the torture case, choosing the torture would be buying one severe harm with an astronomically large pile of tiny benefits—each of the trillion people would get to avoid their speck. I refuse that trade even with stakes that huge. In the road case, I'm buying three severe harms with a much smaller pile of tiny benefits—two minutes per commuter—and I'm happy to. So the asymmetry isn't about aggregation in general; it's that I reject the trade exactly when the aggregative case is strongest.

What's the strongest principle that reconciles these judgments? The nautral candidates are doing vs. allowing, intended vs. foreseen, identified vs. statistical victims, certainty vs. risk, or harm vs. benefit. Pick whichever does the most work, or invent a better one. Test it against at least one nearby case—for instance, a drug that spares a billion people a dust-speck-level side effect but causes one person a year of severe pain, or a road project where the three people who will be hurt are identifiable before the vote. Then give me an honest verdict: should I keep both judgments, reverse one, or give the pair up?
