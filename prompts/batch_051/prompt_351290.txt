I have been reporting on sign-language linguistics and keep running into a definitional problem. The criteria I was taught for what makes something a language include: a lexicon of arbitrary symbols; productivity, the ability to combine those symbols into novel messages; displacement, the ability to speak about things not present; and cultural transmission, acquisition by new learners rather than genetic endowment. Now consider the following case. A deaf child born to hearing non-signers, with no exposure to an established sign language, has built up a set of several hundred stable gestures. The child's sibling and parents have learned them, and the family can discuss absent relatives and past events. But word order stays flexible, there are no consistent markers for tense or for questions, and many utterances are one-off inventions that the family understands mostly from context. Is this a language? Work through the criteria one at a time, say where they underdetermine the verdict, and then say whether that difficulty reveals a flaw in the criteria or simply reflects a genuinely borderline case. Please write your answer in British English.
