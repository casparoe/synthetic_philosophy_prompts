I work on public-health communication in a region where several mutually unintelligible languages are spoken and adult literacy is uneven. I follow a rule for approving visual signs: a sign is usable only if people with no prior exposure to our sign system can identify what it depicts or instructs, without a caption, a key, or surrounding context, at a high pass rate in formal testing. I call this the no-lexicon test. If a pictogram fails the test in a nontrivial minority of cases, I reject it, no matter how clean it looks or how well it fits the rest of the system. My justification appeals to Otto Neurath's picture-language ideal: the hope that a well-built pictorial system can speak across linguistic boundaries without inheriting the conventions of any particular written language.

My collaborator says the rule is self-defeating. On her view, all visual signs, including so-called iconic ones, get their meaning from learned visual conventions rather than from resemblance alone. Arrows, red crosses, speech bubbles, and even seemingly obvious pictures of food are, she argues, culturally acquired. So the no-lexicon test does not measure whether a sign is unlearned or language-transcending; it measures whether the sign taps a convention that my test subjects already happen to share, and then reports that shared convention as if it were self-evidence. She adds that this was true even of the ambitious picture-language projects in Neurath's tradition, which had to be taught and accompanied by text at first.

Is my rule defensible as stated, or is it a bias in which familiarity is mistaken for transparency? Is there a weaker version that keeps whatever the rule is really tracking while dropping the demand for interpretation from zero exposure? And what does the rule actually track—iconicity, self-evidence, cultural fluency, or something else?
