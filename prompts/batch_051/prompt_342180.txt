Lately I've been keeping track of what I'd actually pay for bets when I can't pin down the probabilities, and I want to know if there's one rule behind my gut reactions or if some of them just don't fit together.

Setup: an urn has 100 balls, each either red or black, nothing else. A red ticket pays $10 if the next ball drawn is red and $0 if it's black; a black ticket is the same for black. When I say I'd pay $4.10 for something, I mean that's the absolute most I'd pay.

Here are my reactions.

If I know nothing about the mix, I'd pay $3.50 for a red ticket and $3.50 for a black one. If I'm told the mix is exactly 50 red and 50 black, I'd pay $5 for either. I'm fine with small fair bets, so the $1.50 gap is about the unknown mix, not about risk.

If I'm truthfully told the number of reds is between 20 and 80, I'd pay $4.10 for a red ticket. If I'm told it's between 40 and 60, I'd pay $4.70. Those three points feel like they're generated by something, but I'm not sure what.

Here's the puzzle cases.

In the no-info case I'd pay only $3.50 for red and only $3.50 for black, but I'd still pay $10 for a ticket that pays $10 if the ball is red or black. It's a sure thing, I know, but the prices resist adding up.

A friend offered to buy, at $3.60 each, two tickets I'd write on the no-info urn: one that makes me pay him $10 if red is drawn, and one that makes me pay him $10 if black is drawn. I obviously refused both—that's a guranteed loss of $2.80. But I also wouldn't write a single one at $3.60, even though I'd pay $3.50 to buy the same ticket. It seems like my sell price is much higher than my buy price, and I don't know if that's defensible or just incoherent.

If I'm told a computer chose the number of reds uniformly between 0 and 100, I'd pay about $4.60 for a red ticket. This one really messes with me. A plain expected-value view says the overall chance of red is exactly 50%, so I should pay $5. My interval idea below says the possible expected payoffs still go from $0 to $10, so it predicts $3.50. My gut is at $4.60, between the two, so the extra layer of uncertainty is doing something neither rule captures.

Red and black always feel completely symmetrical; swapping the labels wouldn't change anything.

I tried fitting a rule to the interval cases. Think of all the mixes compatible with what I've been told. For a given ticket, compute its expected payoff under each mix, take the worst and best of those expected payoffs, and then take a weighted average that's 65% worst and 35% best. For no-info red, that's .65*0 + .35*10 = 3.5. For between 20 and 80, it's .65*2 + .35*8 = 4.1. For between 40 and 60, it's .65*4 + .35*6 = 4.7. It even says writing one red ticket at $3.60 is a bad deal: my expected payoff conditional on the mix ranges from -6.40 to 3.60, and .65*(-6.40) + .35*(3.60) comes out negative. So the rule has a lot going for it.

But it can't touch the computer-uniform case, and I'm not sure if the buy/sell asymmetry is a feature of a real principle or just me being scared of the worst case.

So: is there a coherent principle that best captures these reactions? Which ones does it force me to drop or adjust? And what would the closest coherent principle say I should pay, in the no-info case, for a ticket that pays $10 if red and $5 if black?
