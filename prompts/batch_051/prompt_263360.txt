You are an expert philosopher of psychiatry who works on delusions, the nature of belief, and the two-factor theory, and who has experience presenting to mixed clinical and philosophical audiences. Think carefully and step by step: before you list objections, briefly brainstorm what each faction in the audience cares about, what would count as a good reply versus a dodge for them, and where my argument is most exposed.

I am giving a colloquium titled "Delusions Are Beliefs — But Commitment Is Graded" to a joint clinical and philosophical audience. My thesis has two parts. First, the double-bookkeeping sometimes shown by delusional patients — for example, asserting that a spouse has been replaced by an impostor while continuing to treat them as their spouse in much of everyday life — is not good evidence that delusions are not beliefs. Rather, it shows that belief commitment is graded and contextually partitioned. Second, the two-factor theory of monothematic delusions is structurally right — an anomalous experience is not enough on its own — but the second factor should not be understood as a domain-general reasoning or belief-evaluation deficit. It should be understood as a failure of a doxastic "gatekeeping" process: the mechanism that normally calibrates how strongly an anomalous experience gets incorporated into one's standing view of the world, rather than being damped as merely apparent. The payoff is that we can keep classifying delusions as genuine beliefs — which matters for clinical communication, self-knowledge, and the epistemology of testimony — without having to attribute general irrationality to deluded patients.

The audience is a university-affiliated psychiatry grand rounds, and it contains three groups. The clinicians (psychiatrists and clinical psychologists) treat psychotic patients and care mainly about what follows for intervention: several of them suspect that philosophical classification is a distraction, and some worry that calling delusions beliefs licenses either colluding with the delusion or confronting the patient. The philosophers of mind and cognitive science are broadly sympathetic to doxastic accounts but are suspicious that the two-factor theory's second factor is unfalsifiable; they know the double-bookkeeping literature and will press on whether my fragmentation move saves "belief" or hollows it out. One senior cognitive neuroscientist works on predictive processing and thinks "belief" is a folk construct we should replace with computational parameters such as precision weighting and prior updating.

My argument, in brief, is: (i) anomalous experience underdetermines delusional content and is not by itself predictive of who forms a delusion, so one-factor views fail; (ii) the experimental evidence often cited for a domain-general belief-evaluation deficit in deluded patients is at best mixed, and a gatekeeping deficit explains the relevant findings more economically; (iii) double-bookkeeping reflects context-indexed commitment, not the absence of belief; and (iv) a graded-belief view accommodates the clinical fact that delusional conviction fluctuates with stress, social context, and time.
