I keep coming back to an argument that seems to prove something absurd: that if every truth is in principle knowable, then every truth is actually known by someone at some point. I cannot find where it goes wrong. Here it is as I have worked it out.

1. Suppose, for the sake of argument, that some particular claim P is true but is never known by anyone.
2. Then this larger claim is also true: "P is true and P is never known by anyone."
3. The principle I am testing says that if any claim is true, then it is possible for someone to know it.
4. So, from 2 and 3, it is possible for someone to know the larger claim "P is true and P is never known by anyone."
5. To know that larger claim, a person would have to know both of its parts: that P is true, and that P is never known by anyone.
6. But if that person knows that P is true, then P is known by someone — namely, by that person.
7. So the second part, "P is never known by anyone," would be false.
8. A person cannot know a false claim.
9. Therefore, no one can know the larger claim after all, which contradicts step 4.
10. So the supposition in step 1 must be false: there cannot be a true claim that is never known by anyone. Every truth is known by someone at some point.

I doubt the conclusion, but I cannot tell whether the argument is valid or, if it is valid, which premise I should give up. Please say exactly which step, if any, fails; if the argument is valid, say which premise is doing the real work and how much is lost by denying it. Also tell me what would change your mind: which alternative reading of a step, or which further argument, would make you reverse your diagnosis, and where you think your own answer is most likely to fail.
