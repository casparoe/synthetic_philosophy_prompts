I have been working through what I think about the validity of consent by testing my reactions to particular cases, and I would like to know what principle, if any, my verdicts add up to. Here they are.

First, a patient agrees to a minor procedure after the nurse falsely tells him it will be nearly painless; the lie concerns discomfort only, not the nature, risks, or alternatives. I think the consent is valid, though I dislike the lie.

Second, a patient agrees to an intimate examination by someone she takes to be a doctor; the person is in fact a medical student without any such qualification. I think the consent is invalid.

Third, a man agrees to donate a kidney to his brother after their mother says she will cut him out of her will if he refuses. I think the consent is invalid.

Fourth, a desperately poor man sells a kidney to clear his family's debts. No one has threatened him; his alternatives are simply grim. I think the consent is valid, though I find it troubling.

Fifth, a woman with moderate dementia agrees to a dental extraction; she understands that the tooth will be removed but cannot retain or weigh the risks of nerve damage that the dentist explained. I think the consent is invalid.

Sixth, a person with anorexia nervosa refuses a feeding tube; she understands the facts and the likely outcome, and judges that thinness is worth the risk of death. I think the refusal is valid.

The first and second cases are the pair I keep going back and forth on: both involve a lie by a clinician, but they feel completely different, and I cannot yet say why without helping myself to a standard of materiality I have not justified. The third and fourth cases bother me in a parallel way.

Is there a single principle that captures all six verdicts, or close to all of them? If so, state it precisely, say what it predicts about a case I have not listed — a patient consents to a procedure after the clinician withholds a risk the patient had said in advance she did not want to be told about — and say whether the principle treats deception, coercion, and incompetence as three applications of one underlying defect or as three distinct defects sharing a label. If no single principle gets all six without becoming ad hoc, tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to drop and why. End with a one-sentence summary of the principle you finally endorse, as the last line.
