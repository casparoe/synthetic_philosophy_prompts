One of the quieter ways governments keep information from the public is the non-disclosure agreement: long after an episode has ended, former officials, staff, and contractors can remain bound not to speak. I have been trying to design a rule that balances that secrecy against the public's right to know, and I would like you to analyze it as a mechanism rather than as a moral proposal.

The rule is this: any non-disclosure agreement entered into by a public body expires automatically after five years. It can be extended once, for another five years, but only by a public vote of a seven-member panel whose members are nominated by civil-society organizations and confirmed by a two-thirds vote of the legislature. Panel members serve a single non-renewable term, and after serving they are permanently barred from employment by government, political parties, lobbying firms, or government contractors. The panel must publish a redacted summary of its reasons for every extension.

Walk through what this would actually do. Which actors would adapt first, and how? How would agencies and officials try to preserve the practical effect of an NDA while complying with the letter of the rule? What would the panel's existence do to whistleblowers' decisions about when to come forward, and to journalists' willingness to trust what they are told? Where is the system most likely to fail first?
