In class we learned a version of an argument that shows up in both economics and artificial intelligence: if you observe a person's choices, you can infer what they actually prefer, because a rational agent will choose the option that best satisfies its preferences from among the alternatives it believes are available. In AI, the same idea appears in inverse reinforcement learning, where the goal is to recover the reward function an agent was maximizing from observations of its behavior, on the assumption that the behavior is near-optimal for that reward.

That argument, however, was built for a world where "behavior" meant things like purchases, career choices, or moves in a game — decisions with real costs, made from a fairly stable and knowable set of options. The behavioral data now collected by online platforms look different: a click, a scroll, or a few extra seconds of watch time is nearly costless; the set of options is itself generated by a recommender system tuned to maximize engagement rather than to reveal what the user wants; and a user's preferences can shift in response to what the system shows them. I am trying to work out whether the behavior-reveals-preferences argument survives this change.

Please assess the argument itself, not the surrounding policy debate. Specifically: which premise of the original argument is most directly under pressure once choices are cheap, the option set is algorithmically filtered, and the system is optimizing for its own engagement objective rather than passively recording the user's goals — the premise that choices are deliberate, that the menu is known, that preferences are stable, or that the agent is optimizing its own reward? Is there a repaired version of the argument that would still license some weaker but real inference about preferences from this kind of behavioral data, and what exactly would that weaker claim be? Finally, under what conditions would the original strong inference remain justified even with an algorithmically curated menu of options?
