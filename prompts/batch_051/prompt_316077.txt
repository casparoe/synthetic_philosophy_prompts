I am a novelist writing a near-future novel set roughly one generation after machine intelligence has made most paid human labor unnecessary. A guaranteed dividend covers necessities, and people occupy themselves with volunteer work, make-work, craft, care, games, and status competitions. My difficulty is not plot but ideas: I want to map the distinct philosophical positions one could occupy on the question of whether, and how, work can remain a source of meaning once it is no longer required for survival.

Please construct a decision tree or taxonomy of positions on that question, suitable for use in designing characters. The instrument should be built from the logical space of answers rather than from a list of labels. Each branching node should be a concrete case or claim on which positions genuinely come apart, phrased so that a thoughtful layperson can answer it without already knowing which position it leads to. Each leaf should carry a name for the position, or "unnamed" if no one defends it, followed by one sentence stating its most awkward commitment.

Make the tree discriminate among at least these positions: work essentialism; post-work liberationism; a craft or excellence view; a status or recognition view; a view centered on being needed by others; and a pessimist view that automation mostly produces ersatz work. If the structure shows that one of these cannot be separated from another under your questions, say so plainly rather than forcing a distinction. If your questions produce a coherent cell that no one occupies, mark it as unoccupied and say briefly whether that is because the position is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

I have drafted four recurring situations in the novel, and I would like the branching questions to be built around them, using each only if it earns its place:

1. The last weaver: a woman weaves cloth slowly and less well than the machines, and says the weaving itself is what makes her days feel real.
2. The automated ward: patients in a hospital are cared for by reliable machines but request human visits they cannot justify on medical grounds.
3. The ranked player: a man spends his days in a competitive virtual game with global rankings and treats his ranking as his profession.
4. The ceremonial fire brigade: a town keeps a human fire brigade on salary although drones handle every actual fire, because the residents want humans standing ready.

I expect roughly eight to twelve leaves; do not exceed fifteen, and if the defensible number is smaller, explain where the logical space collapses. Present the final version as a text tree or indented taxonomy whose branches are labeled with the question or claim that splits them. The audience is myself, so I can answer a diagnostic question for each character, but the leaves should let me check that I am not writing the same position in different costumes.

Approach this as a professional philosopher specializing in theories of meaningful work and technological change, and as an experienced designer of classification instruments. Before writing the final version, work through the task in stages: first brainstorm candidate distinctions and test them against the four cases; then check whether any two positions end up indistinguishable under your questions; only then write up the clean instrument. Do not include citations, reading lists, or scholarly apparatus; the value of the instrument should rest entirely on the reasoning and the diagnostic questions themselves.
