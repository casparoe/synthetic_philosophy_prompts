You are a professor of environmental philosophy who works on technology ethics, and you've been asked to help a group of conservation biologists think through something. Please start by brainstorming at least three possible reconstructions of the view they might be presupposing, then pick the most plausible and defend it, and say where the practice is underdetermined.

I keep running into an asymmetry in conservation biotech that I can't make sense of. Gene drive researchers — people trying to use CRISPR to push a trait through a wild population, e.g. malaria-resistant mosquitos — are constantly asked about reversibility. Reviewers and funders talk as if it's irresponsible to even test a drive unless you've built a reversal drive or an overwrite drive or some molecular off-switch first. But de-extinction projects get a much lighter pass. People are seriously discussing putting woolly mammoth proxies or passenger pigeons back into ecosystems, and nobody demands a freezer full of an undo for a wandering herd of elephants. Both are deliberate, probably irreversible, large-scale interventions in an ecosystem. So what does the asymmetry presuppose? Does the practice commit people to thinking that restoring a lost species is a return to a prior state while a gene drive is a new modification — and is that distinction philosophically defensible? Or does it presuppose something about the difference between adding species and altering existing ones? If the implicit theory were made explicit and rejected, what would the practice have to look like to be consistent — would de-extinction need a reversal plan, or would gene drive review need to stop treating reversibility as a precondition?
