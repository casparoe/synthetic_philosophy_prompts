I keep saying 'greed' in lectures as though it were obvious what it picks out, and the moment I try to define it the distinction between greed, ordinary self-interest, ambition and plain prudence goes wobbly. So here's the task. Give me a working definition of greed — not a dictionary gloss — that seperates greed from those neighbours and then test it against these cases:

(i) a billionaire pharma boss who triples the price of an orphan drug because the market will bare it, even though the old price was already profitable;
(ii) a landlord who raises the rent every year to whatever the local market rate happens to be, though his own costs have barely budged;
(iii) a twentysomething who works ninety-hour weeks and skips every holiday and wedding for a decade so she can retire at forty-five, with no dependants and no intention of spending the pile;
(iv) a five-year-old at a party who scoops most of the sweets into a party bag before the other children get any;
(v) a collector who owns more pictures than he can hang and outbids a public gallery for a portrait he will keep in storage;
(vi) a monk who has renounced money but talks of hoarding spiritual merit and goes cold with envy when another monk is praised.

My hunches: (i) is greed, (iii) isn't, and (vi) is greed even though no money is involved — that's why it's in the list. I'm honestly unsure about (ii), (iv) and (v). Propose the definition, run it through all six, say where it misclassifies or falls silent, then revise it or defend the surprising verdicts. I also want to know whether your definition makes greed always a vice, or whether it leaves room for the thought that a certain amount of acquisitiveness is just what makes markets go. Write your answer in British English.
