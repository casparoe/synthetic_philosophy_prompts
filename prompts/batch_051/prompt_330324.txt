I write a fortnightly column on science and ideas for a British monthly, and I am preparing an interactive feature for our website: a "which theory of the ethics of belief do you actually hold?" quiz. The feature will run alongside a short essay on W. K. Clifford's "The Ethics of Belief" (1877), and I want the quiz to sort readers by the commitments they reveal in answering concrete cases, not by which label they prefer.

The essay will open with this passage from Clifford:

"The question of right or wrong has to do with the origin of his belief, not the matter of it; not what it was, but how he got it; not whether it turned out to be true or false, but whether he had a right to believe on such evidence as was before him."

The sentence comments on the shipowner case: a man sends out an emigrant ship after stifling his doubts about her seaworthiness, persuades himself she is sound, and collects the insurance when she sinks. I would like the whole instrument to be a running test of that passage. The questions should make readers face the claim that a belief's standing depends on how it was acquired, not on how it turned out, and the scoring should locate readers according to whether, and where, they depart from it.

Please draft the quiz to these specifications:

- Eight to ten questions. Each should be a concrete case drawn from ordinary, professional, or journalistic life. Examples you might use: an editor who spikes an explosive story that later proves true; a GP who prescribes off-label on anecdotal evidence that later proves sound; a juror weighing a defence she half-believes; a person deciding whether to believe a friend's denial; a patient who believes he will recover partly to improve his odds; a newsroom deciding whether to run a single unreplicated preprint. At least one question should transpose the shipowner case into a contemporary setting, asking about blame rather than outcome. No question should ask the reader to pick a named position or to state a principle; two or three answer options per question.

- The scoring key should map answer patterns onto at least these leaves: strict Cliffordian evidentialism; Jamesian permissivism, understood narrowly as a licence for belief on passional grounds only where the option is forced, living, momentous, and the intellect cannot settle it; a broader pragmatic voluntarism that allows evidential standards to be relaxed whenever believing would serve other goods; contextual evidentialism, which keeps evidence as the rule but varies the standard with role and stakes; and a consequentialist view on which what matters is the harm a belief actually causes, not how it was acquired. If any two of these cannot be separated by your questions, say so explicitly and state which further question would split them.

- The scoring key should register at least three distinctions independently rather than collapsing them into one spectrum: (1) origin versus outcome, Clifford's own contrast in the quoted sentence; (2) scope, whether the evidential duty binds only beliefs that are acted on in ways that expose others to risk, or all beliefs whatsoever; (3) forced practical options, whether there is a class of cases where evidence underdetermines and yet a decision, and perhaps a belief, cannot be avoided. You may render James's examples about trust, friendship, and courage as cases, but do not cite his works.

- The quiz should also probe a further commitment Clifford defends later in the same essay: that private credulity is itself a public wrong because it seeds credulity in others and corrupts the practice of telling the truth. Treat this as a separate diagnostic dimension, so that the key can flag readers who accept Clifford's origin criterion but reject this social argument, and vice versa.

- Include one or two answer combinations that no defensible position occupies, and briefly diagnose each: is the combination presumably empty because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because nobody has yet worked it out?

- The tone should suit a serious general readership: no unexplained jargon, and any technical term that must appear should be glossed in plain words. The full quiz with scoring key should run about 1,200 to 1,500 words.

The quiz should be answerable by someone encountering the passage for the first time, with no prior knowledge of Clifford, James, or the philosophical literature.
