I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, mostly the kind of beat where a small study turns into a headline like "mosquitoes carry malaria." I've spent a couple years collecting bare-plural generics — the unquantified ones — and I've worked out a little theory about when they're acceptable. I want it checked honestly.

Call it the Exception Sink account. A generic "Ks are F" is acceptable just in case the Ks that aren't F can be dumped into an exeption kind we're already tracking for other reasons, so the sentence never forces us to mint a new sub-kind just to hold the leftovers. "Ducks lay eggs" works because the non-layers are males and ducklings, and we already track "male" and "juvenile" regardless of anything to do with eggs. "Books are paperbacks" fails because the non-paperbacks are hardcovers, and "hardcover" — at least the way I see it — is only a kind relative to the paperback default, not a slice of book-space we'd carve out on its own. So far so good.

Then "mosquitoes carry malaria" comes along and wrecks it. Almost no mosquitoes carry malaria, and the non-carriers are just "mosquitoes that don't carry malaria"; there's no pre-existing sink. But the sentence is fine, so I added a hazard clause: if F is the sort of property we'd warn someone about, no sink is needed, because the sentence is about what to watch for rather than what the kind is like. Then I added a design clause for "ducks lay eggs" anyway, and now there are three routes and I suspect I've built a machine with no off switch.

So: reconstruct this in its strongest form and tell me what it gets right. Is it already a thing under some name I'd probably know? Give me the three or four hardest counterexamples or objections, and for each say whether it's fatal or just a patch I haven't considered. And be candid about whether the theory has any teeth left once the hazard and design clauses are in, or whether it's just redescribing the data. Also, since I'll likely relay this: tell me how confident you are in each main claim, and single out the point you're least sure of.
