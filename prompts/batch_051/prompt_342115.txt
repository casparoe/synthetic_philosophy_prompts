Below are my own verdicts on a batch of generic sentences — statements of the form 'Ks are F', said of a kind, with no overt expression of how many. Please treat the verdicts as data and help me find the principle, or small set of candidate principles, that captures them.

I judge these generics true:

'Ducks lay eggs' — true, although most ducks never lay an egg: drakes and ducklings never do, and adult females do so only at certain times.

'Mosquitoes carry malaria' — true, although only a very small minority of mosquitoes ever carry the parasite.

'Sharks attack bathers' — true, although a vanishingly small proportion of sharks ever attack a person.

'Cardinals are red', said of the bird — true, although only the males are red.

I judge these generics false:

'Books are paperbacks' — false, although most books are paperbacks.

'Bees are female' — false, although the overwhelming majority of bees are female.

The pair that troubles me most is the first and the last: I accept a generic that attributes to ducks a property only a minority of ducks ever possess, while rejecting a generic that attributes to bees a property the overwhelming majority of bees possess, so the difference cannot simply be one of statistical prevalence.

I am tempted by a principle along these lines: 'Ks are F' is true just in case F is characteristic or normal for the kind K, and false when F is merely a widespread accidental fact about the Ks. But I cannot yet make 'characteristic' precise. It would need to explain why laying eggs is characteristic of ducks although most ducks at any given time are not laying; why carrying malaria is characteristic of mosquitoes although almost none of them carry it; and why being female is not characteristic of bees although most bees are female.

Can a single principled account of generics deliver all six of these verdicts? If so, state it precisely. If the account has distinct clauses or parameters, say which of my six cases motivates each clause. Then say what the account predicts for further cases I have not listed, such as 'cats have four legs', 'birds fly', 'prime numbers are odd', 'humans are right-handed', and 'peacocks have colourful tails'. If no single account delivers all six verdicts, say which of my verdicts it seems best to overturn, and which repair to my tentative principle loses the least. In either case, be explicit about which of my verdicts, if any, the resulting account cannot accommodate. Please write the answer in British English.
