I am preparing a handout for a graduate seminar on constitutional theory, and I want it to take the form of a sorting instrument: a decision tree that takes a respondent from their reaction to a case of constitutional silence or exhaustion to a named position about who, if anyone, legitimately governs when the rules run out.

Build the tree from the logical space of the debate rather than from a list of labels. First identify the features of such cases that could split respondents, then phrase each split as a question someone can answer without already knowing which position it will sort them into. Then locate the established positions as leaves in that space, and mark any combination of answers that has no obvious occupant, saying briefly whether it seems incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag explicitly which positions the tree cannot separate from one another and why.

The tree should include at least these leaves, under whatever names you find least tendentious: a legal-continuity view in the Kelsenian spirit (the legal order never really runs out, because every order contains closure rules or authorizes someone to fill its gaps); a necessity view in the Lockean spirit (officials may act beyond the letter of the law when the public good requires it, and the act is legitimated retrospectively if the community accepts it); a decisionist view (the exception is the moment at which someone shows, by succeeding, that they are the sovereign); a popular-constituent-power view (when constituted powers lapse, authority reverts to the people, who may reconstitute the order); and a skeptical view (there is no fact of the matter about legitimacy in a gap, and talk of who governs legitimately is a rationalization of whatever prevails). Add any further leaves you think are genuinely distinct, and in particular address whether ordinary officials simply carrying on by continuity of function is its own position or collapses into one of the above.

Use these cases as anchor points, and add one or two of your own if they sharpen a split: (a) a written constitution specifies a complete line of succession, and an accident kills every person on it at once; (b) a constitution whose amendment procedure can only be initiated by a constitutional court that has just been dissolved by the last act of the outgoing government; (c) a public-health emergency in which the statutes are silent on curfews and the executive imposes one, citing necessity alone; (d) a failed coup, in which the generals hold the capital and the broadcasters for a month before withdrawing, while provincial governments and civil servants simply carried on obeying the old statute books throughout; (e) a constitution that remains formally valid but is treated as illegitimate by nearly everyone, while a mass movement starts issuing its own tax and land records.

Each branch node should discriminate rather than decorate: include a question only if there is at least one pair of leaves it reliably separates, and say which pair. Keep the whole tree usable in a ninety-minute seminar: no more than nine leaves, no more than four branch levels, and each leaf should carry one sentence stating its most awkward commitment.
