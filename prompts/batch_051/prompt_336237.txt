I'm working through some philosophy of law stuff for class and I keep tripping over this analogy, and I'd like someone to actually audit it rather than just nod along. The analogy is the one you see in Raz, or at least in the Razian line my professor assigns: the rule of law is to a legal system what sharpness is to a knife.

The argument the analogy gets used for goes roughly like this. A knife is for cutting, so sharpness is a virtue of a knife only as an instrument. It doesn't matter whether you're cutting an apple or cutting a throat; the sharpness is the same, and it's good in exactly the same thin, instrumental sense. Similarly, law is for guiding conduct. So the rule of law—rules that are general, public, prospective, clear, consistent with one another, possible to comply with, relatively stable over time, and actually administered as announced—is a virtue of law in that same instrumental way. A legal system could do all of that beautifully and still be used for awful ends. So the rule of law is not a moral achievement in itself; it's more like a negative or instrumental virtue. That's the conclusion the analogy is being used to carry.

I half buy it, but I can't tell how much work the analogy is actually doing. Sharpness is a single physical feature, while the rule of law is a cluster of desiderata that can come apart and even conflict at the margins. A knife is still a knife if it's dull; is a legal system still a legal system if it's radically secret, retroactive, or internally contradictory? And sharpness doesn't have a history of being demanded in the name of dignity or fair notice in the way rule-of-law ideals do—maybe that's a change of subject, maybe not. Also, a knife's sharpness isn't something the knife itself attains through official practice over time, but a legal system's conformity to the rule of law is in part a matter of official behavior matching announced rules, which feels closer to a kind of integrity than to a physical property.
