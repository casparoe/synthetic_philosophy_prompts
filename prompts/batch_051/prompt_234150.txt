I keep thinking about a passage I came across in Ovid's Metamorphoses, Book 4. After Perseus kills the sea-monster and rescues Andromeda, he sets Medusa's head down on a bed of seaweed so it won't hurt the sand. Ovid writes: "Even from that day the coral has retained such wondrous nature, that exposed to air it hardens.—Thus, a plant beneath the waves becomes a stone when taken from the sea." Now here's what bothers me. People talk about "the" Medusa myth as if there's a standard version: her head turns things to stone. But Ovid's coral detail is just one telling among several, and plenty of people who know the myth have never heard it. We usually say Ovid's version is canonical because it's the fullest and most influential, but that's a claim about reception, not about the story itself. Why should the most-quoted version count as more real? If you had only, say, a Roman handbook that omitted the coral, and a local Greek aition that included it, on what grounds would you decide which one is the myth? I want the strongest principle you can find for distinguishing a canonical version from a variant, and an honest verdict on whether it can justify the way people actually pick. If it can't, what should we say about the whole idea of a false version of a myth?
