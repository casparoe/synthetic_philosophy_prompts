My colleague advises teams on when to lock into a plan and when to leave the work open, and her past verdicts have been stable enough that I want to reverse-engineer the principle before a new case comes up. I have tried three candidate variables on her record—how much can genuinely be known or rehearsed before acting, how many separate workstreams must stay in step with only delayed or indirect feedback, and how expensive it is to revise a mistaken first move—and the trouble is that in all six past cases the three variables point the same way. So the record does not yet tell me which of them, if anything, is doing the work.

Her six verdicts:

1. A jazz quartet recording an album of newly written tunes. She said to do almost no pre-production, sketch each tune once, and let the arrangement emerge in the room rather than fixing it in advance. Verdict: improvise.

2. A hospital commissioning a new surgical wing. She said to complete full specifications, freeze the design before construction begins, and resist nearly every on-site change after that. Verdict: plan.

3. A five-person startup building a home robot for a market that does not exist yet. She said to write no five-year plan, get a crude prototype in front of users quickly, and let the product be found through iteration. Verdict: improvise.

4. Three agencies coordinating a wildfire evacuation of a populated valley under a standard incident-management system. She said to assign roles and rehearse the playbook every season, and to prohibit freelancing during the event. Verdict: plan.

5. A theatre ensemble creating an original piece. She said not to commission a script, but to generate material through workshop exercises and let the show emerge from what those exercises produce. Verdict: improvise.

6. An airline pilot handling an engine failure just after takeoff. She said to execute the written emergency procedure and the rehearsed flows exactly, and to treat an improvised workaround as a mistake. Verdict: plan.

Next week she must advise a software company that has five days to replace its customer-facing login system after a vulnerability disclosure for which there is no documented fix and no vendor guidance. Four groups—security engineering, customer support, legal, and communications—must stay in near-lockstep, because each group's choices constrain the others'. The technical picture is changing daily, no one in the company has handled this failure mode before, and once the migration begins several choices cannot be undone without taking the whole system down again.

Given that record, would she be consistent in telling them to improvise day by day, or to pause and build an integrated plan before acting?
