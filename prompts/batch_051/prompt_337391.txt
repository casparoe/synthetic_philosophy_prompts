I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am drafting a feature on decision theory — specifically the puzzles in which a reflective agent seems unable to do what rationality appears to demand. The feature is built around Gregory Kavka's toxin puzzle: a billionaire offers you a million dollars if, at midnight tonight, you intend to drink a vial of toxin tomorrow afternoon. The toxin will make you ill for a day but will do no lasting harm. The billionaire has a reliable way of detecting the intention at midnight; if it is present, the money is transferred to your account at once, and you are free to change your mind afterward. Tomorrow afternoon you will already have the money and will have no reason at all to drink, so you know you will not do it. That very knowledge seems to make it impossible to form the intention tonight, no matter how much money is attached to forming it.

I would like to open the feature with a pantoum: sixteen lines in four quatrains, in the voice of the person who has just been given the offer and is trying to form the intention. Please do not merely name the puzzle; build it into the form. In a pantoum the second and fourth lines of each quatrain return as the first and third lines of the next, and the closing quatrain folds the poem's opening lines back in. Use that mechanism so that the speaker's resolve (I will drink) and the certainty of defection (tomorrow I will refuse) keep reappearing in new combinations — each time as if the intention is about to be formed, and each time entangled again with the knowledge that defeats it. In the closing quatrain, the opening vow should come back altered by what has intervened: not resolved, but exposed as the very thing the speaker cannot lay hold of. The final line should land on the real subject of the poem — whether intending is something one can perform at will, or whether it must answer to one's beliefs about one's own future action.

Someone who knows Kavka's puzzle should find the poem faithful enough not to wince, and someone who has never met it should come away understanding why it is a puzzle rather than a story about weakness of will. After the poem, add a short prose note in which you say how confident you are that the poem is faithful to the puzzle, where it has taken liberties with the details or leaned on one contested reading, and which single interpretive choice you are least sure about — for example, whether the poem treats the speaker as genuinely unable to form the intention or merely unable to avow an intention the speaker distrusts. Please write the poem and the note in British English.
