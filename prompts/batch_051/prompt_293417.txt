For a long piece I'm working on about methods in moral and political philosophy, I've been interviewing philosophers about whether reflective equilibrium — the method of bringing general principles and particular judgments into mutual adjustment until they cohere — is best understood as a method of justification, a method of discovery, or merely a method of systematization. In my notes I have the following objections, which come up over and over again. (1) The conservatism objection: the method begins from pretheoretic judgments and can therefore end up laundering whatever biases a person or culture already holds, since coherence alone never forces the revision of the starting points. (2) The multiple-equilibria objection: the method offers no reason to expect a unique endpoint; different people, or the same person at different times, can arrive at incompatible but equally coherent packages of principles and judgments. (3) The reliability objection: coherence among intuitions is no evidence of truth unless the intuitions themselves are reliable, and the method provides no independent check on that reliability. (4) The elasticity objection: the notion of a considered judgment is left loose enough that one can protect favored commitments by reclassifying recalcitrant judgments as unconsidered, which threatens to make the method vacuous.

What I need is a further objection to reflective equilibrium as a method — not a variant, special case, or combination of any of the four above, and not merely an objection to how someone has applied the method to a particular case. Ideally it should be the kind of criticism that already has currency among working philosophers but is unlikely to appear in an introductory survey, or else a new argument that does real work. After stating it, give me an honest appraisal of its strength: does it seriously damage the method, or is it closer to a restatement of one of the others, and what is the most plausible reply a defender of reflective equilibrium could make?
