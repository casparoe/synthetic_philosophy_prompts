Suppose a legal system adopts the rule that a person now counts as the same person as an earlier one — for owning property, being bound by contracts, and being punished — only if the later person has a genuine first-person memory of the earlier person's relevant experience. The rule is meant to make legal identity track Locke's memory criterion. Debts you've actually forgotten aren't yours; crimes you can't remember committing can't be punished. Set aside whether that's just — analyze it as a mechanism. Walk me through how it actually plays out. What does it do to lending, to the incentive to forget or to avoid forming memories, to the market for memory-enhancing or memory-dulling treatments, and to how a court can ever verify a 'genuine' memory? Then consider the transitivity problem: Reid's brave officer — the officer remembers being flogged as a boy for robbing an orchard, the general remembers being the officer but not the flogging, so the general is the officer and the officer is the boy, but the general isn't the boy. Does legal identity under this rule chain across overlapping memories, or does it come apart? Is there a patch — say, counting any chain of overlapping memories — that fixes it, and where does the patch get gamed first?
