Something's been bugging me for a while, and I'd like a proper philosophical take on it rather than the usual science-wars talking points. I sit on a university research committee, and I keep noticing a pattern that makes me wonder what the people making these decisions actually believe about evidence.

Take string theory. After something like five decades it still has no accepted empirical prediction; searches for supersymmetry at the LHC came up empty; its main advertised virtue is that it might unify quantum mechanics and gravity. Yet it remains the most prestigious game in theoretical physics. Journals publish it, funders fund it, departments hire it. The multiverse stuff is even more stark: a paper can explain away the value of the cosmological constant by saying that our universe is just one draw from a nearly numberless landscape of other universes that we cannot observe directly. That approach is treated as serious cosmology, not as a last resort.

But the same community will turn around and treat unfalsifiability as a fatal objection when examining a rival model or a speculative proposal from outside the mainstream. So there looks like a double standard: empirical testability is treated as essential in most contexts, and as optional precisely in the region of theory where the absence of tests is most conspicuous.

What I want to know is what theory of confirmation, evidence, or theory choice this practice presupposes. Does it actually commit the practitioners to the idea that non-empirical virtues -- mathematical elegance, explanatory unification, the absence of serious rivals -- are themselves evidence that a theory is true? Or is the practice better read as a pragmatic strategy for allocating scarce research effort under uncertainty, with no epistemological commitment at all? Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits alongside. And I would like you to take a definite view: if you think the practice is defensible, say exactly which version is defensible and which is not; if you think it isnt, say what would have to change in how journals, grant panels and hiring committes would operate under a consistently empirical standard.
