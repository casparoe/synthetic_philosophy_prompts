Over break I fell down a rabbit hole about what money is allowed to buy, and I can't stop thinking about one asymmetry. Crab fishermen are paid to work in water that can kill them; loggers are paid to stand under trees that might crush them; boxers are paid to take head trauma in front of a crowd — and we call all of it a job, maybe even a gritty form of freedom. But the moment someone pays a stranger to take a single punch in a parking lot, or pays a poor person for a kidney, people reach for words like assault and trafficking, and the law mostly agrees — even if the kidney surgery were safer than a decade on a fishing boat. I know the standard words: dignity, coercion, exploitation, commodification. I just can't tell which one, if any, actually draws the line.

So I want the strongest principle you can find that separates the dangerous job from the banned body sale, and then I want you to test it on a matched pair. A scaffolder sells twenty years of fall risk; a kidney seller sells one afternoon of surgical risk. Same pay, both fully informed, both genuinely voluntary, and by any actuarial measure the scaffolder is worse off. If your principle can't seperate the two cases, say so plainly, and tell me whether that means we should ban the job or legalize the kidney.
