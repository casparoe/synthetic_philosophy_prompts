Test this argument for me. I've encountered it in discussions of moral testimony, and each step looks right to me, yet the conclusion seems too strong, because testimony plainly transmits knowledge in non-moral domains. Here it is:

(1) You can come to know a moral proposition only by responding to the reasons for it.
(2) When you accept a moral proposition purely on another person's say-so, you are not responding to what makes the proposition true; you are responding only to the fact that your informant asserted it.
(3) The fact that your informant asserted it is not what makes the proposition true — that fact is about the speaker, not about the moral state of affairs itself.
(4) So, in accepting a moral proposition purely on testimony, you have not responded to the reasons for it.
(5) Therefore, testimony cannot give you moral knowledge.

Is the argument valid? If not, locate exactly where it goes wrong — whether that is a false step or a slide in the meaning of a term — and state the corrected version of that step.
