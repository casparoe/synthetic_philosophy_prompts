I write a column on the ideas behind political and economic disputes, and I am currently rereading Robert Nozick's Anarchy, State, and Utopia because so much contemporary argument about taxation, inheritance, and reparations gestures at the book without actually engaging with it. The distributive justice chapter has left me with a puzzle I cannot resolve to my satisfaction, and I would like a philosophical assessment of whether two of its central moves can be reconciled.

Early in that chapter, Nozick develops the Wilt Chamberlain scenario: grant any starting distribution you regard as just, then let a million fans voluntarily pay an extra twenty-five cents apiece to watch Chamberlain play basketball. The resulting inequality, he argues, must itself be just, because each step from an already just situation was a just transfer; he then concludes that no patterned or end-state principle of distributive justice can be maintained over time without continual interference in people's lives, since free choices will constantly pull any fixed pattern apart. Later in the same chapter, in the discussion of rectification, he concedes that actual holdings are not the product of a clean sequence of just acquisitions and transfers. They bear the marks of theft, conquest, enslavement, and fraud, and because the historical record required to undo those wrongs is largely unavailable, he suggests that the best available response to past injustice may be to apply a rough patterned rule—one designed to maximize the position of the group that ends up worst off. That looks strikingly like the sort of distribution-sensitive principle the Chamberlain argument was meant to discredit.

The tension is this. The Chamberlain case seems to establish, as a matter of principle, that patterned approaches to distribution cannot accommodate liberty; the rectification passage seems to admit that in the actual world, where the starting point is unjust, a patterned approach is needed precisely because ordinary liberty-preserving transfers cannot repair the past. Are the two positions inconsistent, or is the Chamberlain argument merely a conditional claim about an ideal starting point from which nothing follows for a world as historically compromised as ours? I can see several candidate reconciliations—a distinction between ideal and nonideal settings, a distinction between principles of justice and rough practical rules of thumb, a retreat forced by missing information—but I need to know which reading does the least damage to the text and whether the standard libertarian use of Chamberlain against redistribution depends on quietly setting aside Nozick's rectification concession. Please present the strongest versions of both passages, say where exactly they do or do not conflict, and give a verdict on which reconciliation is honest and which is special pleading.
