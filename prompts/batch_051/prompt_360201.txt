I am a novelist, and I have noticed that the language of ownership has colonized the way writers describe their working lives. We say a premise was stolen, a subject belongs to the author who got there first, a derivative novel has taken something that was not its author's to take. I want a working definition of what it is to steal an idea—or, what comes to the same thing, of what it means to own one—that can handle the cases a working novelist actually meets, including some that are not legal disputes at all.

Here are the cases I want the definition to classify:

1. A writer copies three sentences from my published novel into hers, word for word, without quotation marks or attribution.
2. A writer I have never met publishes a novel built on the same unusual premise as mine—say, a city where memories are bought and sold at auction—after having seen only a one-line description of my book in a review. The two novels share no characters, scenes, or sentences.
3. A writer in my workshop read a draft of my novel and praised its premise, then two years later published a novel using that premise with different characters and a substantially altered plot. There was no contract and no record of what I showed her.
4. Another novelist takes the plot architecture of my book—the sequence of reversals, the central relationship, the way the mystery resolves—and moves it to a different country and century, never copying a sentence.
5. A friend tells me a traumatic story from her youth; I use it, nearly unchanged, as the center of my next novel without asking her.
6. A younger writer has plainly absorbed my body of work: the atmosphere, the recurring images, the rhythm of the sentences, and one especially distinctive image all appear in her novel, but no sentence or plot event is copied.
7. A contemporary writer lifts a translator's rendering of a paragraph from a nineteenth-century novel and sets it, uncredited, inside her own book. The original author has been dead for a century.
8. The same writer later lifts, nearly verbatim, a paragraph from the essay of an obscure living blogger who is unlikely ever to notice or object.
9. An economist spends a decade developing an account of how intermediaries corrupt markets; I use the substance of that account as the intellectual framework of my next novel without naming or citing her.

I am not asking what the law says, because copyright, plagiarism, and breach of confidence will slice these cases differently, and my question concerns the concept I should actually use in my own practice. My provisional position is that the first case is theft and the second is not, and I am unresolved about nearly everything between them. Give me the definition, run it through all nine cases, and tell me where it forces a revision and where it produces a verdict I should accept even if my instincts resist it.
