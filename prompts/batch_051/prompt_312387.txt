I'm compiling a list of the candidate moral foundations proposed in moral foundations theory, and I want you to help me test whether the standard inventory is genuinely closed or just stops where the theory's proponents happened to stop. Here is the list as I understand it, with all six standard entries:

1. Care/harm — associated with the adaptive problem of protecting vulnerable dependents; characteristic triggers are suffering, distress, or vulnerability; characteristic emotion is compassion.
2. Fairness/cheating — associated with the adaptive problem of reciprocal cooperation; triggers are cheating, freeriding, and disproportionate shares; characteristic emotions are anger, guilt, and gratitude.
3. Loyalty/betrayal — associated with the adaptive problem of holding coalitions together; triggers are threats to the group and defection to an outgroup; characteristic emotions are group pride and rage at treachery.
4. Authority/subversion — associated with the adaptive problem of navigating dominance hierarchies; triggers are signals of rank and violations of rank; characteristic emotions are respect, fear, and anger at insubordination.
5. Sanctity/degradation — associated with the adaptive problem of pathogen avoidance and disgust; triggers are contamination and things coded as base or degrading; characteristic emotion is disgust.
6. Liberty/oppression — associated with the adaptive problem of resisting domination; triggers are coercion, bullying, and monopolies of power; characteristic emotion is reactance or resentment.

I already know the sixth was added after the original five, so as far as I'm concerned the standard extensions are on the table and you shouldn't offer me any of these six under another name.

Give me one more candidate foundation that is genuinely different from all six. By genuinely different I mean: it names a distinct adaptive challenge, a class of triggering situations that none of the six handles naturally, and an emotional signature that is not just one of the above or a mixture of them. Then do three things with your proposal. First, say what cultural elaborations it predicts — virtues, taboos, institutions, ritualized disputes — and whether anything like that pattern already exists. Second, say what it predicts about the moral psychology of the political left and right that the six don't, and whether the resulting picture gives us any reason to think one side's moral sensitivity is more or less distorted, or whether it remains purely descriptive. Third, say whether adding it strengthens or weakens the idea that the foundations are a fixed set of pre-cultural modules rather than a useful post hoc summary of current moral talk.

Be explicit about confidence at each step: which features of your proposal follow from known psychology, which are plausible invention you couldn't defend without evidence, and which would need cross-cultural, developmental, or twin-study data to settle. If you think the request is badly posed — for example, because the boundary between a new foundation and an elaboration of an old one is not a real boundary, or because the inventory can be gerrymandered to absorb any candidate I produce — say so directly and argue that instead of pretending to force a seventh entry.
