In a seminar on self-knowledge we've been arguing about Avicenna's floating man thought experiment, and I keep flip-flopping. Just in case it isn't fresh: Avicenna has you imagine a person created all at once, an adult, but suspended in perfectly empty air, blindfolded, limbs spread so he touches nothing — no sights, no sounds, no bodily contact, and crucially no accumulated experiences to draw on. The question is whether this person would still know, with certainty, that he himself exists. One side says yes — and that this shows self-awareness isn't built up from the senses; the self is present to itself directly, while the body and its sensations are just objects of experience rather than conditions for it. The other side says the whole setup is question-begging: Avicenna already loaded the floating man with a fully formed mind that possesses the concepts 'I' and 'exists,' plus attention and the capacity to affirm anything at all — so the experiment only shows that Avicenna can't imagine himself out of the picture, not that self-awareness is innate or non-sensory.

I'd love too see a dialogue between these two positions. Make both people sharp and equally competent — neither one gets to be a caricature — and have them actually respond to each other's moves instead of taking turns restating their side. Ideally by the end one of them pins down the exact step where the other's argument quietly assumes the thing it's trying to prove.
