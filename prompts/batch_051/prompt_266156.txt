Question: Describe the rules of the dollar auction and the incentive structure they create. Suppose Alice has bid $0.95 and Bob has bid $1.00, and Alice must decide whether to drop out or raise to $1.05; after that Bob faces the same kind of choice. Walk through the short-run comparison of future payoffs that would lead Alice to raise, and explain how the bidding would continue from there. Finally, evaluate the claim that the escalation produced by this auction is best explained by the sunk-cost fallacy.

Student answer: In the dollar auction, a one-dollar bill is sold under a rule that makes the two highest bidders pay their final bids: the highest bidder receives the bill, and the second-highest bidder receives nothing. That rule creates a trap once two bidders are in, because each can compare the large loss from quitting with the much smaller loss from raising and winning. Suppose Alice has bid $0.95 and Bob has bid $1.00. If Alice drops out, she pays $0.95 and gets nothing: a certain loss of 95 cents. If she raises to $1.05 and Bob then drops out, she pays $1.05 and receives the dollar, leaving her with a loss of only 5 cents. A 5-cent loss beats a 95-cent loss, so Alice raises. Bob's calculation is then analogous: dropping out costs him $1.00, but raising to $1.10 would cost him only 10 cents if Alice quits. In this way the bids climb past the value of the bill. The engine of the escalation is the sunk-cost fallacy: each bidder carries on because they cannot stand to give up the money already paid, even though that money is gone either way. Each step feels rational locally, but sunk costs commit the bidders to an increasingly ruinous outcome.
