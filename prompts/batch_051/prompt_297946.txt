I recently began volunteering with a small anti-corruption organization that runs workshops for newly elected municipal officials, and our internal debates have made me aware that we use the word incorruptible without being able to say what would show that someone is it. One of the researchers keeps arguing that 'incorruptibility' is mostly a post hoc honorific: we apply it to people after they have refused a few conspicuous bribes, but we have little evidence that refusing one kind of inducement predicts resistance to another — a threat, a back-channel favor, a job for a family member, or the slow blurring of a conflict of interest. I am not convinced by this, but I also cannot say what evidence would change my mind. So I would like to treat the following as a testable claim: that there is a genuine disposition of incorruptibility, distinct from merely not yet having encountered the right price or the right pressure. My question is how one could test that claim well.

Could you propose a concrete study, or a small set of studies, that would separate a stable disposition to withstand improper inducements across situations from a collection of situation-bound refusals? Please specify how you would operationalize 'incorruptible' behaviorally, what population you would study, what pattern of results would count in favor of the dispositional view and what pattern would count against it, and what the test would fail to settle. In particular, I suspect that no empirical result alone could settle whether incorruptibility, even if real and stable, is always a good thing rather than sometimes a costly rigidity or a fidelity to unworthy objects; I would like that residual question addressed explicitly as well.
