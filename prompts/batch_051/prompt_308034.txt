I'm a science reporter, and our stylebook has a rule that's been driving me up the wall. It says: "A story shall not attribute a trait or behavior to a class of people, animals, or things unless the trait holds of every member of that class, or the sentence is qualified by some, most, or typically." Last week the copy desk killed a sentence of mine that said "mosquitoes carry malaria" for violating it. Fine, it's not literally every mosquito. But "ducks lay eggs" is perfectly ordinary English even though drakes and ducklings don't, and I'd bet a year's salary the drafters of that rule were thinking about lazy overgeneralizations and stereotypes, not about generics that tolerate exceptions. I've been stewing about it every since. You're an editor who also knows philosophy of language; think carefully, maybe start by brainstorming the possible readings before you commit. Work through four angles for me: the plain words, the purpose of the rule, what the drafters would have said if someone had asked them about "ducks lay eggs" when they wrote it, and what a reasonable reporter would take the rule to mean. Then give me a committed, reasoned verdict: does the malaria sentence breach the rule, and does any coherent reading catch it without also banning stock scientific English? Tell me which approach should win and why, not a survey of options.
