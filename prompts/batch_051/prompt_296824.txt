I'm a retired chemist, and I've been reading around in linguistics, where I keep bumping into an argument that sounds odd to someone trained to think in terms of learning from data. The poverty-of-the-stimulus argument, as I understand it, says that the speech children hear is too messy, too finite, and too light on explicit negative feedback to determine the grammar they end up with, so some grammatical machinery must be built in. Fine. But then I look at modern large language models. They are shown enormous amounts of plain text, with no grammar rules handed to them and no explicit negative examples, and they still wind up producing mostly grammatical sentences and even getting a lot of acceptability contrasts right. On its face, that looks like a learner extracting grammar from positive data alone.

Here's the assignment. I don't want a verdict on whether children have innate grammar. I want to know whether the poverty-of-the-stimulus argument survives this development, whether it has to retreat to a weaker claim, or whether it was always about something the language models don't touch. Which premise is actually under pressure, and is there a repaired version that says something weaker but still does real work? Please seperate what is settled among linguists from what is genuinely contested, and don't present a contested view as the consensus. American English, please.
