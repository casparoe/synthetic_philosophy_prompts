I am the sixth-form representative on my school's disciplinary panel, and next week we have to decide a case on which the panel is genuinely split. The headteacher chairs the panel and always says she wants each decision to be consistent with the school's own previous decisions, so I have gone back through her record from the past two years. Her most relevant decisions are these:

- A Year 9 pupil who pulled the fire alarm as a dare during exam week was excluded for three days.
- A Year 7 pupil was not excluded for bringing a small penknife in his bag. He had forgotten it was there, had shown it to no one, and handed it in as soon as he noticed; he was given a pastoral session instead.
- A Year 11 pupil who copied several paragraphs into a practice essay without proper citation was given zero for the assignment and a session on academic honesty, but no exclusion, because she admitted the copying and her teacher accepted that she had genuinely misunderstood the school's citation rules.
- A Year 12 pupil who deliberately broke a classroom window in anger was excluded for a week. The headteacher accepted that the pupil was under severe strain at home, but said that deliberate destruction of school property was too serious for that background to change the sanction.
- Two Year 10 pupils caught selling fake tickets to a school concert were not excluded; they repaid the money and did a month of supervised litter-picking. They had no previous record and had confessed without being caught.
- A Year 13 pupil with a previously clean record was excluded for five days for bringing a small amount of cannabis to a school social. The school's own counsellor said he was at very low risk of reoffending, but the headteacher argued that other pupils knew about the incident and needed to see the rule enforced.

Next week's case is a Year 12 pupil who handed in a history coursework essay in which several long passages were copied from an online essay bank with light rewording. She says she misunderstood this year's tightened citation rules and genuinely believed that light rewording plus a reference-list entry was acceptable; two of her teachers regard that as plausible, though another does not. The school has had an unusual run of plagiarism cases this term, and some pupils have cited the lenient Year 11 case as evidence that plagiarism is not really punished here. The standard response for plagiarism in assessed sixth-form work would award her zero for the component and place a note in her file which would be disclosed in university references. The lighter option would give her zero for the essay itself, require a supervised resubmission capped at a pass mark, and leave her file clean.

If we want to decide in line with the headteacher's record, what should we do? Please set out the principle or principles that would support each option, identify which earlier decision is the closest analogue and which others cut against it, give your best prediction of how the headteacher herself would vote, and name a further case that would most clearly reveal whether her deeper commitment is retributive, consequentialist, or something else.
