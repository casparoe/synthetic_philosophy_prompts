I'm putting together a half-day session for my department on force and motion misconceptions, and I want to stop treating every persistant wrong answer as the same kind of thing. My hunch is that a student who says a thrown baseball "carries the force of the throw inside it" has a different sort of error than one who thinks heavier things fall faster, or one who keeps mixing up speed and velocity. But I can't quite articulate what the kinds are.

Write me a decision tree (or a short sorting quiz with a scoring key, whichever works better) that takes a single student's wrong answers on a set of conceptual questions and sorts that student into a kind of systematic error. I keep hearing people talk around a few candidate kinds: a coherent alternative theory (like an impetus theory), a loose piece of intuition that only fires in certain contexts, a category error (force treated as a stuff that objects have and use up), a missing distinction (speed vs. velocity, velocity vs. acceleration), and a right intuition applied in the wrong place (closeness making something stronger, dragged somewhere it isn't true). Tell me if some of those are the same thing or if I'm missing the important ones.

The branch questions should be answerable by a teacher grading a quiz, not by someone who already knows the labels. So each node should be a question about what the student actually wrote or did: do they give the same wrong answer across superficially different contexts, can they state their wrong idea as a general rule, do they resist counter-evidence, do they talk about force as if it's a substance, does the error look like it came from our own notation or diagrams rather than from the student. Use whatever questions you think actually seperate the kinds.

Build it from the logical space first, not from a list of names: what are the underlying questions, which combinations of answers are possible, and then where do the actual kinds of error sit in that space. For combinations of answers nobody seems to occupy, say so and say whether that's because the position is incoherent, just unattractive, or plausibly real but unstudied. And don't paper over the ugly bit: I suspect a student with one very general wrong rule and a student with dozens of little fragments that happen to point the same way will look identical on my quiz. If your tree can't seperate those, flag it clearly instead of pretending it can.

Keep it usable: maybe seven or eight leaves at most, each with a one-sentence note on what my re-teaching move should be for that kind. And work these cases into the branches or examples, since they're the ones I actually asked on last semester's quiz: the ice puck sliding off a table, the ball at the top of its arc, a passenger lurching forward when a bus brakes, the claim that a thrown ball has the thrower's force stored in it, and the heavier-object-falls-faster item.
