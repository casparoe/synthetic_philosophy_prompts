Take the way a bunch of very old state abortion bans in the US suddenly became live law again after Dobbs as a case study in desuetude and the rule of law. I dont want the constitutional-law story, I already know that part: Roe essentially froze those nineteenth-century statutes for fifty years, and once Dobbs removed the federal floor, several states found themselves in the weird position of having laws that had been dead letters for generations suddenly become enforceable, or at least the subject of immediate litigation — some never formally repealed, some predating modern medical practice entirely. Analyze that through the lens of desuetude, the idea that a law can lose its status as law through sustained non-enforcement and public disregard, not through formal repeal. I want a careful mapping. While those laws were dormant, what exactly did the desuetude framework say about their validity or bindingness? What would it say the relevant actors — state legislators, prosecutors, judges, an ordinary person planning her life — faced and should have done? Which features of the episode does it explain nicely (fair notice, reliance, law drifting out of line with social practice), and where does it misdescribe or just go silent? In particular, the non-enforcement wasn't a policy choice by the state; it was a constitutional constraint imposed by a federal court. So is it even desuetude, or just a suspension? Then run it the other direction: does the episode confirm the idea that law and enforcement can't really be pulled apart, or does it show that desuetude was never a coherent doctrine in a federal system where courts do the ignoring? Be honest about where the framework has nothing useful to say rather than forcing it to speak.
