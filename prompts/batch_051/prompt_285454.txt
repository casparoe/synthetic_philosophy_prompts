In 427 BCE, after the revolt of Mytilene, the Athenians first voted to execute the adult male population and enslave the women and children, then voted the next day to reconsider. The passage below is from Diodotus's speech against that original decree, in Thucydides's History of the Peloponnesian War, Book 3, section 44, translated by Richard Crawley (who spells the city "Mitylene"):

"However, I have not come forward either to oppose or to accuse in the matter of Mitylene; indeed, the question before us as sensible men is not their guilt, but our interests. Though I prove them ever so guilty, I shall not, therefore, advise their death, unless it be expedient; nor though they should have claims to indulgence, shall I recommend it, unless it be clearly for the good of the country. I consider that we are deliberating for the future more than for the present; and where Cleon is so positive as to the useful deterrent effects that will follow from making rebellion capital, I who consider the interests of the future quite as much as he, as positively maintain the contrary. And I require you not to reject my useful considerations for his specious ones: his speech may have the attraction of seeming the more just in your present temper against Mitylene; but we are not in a court of justice, but in a political assembly; and the question is not justice, but how to make the Mitylenians useful to Athens."

Examination question: (i) State in your own words the distinction Diodotus draws between a court of justice and a political assembly, and the contrast between "the future" and "the present" on which he insists. (ii) Show how this distinction is meant to take the force out of Cleon's appeal to the Mytilenians' guilt. (iii) Identify one respect in which the distinction does not by itself settle the dispute with Cleon, and say what further considerations Diodotus needs to supply. Answer in no more than 400 words.

Student's answer:

Diodotus's key move is to separate two kinds of judgment. A law court looks backward: it asks whether the accused are guilty and apportions punishment to guilt. A political assembly looks forward: it asks which policy will leave the city better off. Because the Athenians are sitting as an assembly, not a court, the guilt of the Mytilenians — which Diodotus does not dispute — is not the decisive question; what matters is the benefit to Athens. He reinforces this with the contrast between deliberating "for the future more than for the present": the assembly should set aside the anger it feels now and attend to the consequences of its vote. On this basis he disarms Cleon's justice argument by showing that it belongs to the wrong tribunal. He then concedes the deterrent effect of the original decree, but replies that executing the common people would be a strategic mistake, since the commons in the allied cities are Athens' natural friends and would be driven into the oligarchs' hands. Thus the original vote went wrong because it answered a moral question rather than a prudential one, and the Athenians corrected it by accepting Diodotus's distinction.
