I keep turning over a parallel and I can't tell if it's real or just a writer's-brain pattern match. In Bayesian epistemology there's the problem of old evidence: roughly, if you already know a fact with probability one, standard conditionalization seems unable to explain how that same fact later supports a new theory — the stock example being Mercury's perihelion anomaly, known for decades before general relativity, and then cited as support for GR the moment the theory lands. In my line of work the same beat shows up near the end of every mystery novel. Some detail gets planted in chapter two — the scuffed left shoe, the missing cufflink — and the reader already has it; it sits there with no evidential force at all. Then in the final scene the detective's explaination retroactively turns it into the clincher. That sure looks like old evidence functioning as new support. So: is this the same structural phenomenon, or just a loose metaphor? If it's deep, do the fixes epistemologists have for old evidence (the counterfactual-prior business, or whatever the respected move is) transfer to the question of when a planted clue actually confirms a solution instead of merely feeling like it does? If it's superficial, where does the analogy snap — my hunch is it has something to do with the author controlling what the reader is cued to compute, but I can't make that precise.
