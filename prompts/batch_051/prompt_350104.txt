I'm grading a midterm in my early modern survey and I'd like a second opinion on one answer. The exam question was:

What does Leibniz mean when he says God chose the best of all possible worlds, and why can that best world still contain genuine evil? Explain the role of the principle of sufficient reason, and say how Leibniz distinguishes divine freedom from mere necessity.

Here's the student's answer, typos and all:

Leibniz thought that before creation God surveyed every possible world, and since God is perfectly good, he had to choose the one with the greatest total happiness. Calling this world the best of all possible worlds just means it maximizes aggregate well-being. God could not pick arbitrarily, because the principle of sufficient reason demands a reason why this world rather than another, and its being best is that reason. Evil is compatible with all this because some evils are necessary conditions of greater goods, the way a dissonant chord makes the resolution sweeter; the whole world is better with them than without them. Voltaire's Candide is usually read as satire, but the reader is supposed to see through Pangloss's caricature, because Leibniz never said individual suffering is pointless. Since God had a sufficient reason to choose this world, the actual world follows from God's nature with absolute necessity. Leibniz calls it hypothetical necessity to avoid sounding like Spinoza, but really there is no difference: in both systems everything actual is necessary. So Leibniz is committed to saying every particular event is just when seen as part of the whole, even if it seems evil to us.
