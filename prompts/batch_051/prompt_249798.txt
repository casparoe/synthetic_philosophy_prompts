Chase and Sanborn are the two people on the quality-control panel at the coffee shop where I work, and they've been arguing about the house roast for weeks now. Chase says the coffee tastes exactly the way it did the day we opened, but he no longer likes it — the taste hasn't changed, his reaction to it has. Sanborn says he still likes it just as much as ever, but the coffee itself tastes different now — the experience changed and his enjoyment didn't. Both are careful tasters; we've ran blind triangle tests a bunch of times and they agree about which cup is which, so "one of them just has a bad palate" doesn't seem to explain it. A regular who teaches philosophy says we're going in circles because qualia — the private, intrinsic, ineffable what-it's-like of an experience — are an incoherent posit, so there is no fact of the matter about whether Chase's taste changed or only his preference did. I don't really know whether to believe him.

So here's what I want: how would one test the claim that there's no fact of the matter between "the experience changed" and "only the evaluation changed" in a case like this? Design the strongest concrete test you can — measures, tasks, observations, whatever — and say exactly what result would favor each of the two tasters, and what result would favor the professor's deflationary view. Then be honest about what even a decisive-looking result would still leave open: are we measuring the qualia themselves or just their causes and correlates, and does the incoherence claim actually dissolve the argument or just change the subject — in other words, is the part we can't separate a limit of the experiments or a defect in the idea of qualia itself?
