I do local history as a hobby, mostly reading old town records, probate inventories, and church registers from the 1600s and 1700s in New England. A while back I noticied a pattern: the people who show up in these sources are a weird sample — property owners, officeholders, people who got fined or sued, occasionally somebody's widow. Day-to-day life for everybody else just wasn't what the records were trying to capture. So I've developed a rule I use all the time now. When someone says "the records don't show X," I mentally translate that as "the kind of people who kept records didn't have a reason to write X down," and I don't lower my confidence in X at all — unless the record was set up to catch X. Last week I used it in an argument about whether women in one of my towns ran a small informal economy in the 1600s, bartering eggs, butter, cloth, that kind of thing. My friend said there's no ledger or diary that records it. I said of course there isn't; the men writing these documents would record a woman as somebody's wife or widow, not as a trader. So the silence tells you about the record-keepers, not about the women. I'm a hobbyist and can't go read the scholarship, so I can't fact-check my friend either way. What I want to know is whether my rule is actually sound or just a bias dressed up as a rule — a way for me to wave off negative evidence whenever I don't like it. What would a defensible version of the rule look like? Specifically, when does silence in a biased archive still count as evidence against a claim, and when genuinely doesn't? And please write the answer in American English.
