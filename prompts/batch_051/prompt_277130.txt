Consider a research collective whose members each have the technical power to take actions that are irreversible and would affect the whole group—publishing a dual-use dataset, releasing a model, pushing a protocol into the field. The community is aware that it sits inside the familiar unilateralist's curse: each member's estimate of an action's expected value is noisy and roughly independent, any single member may act, and so the group tends to act whenever the most optimistic member happens to be optimistic enough, even when the median member—and a bare majority vote—would oppose the action.

To blunt the curse, a member proposes the following standing rule, which I would like you to treat as a mechanism rather than as a proposal to approve or reject. Before taking any action on a predefined list of irreversible acts, the would-be actor must post a public notice stating a numerical estimate of the action's expected value, and must put down a deposit proportional to the absolute value of that estimate—say one-tenth of one percent, capped at a fixed sum. Over the next week, any other member may post exactly one response, either an endorsement or a challenge, each of which must also state a numerical estimate and carry a deposit of the same proportion; members may instead abstain. If no challenge appears, the action proceeds at the end of the week. If a challenge appears, the matter goes to a three-person panel drawn by lot from the filed endorsements and challenges, excluding the original actor; seats left unfilled by a shortage of responses are filled by lot from the full membership. The panel deliberates for forty-eight hours and votes by secret ballot. A two-thirds vote permits the action; otherwise the action is suspended for six months, after which a new notice may be filed. In every outcome, deposits are returned in full to whichever side prevails and are forfeited by the other side to an independent harm-remediation fund, not paid out to the winners.

Walk through what this rule does at each stage. What incentives does it create for how members form, express, and distort their numerical estimates; which projects get proposed in the first place; when members find it worth endorsing or challenging; and how the panel lottery interacts with self-selection into the response pool? Where is the rule gamed first, and where does it fail even in the absence of bad faith? Finally, indicate whether the failures could be patched—by altering the deposit schedule, the voting threshold, the composition of the panel, or the length of the suspension—without reintroducing the unilateralist's curse the rule was meant to dampen.
