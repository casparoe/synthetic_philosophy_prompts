I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of law — fourteen students, ninety-minute sessions — and I want to open our unit on anti-discrimination law with an activity that makes the problem of protected characteristics arise for students before they read any doctrine. The idea I want them to feel is that no single criterion (immutability, involuntariness, visibility, a history of invidious discrimination, political powerlessness) cleanly sorts the traits the law does protect from the traits it does not; any line leaves counterexamples, and any principle a student offers will sweep in neighboring cases they did not mean to protect. In past years, seminar discussion of the familiar examples lets students trade intuitions without ever paying for them, so I want an exercise in which the cost of each criterion is actually incurred, with no framing lecture beforehand.

I am imagining a structured deliberation in which the class acts as a constituent assembly or equality commission given a hard constraint: they may enter only seven grounds in the draft equality clause, because each protected ground creates a new avenue of claims and the enforcement budget can absorb no more (or, if you think some other constraint works better, use that). Students first commit to a list individually or in pairs, then defend their entries in plenary. The central mechanism I would like you to design around is this: no ground may be added unless its proponent states the general criterion that qualifies it, after which any other participant may propose a "neighbor" trait that satisfies that criterion and ask whether it must be admitted too; if it need not be, the proponent must say which further feature breaks the tie. I would like the menu of candidate grounds to mix familiar ones (race, sex, religion, disability, age) with less familiar ones — physical appearance or height, weight, criminal record, political affiliation, ancestry or caste-like inherited status, accent or dialect, caregiver status, genetic predispositions, veteran status — so that their criteria get stressed in directions they had not anticipated.

Please give me a fully runnable plan: a precise charge to the commission, rules of debate and amendment, any ballots, slides, or handout text, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps the moments of the session onto the conceptual terrain — immutability, the chosen/unchosen distinction, political powerlessness and the political-process rationale, the split between treating discrimination as wrongful classification and treating it as wrongful subordination, concealability, and the recognition that the doctrine of suspect grounds is a patchwork of several of these rather than a single principled test. The debrief should name these only after students have discovered the tensions themselves. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be frank about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply afterwards.
