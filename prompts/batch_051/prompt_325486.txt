I work in a small statutory body that decides, case by case, whether an organisation qualifies as a religion or belief for legal purposes: charitable status, the solemnisation of marriages, representation on school religious-education bodies, inclusion in hate-crime and equality protections, and access to chaplaincy facilities. The governing statute deliberately avoids defining either 'religion' or 'belief', and I need a decision procedure I can use repeatedly, not a reading list or a set of authorities.

I am not a scholar of religion. The procedure must be usable by a non-specialist case officer in one working day per application, with a lawyer reviewing the output before the Board signs it off. It should take seriously the view that 'religion' is not a natural kind but a historically particular, roughly Protestant-shaped category built around beliefs, texts, a congregation and a transcendent deity, and that the category strains when applied to Buddhism, Confucianism or self-described secular religions. At the same time, it must not collapse into 'whatever a person sincerely and deeply holds', since then every organised enthusiasm would qualify, and it must not require a supreme being, since that would exclude much of Buddhism.

Give me a usable procedure: a checklist, a decision tree, or ordered tests. The answer should (a) set the procedure out step by step, (b) mark which steps are mechanical and which are judgement calls that no procedure can eliminate, (c) state explicitly whether the procedure relies on necessary-and-sufficient conditions, family resemblance, social function, or some other principle, and what follows from that choice, and (d) say where the procedure is most likely to misfire.

Then apply the procedure to three worked cases: a conventional Theravada Buddhist monastic foundation seeking charitable status; a secular humanist association seeking the same marriage-solemnisation rights as religious bodies; and one case in which the procedure yields a decision an officer would resist — for example, requiring recognition of a group the officer finds unserious, or refusing recognition to a group the officer personally regards as obviously religious. Conclude by stating what the procedure cannot settle about the category 'religion' itself. Write the answer in British English.
