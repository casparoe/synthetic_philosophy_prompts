Next week I'm leading the first discussion section of an introductory metaphysics course on the problem of universals, and I want to hand out a sorting instrument before I have taught any of the labels. The students have done one background reading that poses the one-over-many problem through a stock case—two billiard balls of exactly the same shade of red—but the taxonomy itself is still untaught. Last year the room split almost perfectly between "there has to be one shared thing present in both balls" and "there are just the two balls and that's all," and I want this instrument to make the neglected middle options visible. Please write a "which position on universals do you actually hold?" decision quiz that sorts students by what they say about cases and plainly stated claims, not by which label they prefer. Accordingly, no quiz question may use the words "universal," "trope," "nominalism," or "realism," or any named position; every question must be a concrete case or a claim a novice could assess.

Build the tree from the logical space rather than from a menu of names: lay out the diagnostic questions and the answer-combinations they permit, then locate the named positions inside that space, and explicitly flag answer-combinations nobody occupies, saying in each case whether the cell is unoccupied because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. There are three separations I especially need. First, the two red billiard balls must sort respondents into three camps: (a) one and the same redness is wholly present in both balls, (b) each ball has its own particular redness and the two rednesses exactly resemble, and (c) no individual redness exists in the world at all—there are just the two balls, and they resemble each other. Second, within the camp that denies individual rednesses, force a further three-way split among explaining true predications by resemblance among particulars, by membership in a class or set of resembling particulars, and by refusing to explain—the view that the ball's simply being red is a permissible stopping point, sometimes called ostrich or predicate nominalism. Third, use one question about an uninstantiated shade or property—a shade of red no actual thing has ever had—to divide Platonic (ante rem) realists, who allow such properties to exist anyway, from Aristotelian (in re) realists, who deny them, and use one question about whether facts about the balls' color would hold if no minds ever existed to see whether any leaf should be conceptualist. The leaves that must appear, with names, are: Platonic (ante rem) realism, Aristotelian (in re) realism, trope theory, resemblance nominalism, class nominalism, ostrich nominalism, and conceptualism; flag any pair of leaves your questions cannot separate rather than papering over the difference.

Make it exactly six diagnostic questions pitched at students with no more than thirty minutes of exposure, and phrase every question so that someone can answer it without knowing which position it routes toward. Format the response as a numbered list with exactly seven items and no introduction or conclusion: items 1 through 6 are the diagnostic questions, each one or two sentences, each giving two or three answer options and stating the route each answer takes through the tree; item 7 is the key, in two sentences, mapping answer patterns to the seven leaves and listing any empty cells with a one-clause diagnosis.
