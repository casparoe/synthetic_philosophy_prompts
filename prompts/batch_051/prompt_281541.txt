I find myself drawn to a view of psychiatric classification, developed in an especially clear form by Ian Hacking, on which many psychiatric categories are interactive kinds rather than indifferent kinds. A natural kind such as a chemical element is indifferent: classifying a sample of matter as gold does nothing to the gold. But when human beings are classified — as depressed, autistic, or ADHD — the classification enters their self-understanding, their social world, and the expectations of clinicians and institutions; their behavior and experience change in response; and this feeds back into the category itself, so that the phenomenon the category was meant to capture is altered by the act of naming it. This is the looping effect, and it makes such kinds unstable, historical, and partly made rather than simply found. The view attracts me because it unifies otherwise puzzling facts: why some diagnostic categories swell into apparent epidemics and then fade, why some conditions seem bound to particular cultures, and why the announcement of a diagnosis can itself alter the trajectory of the diagnosed condition.

But I also have a firm intuition that this cannot be the whole story for every psychiatric category. It seems to me that at least some categories — schizophrenia, say, or severe depression — pick out recurrent, disabling patterns of thought, affect, and behavior that would be there even if no one had ever named them. I do not believe their prevalence and course are wholly indifferent to classification, but I cannot shake the sense that these cases answer to something mind-independent and discovered, not merely stabilized by practices of classification. The looping-effect view, read flat-footedly, seems to imply that no psychiatric category is ever straightforwardly answerable to a mind-independent condition, and I find that implication hard to accept.

I want to know which of these should give way, and what would justify giving it up. Should I treat my realism about certain psychiatric kinds as a hangover of naive essentialism and debunk it? Should I amend the looping-effect view so that it applies only to some unstable or culturally plastic categories, while allowing that others are substantially constrained by an underlying reality? Or is there a reading on which both are correct — on which a kind can be interactive and still answer to something real, so that looping effects and mind-independence are compatible features of one and the same category? Give me the strongest version of each position and state, for each possible resolution, what would justify it; do not merely announce which side you favor.
