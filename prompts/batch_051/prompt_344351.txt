Write a four-stanza pantoum in the voice of the old general from Thomas Reid's brave-officer objection to Locke's memory criterion of personal identity. Use this repetition scheme: stanza 1, A B C D; stanza 2, B E D F; stanza 3, E G F H; stanza 4, G C H A. Stanza 1 should present the boy being flogged for robbing an orchard, as something the speaker now knows only by report; stanza 2, the young officer who remembers the flogging; stanza 3, the old general who remembers taking the enemy standard but has lost the boy entirely. Let the pantoum's repetition carry the argument. The lines the scheme propagates from one stanza to the next are the direct memory-links: B and D let the officer take up the boy's story, E and F let the general take up the officer's. The dropped lines, A and C, are what memory lets go, and they disappear until the final stanza, where the scheme itself restores them as C and A — the boy's story set next to the general's, not through any single unbroken recollection, but through the overlapping chain that runs through the officer. Make that final turn enact the continuity reply to Reid: personal identity is not one act of remembering but overlapping memory-links, and the form, not the speaker's recollection, is what finally bring boy and general together. A reader who knows the debate should be able to locate Locke's criterion, Reid's objection, and the continuity reply in the poem's structure; a reader who does not should still find it a coherent poem about being estranged from one's own childhood. Keep the repeated lines exact, and make each read naturally in both of its positions rather than as padding.
