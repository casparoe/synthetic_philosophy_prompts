I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I've been going down a rabbit hole on Locke's labor theory for a book I'm writing about a breakaway settlement where every argument comes down to who worked the land and who didn't. I've got a passage from Locke's Second Treatise, Chapter V, section 28, and I thought I understood it, but then I read an online essay that summarized Locke like this:

"In the state of nature, everything is owned in common. But when a person mixes his labor with something — say, by picking an apple or digging ore — that thing becomes his private property. The labor is his own, and whatever it touches is removed from the common stock. No one else's consent is needed, and the right he acquires is absolute. This is also why Locke thought European settlers could take land from Native peoples who weren't improving it by European standards: they hadn't mixed their labor with it, so the land was still common property."

The article was confident and fluent, but something about that last move feels too quick, and maybe the word "absolute" is doing more than it should. I'd rather not just trust my novelist's instinct. Here's the passage I was reading:

"We see in commons, which remain so by compact, that it is the taking any part of what is common, and removing it out of the state nature leaves it in, which begins the property; without which the common is of no use. And the taking of this or that part, does not depend on the express consent of all the commoners. Thus the grass my horse has bit; the turfs my servant has cut; and the ore I have digged in any place, where I have a right to them in common with others, become my property, without the assignation or consent of any body."
