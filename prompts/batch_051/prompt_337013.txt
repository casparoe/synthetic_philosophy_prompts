It's an extra credit thing our econ teacher did, and after class two friends of mine got in a huge argument about it. We each wrote "share" or "take" on an index card, secretly, and then the cards were matched with a student from the other period of the same class. Teacher said the matching was by seat number, so you get paired with whoever sits in that seat in the other room. You never find out who it was. If both share, both get 5 bonus points on the next test. If both take, both get 1 point. If one shares and one takes, the taker gets 8 and the sharer gets 0. One round, no repeats.

Afterwards Lena and Priya argued and I just listened.

(1) Priya: "Take is obviously right. Whatever the other person does, take gets you more. If they share, take gives 8 instead of 5. If they take, take gives 1 instead of 0. It's the textbook definition of a dominated strategy."

(2) Lena: "That only works if you hold their choice fixed while you decide. But they're deciding the same problem from the same information, and we got matched by seat — same position, same class, same payoffs. If my reasoning says 'share,' the person in my seat in the other period probably reasons 'share' too. So the actual comparison is both-share, where I get 5, versus both-take, where I get 1."

(3) Priya: "You're using your own decision as evidence about what's going on inside someone else's skull, but your decision doesn't get transmitted over there. You can't do expected value by conditioning on your own action. That's like saying people who go to the hospital are sicker, so I'll choose to go to the hospital because my choosing to go is evidence I'm sick."

(4) Lena: "No, in the hospital case the correlation goes through a hidden thing — actually being sick. Here the correlation would run through the reasoning itself. If the partner runs the same reasoning on the same inputs, then 'I share' basically implies 'they share.' In that case sharing gets me 5 and taking gets me 1."

(5) Priya: "But they're not a copy of you. The person in my seat might not care about bonus points, might misread the payoffs, might think it's funny to take. The second they're not a perfect copy, your implication breaks, and we're back to take being safe. Real people are seperate reasoners."

(6) Lena: "Ok, but then how similar do they have to be? Suppose the partner is 90% likely to make the same choice as me either way. Then share gives .9×5 plus .1×0, which is 4.5. Take gives .1×8 plus .9×1, which is 1.7. Sharing wins without them being a copy at all."

(7) Priya: "Where did 90% come from? You're just making up a number so the math comes out your way. With a random matchup we'd match half the time, and take would crush share. You can't rescue a dominated strategy by assuming your partner is your doppelganger."

(8) Lena: "Then the whole argument is about how much correlation is justified, not about whether take 'dominates.' Dominance only works under independence. The teacher matched us by seat, same class, same payoffs — that's some correlation, and the textbook answer to 'take or share' changes depending on how much."

I couldn't tell who was winning. Can you go through their exchange move by move and say who's right about what, where each of them overreaches, and what they're both missing? Be specific about the difference between a choice being evidence about the partner and a choice causing what the partner does, when it's legitimate to condition expected value on your own action, and whether Priya's "they're not a copy" point actually kills Lena's argument or not.
