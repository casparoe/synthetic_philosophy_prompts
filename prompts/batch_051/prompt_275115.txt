I'm a philosophy professor and I've been asked to give the eulogy at the memorial for my colleague Daniel, who died last month at seventy-two. For forty years he worked on one question: how do moral revolutions actually happen? He wrote about the abolition of slavery and the end of dueling, and his settled view — the thing he defended in print and at every confernce — was that they are not won by moral argument. He thought arguments are mostly the noise the cart makes after the horse has already turned. On slavery he sided with the Williams thesis: abolition came when it did because the material interests of the powerful shifted, not because the humanitarians finally found the right argument. On dueling he followed the Appiah line: it ended when honor got redefined so that dueling started to look absurd and shameful rather than gallant. He really believed this. It wasn't a pose he struck for the journals.

The trouble is that every natural eulogy move is exactly the move he spent his career warning against. The obvious thing is to say his arguments changed the field, that he persuaded a generation, that the force of his reasoning moved us. He would have heard that as a gentle, affectionate refutation of his whole life. And the room will be half people who knew his work and will hear the irony if I say it, and half family who just want to be comforted and won't want a philosophy lecture. So the speech has to do real work: it has to honor him without claiming he won by argument, because on his own account that is not how anything important ever changes, and it has to land as grief and love rather than as a seminar. Can you write it? Five minutes or so read aloud. And please no formatting — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables; just continuous prose, the way you'd write a letter.
