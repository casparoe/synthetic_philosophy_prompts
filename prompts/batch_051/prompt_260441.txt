I am a PhD student working on AI alignment, and a dispute that has been running through our reading group for over a year has come to interest me less as a fight to be won than as a diagnostic puzzle. One camp holds that a trained system, especially after reinforcement learning, is best modeled as an expected utility maximizer with some possibly learned utility function; they treat that formalization as the load-bearing premise behind the familiar claims that such systems pursue instrumental subgoals, exploit whatever proxy their reward signal tracks, and generalize the same underlying objective to new regimes. The other camp regards the expected utility maximizer as a projection rather than a structure in the network: what the training process actually installs, on their view, is a large population of contextually activated value fragments, reinforced at different moments and active in different situations, with no single coherent global goal sitting in the weights. Each side says it can explain the same canonical phenomena—instrumental convergence, power-seeking, reward hacking, misgeneralization—and each side seems unable to state what the other would have to show to change its mind.

My working hypothesis is that the stalemate is not primarily an empirical argument about what is inside the network, but a difference about what a good explanation of a system's behavior is supposed to do: one side wants a compact predictive theory that supports reasoning about what the system will do in new situations, the other wants a decomposition faithful to the system's actual computational structure, and something like the distinction between adopting a stance and discovering a mechanism is doing the background work. But I can see at least three other possibilities—a hidden empirical question (whether reward-based optimization tends to produce a unified utility function under some conditions), a formal question (what the power-seeking results actually require), and a merely verbal equivocation on the word goal. I would like a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is, grounded in how each side actually argues, and an honest account of what would count as progress given that diagnosis: a theorem, an experiment, a distinction, a reframing, or a reason to think no progress should be expected because the two sides are answering different questions under one heading.
