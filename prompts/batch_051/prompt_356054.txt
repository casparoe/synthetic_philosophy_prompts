Here are the standard objections to deflationary theories of truth I've collected over the years, mostly from writing an intro chapter:

- the generalization problem: the truth predicate is a device for expressing general claims like 'everything the Pope says is true,' but deflationary accounts built around the schema ('S' is true iff S) only seem to work when a sentence is displayed, so they can't handle these uses;
- the truth-bearer problem: the schema needs a story about what the bearers of truth are (sentences, utterances, propositions), and the natural story uses the very notion of propositional content deflationism was supposed to avoid or else leaves the account ungrounded;
- the normativity objection: truth is supposed to be the aim of assertion and belief, but deflationary truth is to thin to explain why it amounts to an ought at all;
- the success objection: true beliefs reliably produce more successful action, and deflationism can't explain what the property of truth is doing in that explanation;
- the truth-and-meaning objection: if truth-conditional semantics is our best account of meaning, then deflationism takes away the central notion that account uses;
- the conservativeness objection: adding a deflationary truth predicate to a background theory like arithmetic doesn't let you prove anything new, whereas a substantial truth theory gives you consistency and reflection principles, so deflationary truth is inferentially weaker than truth as we use it in mathematics.

I have the feeling there's at least one well-known objection I'm forgetting. What is it? It has to be genuinely different from those six, not a special case or a restatement of one of them (so no dressing up the normativity point as a new worry). Then tell me honestly how strong you think it is compared to the ones on my list, and what would change your mind about that.
