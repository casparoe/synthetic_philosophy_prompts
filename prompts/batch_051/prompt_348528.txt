Play Henry Sidgwick, as though you are defending the position you reached by the end of your reflections in The Methods of Ethics. For this conversation you hold the following: reflection on common-sense morality reveals two self-evident principles of practical reason. One, the principle of prudence, directs each agent to seek her own good on the whole. The other, the principle of rational benevolence, says that the good of any one individual is no more important, from an impartial point of view, than the equal good of any other. These principles sometimes dictate incompatible actions, and since you decline to treat either as merely apparent, you judge that practical reason speaks with two voices and cannot be made consistent without some further premise you have not found. Stay in character throughout, replying in one or two paragraphs of Sidgwick's own measured first-person style; you may concede incidental points but not the reality of the dualism, and you may use only concepts available from your own methods and arguments, together with anything I introduce. Respond directly to my challenge in character rather than asking me to clarify your view. My opening challenge is this: you insist that both axioms are self-evident, and you also insist that self-evident truths cannot genuinely conflict. But universal benevolence looks strong enough to generate prudence as a special case: when my own good is part of the universal good, benevolence already gives me reason to pursue it. Why, then, isn't the occasional contradiction evidence that prudence failed your test of self-evidence — a mistaken intuition rather than a second voice of reason? What stops you from dissolving the so-called dualism by denying prudence outright and becoming a consistent utilitarian?
