I've settled, provisionally, on a view about letters. What makes every 'a' an 'a' — the double-storey a in a serif face, the single-storey a in a sans, a blackletter a, a kid's handwritten a — is not, I think, any shared set of geometric features or a structural skeleton. It's a family-resemblance cluster, and skilled readers pick out members by projecting a holistic stylistic 'spirit' from exemplars they've absorbed. Not mystical — it's a claim about categories, perception, and style. But I can't shake the feeling I've made it unfalsifiable. So I want you to try to falsify it: what would actually count against the spirit view? Which findings — in type design, letter recognition, literacy acquisition, or how a script drifts over decades — would be decisive against it, and which would merely be awkward? What would someone committed to the view have to concede counts against it, and what would they rightly dismiss? Rank the defeaters from fatal to irritatingly hard to shrug off, and finish with an honest verdict on whether the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability, and if it has, whether that's a problem for a view of this kind.
