You are an expert philosopher of science who works on the epistemology of thought experiments and scientific modeling. I teach an upper-level undergraduate philosophy of science course, and I want to design a short in-class instrument called "How do you think thought experiments work?" It should be a decision tree that sorts a student from a starting opinion (roughly: scientific thought experiments sometimes give us knowledge we did not have before) through a series of branching questions to a named epistemological position.

The domain is how, or whether, such exercises as Einstein's elevator, Maxwell's demon, Galileo's falling bodies, Newton's bucket, or Schrödinger's cat can yield knowledge about the world.

The following positions must appear as leaves. You may add at most one further leaf if the logical space genuinely forces it.

- Eliminativism/illustrationism: scientific thought experiments never produce new knowledge; they are vivid aids for packaging, teaching, motivating, or reorganizing results that could be or already were obtained another way.
- Argument view: a successful thought experiment is a valid argument whose premises come from existing empirical knowledge or background theory; whatever its conclusion "yields" is already contained in those premises.
- Platonist/a priorist intuitionism: thought experiments can yield genuinely new knowledge by giving direct rational or intuitive insight into laws of nature or abstract features of reality, going beyond what accepted empirical premises entail.
- Mental-model/simulation view: thought experiments yield knowledge by running an internal model of a real process, drawing on tacit empirical know-how of the same kind real experiments use, but without physical execution.
- Constructivist/conceptual-change view: thought experiments yield knowledge by exposing tensions, ambiguities, or inadequacies in our concepts and sometimes forcing conceptual revision; what is learned is primarily about our conceptual framework and its adequacy, not directly a mind-independent fact.

Build the tree from logical space rather than from the list of names. First settle the key branching questions (for example: does the exercise yield something new? If so, is the source empirical or non-empirical? If non-empirical, is it intuition into laws or scrutiny of concepts? If empirical, does it work by simulation or by argument?). Then locate the positions in the resulting space.

Include every mutually exclusive combination of answers your branching questions define, and for each combination that has no occupant, diagnose why: is it incoherent, unattractive, merely unexplored, or something else? For instance, I suspect the combination "yields new knowledge, source empirical, but neither simulation nor argument" is empty or unstable, but you should say why and whether that is right.

Keep the instrument to roughly 6–9 branching nodes and 5–6 leaves. Phrase every node as a question or a concrete case verdict that a student can answer without knowing which position it leads to. Use the anchor cases where they help: for example, ask whether Galileo established something new by reasoning about falling bodies before any real test, and whether whatever is "new" there concerns the world or our framework. Do not ask anything like "are you a Platonist?"

Finally, flag any pairs of leaves your tree cannot really separate, and say what further case or question would be needed to pull them apart. I would rather have two positions honestly marked as under-separated than a question only a specialist could answer.

Present the tree in two forms. First, a human-readable outline with node IDs, the question at each node, the answer options, and which node or leaf each option leads to. Then a JSON object with this schema (all fields required):

{
  "nodes": [
    {"id": "string", "question": "string", "options": [{"text": "string", "leads_to": "string"}]}
  ],
  "leaves": [
    {"id": "string", "name": "string", "gloss": "string", "path": ["string"]}
  ],
  "unoccupied_combinations": [
    {"combination": "string", "diagnosis": "string", "status": "incoherent | unattractive | unexplored | other"}
  ],
  "inseparable_pairs": [
    {"position_a": "string", "position_b": "string", "why": "string", "separating_question_if_any": "string"}
  ]
}

For "nodes", "leads_to" must be the ID of another node or of a leaf. For "leaves", "path" is an ordered list of strings, each of the form "node_id: chosen option text", recording the route to that leaf.

Please think through the design carefully before finalizing it. Begin by brainstorming 10–12 candidate diagnostic questions and how each of the five named positions would answer them, then select the smallest set of questions that still separates the positions, then assemble the final tree and fill in the JSON exactly according to the schema above.
