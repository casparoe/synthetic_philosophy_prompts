In his essay “The Problem With Counterfeit People” (The Atlantic, May 2023), Daniel C. Dennett writes:

“Our natural inclination to treat anything that seems to talk sensibly with us as a person—adopting what I have called the ‘intentional stance’—turns out to be easy to invoke and almost impossible to resist, even for experts.”

Dennett’s worry in the surrounding argument is that this built-in interpretive reflex makes us defenseless against AI systems that merely seem to be persons. I have developed a personal rule as a partial defense, and I want to know whether the rule is actually defensible.

In my work I regularly receive reports, emails, and drafts of unknown or mixed provenance, and I also occasionally interact with chatbots that are openly labeled as such. My rule is this: whenever I know, or seriously suspect, that a stretch of text was produced by a language model, I refuse to take the intentional stance toward it. I do not treat the text as testimony. I do not take it to be anyone’s assertion; I give the purported speaker no credit for candor or competence; and I treat any argument it contains as a mere object to be checked against another source rather than as a reason-giving act addressed to me. A colleague says the rule is incoherent. If Dennett is right that the intentional stance is almost impossible to resist, I am probably still exercising it covertly while telling myself I am not; and if I really could shut it off, I would be ignoring sound reasoning solely because of its origin. Taking Dennett’s passage as your point of departure, is my rule defensible? Should I keep it, abandon it, or amend it—and on what principle?
