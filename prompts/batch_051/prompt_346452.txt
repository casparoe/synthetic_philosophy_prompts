I work as a novelist, and a technical concept from AI alignment has begun to feel, to me, like the same problem I deal with every time I revise. The concept is legibility: for systems assembled from current methods — fine-tuning, reinforcement learning from human feedback, chain-of-thought — one increasingly hears that it is not enough for the outputs to be correct; they must be understandable. The thought is that a human overseer should be able to reconstruct, from the system's answer or its stated reasoning, why it answered as it did, and that a slightly less capable system whose answer can be traced may be preferable to a highly capable system whose correct answers arrive by unexaminable means.

I am unsure whether this is the same principle I already accept as a writer: a story must be legible before it is true. I do not mean plausibility. A scene whose events are realistic, and whose character's actions are consistent with her psychology, can still fail. It is finished only when a reader can reconstruct, from the page alone, why each character did what she did. I have seen a draft fixed not by changing what happens but by making the chain of reasons visible to the reader — often at the cost of some surprise, or some realistic detail that would have read as arbitrary. In both cases the property being optimized seems to be a property of the output as read by an interpreter, not of the output alone, and in both cases imposing it narrows the space of acceptable outputs.

I would like a verdict on whether this is deep or superficial, but also the mapping made explicit: what corresponds to what, what would have to be true on both sides for a result to transfer, and at least one thing the parallel predicts that does not hold on one of the two sides. There is one disanalogy I cannot stop circling. A character's reasons are artifacts I have invented, or failed to invent, for the reader to find; a model's reasoning is supposed to be the actual cause of its output, not a performance staged for the overseer. Is that disanalogy fatal, or is the author's actual process — which no reader ever sees directly — the better analogue to the model after all?
