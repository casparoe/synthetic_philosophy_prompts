I'm taking a philosophy of biology seminar and got more or less forced into defending the selected-effects (etiological) account of biological function — the view that a trait's function is what it was selected for, not whatever it happens to be doing now. I've already been hit with the standard counterexamples: the swampman, the novel beneficial mutation that has no selection history yet, the proto-heart that has never actually pumped blood. I want to go past those. What else would count against the view, and how badly?

Lay out for me the defeaters a committed selected-effects theorist should actually lose sleep over, not just the ones critics trot out. I'm especially curious about exaptations and co-option (feathers before flight, pharyngeal jaws), vestigial organs, knockout experiments where function is inferred from what breaks when you remove something, and the way developmental biologists and physiologists talk about function in what sure looks like a causal-role way rather than a historical one. For each, say whether it's fatal, merely awkward, or absorbable with a distinction. And I'd like an honest verdict at the end: has the view been qualified so much that it's stopped risking anything, and if so is that a real problem for a account of function in biology or just a feature of how biologists talk?
