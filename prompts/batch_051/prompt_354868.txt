In my moral psychology course, the assigned textbook summarizes the Knobe effect like this. In the original experiment, subjects read about a corporate chairman deciding whether to launch a program that will increase profits. One group is told that the program will also harm the environment; another group is told that it will also help the environment. In both versions the chairman responds the same way: he says that he cares only about making money, not about the environmental effect one way or the other, and that the program should go ahead. Subjects are then asked whether the chairman intentionally harmed (or helped) the environment. Most say that he intentionally harmed it, while most deny that he intentionally helped it. Because the chairman’s deliberation is identical across the two conditions, the textbook says, the experiment isolates the moral valence of the side effect as the one variable that changes. It concludes that ordinary people’s moral assessments—specifically, how much blame they assign to the agent—determine whether they classify a foreseen side effect as intentional, and it draws the broader lesson that moral evaluation distorts people’s factual judgments about what an agent did.

I have to write a short response paper evaluating whether this textbook summary is fair. I suspect that the move from the reported asymmetry to the conclusion about distortion is doing more than the experiment supports, but I cannot reliably tell where the summary stops reporting the finding and starts interpreting it. Could you audit the summary for me? Please indicate which parts an experimental philosopher would accept as accurate, which parts are accepting a common simplification or overstatement, and whether the final lesson about distorting factual judgments follows from what Knobe’s experiment actually shows. Where useful, say how a defender of the Knobe effect and how one of its critics would each frame the disputed points.
