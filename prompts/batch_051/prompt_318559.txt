I'm a new trustee at a small foundation that funds basic research, and I've noticed my votes on renewals don't fit the simple rule the rest of the board uses — something like "once a theory has been falsified, you cut it." Here are my honest gut calls on seven recent cases.

A. This team has spent nine years on one model of ecosystem collapse. Every time a field prediction failed, they added a parameter or a boundary clause; there are now fourteen of them. The model fits everything it was built to fit, but I can't find a single risky prediction it got right beforehand. I'd cut them, even though no individual experiment killed the model.

B. This group works on a model of protein folding whose core assumption has a known anomaly: there's a whole class of proteins it simply mispredicts. They bracket that class openly. But outside it they keep generating testable predictions, and three of them have been confirmed in the past two years. I'd renew them, anomaly and all.

C. This group made a bold new prediction last year, and it failed. Instead of touching the core, they blamed an auxillary assumption about the chemical assay, replaced the assay with one that had been independently validated, re-ran, and the prediction came out confirmed. I'd renew them. The failure was in the instrument, so why kill the model?

D. I have to choose between two teams to fund. D1's model just systematizes the existing database, and it's simpler. D2's model fits the old data no better, but it sticks its neck out with a precise, risky prediction that can be tested next grant period. My gut says fund D2, even though on simplicity D1 is neater.

E. This group made a novel prediction that was disconfirmed. They then rewrote the model's auxiliary assumptions so that the failed prediction is no longer derived. The new version hasn't yet produced any new testable prediction, though they insist one is coming. My gut says give them one short grant, but not indefinitely — and I can't articulate the clock.

F. This group looks, on the surface, as patch-work as A: they adjust the model after every failure. But each adjustment was prompted by independent evidence and then made a further prediction, two of which have been confirmed. I'd keep funding them.

G. The mirror of C. Another team had a failed prediction and also blamed the assay — but their fix wasn't independently validated beforehand; it was a post-hoc story about why the experiment went wrong. I'd cut them, even though it sounds like the same move as C.

My provisional principle is something like: feed a research program when its fixes pay their way with surprising new confirmations, and starve it when its fixes only explain away failure. But that feels circular, and I can't tell whether it actually captures the A-vs-F distinction or the C-vs-G distinction, or what it says about a once-productive program that has just gone quiet while its anomalies pile up.

You're an expert philosopher of science who works on scientific change. Please think carefully, try out candidate principles against A–G, and then state the best principle or small set of candidate principles explicitly. Don't give me generic foundation-management advice; I want the underlying principle, and I'd rather you tell me one of my verdicts has to go than flatten the cases to save all of them. No citations or reading list needed. At the very end, as the final line of your response, give a one-sentence statement of the principle you endorse, in the form \answer{...}.
