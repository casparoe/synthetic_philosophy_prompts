I'm grading a short-answer question from my upper-level archaeological methods course and I'd like a careful, point-by-point marking of one student's submission.

The exam question read:

"In a shallow pit cut into a river terrace, excavators recovered 41 deliberately shattered ceramic vessels whose sherds refit to make up nearly all of each vessel, a thin scatter of charred grain, and six small worked animal bones. There is no hearth, no ash layer beyond the charred grain, no unworked animal bone, and no other refuse in the pit. The pit lies 150 metres from a contemporary settlement. In an essay, assess (1) how confidently we can infer that this deposit is ritual rather than the product of everyday activity, and (2) what sort of belief or institution, if any, the deposit licenses us to infer. Be explicit about the rules of inference you are using and about the alternative explanations your conclusion must exclude."

The student wrote:

"The key is to read the deposit in association and context rather than treating the pots as isolated finds. Because the sherds refit into almost complete vessels, the pots were probably broken at or near the place of deposition rather than swept in as ordinary rubbish, which tends to be fragmentary and dispersed. The lack of hearth, ash, and unworked bone also tells against a domestic midden. The pit looks like a single deliberate act of deposition rather than the slow accumulation of household waste.

A useful rule here is equifinality: the same material pattern can be produced by more than one past behaviour, so we should compare competing scenarios rather than assume our favourite. The plausible alternatives are accidental breakage during storage or transport, disposal of pottery-production failures, and intentional ritual breakage of vessels. Accidental breakage is unlikely on this scale, since 41 vessels in a small pit should not result from a single mishap and would not arrive so complete. Production failures are also unlikely, since no kiln debris, overfired wasters, or potters' tools were found. Having excluded these utilitarian explanations, we can attribute the deposit to ritual.

Once ritual use is established, the associated belief follows fairly directly. Deliberately broken vessels and burnt grain buried together near a settlement are the classic signature of offerings to ancestral or underworld powers: the pots were ritually killed so they could pass to the otherworld. The absence of burials does not weaken this, because offerings need not accompany a body. We cannot be absolutely certain, since no texts survive, but archaeological inference aims at the most reasonable explanation of the evidence, and that is what we have here."
