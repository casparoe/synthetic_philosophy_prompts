Novelist here. In my next book a tech billionaire runs a betting pool where you sit in a booth and choose one or two boxes, and the million-dollar box is filled based on a simulation of you that runs while you're in the booth. The simulation is a copy of your current brain state when you enter, it receives exactly the same sensory inputs you receive up to the moment of choice, and it runs on hardware in the same room, faster than real time, while you deliberate. The opaque box is filled the moment the simulation commits, which is usually a few seconds before you commit. The machine destroys the simulation right after. This is obviously Newcomb's problem, except the predictor is not an outside oracle; it's a running copy of you embedded in the same physical situation.

I'm trying to figure out which features of the original Newcomb's problem are doing the philosophical work and which ones my booth is accidentally discarding. Specifically: does the causal decision theorist's two-box dominance argument still go through when the prediction and the choice are two token runs of the same computational process? Does the evidential decision theorist's one-box argument get stronger or weaker when the predictor isn't merely statistically correlated with you but is, in some sense, you? And if the simulation has, say, a 1 percent chance of drifting from your actual decision, at what point does that break the one-box argument? I'd mainly like to know wether my version still reproduces the original dispute or just collapses into something else, and whether my character should one-box, two-box, or refuse to play.
