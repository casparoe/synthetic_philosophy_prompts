I've been trying to pin down what AI labs actually think they're doing when they supervise a model's reasoning, and the picture feels philosophically muddled. Take how reasoning models are trained for competition-level maths. The reward is almost always just whether the final answer matches the known answer, nobody scores the intermediate steps. A few teams have payed annotators to check each line of a proof and they report that this step-level feedback makes models much more reliable, but it's too expensive to use broadly. Fair enough. Yet the same labs, once the model is deployed, audit it almost entirely on outcomes: benchmark scores, user satisfaction, harm reports, whether the downstream task gets done. They dont systematically inspect the chain of thought when things are going well, and some of them deliberately hide the raw reasoning from human reviewers. At the same time they claim to care about process-style properties: the model shouldn't be sycophantic, shouldn't have hidden objectives, shouldn't get the right answer for the wrong reason. But the training signal I just described can't tell any of those apart. A lucky guess, a memorised template, and a genuinely good piece of reasoning get identical reward as long as the final answer is right. Then, when a model does fail, everyone suddenly reads the reasoning traces to work out what went wrong. So the process is consulted as evidence about future outcomes, but never seems to be valued on its own terms.

What view of evaluating an agent does this whole setup presuppose? Does outcome-based supervision commit them to a kind of black-box consequentialism about cognitive work, where intermediate steps are just causal machinery and only the final result has value? Or is the practice compatible with thinking process matters for its own sake but is simply too expensive or too private to score? Is the law's strict liability versus negligence distinction the right way to understand the difference? And what would actually have to change in the training and auditing setup if a lab came to think that evaluating a reasoner is more like appraising a colleague's intellectual character than marking an exam by the final answer? Reconstruct the implicit theory as far as it goes, but be precise about what the practice strictly presupposes, what it merely fits well with, and where it's underdetermined between several views.
