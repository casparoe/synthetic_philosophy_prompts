I overheard a long argument between two colleagues, Mira and Daniel, about whether states have a moral right to exclude would-be immigrants, and I could not referee it. Please go through their exchange move by move and say who is right about which specific points, what each overstates or fails to address, and who, if either, is more right overall. The argument started as a dispute about where the burden of proof should fall.

Mira opened with the claim that the burden must lie on states that close their borders. Freedom of movement, she said, is a basic liberty; a border policy is a standing threat of coercion against would-be entrants, and a state that coerces outsiders owes them a justification. Since a person born in one place rather than another has done nothing to deserve either exclusion or entry, treating birthright citizenship as a near-absolute entitlement looks like the morally arbitrary sorting of persons into better and worse life prospects.

Daniel disagreed about the default. He said there is no complete liberty to move unless some agent has a duty to admit, and in ordinary cases we do not think a person has any such claim against a household, a club, or a congregation. Those associations, he argued, have a right to choose their own members; that right is part of what it is for them to be associations at all. A political community, he said, is more like an association than like an unowned field, so the default runs the other way: exclusion is permissible unless a particular entrant has a claim that overrides the community's interest in choosing its own members.

Mira came back by denying the analogy. A state, she said, is not a club or a house, because it is a coercive territorial monopoly that people cannot realistically exit; being born under a jurisdiction is not like signing up for a congregation. And unlike a private club, a state's exclusion does not leave the excluded person with the rest of the world in which to associate freely, since every liveable region is under some jurisdiction. Daniel replied that these differences affect the weight of the associational interest, not its existence, and that a community that cannot control its own membership cannot exercise collective self-determination, because membership rules determine who will make the community's future decisions.

Mira then said she could grant that self-determination in the sense of running one's own institutions is valuable, but she distinguished that from the further claim to constitute the demos, to decide who belongs in the first place. That further claim, she argued, is circular when used to justify excluding outsiders: the existing members' right to decide who gets a say is exactly what is at issue, so it cannot settle the issue. Daniel answered that this circularity is a feature, not a bug: every association's identity depends on boundary rules, and a group that cannot decide whether to admit or reject someone is not a self at all but a mere collection of individuals.

Mira then shifted to history. Even if some right of self-determination exists, she said, the present distribution of territory and wealth is so deeply shaped by conquest and exploitation that states cannot treat their current jurisdiction as a legitimate baseline; communities built on those origins should be required to accept free movement by default. Daniel replied that this proves too much, because it would equally undermine obligations to one's own compatriots, and it would also authorize every historically disadvantaged group to repudiate its existing political arrangements, not just would-be migrants.

At the end, Mira said the disagreement came down to whether border coercion is more like arbitrary interference or like the constitution of a collective enterprise. Daniel said it came down to whether there is any non-circular way to justify a default liberty of movement across jurisdictions. They remained at an impasse.

Please adjudicate: for each of the main moves in the exchange, say who is right and why; identify each person's strongest and weakest move; evaluate the disagreement about the burden of proof; and say which side you find more persuasive overall, if either, while noting any important argument that neither of them made. Throughout, separate what is actually settled in philosophical discussion of migration from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as if it were the consensus.
