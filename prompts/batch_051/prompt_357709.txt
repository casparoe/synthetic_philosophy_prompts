I am a machine learning researcher with only an amateur's grasp of philosophy of science, and the vocabulary in which I naturally reason about evidence is that of models, training distributions, out-of-distribution generalization, distribution shift, latent variables, identifiability, calibration, risk, and reference classes. I keep encountering philosophers of science appealing to something they call the pessimistic meta-induction, and from the informal summaries I have read it sounds as though they are making an argument I would already recognize from my own field, just in older dress. I do not want an analogy; I want a systematic translation, and I want to know where the translation fails.

My rough understanding of the position is this. The historical record contains many theories that were impressively accurate by the empirical standards of their day, including Ptolemaic astronomy, phlogiston chemistry, caloric and ether theories, and Newtonian mechanics, yet each was later replaced by a successor theory that treated the earlier theory's central commitments as false or non-referring. The pessimistic induction takes that record as a sample of transitions among successful theories and projects the same fate onto present science: our current successful theories are likely to be overturned too, so predictive success is at best weak evidence that a theory's unobservable posits are true. Realists reply, roughly, that the induction is too coarse: the parts of past theories responsible for novel predictive success tended to survive into successors, while only idle or peripheral posits were abandoned, so the record actually shows the retention of whatever genuinely latched onto structure in the world.

Restate the pessimistic meta-induction and this selective realist reply as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I listed. Then give me the residue report: which elements map cleanly onto distribution shift between training and deployment, induction over a reference class of past theory changes, and extraction of invariant components; which elements fit only with distortion; and which elements have no counterpart at all in a framework built around prediction and risk. I especially want to know what happens to the notion of approximate truth and the claim that a theory refers to unobservable entities: do these become claims that latent variables are correctly identified, claims about generalization under future shifts, or something else entirely? My working hypothesis is that the pessimistic meta-induction is just a pessimistically tuned out-of-distribution generalization argument; tell me whether that hypothesis is right, or what the translation omits.
