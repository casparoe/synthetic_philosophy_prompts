I'm a PhD student in number theory, and a throwaway comment in our Galois theory reading group has turned into a suprisingly heated argument about whether the roots of a polynomial have an identity before you choose labels. Someone laid out the following case about the cube roots of 2, and no one can agree on where it goes wrong, if it goes wrong at all. I keep feeling like step 5 is doing the damage, but I can't make the objection precise without sounding like I'm just refusing the conclusion.

(1) Work inside C and name the three roots of x³ − 2 as r₁ = ∛2 (the real one), r₂ = ∛2·ω, r₃ = ∛2·ω², where ω = e^(2πi/3).
(2) K = Q(r₁, r₂, r₃) is the splitting field of x³ − 2 over Q, and its Galois group over Q acts transitively on {r₁, r₂, r₃}; in particular there's an automorphism σ of K fixing Q with σ(r₁) = r₂.
(3) A field automorphism fixing Q sends true statements in the language of fields with parameters in Q to true statements. So any first-order formula φ(x) with parameters in Q that holds of r₁ in K also holds of r₂ in K, and conversely.
(4) Thus r₁ and r₂ satisfy exactly the same Q-definable properties in K; the field structure treats them as fully interchangable.
(5) But r₁ and r₂ are both complex numbers, and no Q-definable property distinguishes them. So inside C there is no principled, non-arbitrary fact of the matter about which of the two is "the real cube root of 2" and which is the complex one.
(6) So the phrase "the real cube root of 2" only records a choice of embedding into C; it doesn't track a genuine mathematical fact about the roots themselves.

The conclusion seems deeply wrong to me, because r₁ literally lies on the real line and r₂ doesn't, and that sure feels like a fact. But maybe I'm smuggling in an ordering and calling it obvious? Could you go through the six steps and tell me whether the argument is valid, and if not, exactly which step fails and why?
