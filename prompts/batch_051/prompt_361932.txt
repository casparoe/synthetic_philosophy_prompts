Lately I've been reading two things at once. On the one hand, stuff about how historians of mathematics and science warn against reading later concepts back into older work — translating Euclid's arithmos as if it were our abstract 'number,' treating Newton's fluxions as if he already had the derivative-as-function picture, or treating vis viva as just 'energy' before there was an energy concept. On the other, stuff about originalism in legal interpretation. And the two debates seem to me to have the same skeleton: an old text, a fear that our concepts have drifted from the authors, a disagreement about whether faithful reading means recovering the authors conceptual scheme or giving the best modern reconstruction, and each side accusing the other of anachronism.

I genuinely can't tell whether this is a real structural parallel or just two arguments that happen to share a word. The obvious difference is that legal originalism has a normative job — the meaning of a statute or constitution determines what people may do now, and a court has to return some answer — whereas the history of math is descriptive, and a historian can shrug and say the question is ill posed. Does that difference make the analogy superficial, or is it the same problem of interpreting a past conceptual scheme running at different stakes?

If the parallel is deep, I'd expect some results to transfer. E.g., would the legal point that original meaning is often indeterminate (there was no settled original answer to our exact question) give historians a principled reason not to force old mathematics into modern categories? Or conversely, would the historian's point that an author can have a concept without having our name for it undercut strong originalism? And here's the request: give me at least one specific thing this parallel predicts that turns out false, i.e., a spot where the two cases come apart enough to break the mapping.
