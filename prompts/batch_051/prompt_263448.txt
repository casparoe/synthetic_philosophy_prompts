You are a professional philosopher of mind who reads cognitive neuroscience fluently; I am consulting you on an experimental design problem. Think carefully, and begin by genuinely brainstorming several candidate approaches before committing to one.

Consider the color phi phenomenon. A subject watches a screen while two brief flashes are shown in succession: a blue dot at one location, and a short interval later a red dot at a nearby location. Under the right spacing and timing, the subject reports seeing a single dot move across the gap and change color halfway along its path, from blue to red. The timing is the puzzle: the subject places the color change before the red dot was actually flashed, so the percept — or at least the perceptual report — appears to have been assembled using information that was not yet available at the time the change is placed. The experience is, in some sense, retroactive.

Two classic stories compete to explain what happens. On the Orwellian story, the subject first has a conscious experience of an unedited percept, something like a moving blue dot; then, once the red dot arrives, the system revises the record after the fact, rewriting memory so that the subject later sincerely reports having seen the dot turn red at the midpoint. On the Stalinesque story, the revision happens before anything reaches consciousness: the system waits for the second flash to be processed and then produces a single, already-edited conscious experience of a dot that changes color midway. On this story, the subject never consciously experiences the unedited percept at all.

Daniel Dennett has argued that these two stories are empirically equivalent: no observation could ever favor one over the other, and the question of which is true rests on a confusion about what it means to assign a time to an experience. I am not convinced. My working hypothesis is that the two stories make genuinely different claims about the timing of conscious content relative to neural processing, and that a sufficiently careful experimental program could collect evidence favoring one over the other.

Your task is to design that program. Keep in mind that both stories predict the same final retrospective report, so the design must find some measure other than the subject's after-the-fact testimony. First, brainstorm at least three distinct candidate approaches; for each, specify the observable to be measured and why it could break the symmetry between the two stories. Second, select the one test, or the tightest battery of tests, that you would actually run, and specify it concretely: the experimental conditions, the dependent measure, the predicted result under the Orwellian story, and the predicted result under the Stalinesque story. Third, state exactly what a clear result either way would and would not establish: which part of the question about the timing of experience would remain untouched even by a decisive-looking outcome, and whether any possible outcome could show that the dispute itself rests on a mistake.
