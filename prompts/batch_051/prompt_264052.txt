I'm on my university's campus life committee, and we've got a small one-time bequest that has to be spent by the end of the fiscal year. Two proposals are in front of us. One is to turn a mostly unused atrium into a social commons: cheap furniture, coffee, evening events, the sort of place where students linger and actually make friends. The other is a small student gallery and sculpture garden, mostly for showing student work and making the campus more beautiful. The money isn't enough to do both properly. The committee is split, and a few of us have been reading G. E. Moore's Principia Ethica because the philosophy department ran a reading group on it last term. As I understand Moore, he thought the most valuable states of mind we know involve either close personal relationships and affection, or the appreciation of beautiful things — so both proposals seem to touch his top goods. But he also had this doctrine about organic wholes, something like: the value of a whole isn't just the sum of the values of its parts. That seems to warn against just adding up benefits in a spreadsheet.

I've been asked to draft the short memo the committee reads before we vote, and I honestly keep going back and forth. I'd like it to recommend one concrete allocation (for example a percentage split between the two, including 100/0 if that's actually right), and to take seriously the strongest case for the proposal I end up not favoring, rather then just knocking it down. If the right move is to combine the two into a single redesigned space, the memo should say that and explain the trade-offs. Some members care about the philosophical side, so the memo should engage Moore's ideas where they help, without turning into a philosophy seminar.
