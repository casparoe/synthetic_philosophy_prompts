I spent four decades working on deterministic nonlinear systems, and since retiring I have begun reading the philosophical literature on determinism in earnest. A younger colleague has pressed on me the following argument, which he says shows that chaos and determinism cannot both be taken at face value: either determinism is false, or the unpredictability of chaotic systems is entirely a fact about our finite precision rather than about the world. I cannot accept either horn, and I cannot find the misstep myself, so I am asking for a diagnosis of the argument rather than a verdict.

(1) If the world is deterministic, then the exact state of the world at any time, together with the laws of nature, fixes the exact state at every later time.

(2) Many real systems — weather, turbulent flow, the long-term motion of the planets — are chaotic: a difference δ in the initial state grows, on average and over some range, as δe^{λt} for some λ > 0.

(3) It follows that, to keep a prediction within a fixed tolerance ε at time t, the initial state must be specified to within roughly εe^{−λt}.

(4) No finite observer can establish an exact real-valued state by measurement; every measurement has a finite resolution, and every numerically stored value is rounded at some decimal place.

(5) Therefore, for any chaotic system, every finite observer faces a horizon beyond which prediction is impossible in principle — not merely difficult in practice.

(6) But the state that determinism says fixes the future is an exact state, not a state-known-to-some-decimal-place.

(7) So either such an exact state can be supplied to a finite predictor, in which case the horizon in (5) is no real barrier; or it cannot, in which case determinism rests on a state that no finite scientific inquirer could ever specify, and its assurance that this state fixes the future does no scientific work.

(8) Therefore determinism is either false or idle, and the difference between a deterministic chaotic process and a genuinely stochastic process is a difference in our information, not a difference in the processes themselves.

I should most like to keep determinism, and I should also like to keep the conviction that the weather and a fair coin differ in a mind-independent way. Which step of the argument should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost?
