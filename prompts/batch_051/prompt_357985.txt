I have a set of verdicts about gratitude and I want to know whether any single principle can capture them, or which of them must be revised. Consider seven cases.

1. A stranger shoves me out of the path of a speeding car at serious risk to himself. I owe him deep gratitude, even though I never asked for his help.
2. A contractor mistakenly working on the wrong address replaces a rotted beam in my porch; it turns out the beam would have collapsed within days. He never intended to help me and does not know who I am. I find it bizarre to say I owe him gratitude, although I was massively benefited.
3. A friend with little money spends it on a birthday present for me that I never use. I think I owe her fuller gratitude than I would for a cheaper, useful present from a wealthy friend who could easily afford it.
4. A cousin helps me move apartments plainly in order to create a debt he intends to call in later. His help is real, but I think the instrumental motive undercuts most of the gratitude I would otherwise feel.
5. A rich donor funds a scholarship I receive solely to get his name on a building. I think I owe him something — acknowledgment, perhaps a written thanks — but not the same thing, or as much, as I would owe a donor who gave out of care for students.
6. A friend gives me sincere but incompetent advice that costs me money. I think there is something to acknowledge in her good will, but I do not owe her gratitude in the full sense I would if the help had actually gone well.
7. A person who died before I was born made decisions that made my career possible, without ever foreseeing me. I can feel something akin to gratitude, but I hesitate to call it full gratitude, since there was no relationship and no benevolence aimed at me.

I am unsure what single feature explains when gratitude is owed and how much is owed. Benefit actually received seems to matter: case 2 looks as if it should generate gratitude if benefit were enough, yet it does not; case 3 looks as if it should not track benefit, yet it does. Benevolent intention seems to matter: case 2 lacks it, but case 6 has it and yet full gratitude still seems absent. Cost or sacrifice to the benefactor seems to matter: case 3 fits, but cases 5 and 7 complicate it. Is there one principle — perhaps a composite, or a feature I have not named — that delivers all seven of my verdicts? Please state it precisely, show how it handles each case, say which of my verdicts it forces me to revise, and indicate what it predicts about a case I have not listed: someone who saves my life in the course of doing the paid job for which they were trained, with no personal good will toward me.
