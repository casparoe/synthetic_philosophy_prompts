A standard argument for veganism runs like this: consuming animal products is wrong because doing so helps sustain an industry that inflicts severe suffering on animals, and even a causally negligible purchase is still participation in the aggregate demand that keeps the industry in business. Now suppose the causal link is severed. One person eats meat, milk, or eggs only when they have been discarded and would otherwise be wasted, buying nothing. Another keeps rescued animals and uses what they produce — eggs from hens who would be fed and sheltered anyway, wool from a sheep who would be shorn anyway — while no breeding, slaughter, or sale gives anyone an incentive to produce more such animals. Grant, for the sake of argument, that these practices genuinely create no additional demand for animal products and leave the industry no better off than the person's complete abstention would.

Does the demand-based case for veganism survive this changed assumption intact, does it need repair, or does it turn out to have been an argument for not purchasing animal products rather than for never using them? Once demand is removed, what, if anything, could still ground a vegan's refusal of the waste-bound cheese or the rescued hen's eggs? Say which premise the original argument depended on, whether that premise can be restated to cover these cases, and whether a repaired version still reaches the conclusion that one ought not to consume them at all.
