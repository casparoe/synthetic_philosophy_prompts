I'm a PhD student in cognitive science, and I've been puzzling over a tension in how introspective reports are treated in clinical practice and in my own field, and I'd like help turning it into an explicit philosophical question. In pain medicine, a patient's verbal report is treated as effectively authoritative: the 0–10 numeric rating scale is the standard instrument in hospitals, clinical guidelines describe the patient's self-report as the single most reliable indicator of pain's presence and intensity, and there is no widely accepted physiological measure that overrides a sincere patient report. Yet in experimental psychology, introspection is treated, in a different context, as a source of confabulation. In the classic findings, subjects asked to explain why they judged, chose, or behaved as they did produce fluent, confident explanations, but experimental manipulations show those explanations track a plausible-sounding story rather than the actual cause. The same person's introspective authority thus seems to be treated as near-decisive when the object is a present internal state such as pain, and near-worthless when the object is the causal source of one of their own behaviors.

What I want to know is what theory of introspection, if any, this joint practice presupposes. Does treating pain self-report as authoritative commit the clinician to something like privileged access or first-person authority about one's own mental states, and is that commitment compatible with the confabulation findings? Or does the clinical practice only presuppose something much thinner—that pain is operationally defined by report, or that a sincere avowal is useful evidence without being authoritative testimony—while the psychology results concern only introspective access to causes rather than access to present states? Could a single coherent epistemology of introspection license both halves of the practice, and what distinction would it have to draw between the object of a pain report and the object of a "why did I choose that?" report? I'd also like to know where the practice is underdetermined rather than forced: if the confabulation findings really do generalize to all first-person reports, what would have to change—would pain self-report have to lose its gold-standard status, or could it be retained as evidence while abandoning any claim of authority? Finally, please say how confident you are in the specific reconstructions you offer, and single out the point in your answer you are least sure of.
