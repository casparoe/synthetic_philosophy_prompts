Here's a bind I'm in. I've been reading the cross-cultural moral psychology stuff — the WEIRD problem, the finding that people in some small-scale societies blame unintended harms much more like we blame intended ones, and that the 'side-effect' assymetry doesn't show up everywhere these things get tested. And I buy the broad picture: our WEIRD moral psychology, with its heavy weighting of intentions and its sharp act/omission line, is itself a cultural product shaped by particular kinds of social worlds. That picture has a lot going for it systematically — it's simple, it unifies a pile of otherwise bizzare findings, it gives a tidy story about where our distinctive moral judgments come from, and it doesn't multiply moral facts beyond need. The natural upshot is that the intention/outcome distinction isn't tracking some objective moral truth; it's a local custom running on cultural software.

But I can't shake the intuition on the other side. If my neighbor accidentally backs into my car versus deliberately does it to spite me, one is blameworthy in a way the other just isn't; the distinction feels as firm as anything in my moral life. Same with the failed murder versus the careless accident that causes the same harm: the person who tried and missed is more culpable than the person who didn't mean it, and I don't think that's just how I was trained to feel. When the theory tells me this is parochial cultural software, I can't tell whether I've discovered something real or just talked myself out of an intuition I have every reason to keep.

So what I want isn't a verdict where you pick a side. I want a negotiated settlement. Tell me what it would actually take for each move to be justified: (a) the intuition gets debunked and dropped, not by a cheap 'your culture made you feel that' inference but by something that would survive even if the WEIRD data had never been collected; (b) the theory gets amended, e.g., intent-weighting is a culturally-triggered acquisition of a real moral distinction, the way depth perception needs certain visual input to come online; or (c) the two are compatible, e.g., the cross-cultural variation is really about excusing practices or compensation norms rather than about blameworthiness itself. Say what evidence or argument would settle which of these it is, don't just gesture at it.

You're a professional philosopher who works on moral psychology and metaethics. Think carefully, start by steelmanning both the theory and the intuition for a few paragraphs, and then work through the three options on their merits.
