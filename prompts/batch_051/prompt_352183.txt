Next month I'm giving a talk at the rationality workshop, and I'm nervous about the Q&A. I'll be defending a version of Parfit's rational irrationality claim, focused on the hitchhiker case. The thesis, roughly: the fully rational self-interested hitchhiker is worse off than the hitchhiker who has made himself conditionally irrational — disposed to pay the driver once he's safely home, even though at that point paying no longer serves his interests — and this gap doesn't dissapear once you point out that in a repeated version reputation would make paying rational after all. My line is that paying isn't 'really rational all along'; rather, it's rational to be the sort of person who will pay, while each individual act of paying is irrational on its own terms.

The audience is a joint philosophy/economics rationality seminar: mainly game theorists and Bayesian epistemologists who think backward induction closes the case, plus a few moral philosophers who work on Parfit and know all the standard moves. I know the friendly version of this discussion; I need the hostile one. So draft my Q&A prep sheet: the eight objections I'll actually get from this crowd, ordered by how likely and damaging they are, phrased the way they would actually put them, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is softest. And include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
