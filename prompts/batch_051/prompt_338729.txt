Everyone I know says they agree with Orwell that political language is a moral problem, not just an aesthetic one: when an ugly act gets a clean name, the speaker isn't just tricking the audience — he's making it easier to avoid picturing what he's actually doing, and the clean name then does the thinking for later speakers, who inherit the vagueness and never have to look at the thing itself. What I can't figure out is why this agreement is so lopsided in practice. In a discussion group last week, the same person who called "enhanced interrogation" Orwellian doublespeak — and said the people who coined it should of been made to say "torture" in every press briefing — got genuinely annoyed when someone called "pro-choice" a euphemism, insisting it was just the accurate name for a real value. Across the room the reverse was true: the people who thought "pro-choice" was the euphemism had their own list of softer words they refused to part with, and their own story about why those were precise or kind rather than Orwellian. The asymmetry underneath all of it seems to be: when the other side softens an ugly or contested act, we treat the softening as thought-corrupting language; when our side does the same thing, we treat it as ordinary tact or legitimate framing that foregrounds what actually matters.

I want to know whether that asymmetry can be defended rather than just explained away as tribalism. Give me your best shot at a principle that could justify applying Orwell's standard to the other side's language while exempting our own, state the hardest nearby case against that principle (ideally one where the two descriptions are near-symmetric across sides), and then take a definite position: should the asymmetry be kept, reversed, or given up? Be honest about how much of it survives once you apply Orwell's point about language corrupting thought to the speaker themselves, not just to their opponents.
