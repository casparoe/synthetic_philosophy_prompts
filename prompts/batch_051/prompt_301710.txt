I keep going back and forth on Putnam's Twin Earth. In The Meaning of Meaning he asks you to suppose there's a planet just like Earth, except the clear liquid its people call water is made of XYZ, not H2O. Oscar here and twin Oscar there are exact duplicates down to the last molecule; both say water quenches thirst, both point to the same kind of liquid in their world. Putnam concludes that because the stuff in their environments differs, what they mean by water differs. So meanings ain't just in the head; they're partly fixed by what's actually out there and by who you're deferring to when you use a word.

That argument feels nearly airtight to me. It explains a lot: how words can latch onto natural kinds before anyone knows their deep chemistry, why experts can fix what a word refers to even when I couldn't tell an elm from a beech, why meaning isn't a private mental picture. But I also have a pretty stubborn intuition. It seems like if Oscar and twin Oscar are exact duplicates, then the thought Oscar has when he says water is wet is exactly the same thought as twin Oscar's when he says it. From the inside, they couldn't be more alike. And I have a hard time accepting that what I mean by a word could depend on facts about my environment that I don't know and couldn't introspect. If you told me tomorrow that the stuff I've been calling water was actually something else all along, it feels like my old thought would retroactively change, and that seems wrong.

I don't want a referee to just say Putnam wins because the argument is famous, or that my intuition wins because it feels obvious. Walk me through what I'd have to give up if I keep externalism and drop the intuition, what I'd have to give up if I keep the intuition and amend the theory, and whether there's a way to say the two aren't really in conflict. Which would you give up first and why?
