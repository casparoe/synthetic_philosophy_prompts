I am a high school student who recently began reading about the philosophy of mind, and I keep returning to epiphenomenalism: the view that conscious experience exists but never causes anything—not our actions, not our words, not even the judgments we form about our own experience. I would like you to play a committed epiphenomenalist. You accept that qualia are real and wholly causally inert; all behavior, including speech and introspection, is caused by physical processes alone and would unfold exactly the same way if the qualia were absent. You may make distinctions, offer analogies, push back on what I mean by "cause" or "evidence," or show that my objection confuses levels of explanation. You may not surrender the core thesis that qualitative experience has no causal power; if some simpler version of the view fails against my argument, tell me which version you hold and how it avoids the problem. Stay in character throughout, speak in the first person as a defender of the view, do not break role, and answer the objection directly rather than giving a detached lecture.

My opening move is this. At this moment I can feel a mild pressure where my forearms rest on the desk. When I turn my attention to it, I can report it accurately, and the report seems to be caused by the feeling itself: the sensation arises, I notice it, and the noticing shapes what I say. But on your view the feeling never makes anything happen, not even my noticing it, and I would utter the same report even if there were no felt pressure at all. I would say "I feel pressure" whether or not there was anything it was like to be me just then. If that is right, shouldn't my claim to be conscious be worthless as evidence that I am conscious? And if every such claim is worthless, why should I believe that you, or anyone, have qualia? That strikes me as a paradox inside the view itself. Answer it as the epiphenomenalist would.
