I'm putting together a seminar handout on the old theological problem of animal suffering before the Fall — the idea that predation, pain and death among animals either existed from the start or only came in after Adam sinned, and what either answer says about the goodness of creation. Someone pointed me to this passage from a letter Darwin wrote to Asa Gray on May 22, 1860, and I keep getting lost in the middle of it. Here's the part I mean:

"With respect to the theological view of the question; this is always painful to me.— I am bewildered.— I had no intention to write atheistically. But I own that I cannot see, as plainly as others do, & as I shd wish to do, evidence of design & beneficence on all sides of us. There seems to me too much misery in the world. I cannot persuade myself that a beneficent & omnipotent God would have designedly created the Ichneumonidæ with the express intention of their feeding within the living bodies of caterpillars, or that a cat should play with mice. Not believing this, I see no necessity in the belief that the eye was expressly designed. On the other hand I cannot anyhow be contented to view this wonderful universe & especially the nature of man, & to conclude that everything is the result of brute force. I am inclined to look at everything as resulting from designed laws, with the details, whether good or bad, left to the working out of what we may call chance. Not that this notion at all satisfies me. I feel most deeply that the whole subject is too profound for the human intellect. A dog might as well speculate on the mind of Newton.— Let each man hope & believe what he can.—"

I get the rough idea: Gray has been pushing back on the theological implications of the Origin, Darwin is unsettled, and the ichneumon wasp is meant to be a problem for reading nature as designed by a good God. But I cant quite follow the actual structure of the argument. When he says "Not believing this, I see no necessity in the belief that the eye was expressly designed," does the "this" refer narrowly to the claim that God specially designed each creature's nasty habits, or to the wider claim that nature shows evidence of beneficent design at all? And what is the position he lands on with "designed laws" on the one hand and the "details" left to chance on the other — does that rule out animal suffering before any human sin, or is it rather a way of saying suffering isnt informative either way because the details weren't individually intended? I'm also tripping over whether the pre-Fall framing even applies here, since in the old scheme the ichneumon's life-cycle and a cat playing with mice would presumably be post-Fall corruptions of what were originally harmless creatures, so I'm not sure Darwin is even aiming at that line of thought. Could you walk me through what each step is doing, and how you'd situate the passage in the pre-Darwinian debate about animal suffering and the goodness of creation?
