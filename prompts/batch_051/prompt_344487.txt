I've been kicking around a theory I call the leakage account of effort, and I want an honest assessment. The claim is that effort has no value in itself; what actually has value is caring about the thing, and effort matters only because it's the most legible sign of caring that isn't fully under voluntary control. I split effort into two kinds. Leakage effort is the kind you can't convincingly fake: losing sleep before a deadline, forgetting to eat, rereading the same paragraph ten times, staying after everyone has left. Presentation effort is the kind you can perform: the announced all-nighter, the "I worked on this for weeks" preamble, the visible grind. My view is that institutions say they reward hard work but can only reliably observe presentation effort, so as soon as they try to reward effort they select for people who are good at performing it, while leakage effort stays legible only in close-range relationships. Hustle culture, then, isn't a corruption of the idea that effort matters; it's what happens when the demand for effort scales.

So my answer to "is effort valuable in itself?" would be: no, but we keep rewarding it because we need a legible proxy for caring. I worry this is just revealed preference with a sad story attached, or costly signaling with extra steps. Is the leakage/presentation distinction real, and does it do any work those ideas don't? Where does it break—what genuine effort does it misclassify as fake, and what fake effort does it count as real? And if the distinction is basically right, does it actually support the conclusion that institutions should back off from rewarding effort, or does it just mean they should reward worse proxies?
