I'm doing a module on the philosophy of long-run prediction and I keep circling back to the way people defend Malthus. The line I keep hearing is that he wasn't wrong, just early: population really does tend to outrun the food supply unless some check bites, but he underestimated how far technology could expand the food supply, so the catastrophe got pushed back rather than cancelled. I want to stress-test that defence against a concrete episode rather than the usual hand-waving.

Take the Green Revolution in the 1960s and 70s. Borlaug's semi-dwarf wheat varieties, developed in Mexico, were introduced in India and Pakistan after the Bihar drought and near-famine in the mid-1960s, and yields then increased dramatically. Ehrlich's The Population Bomb (1968) is the obvious Malthusian statement of the period: mass starvation in the developing world by the 1970s and 80s, hundreds of millions of deaths. In the event it largely didn't happen, or at least not on that scale, and the yield shock usually gets the credit. But the Malthusian can always reply that the check was merely postponed: the Green Revolution bought time by drawing down groundwater, fossil fuels, soil fertility and the like, so the same arithemtic is still coming for us, just later.

Analyse the Green Revolution as a case study in whether a theory can be 'right but early.' I want a disciplined mapping, not a recap of the history. What exactly did a Malthusian model of the period predict, and what were the checks supposed to be by the time we get to India in the 1960s? Which parts of the episode does Malthusian logic explain, and which parts does it misdescribe or stay silent about? Then tackle the rescue move head on: is 'right but early' ever a legitimate reformulation, and what would have had to happen for the episode to count as a genuine falsification instead? Finally, say what this case does to your confidence in long-run Malthusian predictions generally.
