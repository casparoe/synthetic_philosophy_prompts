I have spent several years, on my own, trying to get clear about when a fact genuinely supports a belief and when it merely matches one. I have settled on a two-part test that a fact must pass before I count it as support.

First, the fact should be able to choose between the belief and its rivals. If the belief were wrong, the fact would be at least somewhat surprising; this is what keeps a fact from merely being consistent with the belief.

Second, the fact must not have been built into the belief at the start. If a person assembled the belief around a fact, the match between them does not show that the belief tracks anything beyond that fact.

Recently I came across a case that sits uneasily between those two tests. A neighbor has been trying to understand why certain gardens in our town flood badly each spring. She worked out her explanation from the town's drainage map and the gradients of the streets, not by starting from the flood record. Her explanation, worked through in detail, has a striking consequence: the gardens that flood should be precisely the four lowest-lying ones, and they should flood even in moderate rain while the others stay dry. That consequence is exactly what the town has observed for as long as anyone can remember. The pattern of flooding was common knowledge years before she formed the explanation, and she had heard it many times, though she insists it played no role in her reasoning.

Does the long-known flooding pattern count as support for her explanation under my two-part test? Go through the tests one by one and say candidly where each one points. Where a test gives no clear answer, say so and say why. Then tell me whether this case shows that one of the tests needs to be revised, discarded, or kept as it is.
