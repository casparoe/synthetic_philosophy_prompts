I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I help run a reading group that has been circling the concept of rights for months. Two members have settled into opposing camps. One holds what I understand as the side-constraints view: a right is not a goal to be promoted or a benefit to be weighed, but a constraint on what any agent may do, the way an access-control rule blocks a call regardless of how useful that call would be downstream. The other holds an instrumental view: rights are high-priority but defeasible protections for important interests, and their point is to secure things like welfare, autonomy, or equal standing, so that when enough comparable interests are on the other side a right can legitimately be traded off. A third member keeps pressing a deflationary worry, that we may not need a distinct concept of rights at all and could make do with duties, interests, and permissions. I also suspect the first camp is running together two separable ideas — that rights outrank aggregate goals, and that rights bind each agent independently of consequences — and I would like the document to keep them apart if they really are distinct.

Please draft the document this adversarial collaboration should have produced after a year of good-faith argument: the joint statement the first two camps could both sign. Lay out, first, the substantive claims both sides accept, stated precisely enough to be non-vacuous; then the exact residual disagreement, with each side's view formulated in wording its own camp would accept; then the cruxes — the sub-questions such that answering them would move at least one side — and, for each crux, the case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. End with a candid note on which parts of the dispute are, or might be, merely verbal. Use the third member's deflationary doubts as a stress test: if a claim comes out true only because the word right is silently doing all the work, it is not something the two camps should be allowed to hide behind.

Please answer as a professional philosopher working in normative ethics and the theory of rights, and take your time: it may help to brainstorm each camp's strongest formulation and the strongest objection they would have to the other's wording before you commit to the final document. Write the response in continuous prose, the way one would write a long email or letter, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables. Where the cruxes would naturally be laid out in a table, spell each one out in ordinary sentences instead.
