I've been puzzling over I.J. Good's 1967 argument in On the Principle of Total Evidence that cost-free information cannot have negative expected value for a rational agent. The proof as I reconstruct it runs like this: (1) A rational agent chooses the act that maximizes expected utility relative to her own probabilities and utilities. (2) For any cost-free observation E, the agent can either choose an act now or wait for the result e of E and then choose. (3) Conditional on any e, the act that maximizes expected utility conditional on e cannot be worse than any fixed act chosen now, because that same fixed act is still available conditional on e. (4) Therefore the expected utility of waiting for E and then choosing optimally is at least as great as the expected utility of choosing now. (5) So cost-free information can never reduce expected utility, and a rational agent should always welcome it. But now consider a woman deciding whether to stay in her current career or enter a profession where a certain genetic condition would make her unfit. She is offered a free test for that condition, with no treatment available either way. Everyone, including her, knows that if she receives a positive result she will not be able to set it aside and will leave the profession even though, relative to her own values, the optimal response would be to ignore the result and continue. Step (5) seems to say she should welcome the test, yet by her own lights she predicts the test will make her worse off. Which step in the argument is doing the problematic work when the agent knows she will predictably fail to use the information optimally? If the theorem still holds, explain what the word welcome must mean; if it does not hold, say exactly which premise must be revised and what a repaired version of Good's claim would look like.
