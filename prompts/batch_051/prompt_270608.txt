I have been reading Émilie du Châtelet’s *Discourse on Happiness*, and a specific passage has unsettled me in a way I would like to work through carefully. I cannot quote it in a translation I trust, so I will paraphrase: in her discussion of the passions, she claims that illusions are necessary to happiness, and that the belief that one can be happy without illusions is itself among the greatest of illusions. Her point, as I understand it, is that our projects, attachments, ambitions, and even the love of study depend on our seeing their objects as more durable, more important, or more attainable than a fully sober estimate would allow. Remove every such illusion, and what remains is not a clear-sighted life but an affectively flat one, emptied of the very desires that make happiness possible.

The surrounding theory is attractive to me for its systematic virtues. She begins from the principle that happiness consists in living in a way that conforms to our organization, treats the passions as unavoidable sources of human energy, and then distinguishes the passions that can be made to serve our happiness from those that destroy us. Her practical rule is tidy: do not try to extirpate the passions, but direct them; cultivate the passions that renew themselves rather than exhausting us, above all the love of study; and accept the illusions that sustain those passions. This gives her an elegant, non-moralistic account of why a purely rationalist program of self-purification would fail, and why certain useful false beliefs might be rationally retained.

But I have a firm intuition from my scientific life that I cannot shake. I was trained to seek truth and to regard a false belief as something to be removed, even when it is comfortable. The idea that I should deliberately retain, or even cultivate, an illusion because it makes happiness possible strikes me as standing in tension with the epistemic integrity that I value. My question is not whether du Châtelet was right or wrong, but which of these two things should yield, and on what grounds. Should I treat my intuition as a residue of a narrow Enlightenment ideal that her theory correctly diagnoses and drops? Should I amend her theory so that it licenses only self-knowing illusions, or only illusions that would survive reflection in some second-order sense? Or is there a reading of “illusion” on which the scientist’s devotion to truth is itself one of the self-renewing passions she commends, so that the apparent conflict dissolves? I want the strongest case for each move, and a verdict about what would justify giving up the intuition, amending the theory, or declaring the conflict an artifact of an ambiguous term.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
