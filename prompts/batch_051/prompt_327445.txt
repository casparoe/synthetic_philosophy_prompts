I'm a journalist working on an essay about Galileo's *Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina*, specifically about his attempt to stop the Church from using Scripture to settle astronomical disputes. I've been trying to reconstruct the distinct moves he makes in the letter, and I have a list of four so far. First, nature and Scripture cannot genuinely conflict because both proceed from God, so an apparent conflict is always a sign that the scriptural passage has been misread. Second, biblical language is accommodated to the capacities and ordinary beliefs of common people, so it often describes the world as it appears rather than as it is. Third, the purpose of Scripture is to teach salvation and morals, not to give instruction in astronomy, so its words about natural facts need not be read literally. Fourth, the caution he borrows from St. Augustine that on obscure physical questions not yet demonstrated we should avoid premature pronouncements that would make Scripture seem wrong when the truth is later found. Here is the passage from Augustine as Galileo quotes it:

"Now keeping always our respect for moderation in grave piety, we ought not to believe anything inadvisedly on a dubious point, lest in favor to our error we conceive a prejudice against something that truth hereafter may reveal to be not contrary in any way to the sacred books of either the Old or the New Testament."

Act as an expert historian of early modern science with philosophical training. I'd like you to do three things. First, explain exactly how this Augustine passage functions in Galileo's overall argument: does it do any work that the first or third move does not, or is it largely rhetorical? Second, give me one more distinct argument that Galileo actually deploys in the *Letter* and that is not on my list and not a restatement of any of the four. I suspect he also argues from the way theologians already treat many other biblical passages non-literally, but if you think there is a stronger candidate, give that one and say why. Third, assess whether the new argument genuinely strengthens the case or merely rephrases the same underlying principle.
