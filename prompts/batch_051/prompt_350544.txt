I am a student helping to run my school's Philosophy Club, and I have been asked to write this term's revision questions for our entry in a regional high-school philosophy competition. My assigned topic is Dutch book arguments. The club members have all read the same two-page handout explaining how a Dutch book can be made against someone whose degrees of belief violate the axioms of probability, and why that is supposed to support probabilism, but in our practice sessions I keep noticing that people who can define the terms still confuse the argument's actual claim with a cluster of nearby ideas. I would like four multiple-choice questions, each with four options, worth five marks each (twenty marks in total) and intended to take about twenty minutes, aimed at students who have read the handout but have had no formal probability theory. Every wrong option should encode a specific, identifiable misunderstanding someone at that level might genuinely have — for example (you need not use these): conflating the claim that coherent credences obey the probability calculus with the claim that credences should match known objective frequencies or chances; thinking the argument is about bets one would regret after the fact rather than about a set of bets that jointly guarantees a sure loss no matter what happens; thinking the bookie must know more than the gambler for the argument to work; or treating it as a practical warning against gambling rather than as a diagnosis of a structural inconsistency in a person's fair betting odds.

The questions should be answerable only by working through why an incoherent set of fair betting prices can be exploited, what the probability axioms rule out and why violating them produces the sure loss, and what the argument does and does not establish about rational belief. Please make three of the questions test different parts of the argument — for instance, what accepting 'fair odds' commits you to, how the axioms are jointly violated in a simple betting example, and what conclusion the argument can actually support — rather than all probing the same point. Then provide a model answer key that gives the correct answer for each question and, for every distractor, states the precise misunderstanding it is designed to catch. The questions should be self-contained, so that no one needs to look anything up in order to answer them.
