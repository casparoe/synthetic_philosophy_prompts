I run a specialty insurance underwriting team. We get two or three pricing calls a week for risks whose claims history is thin, absent, or drawn from reference classes we have reason to distrust: novel industrial processes, flood exposure after upstream land-use change, first-of-a-kind technology liabilities. Mispricing in either direction is costly — a single underpriced policy can erase a year's profit, and systematically overpricing drives away the only business this firm was founded to write. The pricing committee will not let an underwriter depart from standard actuarial pricing unless the departure is justified through a written, repeatable procedure, and junior underwriters have no formal training in probability theory, so the procedure must be runnable in an afternoon on two pages.

I want a procedure for deciding when a case may be priced from estimated probabilities and when it should be treated as a case of genuine uncertainty and escalated to judgment. Build it from Keynes's distinction between risk and uncertainty — roughly, cases where the evidence supports a stable, measurable probability versus cases where it does not — and from his further point that the weight of evidence behind a probability can vary independently of the probability itself: two risks can both be priced at 20 percent, one on a century of claims and the other on a single loose analogy, and those are not in the same epistemic situation. The procedure should also flag when we are falling back on market convention as a substitute for evidence, since that is itself a symptom of uncertainty rather than a solution to it. It should say which questions to ask and in what order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where exactly it returns a verdict of price-from-data, adjust-the-probability, or escalate-to-committee, at which step a matter of fact turns into a matter of underwriting values, and what the procedure cannot decide.

Then apply the procedure to two cases and make the practical consequences explicit: (1) flood coverage for a plant on a river with an 80-year flood model, but where the upstream watershed has been heavily paved and built over in the past decade; (2) liability coverage for a business whose product uses a novel AI deployment mode with no comparable claims history and only speculative failure scenarios. I expect that at least one of these will produce a recommendation the actuarial team would resist; say which one, why it would meet resistance, and whether in that case the procedure or the institution should give way.
