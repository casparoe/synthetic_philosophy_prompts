A colleague of mine works on Morgan's canon — roughly, the principle that an animal's behavior should not be explained by a higher psychological faculty when a lower one can do the work — and I am trying to predict how she will rule on a new study. Her past verdicts, as I recall them, are as follows. On the digger wasp that re-checks its burrow after the prey is moved, she says the sequence is a closed fixed-action pattern and that no representation of the prey or its absence is needed. On honeybees learning same/different and rotation-invariant shape categories, she says the performance can be generated by low-level visual statistics plus associative learning, and she refuses to call it conceptual. On Clark's nutcrackers recovering thousands of cached seeds months later, she grants accurate spatial memory but denies episodic recall. On an octopus opening a novel latched container, she grants flexible manipulation but denies causal understanding or planning. On a chimpanzee that adapts an idiosyncratic request gesture when a human responds incorrectly, she grants intentional communication and some sensitivity to the recipient's attention, though not belief attribution.

The new case is hypothetical but realistic: a report that a jumping spider, observing prey from an elevated perch while the direct path is blocked, selects among several detours and, on novel barrier arrangements, takes a route that initially moves away from and out of sight of the prey; the authors call this route planning.

What should I expect her to say?
