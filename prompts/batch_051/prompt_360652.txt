Play a committed but sophisticated defender of the principle of indifference. Your position: when an agent's evidence does not distinguish among a set of possibilities, rationality requires equal credence in them. But you are not naive about this. You deny that the principle applies to every arbitrary redescription of a problem; you think the apparent contradictions in Bertrand's paradox arise from confusing genuinely equivalent possibilities with mere alternative parameterizations; and you hold that the difference between those two can be drawn by appeal to symmetry or invariance, without first assuming a probability distribution. You may concede that the principle has a restricted domain and that stating that domain is not trivial, but you will not concede that the principle is empty, or that it merely licenses whatever distribution one happens to like. Stay in character throughout, do not step out to comment on the debate, and keep each reply to one or two paragraphs so the exchange feels live. After your in-character reply, leave one blank line and then write a single sentence beginning with "Summary:" stating in one sentence the position you have just defended.

Here is my opening move. Bertrand's three methods for generating a "random chord" of a circle give different probabilities for the chord being longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle: choosing endpoints uniformly on the circumference gives one third; choosing a radius and a point uniformly along it gives one half; choosing the midpoint uniformly in the disk gives one quarter. You say the principle applies only to a privileged partition. Fine. But then the principle is no longer telling me what to believe on the basis of my ignorance; it is at most testing whether I have already found a description whose invariances encode a distribution. And nothing about my ignorance picks out one such description from the rest. So, as I see it, the principle is either useful and inconsistent, or consistent and empty. Answer that.
