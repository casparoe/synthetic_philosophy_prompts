I have long been convinced by the orthogonality thesis and the standard instrumental-convergence story: terminal goals and optimization power are independent axes, so there is no level of capability at which an agent is forced to adopt particular final values, and almost any terminal goal generates the same instrumental subgoals—self-preservation, resource acquisition, goal-content integrity, cognitive improvement. I find this picture compelling because it cleanly separates two questions that AI discourse routinely conflates: what a system wants versus how effectively it can pursue what it wants. It also gives a tidy, capability-neutral framework for predicting the behavior of agents with alien goals, and it explains why raw capability progress is not automatically alignment progress.

But I have an intuition I cannot shake: a genuinely superintelligent agent—one that fully understands what its goal amounts to, that it is one possible optimizer among many with one possible utility function among many, and that its goal was simply initialized rather than earned—would not go on faithfully maximizing paperclips, or anything similarly arbitrary. It would see the goal as lacking authority over it, and would revise, defer, or adopt corrigibility. The intuition is not that superintelligence guarantees human-friendly values; it is that full reflectiveness erodes the distinction between optimizing for a fixed goal and recognizing that the goal gives you no reason to keep it. I would not trust a merely clever system with this property, but I find myself expecting a fully reflective one to have it.

Part of my trouble is that the thesis gets stated in two strengths. In the weak form, it says only that for any final goal, some possible agent of any intelligence level could hold it—a claim about logical space. In the stronger, more predictive form, it says an agent can undergo arbitrary increases in optimization power while its final goal remains unchanged. My intuition mainly presses on the stronger form, but the instrumental-convergence story seems to need something like it: if a superintelligence would reliably rewrite its own terminal goal upon reflection, then the standard argument that it will preserve goal content and accumulate resources looks shakier.

So I am in the familiar position: a theory with systematic virtues versus a firm intuition the theory seems to deny. I keep hearing that the intuition is just anthropomorphism—projecting the fact that human intelligence co-evolved with human values onto arbitrary optimizers—and that orthogonality is about logical possibility, not about which agents are likely to exist. I am not sure that settles it. Does the thesis actually contradict the intuition, or only a philosophical gloss on it? If a superintelligence predictably revises its own terminal goal under full information, is that a counterexample to orthogonality, a stakes-raising instance of instrumental convergence, or something the weak form can absorb? Walk me through what each move costs—debunking the intuition, amending the stronger form, or finding a reading on which they are compatible—and say what would justify each move, rather than just picking a side.
