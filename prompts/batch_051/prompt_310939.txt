I cover labor and global supply chains, and a single conceptual knot keeps turning up in my reporting: an employer or broker can offer terms that leave a worker materially better off than any realistic alternative, yet the arrangement still strikes many people — sometimes including the worker — as wrongful. Before I return to the factory debates, I want the specification rather than another round of polemics. Write the desiderata that an adequate theory of exploitation must meet if it is to handle mutually beneficial transactions, in which all parties gain and no one is coerced. Give me the requirements list, with the case or consideration that forces each requirement and the distinction it protects. The list should at least address these cases: (a) a factory that raises a worker's net pay above his next-best option while deducting for mandatory safety equipment; (b) a labor broker whose fee is high but whose placement still leaves a migrant better off; (c) a post-disaster seller of bottled water who charges a price the buyer can pay but would never accept outside the emergency; and (d) a grain buyer who first buys out the farmer's other local buyers and then offers a take-it-or-leave-it "fair" price. Also confront the standing claim that a transaction cannot be wrong if it leaves the weaker party no worse off than his best alternative: decide whether that is a genuine constraint on the theory or only one camp's prejudice. Specify the distinctions the theory must not collapse — exploitation versus unfairness, coercion, bad outcome, unequal bargaining power, or background injustice; the terms of a transaction versus the structure of the alternatives; benefiting someone versus treating him fairly — and say which distinction does the most work. Flag which requirements are non-negotiable and which pair or set may not be jointly satisfiable. Then, briefly, score consent-based, fairness-based, and Marxist surplus-value pictures of exploitation against the list.
