I just came from a long argument with a friend about whether the Wilt Chamberlain argument is anywhere near decisive. My friend's line was that it shows only that liberty can upset a preferred pattern, not that liberty should win; mine was that any theory with nothing to say about the moment a million people willingly hand over their quarters has not yet stated a theory of holdings at all. We went in circles because neither of us had written down what such a theory is actually for. Before I read any more distributive justice, I want that specification.

Write the desiderata: the full list of requirements an adequate theory of justice in holdings must meet, with the case or consideration behind each, and mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are preferences of a particular camp. Adequacy here means fit to arbitrate, in principle, who may rightfully have what and why across original acquisition, voluntary transfer, and the correction of wrongful past holdings — not merely fit to grade final distributions by a snapshot.

Make sure the list grapples with at least these:

- The voluntary-transfer case: the theory must say explicitly whether transfers freely made from just starting positions can produce distributions that violate a favored pattern while remaining just, and, if not, which defeaters — imperfect starting positions, monopoly power, desperation on one side, effects on third parties — are allowed to block or re-tax the transfer, with the case stated for each.

- The historical versus end-state distinction: the theory must classify itself, and justify whether how a distribution came about matters independently of how it looks now. Two identical snapshots reached by different histories must not be forced to the same verdict unless the theory explains why history is irrelevant.

- Original acquisition: it must give an account of how unowned things are rightfully first acquired, and it must not dodge the Lockean proviso — stated in your own words, roughly that first appropriation must not leave others worse off in some specified sense — or else defend abandoning it.

- Transfer and its voiding conditions: it must say what features of a transfer preserve justice (voluntariness, informed consent, prior title) and what voids it (force, fraud, deception, selling what one does not own), and how far a defect in title propagates through later innocent transactions.

- Rectification: it must say what is owed, and by whom, when present holdings descend from theft, expropriation, broken treaties, or other past injustice, and it must remain usable when the historical record is incomplete, disputed, or lost. I especially want to hear whether the theory licenses a clean-slate convention or a statute of limitations, and whether doing so is a concession rather than something the theory's own principles generate.

- Two distinctions it cannot collapse: justice in holdings versus duties of charity or rescue; and a criterion of rightness (what an ideal title history would look like) versus a decision procedure (what an actual court or agent should do under uncertainty about titles).

For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that forces it, and say which camp, if any, would treat it as optional. Then flag any subsets of the requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — I care more about those pressure points than about completeness. In particular, consider whether unrestricted freedom of voluntary transfer and the continued maintenance of any favored pattern can be had together, and whether full-bore rectification and determinate verdicts for actual holdings can.

After the list, briefly state how three contenders fare against the desiderata: Nozick's entitlement approach, a Rawls-style view that gives priority to a patterned distribution, and one further contender of your choice. Keep the scorecard short; the criteria are the deliverable.

Format it as follows. First, the desiderata as a table with columns: ID; Requirement (one sentence); Status (non-negotiable or camp-specific); Motivating case or consideration; Hardest for (which candidate or family of views it threatens). Second, a short prose section on the jointly unsatisfiable subsets and the single requirement that does the most to eliminate popular candidates, with a sentence of justification each. Third, a compact JSON summary, not repeating the table, with fields:

- jointly_unsatisfiable_subsets: array of objects with fields requirements (array of IDs) and why (one sentence).

- most_discriminating_requirement: object with fields id and why (one sentence).

- scorecard: object whose keys are the three contenders named above and whose values are one-sentence verdicts.
