Construct a thought experiment in which two AI labs, each fully rational and fully informed about the other's position, nonetheless end up in a costly race that leaves both worse off than they would have been under a negotiated slowdown. Build the case so that, among the three mechanisms in James Fearon's rationalist account of war, the commitment-problem mechanism is the only one that explains the breakdown; the other two mechanisms must each deliver a clear negative verdict.

The scenario must satisfy these specifications:

1. The private-information mechanism, with its incentives to misrepresent capabilities or resolve, must be inoperative. Make it plausible within the fiction — for instance, through a prior joint venture or a shared supply chain — that each lab knows the other's compute reserves, timeline, risk tolerance, and fallback options with unusual precision, and be explicit that no uncertainty bearing on the bargain remains to do the work.
2. The issue-indivisibility mechanism must be inoperative. The prize at stake must be genuinely divisible — for example, a revenue stream or an allotment of compute that could in principle be split in any proportion — so the failure cannot be that there is no package to bargain over.
3. The commitment-problem mechanism must be what does the work. State the specific slowdown-and-share bargain both sides would prefer to the race, the promise that would have to be credible for that bargain to hold, and why neither lab can make that promise credible in the stipulated institutional environment. Respect these realism constraints: no world government and no perfect external enforcer, but the labs' leaders must be prudent, sober, and not backing into conflict out of hostility or recklessness.

After presenting the scenario, walk through the three mechanisms one by one and give each its verdict: why the private-information mechanism says no race should occur here, why the issue-indivisibility mechanism says no race should occur here, and why the commitment-problem mechanism says the race is rationally explicable after all.

Then answer three meta-questions. First, which structural feature of the case is doing the genuine work — the non-enforceability of the bargain, the durability of the first-mover advantage, or their interaction — and what happens if that feature is removed, for example by introducing a credible inspection and enforcement regime? Does the case then collapse back into a standard full-information bargaining problem that gets settled peacefully? Second, how would a defender of the private-information account attack the case, and how much damage does the attack do? Third, how would a defender of the view that AI races are mostly driven by status, bias, and organizational irrationality attack the case, and how much damage does that attack do?

Do not include any citations or bibliography; the task is to engineer the case and reason through it, not to survey scholarship. Write the answer in American English.
