You are a cognitive scientist who has been asked to referee a long-running dispute among philosophers and psychologists about the nature of concepts. Before you look at the contestants again, I want the standard they will be measured against.

Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of concepts must meet, where adequacy is fixed by the following job: the theory must explain how adult humans categorize the world, how we combine a finite stock of concepts into indefinitely many new thoughts, and how concepts are acquired and revised across development and expertise. For each desideratum: state it precisely; give the specific case, experiment, or result that forces it; say whether it is non-negotiable or whether it is a preference of a particular camp (and, if so, which camp and why they insist on it); and say whether it is a demand on concepts themselves rather than on word meanings, task performance, or something with which concepts are easily conflated.

Include the difficult cases that the three main theories were not originally built to handle: abstract concepts (justice, odd number), logical concepts (negation, all, if), concept combination and compositionality (the pet fish problem), and concepts whose content is acquired through testimony. Flag any pairs of desiderata that you doubt can be jointly satisfied by a single representational format — for example, graded typicality and strict compositionality — and explain what has to be sacrificed to keep both.

Then, in a compact table, score prototype theory, exemplar theory, and the theory theory against your list. Identify the single requirement that does the most to separate adequate from inadequate accounts, and explain why.

Think through candidate requirements and their motivations first, and discard the ones that are only artifacts of a particular experimental task or a parochial example. The deliverable is the standard, so spend your effort on the criteria and their rationale rather than on a long survey of the theories.
