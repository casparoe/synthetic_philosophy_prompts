I'm in a reading group on philosophy of math and one member keeps dissolving every 'what are numbers?' puzzle with the same analogy: numbers are like offices in a company, not the people who happen to hold them. The natural-number structure gives you a system of offices — 0, 1, 2, ... — and everything there is to say about 2 is fixed by its relations to the other offices; the von Neumann and Zermelo set-theoretic reductions are just two different ways of filling the offices with particular objects. So when someone points out that 2 is an element of 4 on the von Neumann story (4 = {0,1,2,3}) but not on the Zermelo story, he waves it off: that's like asking whether the person in the treasurer's office is a member of the person in the president's office — it depends which filling you picked, so it isn't a question about the offices themselves. Audit this analogy for me. Give the full correspondence table, separate the disanalogies that actually matter for the argument from the harmless ones, and then say whether the analogy really licenses the structuralist conclusion that 'is 2 an element of 4?' has no encoding-independent answer, or whether the analogy was doing all the work. Also tell me how confident you are in that verdict and which bit of the mapping you're least sure about.
