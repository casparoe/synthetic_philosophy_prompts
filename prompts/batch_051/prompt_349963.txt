I've been trying to settle whether the Lucas–Penrose anti-mechanist argument still has force now that large language models exist, and I'd like you to work through one specific tension rather than survey the debate.

The argument, in the form I have in mind, runs like this. Take any machine whose mathematical competence is captured by a consistent formal system strong enough to encode elementary arithmetic. Gödel's first incompleteness theorem gives us a sentence—call it G—that the system cannot prove, but that we can see to be true on the assumption that the system is consistent. The argument then concludes that whatever we are doing when we recognize G as true is not something this formal system could do, and since the machine was arbitrary, no such formal system can capture human mathematical insight. So the mind is not a mechanism of that kind.

Now consider a transformer-based language model trained on a large portion of the human textual record and fine-tuned for mathematical reasoning. It is not obviously a consistent formal system: it assigns probabilities to continuations rather than deriving theorems from fixed axioms; it can affirm contradictions when prompted differently; its outputs change with fine-tuning; and its training corpus includes Gödel's theorem, standard discussions of the Lucas–Penrose argument, and many worked examples of the self-referential, diagonal reasoning that produces Gödel sentences. Asked to do so, it can often state a Gödel sentence for a given formal system and explain why that system cannot prove it.

Does the Lucas–Penrose argument survive this development, or does the development show that the argument was aimed at a caricature of a machine? I want you to do three things: identify the specific premise or presupposition of the classic argument that large language models put pressure on; say whether the argument as a challenge to mechanism is dead, needs repair, or goes through unchanged; and if you think repair is possible, state the repaired argument carefully enough that it still says something nontrivial about systems like transformers—or, if you think it is not possible, say exactly what remains of the original conclusion.
