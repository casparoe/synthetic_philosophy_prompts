Write a satirical policy document of about 700 words, in the deadpan style of a national archive’s official “Policy for the Remediation of Documentary Silences.” Format it as a preamble plus numbered clauses. The voice should be earnest, bureaucratic, and mildly self-congratulatory, as if written by a heritage agency that has discovered the language of archival power but intends to manage it through procedure rather than confront it. The target is the technocratic fantasy that archival silences can be repaired by better intake forms, appraisal quotas, controlled vocabularies, and risk matrices without redistributing the actual authority over what is acquired, described, preserved, and made accessible. Use real archival ideas in exaggerated form—appraisal, provenance, original order, retention and disposal schedules, deaccessioning, access restrictions, the myth of the neutral custodian—so that a specialist recognizes a fair parody of the practice and a lay reader still senses the underlying problem. Invent one central bureaucratic instrument, such as a “Silence Remediation Index” or a “Representationally Weighted Accession Rubric,” and run it through the clauses consistently. The satire should stay deadpan and should never state the objection outright: the document itself should show that converting exclusion into a metric extends the logic of archival power rather than dissolving it. Include at least one clause whose quiet implication is monstrous—for example, that communities unwilling to be externally documented are deemed “self-selecting out of memory” and made ineligible for remediation. End with a brief author’s note of 100–150 words identifying which features of archival thought you exaggerated and where the satire leans on a contested or unfair reading. Commit to one definite satirical diagnosis throughout; do not hedge.
