I work in a civil records office and have recently been assigned to review applications from people seeking to change the sex recorded on their birth certificates. In our system, as in many others, a newborn is registered as male or female within a few days of birth on the basis of a doctor’s observation of the body, and an adult who wishes to change that entry must supply medical reports documenting surgical or hormonal treatment; the original entry is never removed, merely annotated. I have been reading Simone de Beauvoir’s The Second Sex at night school, and her argument that being a woman is not something one begins with but something one is made into through social and historical processes has made me see this everyday administrative practice in a new light. I am not sure whether the practice merely sits alongside that idea or quietly contradicts it.

Could you spell out what philosophical commitments about sex and gender this kind of registration practice presupposes? I would like you to distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with: does it presuppose that sex is a fixed, binary biological fact that legal identity should track, or only that such recording is administratively convenient? Then tell me whether the parts of the practice are consistent with one another, and what would have to change in a civil registration system if it were rebuilt on the Beauvoirian idea of becoming rather than on the assumption that a person’s sex is settled at birth. Please write in British English and use continuous prose only, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
