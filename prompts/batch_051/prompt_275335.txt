In A Letter Concerning Toleration, John Locke writes:

The business of true religion is quite another thing. It is not instituted in order to the erecting of an external pomp, nor to the obtaining of ecclesiastical dominion, nor to the exercising of compulsive force, but to the regulating of men's lives, according to the rules of virtue and piety.

Locke uses this contrast between the state’s business and religion’s business to argue that civil force—fines, imprisonment, confiscation, and the like—is an unfit instrument for bringing people to true religion. The argument seems to depend on a picture of churches as voluntary societies whose members enter by choice and whose penalties, such as admonition or excommunication, touch only spiritual standing, not civil goods.

Now strengthen the contrary case. Consider a contemporary system in which the state collects a church tax through the payroll system, church membership is normally acquired by baptism in infancy rather than by an independent adult decision, and leaving the church requires a formal declaration with civil implications. In such a system, the state is directly implicated in financing the church, and membership is partly inherited and administratively costly to exit.

Evaluate whether Locke’s argument in the quoted passage survives this arrangement. Specifically: which premise in the argument does a church tax system put pressure on? Does Locke’s refusal to countenance compulsive force still rule out state collection of a church tax, or does the church tax exploit a distinction Locke himself could accept? If the argument survives, say how someone accepting it should classify the state’s role in collecting the tax. If it does not survive, identify the exact step that fails and state what residual principle about church and state remains defensible.
