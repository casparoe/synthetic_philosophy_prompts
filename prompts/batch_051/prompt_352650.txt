Okay so I run a discussion group for grad students and postdocs at my university—philosophy-adjacent but not all philosophy people—and we meet for 90 minutes, usually about a dozen of us. I've become a little obsessed with something since I spent two weeks deciding whether to take a job offer. I kept asking myself "what are the odds I actually take it?", and every time I tried to put a number on it, the number either just tracked whichever way I was leaning that hour, or it started acting like a commitment and froze the deliberation in place. I want to run a session that makes other people feel that specific weirdness: from the inside of a still-open decision, you can't cleanly assign probabilities to your own choices the way an outside observer can, and trying to do so crowds out or contorts the deliberating itself.

So: design a 90-minute game or structured exercise for about 12 people that gets them to run into this on their own before anyone names it. It should involve real (small) stakes, payoffs, and scoring so people have an incentive to take their own probability reports seriously. No upfront lecture. I want them to come away seeing both (a) how self-prediction and deliberation interfere, and (b) the contrast with predicting someone else or predicting an already-settled habit, where the problem doesn't arise. Include the rules, roles, a timeline, and a debrief that maps what happened in the game onto the idea. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong when it actually runs—people treating probabilities as vague confidence, people gaming the scoring by deliberately choosing whatever they predicted, people deciding there's no coherent way to score—and how you'd handle each. And be honest about which part of the first-person experience the game can't reproduce even at it's best, where you'd just have to ask people to introspect. I'd rather you commit to one specific design and defend its choices than give me several options.
