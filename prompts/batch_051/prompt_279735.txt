I keep seeing illusionism about consciousness get dismissed as the obviously false claim that there's nothing it's like to be anything, and the illusionists I've read mostly seem to talk past that objection instead of meeting it. Before I read any more of them I want the actual grading sheet: the full list of requirements a credible illusionist theory of phenomenal consciousness has to satisfy if it's going to count as explaining experience rather than just denying it. For each requirement give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, say whether it's a genuine constraint or just a realist's demand smuggled into the brief, and flag any two that are in tension or jointly unsatisfiable. Cover at least: explaining why introspection seems to reveal a private, ineffable what-it's-likeness (the 'yes, but it's just obvious!' objection); explaining why a scientist who knew every physical fact about color vision still seems to learn something on first seeing red, and why zombies at least seem conceivable; keeping a real functional difference between conscious and unconscious access without letting qualia leak back in; not collapsing into ordinary eliminativism about experience; saying why pain still matters if there are no phenomenal properties; saying whether 'experience' talk should be treated as strictly false and to be eliminated or as a useful fiction we keep; and not explaining the illusion by positing an inner little person who is fooled, since the the illusion has to bottom out somewhere. Then tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude the illusionist proposals you know, and briefly why. I care far more about the list and its motivation than about scoring individual authors.
