I am a postdoc in political theory preparing to present a paper next month at a two-day workshop jointly run by a philosophy department and a school of social policy. The paper's thesis is that welfare conditionality — work requirements, sanctions for noncompliance, and the investigative and prosecutorial apparatus aimed at benefit fraud — cannot be justified by the familiar distinction between the deserving and undeserving poor. My argument is not that the distinction is empirically misapplied; it is that the distinction is partly manufactured by conditionality itself and partly rests on moralized judgments about luck, effort, and dependence that no defensible theory of distributive justice can carry. In outline, I argue three things. Reciprocity defenses of conditionality confuse the terms on which we share a cooperative surplus with the preconditions of cooperation: a minimally adequate income is not something the poor must earn by displaying the right attitudes, and making it conditional treats receipt as a privilege rather than a right. Benefit fraud is made to do excessive moral work: the political rhetoric of the undeserving poor treats a claimant who misreports a change in circumstances as a distinctively culpable freeloader, while structurally similar noncompliance by employers, landlords, and relatively wealthy taxpayers is treated as an ordinary regulatory problem to be managed rather than a character defect to be punished. And the real function of conditionality is largely expressive — it marks recipients as second-class claimants and reassures contributors that their money is not being taken for a ride — but a liberal state may not attach status-degrading tests to survival assistance merely to provide that reassurance.

The audience is about forty people, mostly political philosophers and social policy researchers, and the questions I expect will not be the ones my paper preempts. Among those likely to ask: a prominent luck egalitarian who is likely to insist that unemployment benefits are legitimate only insofar as they compensate for brute luck and not for the chosen risks of not searching for work; a social policy professor who has studied sanction regimes longitudinally and believes some conditionality is needed to keep middle-class voters willing to fund transfers at all; a behavioral economist who thinks conditionality can function as a commitment device for myopic claimants; and a philosopher of criminal law who will press me on why benefit fraud is not simply theft from the public. Draft my Q&A preparation sheet: the objections in the order I will most likely face them, not ordered by how they appear in the literature, phrased as those four audience members would actually phrase them, in their own vocabulary. For each, give me a two-minute reply, note the one thing I should concede on the spot, and mark where my reply is weakest. Include the question I should be hoping nobody asks, and say briefly why it is the dangerous one.
