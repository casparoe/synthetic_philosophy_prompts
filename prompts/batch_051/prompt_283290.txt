I help run a philosophy club at my high school, and we are preparing a unit on which computations, if any, can be wronged—that is, whether a system made of the right kind of information processing rather than neurons could be the sort of thing that has moral standing. I would like you to produce a which-view-do-you-actually-hold quiz for our club members, together with a scoring key.

Please make it eight to ten questions. Each question should present a concrete, self-contained scenario and ask the reader to choose among two or three responses about whether the system deserves moral consideration, could suffer, or has a point of view. The questions should not use labels such as functionalism or phenomenal consciousness; people should be able to answer purely on the basis of the case, not by checking a vocabulary box.

The scoring key should map answer patterns onto at least the following positions, expressed in your own words and organized by the underlying commitments rather than by name: a biological-substrate-only view; a substrate-neutral computationalist view; a global-workspace view on which broad broadcasting and reportability matter; an integrated-information view on which the amount of irreducibly integrated information matters; a higher-order representational view on which the system must represent its own states to itself; and a deflationary no-fact-of-the-matter view. If the cases reveal an additional affective or valence-only position that none of these captures, include it and explain why it is distinct.

Please build the answer key from the logical space first: identify the diagnostic distinctions your questions actually test, list the answer combinations that are possible in principle, and then locate each position in that space. Mark any combinations that no position occupies and briefly diagnose whether each is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Also flag any two positions that end up with identical answer patterns on your cases, and say which additional case or question would be needed to separate them.

At least two or three of the questions should use scenarios along the following lines, though you may adjust them: replacing a person's neurons one at a time with silicon chips that preserve each neuron's input-output behavior; a thermostat or very simple robot that acts to maintain a goal state; a large network of simple units that together perform a difficult computation but has no single subsystem aware of the whole; a person under general anesthesia whose brain shows pain-like activity but no self-report or later memory; and an exact whole-brain emulation of a mouse running in a computer. Please make sure the cases are vivid enough for high school students and do not depend on their knowing any current debates about artificial intelligence.

Please do not include any readings or citations; the worksheet has to stand on its own. Finally, in one short paragraph after the key, say which question does the most diagnostic work in sorting our members and which distinction you think the quiz will fail to capture, so I can tell the club where the instrument's limits are.
