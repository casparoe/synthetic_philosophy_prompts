I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I serve as the community member on my hospital's neuroscience research ethics committee. We must decide this month on a proposal from our epilepsy surgery group. A 29-year-old patient with drug-resistant epilepsy has consented to a complete corpus callosotomy, and the same team asks permission to enroll him in a longitudinal study beginning shortly after surgery. The study would present stimuli to one visual field at a time and ask him to respond with one or the other hand, in order to compare how the two hemispheres process language, affect, and self-description. Consent would be signed by the patient before the operation, while his consciousness is still unified. The investigators argue the consent is straightforward: the surgery is clinically indicated apart from the research, the postoperative tasks are noninvasive and low-risk, and the patient is a single legal person from admission through follow-up. Several members of the committee are uneasy nevertheless. Their concern is that if the procedure substantially divides consciousness, then the postoperative subject who performs the tasks may not be the same unified agent who signed the form; a decision made by the preoperative person cannot automatically bind whichever subject or subjects exist afterward, and an assent process may need to be built into the postoperative period. Others respond that this is speculative metaphysics and that importing it into consent policy would make routine neurosurgical research impossible. Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before our Thursday meeting. It should recommend approving, rejecting, or approving with conditions; it should state any concrete changes to the consent or assent process; and it should engage the strongest version of the worry that the signer and the tested subject may not be the same subject rather than dismissing it as mere metaphysics.
