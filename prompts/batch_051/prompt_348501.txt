The exam question was: "Explain the paradox of phenomenal judgment and evaluate whether it gives a decisive reason to reject epiphenomenalism."

Here's the student's answer:

"Epiphenomenalism holds that the raw feel of experience is caused by physical processes in the brain but has no causal powers of its own—think of the old steam whistle that's produced by the engine but can't drive the train. The paradox of phenomenal judgment asks, given that picture, how our beliefs and reports about our own experience could ever be caused by those experiences themselves. Common sense says my pain is what makes me judge 'I'm in pain,' but the epiphenomenalist has to deny that; she has to say the judgment and the pain are two separate effects of one underlying neural cause, while the experience itself does nothing. But then our beliefs about our experiences are just along for the ride and aren't responsive to the experiences, so they're unreliable. It's like a thermometer wired to the boiler instead of the room: it reads 70 whenever the boiler fires, regardless of the actual room temperature. On the epiphenomenalist's own view, every introspective belief—including her belief that epiphenomenalism is true—is in that wired-thermometer boat, so the position is self-defeating in the same way as the liar paradox; it says its own supports are worthless, which makes it incoherent. Therefore epiphenomenalism must be false, since no theory that makes our introspective judgments unreliable can be right."
