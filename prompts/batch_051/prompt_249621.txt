I am a software engineer on a team building a consumer EEG headset that estimates focus and stress from brain signals. The product team is pushing a new feature for corporate customers: an opt-in dashboard that would show managers aggregate measures of employees' "attention drift" and "cognitive load" during the workday, inferred from the headset while people go about their normal work. Employees would consent, but the consent flow would not make clear which brain features are being extracted, how they are mapped to work performance, or whether the raw neural data is ever stored. In a design review, one engineer argued that we already collect plenty of behavioral telemetry—keystrokes, mouse paths, calendar activity—and that brain data is just a finer-grained signal of the same underlying productivity question. A second engineer replied that there is a difference between observing what someone does and observing what someone is experiencing while they do it, and that this difference matters even if the person clicked "I agree." I was asked which way I lean, and I couldn't give a grounded answer. I once came across a paper by Marcello Ienca and Roberto Andorno, "Towards New Human Rights in the Age of Neuroscience and Neurotechnology," which argues that neurotechnology can access and influence features of a person's mental life in ways that existing rights frameworks don't quite address, but I don't have the background to know whether that position can survive a strong objection from a data-is-just-data engineer. Can you help me think through whether there is a principled line between behavioral telemetry and neural data in this kind of workplace product? Give me the strongest version of the second engineer's position and the strongest version of the first engineer's reply, then say which structural feature of the case does the real philosophical work. I would be grateful if you could write the analysis in American English, since I'll be using it as notes for a U.S.-based team.
