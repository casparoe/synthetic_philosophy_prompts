Set the scenario precisely. At the start of a given year, a city of roughly 1.5 million people in a high-income country adopts a constitutional amendment with three planks that cannot be revised for forty years. First, every parcel is zoned as of right for any residential or mixed use up to twelve storeys: there are no minimum lot sizes, no parking requirements, no design review, no residential or small-commercial use permits, no environmental or historic review except for demonstrably unsafe structures, and no neighborhood consultation or veto rights. Second, no new public capacity may be added: roads, transit, water, sewer, electricity connections, public schools, parks, and emergency services are to be maintained at their current capacities but not expanded, and the city may not levy impact fees or require developers to supply off-site infrastructure. Third, each parcel's annual property tax is frozen at its current nominal amount in perpetuity; the city cannot introduce new taxes, and rising service costs must be absorbed within roughly fixed per-parcel revenue.

Give your best argued prediction of what the city looks like after four decades. Do not narrate a story; give the chain of mechanisms, incentives, and constraints, with explicit confidence attached to each link. Organize the answer around the question of who has a right to the city in the substantive sense of effective access to urban goods—employment, public goods, usable public space, and the social networks that dense proximity makes possible—as distinct from formal permission to build or rent. Among the channels to analyze: how the supply-side reforms change the quantity, type, and location of housing and who captures the resulting land-value gains; how the infrastructure freeze constrains growth and through which adaptive behaviors, such as congestion, rationing, private provision, shadow extensions, or out-migration, it bites; how the tax freeze reshapes the fiscal base, the incentive to hold land underused, and the services available to new versus incumbent residents; and which neighborhoods re-sort by income, tenure, and migration status. Then identify which of your predictions are robust and which depend on assumptions about price sensitivity of demand, construction costs, the ability of owners to convert property to tax-minimizing forms, or the willingness of higher levels of government to override the amendment. Finish by stating what would have to be false about one or two of those mechanisms for the conclusion that the reform package makes the effective right to the city more unequal to be wrong.
