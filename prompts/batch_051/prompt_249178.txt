Ok here's one I've been turning over in my head since going down the doctrinal paradox / discursive dilemma rabbit hole on the old voting-methods threads. I want a dialogue between two people who disagree about how a three-judge panel should aggregate their votes.

Setup, so the model doesn't need prior knowledge: the panel has to decide whether a company is liable for breach of contract. Under the relevant doctrine, liability requires two findings: (1) the contract was valid, and (2) the company actually breached it. The three judges vote like this:

- Judge A: contract valid, no breach, therefore not liable.
- Judge B: contract not valid, but the company did fail to perform, therefore not liable.
- Judge C: contract valid, breach, therefore liable.

So if you poll them premise by premise, a majority says the contract was valid (A and C) and a majority says there was a breach (B and C), which jointly imply liable. But if you poll them on the bottom line, a majority says not liable (A and B). Classic setup.

Write a dialogue between a proponent of premise-based aggregation (aggregate the premises and let the doctrine compute the conclusion) and a proponent of conclusion-based aggregation (aggregate the conclusion each judge actually reached). Each should genuinly engage with the other's actual arguments rather than restate their own position. Make the premise-based person grapple with the fact that the court would be issuing a ruling no individual judge endorsed, and make the conclusion-based person grapple with the fact that the court's premise-level majority seems to entail liability. It should read like a real argument between two competent people who have both thought about this a lot, not an intro explainer with one side obviously winning.
