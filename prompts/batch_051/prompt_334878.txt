I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy and I keep going in circles over a paragraph from an intro book on the Scottish Enlightenment that a friend passed along. It summarizes the impartial spectator like this: "Smith thinks that sympathy is the glue of moral life: when we see someone's situation we imaginatively project ourselves into it, and that's how we come to approve or disapprove of the sentiment behind their action. We judge our own conduct the same way, by imagining how an impartial spectator would react to it. But in practice the impartial spectator is basically just the voice of the people around you -- the outlook of a typical member of your society, internalized as public opinion. So for Smith an action is right just in case an impartial observer in your society would approve of it, and wrong if they'd disapprove. Morality therefore turns out to be socially constructed: different societies have different spectators, and within Smith's theory there's no standpoint from which to say that a whole society's moral code is mistaken." Something about this feels off to me, though I've only every read bits of The Theory of Moral Sentiments. I remember the spectator sounding more like an ideal judge or a conscience than like public opinion, and I though Smith had a way of criticizing the actual views of one's own society. But the book is by someone with a real job, so I don't trust my memory. Can you do an accuracy audit of that paragraph? I want to know which parts are faithful, which parts are common simplifications, and which parts Smith would explicitly reject, and in particular whether the relativism at the end actually follows from the earlier claims. It would help if you used the distinctions Smith actually draws -- sympathy as imaginative projection rather than just catching someone's emotion, the internalized spectator as something more than actual onlookers, propriety versus mere approval, the appeal from actual spectators to a higher standpoint -- but keep it to the ideas themselves, not a reading list.
