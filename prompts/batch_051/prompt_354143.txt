I keep going back and forth on the unilateralist's curse. As I understand it: when a bunch of people share goals and each one can, on their own, take an action that affects all of them—publishing a risky paper, sounding an alarm, shipping a product—the person who acts first will be the one who's most optimistic that the action is worth it. So the group's de facto decision gets made by its most extreme member, even when most members would have voted no. That's a simple, general argument, and it seems to explain a lot of group disasters, which is why I can't just set it aside. It pushes me toward a strong anti-unilateralist norm: when you're one of several people who disagree, defer, seek consensus, don't take the decision on yourself.

But then there are cases that won't stop bothering me. The whistleblower who cant get anyone else in the organization to admit the harm. The person in the train carriage who pulls the emergency brake while everyone around has convinced themselves the sound was nothing. These are unilateral acts, against the group's judgment, and I don't just want to excuse them—I think they're the best thing the person could have done. So I'm stuck between the principle that the person most eager to act is systematically the one most likely to be wrong, precisely because they're the outlier, and the intuition that sometimes the outlier is the only one seeing straight. I don't want to be told it just depends on whether they turn out to be right.

Give me the strongest version of each side. Then tell me whether there's a principled difference between the curse cases and the whistleblower cases, and which of my two commitments I should amend or drop: if the norm should bend, say what the amended norm would say and what it loses; if the intuition should go, say what would justify treating it as a bias.
