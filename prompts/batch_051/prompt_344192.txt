I am a novelist, and I have been turning over a structural resemblance between my craft and a problem in artificial intelligence and the philosophy of mind. I want a precise verdict on whether the resemblance is deep or superficial.

The problem is the frame problem. In its technical form, it is the difficulty of representing, in a logic of action, what changes and what remains the same when an event occurs, without writing an endless inventory of frame axioms for every fact that persists. If a window is broken, the pane shatters and the room grows colder, but the floor remains wooden, the neighboring houses remain where they were, and indefinitely many other facts carry forward untouched. In its broader, philosophical form, the frame problem is the problem of how a finite mind, presented with a new event, knows which of its beliefs to update and which to leave alone, without explicitly reviewing everything it knows.

The craft problem I face looks to me like this. When a novel moves from one scene to the next, the prose does not restate all the facts about the world and characters that still hold; it marks only the few that have changed, and the reader is trusted to carry the rest forward by default. The novelist selects which consequences of an action to dramatize and which to leave implicit, and the reader, using genre, narrative point of view, and convention, supplies the ramifications the text never states. This appears to be a frame problem solved not with formal logic but with an implicit contract between writer and reader: a kind of default persistence for fictional worlds, plus a relevance selector called point of view.

Is this a genuine identity of structure, a loose analogy, or a confusion? On one side, fictional worlds are deliberately incomplete and are narrated by an intelligence with designs on a reader, whereas the frame problem arises for an agent in a causally complete world with no author deciding what matters. On the other side, the underlying issue in both cases seems to be default persistence under change, together with the selection of which consequences of an event are worth representing. Could the frame assumption, the qualification problem, or default logic genuinely illuminate how readers update a story world from minimal textual cues, or would a formalization of narrative continuity have to abandon something essential to fiction — the reader's tolerance for inconsistency, retrospective reinterpretation, and the fact that in a novel, unlike in a machine's world model, almost nothing is supposed to be irrelevant? If the mapping is substantial enough that anything could transfer from one side to the other, tell me what the analogy predicts that fiction does not keep.

I am not asking for a bibliography or a survey of the frame-problem literature; I want a judgment on the parallel itself, precise enough that I can see where the two structures match and where they come apart.
