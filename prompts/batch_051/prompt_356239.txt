Last night a friend and I spent the whole evening arguing about whether "wisdom" names a real, distinct quality or is just a flattering label for intelligence plus life experience. I held that we could drop the word without losing anything; he insisted it picks out something more, but every definition he tried either collapsed into "being clever" or "being old" or was so vague that nothing could fail it. Afterward I couldn't stop thinking about it, so I drew up a list of hard cases to force the issue. I would like you to act as a professional philosopher who specializes in moral psychology and ethics, and I would like you to give me a working definition of wisdom that correctly classifies the following people. The cases are: (1) a chess prodigy who calculates further ahead than almost anyone alive but keeps wrecking his health and his marriage, though his practical advice to friends is usually excellent; (2) a con artist with an uncanny ability to read people, whose recommendations about people and money reliably work but are always weaponized to exploit the vulnerable; (3) a grandmother with almost no schooling whose concrete, person-by-person advice has turned out right for decades, though she can rarely give reasons for it; (4) a moral philosopher with a brilliant command of normative theory who can produce devastating arguments in seminars but becomes paralyzed and self-defeating when facing her own practical crises; (5) a person who constantly recites aphorisms about acceptance, gratitude, and letting go, mostly on social media, but has never weathered a serious hardship; (6) a palliative care nurse with no intellectual ambitions who seems to know exactly what to say and do for dying patients and their families; (7) a man who insists he knows nothing, questions everyone's settled beliefs including his own, and refuses to give advice, yet lives what seems to be a decent and reflective life; and (8) a twelve-year-old who, asked why a bully hurts other children, replies that the bully probably hurts because he himself is hurting. My own verdicts are firm only for the con artist and the social-media aphorist—not wise—and for the grandmother and the palliative care nurse—wise. The other four genuinely puzzle me.

Please think carefully, and begin with a brief brainstorming pass over what the clear cases share and where the borderline cases pull apart. Then propose your working definition, run it through all eight cases one by one, and identify any place where the definition forces a verdict you find surprising; at that point, either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict. Conclude by telling me which of your classifications is the least secure and why. Please write your answer in American English.
