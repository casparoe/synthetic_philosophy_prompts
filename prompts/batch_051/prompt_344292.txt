Two people in my reading group got stuck in an arguement about Pearl’s causal hierarchy, and I genuinely can’t tell who is right. We’re reading Judea Pearl, “The Seven Tools of Causal Inference, with Reflections on Machine Learning.” He writes, at the point where he explains why Counterfactuals sit at the top:

“I place Counterfactuals at the top of the hierarchy because they subsume interventional and associational questions. If we have a model that can answer counterfactual queries, we can also answer questions about interventions and observations. The translation does not work in the opposite direction. Interventional questions cannot be answered from purely observational information, from statistical data alone.”

The disagreement went something like this. Maya said Pearl is making an informational point, not a linguistic one: a fully specified SCM with exogenous noise gives you all three levels, an interventional model can give you do-calculus answers without identifying counterfactuals, and a purely associational model can’t even answer interventions. Jonas said that reading makes “counterfactuals subsume interventions” trivial, because if youve already assumed the full structural model there’s nothing surprising about getting the lower levels; what Pearl actually means is that a particular counterfactual capacity, the ability to answer “what if things had been otherwise” queries, can be repurposed to answer interventional questions. Maya came back: if a model can answer counterfactual queries, that capacity already includes enough causal information to compute interventional distributions, but the reverse capacity to answer interventional questions may not give you counterfactual distributions, because interventional distributions don’t fix the joint distribution over potential outcomes. Jonas replied that the example in the passage is weaker than that: Pearl asks the counterfactual “what would happen had the price been twice its current value?” and treats it as equivalent to the interventional “what will happen if we double the price?” but these are not literally the same query—the first is retrospective and can condition on the observed price, where the second is a policy query. Then Maya says that’s a distinction without a difference once the SCM is fixed. Neither would budge.
