I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy. I have been asked to help grade a short-answer question from an introductory moral philosophy course, and I am concerned that I might miss subtle problems in an answer that reads fluently. I would rather receive a point-by-point assessment than a general impression.

Exam question:

Consider the following case. Acme Pharmaceutical's safety approval procedure has two stages. A committee first votes that a new drug's active ingredient is safe; a second vote certifies that its delivery mechanism is safe. Each committee member reviews only one of the two stages, and no member reviews the interaction between the ingredient and the delivery mechanism. The drug is approved and later causes serious harm because of that interaction. No individual employee intended the harm, and it is unclear that any single employee was negligent under the company's written procedures.

Some philosophers argue that in such a case we should blame the corporation itself, over and above or instead of blaming any of the individuals inside it. Others argue that blame applies strictly to individual human beings, so corporate blame is either loose talk or a practical shortcut. Write a short essay that (i) presents the strongest version of each position, (ii) applies each position to the Acme case, and (iii) states and defends your own view.

Student answer:

Supporters of individual-level blame begin with the premise that blame applies only to agents, and that agency requires mental states such as beliefs, desires, intentions, and the capacity to respond to criticism. Since a corporation has no mental states of its own, statements about what Acme intended are shorthand for what certain human beings intended or negligently allowed. There is also a fairness concern: when a corporation is fined or publicly condemned, the cost often falls on shareholders and low-level employees who had no role in the decision, while the responsible managers may keep their positions. Applied to Acme, the individualist will say that if the two-stage procedure made it nobody's job to review the combination, then the appropriate target is not some abstraction called "the company" but whoever designed the procedure, such as the executives who knew that committee members each saw only one stage. Blame should track actual human decisions.

Defenders of corporate blame respond that this misses the way an organizational structure itself can cause harm. In the Acme case, the procedure may not have been any single executive's decision; it may have grown out of years of incremental changes, none of which anyone designed with the harmful interaction in view. If we insist on finding one culpable individual, we will either acquit everyone or scapegoat someone whose conduct covered only part of the decision. The company's defective routines seem the more accurate target. Moreover, the law already treats corporations as persons that can contract, own property, and be fined. Since we accept corporate persons for these purposes, there is no obstacle in principle to treating the corporation as a moral agent that can be blamed in its own right.

My own view is that corporations can be blamed, but only in a forward-looking sense. To blame Acme is not to say that it has a conscience or that it can feel guilt; it is to identify a defective organizational pattern and demand reform, in roughly the way a software engineer might blame a crashing application without supposing that the application has feelings. This keeps the individualist insight that corporations lack inner mental lives while still allowing blame to attach to the organization rather than to scapegoated employees.
