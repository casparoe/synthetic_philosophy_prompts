I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, but I've been reading around moral particularism vs. generalism and it's quietly colonizing the book I'm drafting. My protagonist is a hospice nurse who keeps getting cornered — by her sister, by a patient's family, by her own memory — into giving a reason why she acted as she did, and every time she reaches for a principle ("never lie," "always relieve suffering") the next scene smashes it against a case where the same feature cuts the other way. I want a sonnet I can pin above my desk as a kind of tuning fork for her.

Here's the part that matters. The octave should be spoken by the generalist: a real attempt at a moral principle, done in strict meter and honest rhymes, no strawmanning. Then the volta has to be the arrival of particularist cases, and I want the poem to break its own form at that moment — the rhymes go off or slant, the meter starts to limp, the sentences get less symmetrical — so the form enacts the claim that the principle can't hold the cases. The closing couplet should not recover into a better, subtler principle. It should show what the particularist is left holding: attention to this case, here, with its whole history, rather than subsumption under a rule.

And please get the philosophy right: the particularist is not saying "rules have exceptions" — a generalist can absorb that with fancier rules. The point is holism about reasons: a feature that counts in favor in one context can count against, or count for nothing, in another, and moral judgment isn't best understood as applying a finite set of principles at all. If a philosopher who works on Dancy read it, I'd want her to nod, not wince; if a reader who's never heard of the debate read it, I'd want them to just think it's a good, strange poem.
