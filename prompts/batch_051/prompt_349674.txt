I'm a philosophy professor and I've been re-reading Mill's A System of Logic, specifically the bit in Book VI where he says social science has to use a chemical method rather than a mechanical one: when individuals interact, the result is not just the sum of their separate tendencies, just as hydrogen and oxygen combine into water, whose properties are not those of either gas on its own. I had always treated this as a local point about the difficulty of predicting social wholes. But it struck me the other week that Mill is basically making the same distinction philosophers of biology now draw between aggregative and emergent properties: an aggregative property is just the sum of the parts' properties, an emergent one is not. Is this parallel deep or superficial? In particular, would Mill's argument actually show that social facts are emergent in the modern sense, or does his chemical analogy lack something the modern distinction has? And if the parallel is deep, does anything from contemporary debates about emergence tell us whether Mill was right that the social sciences need a fundamentally different method from the physical ones? I cant tell whether I've found a real continuity or just pattern-matching on the word sum.
