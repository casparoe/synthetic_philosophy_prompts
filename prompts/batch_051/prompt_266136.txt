I retired from a career in experimental physics and have since been reading epistemology, especially discussions of epistemic modesty, alongside the psychological literature on forecasting. I keep noticing a structural resemblance that I cannot resolve, and I would like help in testing whether it is deep or superficial.

The distinction between the inside view and the outside view, as I understand it, is this: one may forecast a project or decision by reasoning from the details of the particular case — the specific plan, people, and causal story — or by assigning the case to a reference class of comparable cases and beginning from the distribution of outcomes in that class. The striking empirical claim is that the second, apparently cruder, method is usually more accurate, and that well-calibrated forecasters start from the base rate and adjust only modestly for the respects in which the present case is unusual.

The parallel that strikes me is that the inside view behaves like a low-bias, high-variance estimator and the outside view like a high-bias, low-variance estimator. Reasoning from the particulars of a case resembles fitting a flexible model to a small, noisy sample: it can represent the instance faithfully but is unstable across cases and prone to overfitting. Reasoning from the reference class resembles strong shrinkage toward the class mean, of the sort familiar from hierarchical models with partial pooling or from the way individual group means are pulled toward a grand mean. Both trade fidelity to the instance against stability across instances, and the sensible mix would seem to depend on how much reliable case-specific evidence one has relative to the homogeneity of the reference class. Is this a real identity of structure, or am I pattern-matching on two uses of the word estimate? If the mapping is real, epistemic modesty would become a kind of shrinkage schedule: begin at the base rate and move toward the instance-specific estimate only as far as the reliability and quantity of case-specific evidence justify. The bias–variance logic would also transfer, implying that base-rate-anchored forecasts are less wrong on average but pay a cost on atypical cases. Yet the mapping would predict that inside-view estimates are merely noisy, whereas much inside-view reasoning looks systematically optimistic rather than unbiased; and it presupposes a well-defined reference class of the sort real planning seldom supplies. I would like to know whether these are fixable defects of the analogy or evidence that it misleads, and whether anything distinctively statistical — shrinkage, the bias–variance decomposition, model selection — genuinely transfers to the debate over how much to trust one's own particular judgment.
