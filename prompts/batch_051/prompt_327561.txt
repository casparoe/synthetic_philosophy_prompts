I keep tripping over a parallel between the Principal Principle and judicial deference to administrative agencies, and I can't tell whether it's a real structural match or just the word "deference." On the probability side: Lewis's Principal Principle says that if X says the objective chance of A is x, and E is admissible evidence, then Cr(A | X & E) = x. All the work is in "admissible"—intuitively, E can't include information about how the chancy process actually turns out (the crystal-ball cases), and stating exactly which evidence is admissible turns out to be genuinely hard. On the law side: courts defer to an agency's interpretation of an ambiguous statute it administers, unless the statute is clear or the interpretation is unreasonable—and here too the exception does all the work, with a huge messy case law about what counts as "defeating" information. Both strike me as: a lower-level epistemic agent has a default duty to adopt a higher authority's verdict, except when it has evidence that undercuts the authority's claim to be authoritative for this question. Is that one abstract structure showing up twice, or am I pattern-matching? If it's deep, does anything transfer—say, does the chance literature's way of sorting undermining from merely surprising information predict anything about the law side, or vice versa, and what would a counterexample to the analogy look like?
