So I just got home from a two-hour argument with a friend about whether this guy we know, Dan, is responsible for bailing on his roommate at the airport — he promised to pick him up, fell asleep, and by the time he woke up the roommate had taken an $80 cab. My friend kept saying the only real question is whether Dan could have done otherwise, and if he couldn't, then resenting him is just cruelty dressed up. I kept feeling that was solving a different problem, but I couldn't say why until the walk home.

Here's what I came up with. I'm calling it the Unforeclosable Question account of responsibility (bad name, fine). The claim: to be responsible for something is to be the person a certain kind of question stays addressed to. Not the informational "why did it happen," but the second-personal "why did YOU do that" — the kind that demands an answer from you and that nobody else can answer in you're place. On this view, an excuse isn't evidence that you weren't really the source of the act; it's an answer that closes the question before we even need to press it. "He was in the hospital" doesn't show Dan isn't the agent, it shows there's nothing left to ask him. But "he was exhausted" only closes things if the exhaustion doesn't itself invite the next question — if he'd been up til 4 drinking, the question just moves one step back ("and why did you do that to yourself"). So responsibility isn't about causal control or alternate possibilities; it's about whether the chain of demand-questions has run all the way to an answer that satisfies us, or whether there's still one addressed to you that the rest of us are not allowed to foreclose.

I know this sounds like Strawson — I've read Freedom and Resentment and I get that for him blame is about the reactive attitudes and the shift to the objective stance, not about metaphysics. But I'm not sure whether my question-addressing piece is just relabeling resentment, or whether it's doing different work. For one thing, it seems to give a cleaner story about why "he couldn't have done otherwise" can be an excuse in some cases and an aggravating detail in others.

So give me an honest assessment. Is this coherent? Is it already a position under a name I don't know? What does it get right, and where does it break? I especially want the three or four strongest counterexamples — like the psychopath you stop asking questions of, or self-blame where the person doing the asking is you — and tell me whether any of them is fatal rather than just a case I'd have to bite the bullet on.
