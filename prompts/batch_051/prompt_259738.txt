I have been keeping a file of a departmental colleague's verdicts to use in my graduate seminar on the nature of mind. She works in philosophy of cognitive science and has spent the term applying the familiar fourfold taxonomy of kinds of minds: Darwinian creatures, whose fixed behavioral repertoire is a product of natural selection over generations; Skinnerian creatures, which shape behavior within a lifetime by operant conditioning, testing actions in the world and retaining what works; Popperian creatures, which test candidate actions against an internal model of the world rather than against the world itself; and Gregorian creatures, which extend and reorganize their cognition around representational tools such as language, notation, and diagrams, and which inherit a stock of such cognitive artifacts from their culture.

Her recorded verdicts so far:

a. A digger wasp whose nesting-and-provisioning routine is a rigid sequence and which, when an experimenter displaces the prey, simply restarts the entire sequence from the beginning rather than adjusting it: Darwinian.
b. A rat that acquires a lever-press response through gradual reinforcement, with no indication of mental rehearsal: Skinnerian.
c. A raven that solves a novel string-pulling puzzle on first exposure after a long pause and inspection, behaving as though it had tried candidate actions internally before acting: Popperian.
d. A child who learns a new sorting strategy after being taught a word and shown a diagram, and then generalizes the strategy to new materials: Gregorian.
e. An adult raised from infancy without exposure to language and without schooling, who masters a mechanical latch through repeated manipulation over many trials and cannot explain how she does it: Skinnerian.

Next week she will rule on the following case: a solitary individual raised away from any linguistic community, who invents a private graphical notation for keeping count. Once the notation is in place, her planning improves markedly; she solves multi-step problems that had previously been beyond her, and she visibly offloads part of the problem-solving work onto the marks themselves.

Please give me your prediction, with your reasons for preferring it over the alternatives, and tell me how confident I should be. In doing so, spell out the rule that best rationalizes her prior verdicts, identify at least one rival rule that fits those same verdicts just as well and say what each predicts about the new case, and then specify a further case that would cleanly decide between the leading rules, with the prediction each rule would make for it.
