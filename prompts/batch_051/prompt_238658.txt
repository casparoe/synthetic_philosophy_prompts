I help run the astronomy side of my school’s joint philosophy-and-astronomy club, a group of about twenty students ages sixteen through eighteen who meet once a week for fifty minutes. We have been discussing the Fermi paradox, but the members tend to treat it as a curious fact about the universe rather than as a sharp problem that should make their own assumptions uncomfortable. I would like to try a structured simulation or game in which they act as civilizations, or as expansion fronts, in a toy Milky Way. The activity should make them feel, before I put a name on it, the version of the paradox I find hardest to escape: if intelligence has arisen even once before, the timescales for spreading across the galaxy are short compared with its age—even a single self-replicating probe moving at one percent of light speed can cross a hundred-thousand-light-year galaxy in something like ten million years, a tiny fraction of the galaxy’s history—so the silence we observe is not just an absence of life but an absence of visible large-scale activity, which is much easier to produce and much harder to explain. I want the activity to lead them to that mismatch themselves rather than having it announced.

Please lay out the design concretely: roles and rules, materials that do not require real money, and a fifty-minute timeline, plus a debrief that maps what happened during the game to the quantities in the argument (number of starting civilizations, probability that any one attempts expansion, speed of a colonization front, how long an expanding volume remains detectable). Also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time a group runs this kind of simulation and how to handle it, and be frank about which feature of the paradox a classroom activity cannot reproduce and therefore must be supplied by discussion.
