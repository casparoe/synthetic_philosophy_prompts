Until last year I worked in industry on a system that had to track what different users knew and could reasonably be expected to infer from a shared document base; our inherited possible-worlds model kept predicting that every user knew every logical consequence of everything in the base, which was false in ways that repeatedly surfaced in production. I have since returned to academic philosophy, and in two weeks I present a colloquium paper to our department's formal epistemology group. The thesis I am defending is that the logical omniscience built into standard possible-worlds semantics is not a harmless idealization but a modeling defect, and that awareness-based semantics—in which an agent's knowledge is closed under classical consequence only relative to an explicit, resource-sensitive awareness set—is the most defensible replacement for agents who acquire knowledge over time. The audience will be mostly philosophical logicians and formal epistemologists who have spent their careers inside possible-worlds frameworks; there will also be two computer scientists who work on S5 epistemic logic for multi-agent systems and care about tractability and completeness proofs, and one epistemologist who suspects the whole project psychologizes knowledge and thereby abandons its normative force. My argument, compressed, is this: the standard framework validates closure under known entailment and necessitation, which together force every agent to know all classical tautologies and all logical consequences of anything she knows; I present two cases in which this yields systematically wrong predictions about agents learning at different rates from a common source; and I argue that impossible-worlds and algorithmic-knowledge accounts repair the problem only by sacrificing too much logical structure or expressive power. I need a prep sheet, not a defense. Tell me the objections I should actually expect, attributed in rough terms to the faction that will raise them, phrased the way they will phrase them, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are. For each objection, say whether it has real force, what I should concede in my opening remarks, what I should hold, the two-minute reply I should give, and where that reply is weakest. I also want the objection I should most hope nobody asks, with a plan for what to say if someone asks it anyway.
