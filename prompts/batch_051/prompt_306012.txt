Here's one I can't get to cohere. Most people I know think a healthy adult selling a kidney to a stranger is repugnant and should stay illegal, even when the buyer would die without it and the seller would be well paid. But the same people think a commercial fishing or logging job is honest work, not repugnant, even though a chunk of the wage is plainly payment for taking on a known risk of death and serious injury. If you added the risk up over a career it would probably dwarf the risk of donating a kidney, yet we treat one as mutilating your body for cash and the other as just a dangerous job. I'm not sure we're being consistent. Is there a principle that generates both verdicts, or is this squeamishness plus normalization? Don't just say exploitation or desperate consent — people can be desperate in both cases, and in one of them the desperate person is the one who dies without the organ. Give me the strongest candidate justification you can find (dignity, bodily integrity, unequal bargaining power, downstream effects on a market or a system, whatever it is), then push it through some nearby cases: paid plasma donation, paying volunteers for human challenge trials or phase I drug studies, paid surrogacy, selling a liver lobe to someone who'll die without it, boxers getting paid to take brain trauma. Which of these does your principle permit and which does it block? And tell me whether in the end you'd keep the kidney ban, reconsider the dangerous job, or both. While you're at it, separate what's actually settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on commodification — don't make a disputed view sound like the consensus.
