A friend and I fell down a computational-complexity rabbit hole, and we now have an argument that seems to prove way too much. Every step looks fine to me but the conclusion is absurd, so one of them has to be bad.

(1) The brain is a finite physical system: finitely many neurons, and each neuron has finitely many discriminable states, so the brain has finitely many total states — a colosal finite automaton.
(2) The input–output behavior of any finite automaton can be simulated by a Turing machine in time linear in the length of the input, hence in polynomial time.
(3) So whatever function the brain computes from sensory input to motor output is computable in polynomial time.
(4) But some of the things we do every day have general forms that are NP-hard: finding a proof of at most n lines in a fixed formal system, planning a route through n errands, scheduling tasks under constraints, that sort of thing.
(5) No polynomial-time Turing machine computes an NP-hard function (assuming P ≠ NP).
(6) So the brain can't be doing what we observe ourselves doing.
(7) Therefore the mind isn't a finite physical system after all, and is doing something no ordinary computer can — non-algorithmic or hypercomputational. That's a lot of metaphysics to squeeze out of a complexity class, so I'm not buying it, but I can't see which step is load-bearing.

Which step do I give up, and what does rejecting it commit me to? I'm sure (7) is false; the trouble is that each of (1)–(5) looked solid when I wrote it down.
