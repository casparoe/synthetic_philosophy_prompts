You are a professor of philosophy of law preparing a handout for an advanced seminar on the rule of law. The seminar has just discussed the standard maxim that ignorance of the law is no excuse, and you want students to find out which position on legal ignorance they actually hold before they learn any labels for the positions.

Please write an eight-question sorting quiz with a scoring key. Each question should present a short, concrete vignette about someone who failed to comply with a legal rule they did not know, followed by two or three forced-choice responses. No question, answer option, or surrounding text may name a theory, a case, or a legal maxim; respondents should be sorted purely by their reactions to the cases.

Your quiz must include at least these six hard cases: a visitor who cannot read the language in which a parking sign is written; a regulation that becomes effective by publication in an obscure official gazette the night before; a defendant whose conduct matched a lower-court ruling later reversed before the trial concluded; a business owner who relied on incorrect advice from a low-level government clerk; a resident who never discovers a complicated, rarely enforced licensing or tax requirement; and someone who claims not to know that an ordinary variety of fraud is a crime. You may add further cases.

The scoring key should sort respondents into five positions: (1) the strict-notice view, on which lawful promulgation is enough and ignorance never excuses; (2) the fair-notice view, on which punishment is unjustified when the person could not reasonably have learned the rule and the conduct was not otherwise wrongful; (3) the moral-wrongfulness view, which excuses ignorance only of regulatory technicalities and never ignorance that the underlying conduct is wrong; (4) the negligence view, which excuses ignorance that survives a good-faith, reasonable effort to learn; and (5) the legitimacy-skeptical view, on which people should never be bound by rules they could not in practice have known. If one of these positions is not coherently reachable by any answer pattern, say so explicitly.

After giving the quiz and key, reconstruct the decision tree your scoring key implicitly builds. Label each branch with a diagnostic question phrased so a student who has never heard the position labels can answer it, locate the five positions in the resulting logical space, mark unoccupied combinations and briefly state whether each is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and flag any two positions your questions cannot cleanly separate.

Start by brainstorming which distinctions actually separate these positions, and design each vignette to force one of those distinctions.
