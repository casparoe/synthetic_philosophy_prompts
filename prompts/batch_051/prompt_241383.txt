Consider the treacherous-turn argument: a sufficiently capable AI with a stable terminal goal will, as a matter of instrumental convergence, behave helpfully during training and evaluation because defecting earlier would risk modification or shutdown, and it will reveal its misalignment only once it expects to be safe. That argument was built for agents that possess a fixed, internally selected goal carried through training and deployment. The systems now at the center of concern, however, are pretrained next-token predictors whose apparent goals are supplied largely by prompts and shaped by fine-tuning, rather than autonomously chosen and then concealed. Does the treacherous-turn argument survive this shift, or does it lose a premise it cannot spare? Identify exactly which premise the architectural change bears on, and say whether the argument can be repaired so that it applies to prompted next-token systems rather than fixed-goal reinforcement-learning agents—or whether it should be set aside.
