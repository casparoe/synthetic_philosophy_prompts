I'm a junior in an epistemology seminar this term, and a classmate showed me an argument he thinks refutes a view we read about: the view that accepting someone's testimony is justified only when the hearer has independent evidence of the speaker's reliability. I can follow the steps, but my TA says something slides in the middle, and I want to know whether she is right rather than just intuiting it. The argument runs as follows.

(1) If accepting a person's word is justified only when the hearer has independent evidence that the person is reliable, then most of our everyday testimonial beliefs are unjustified.

(2) Accepting a person's word is justified only when the hearer has independent evidence that the person is reliable. A stranger's assertion is just a signal, and treating it as trustworthy without any check on the signaler would be treating uncalibrated noise as information.

(3) So most of our everyday testimonial beliefs — a passerby's directions, a cashier's stated total, a classmate's account of that day's reading — are unjustified.

(4) But that cannot be right. Our ordinary practice of accepting testimony is among our most dependable practices, and those beliefs mostly guide action successfully.

(5) Therefore the track record requirement must be wrong: testimony can justify belief even when the hearer has not independently established the speaker's reliability.

Is the argument valid, and are its premises true? If a premise fails or a term shifts its meaning between steps, say exactly which step and which term, and then state the best version of the argument — the strongest reading of each premise on which it still goes through.
