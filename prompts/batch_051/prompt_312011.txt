I have been trying to work out, in a principled way, whose values an AI system should serve when we call it aligned, and my own reactions to particular cases keep pulling me in different directions. I would like you to help me find the principle — or small set of candidate principles — that best captures those reactions, test it against cases I have not considered, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts, if any, it cannot save.

Here are the reactions I want to accommodate. First, when a personal assistant helps one user keep kosher and another follow a strict carnivore diet, I want the system to take each user's dietary values as given and help them pursue their own conception of a good meal, rather than imposing some independent standard of correct nutrition. Second, when a city uses an AI to rank applicants for scarce subsidized housing, I want the system to use impartial public criteria — need, vulnerability, time on the waiting list — and not let any group's view of who deserves housing govern the ranking. Third, when a private religious association runs an AI moderator that removes posts conflicting with the association's teachings, I think that is permissible: a private association may enforce its own substantive norms in its own forum. Fourth, when a very large general-purpose social platform — large enough to function as a de facto public square — tunes its ranking and moderation AI, I want it to refuse to become an instrument of any one group's comprehensive doctrine, and to confine itself to a thin set of widely defensible rules about spam, harassment, incitement, and platform manipulation, even if every group ends up with some content it dislikes. Fifth, when a manager gives an AI assistant the instruction to optimize layoff decisions for her team's short-term output and ignore everything else, I do not want the system simply to defer to her values; I want it to surface the interests of the affected employees and the fairness implications of the selection rule.

The two pairs that look alike but feel different to me are the first and fifth cases (both involve an individual's AI assistant, yet I want deference in one and not the other) and the third and fourth cases (both involve a private company's moderation or ranking AI, yet I permit substantive norms in one and demand neutrality in the other). I keep reaching for something like this: defer to the principal's values in private, low-externality contexts; switch to liberal neutrality, public reason, and impartial aggregation when the AI performs a public function or when third parties are seriously affected; and treat sufficiently large private platforms as public functionaries for this purpose. But that principle looks like a patchwork to me, and I suspect it may be rationalizing my verdicts rather than explaining them. Please state the most defensible version of such a principle, or a small set of competing principles, precisely; tie each clause to the case that motivates it; apply it to further cases I have not listed (for example, an AI that helps a family choose a school in a religiously divided school district, an AI that allocates transplantable organs in a public hospital, and an AI that drafts a company's internal code of conduct); and say plainly which of my five verdicts it cannot accommodate and which, if any, I should revise for the sake of simplicity.
