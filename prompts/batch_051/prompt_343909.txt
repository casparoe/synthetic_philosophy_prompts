I've described my whole moral approach as the golden rule for years, but I've started collecting my own verdicts on cases and they don't obviously hang together. Here they are.

If I see a coworker with spinach in her teeth, I should quietly tell her, because I'd want to be told. That seems like a clear win for the rule.

I love surprise parties and would be touched if friends threw me one. A friend of mine hates them and has told me so. I shouldn't throw her one. So her actual preferences beat what I'd want in her place.

But when the same friend asks me to lie to her husband about where she was, I should refuse, even though that's what she actually wants. Maybe that's just because lying is wrong, but my honest first-pass reason is that I'd want a friend to stop me if I were doing that. So here my projected preference beats her actual one.

I also think a masochist shouldn't act on the positive version at all. That seems to sink do unto others as you'd have them do unto you, but I have a feeling the negative version — don't do what you wouldn't want done to yourself — somehow survives that, and I can't articulate why.

And I'd lie to the murderer at the door about where my friend is hiding, even though the murderer doesn't want to be lied to. So the rule can't be about giving each person what they want.

My closest attempt at a principle is something like: treat others by the standards you'd want applied to you, except that when their actual settled preferences are known, those govern — except when they don't. I can hear that isn't a principle.

What I want is the cleanest principle that captures most of these verdicts, stated precisely, with each clause tied to the case that forces it. Then give me your own verdicts on a few cases I haven't listed, tell me which of my verdicts the principle can't save, and say whether the principle is best understood as a fixed-up golden rule or something else I'd have to call by a different name. Also separate what's just settled exegesis of the golden rule from what's genuinely contested among philosophers — don't present a contested view as the consensus.
