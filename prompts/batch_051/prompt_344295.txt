I'm taking a course on suffering-focused ethics and a real historical episode keeps bugging me, because it looks like a natural experiment for the view. From the mid-20th century until roughly the late 1980s, it was common in many hospitals for newborns and premature infants to undergo major surgery — sometimes heart surgery — with little or no pain relief. The surgeons weren't sadists; a lot of them sincerely believed the infant nervous system was too immature to register pain, and they genuinely worried that anesthesia was too dangerous for a baby that small. Some infants got only a paralytic drug so they wouldn't thrash around on the table, which means that if they did feel anything, they were awake and feeling it while unable to move or cry. Then in the 1980s studies began showing that babies mount an enormous stress-hormone response to surgery and that giving real analgesia actually improves outcomes, and practice changed fast — though I know the standard story is probably a little too tidy as history, and parts of it have been pushed back on.

I want you to work through this episode through the lens of a suffering-focused ethic, by which I mean the family of views that assigns special or overriding moral priority to the reduction and prevention of suffering — the rough idea that suffering is the main thing with intrinsic moral urgency, and that preventing it matters more than producing happiness. What should a thoughtful proponent of that view have said about this practice in, say, 1975, on the evidence and background beliefs available then? Which features of the episode does the view explain well (the pain the infants won't remember, the suffering of a patient who can't report it, the horror of the paralytic specifically), and which features does it struggle with or misdescribe? In particular the real crux looks like an epistemic failure about whose behavior counts as evidence of pain, plus a risk tradeoff between certain severe pain and a small chance of death from anesthesia — not people thinking a little discomfort was acceptable. Does the suffering-focused lens diagnose what actually went wrong, or does it only tell us the wrongness after the facts are settled? And what should the episode teach someone about what suffering-focused ethics still needs to work out before it can guide practice — is the weak spot in the axiology, in the epistemology of other minds, in comparing different kinds of harms, or somewhere else? Be candid: I want the analysis to run in both directions, theory illuminating the case and the case stress-testing the theory, not just a sermon about how the practice was bad.
