I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm drafting a long-form piece about why some of the most consequential decisions — changing careers, ending or committing to a relationship, having a child, moving across a country — seem to defeat the cost-benefit style of reasoning that works well for smaller choices. I don't want a neutral survey of positions; I want the article to take a definite philosophical stand. Please construct a committed argument for the claim that when two options are incommensurable — neither is better than the other, yet they are not exactly equal in value — the choice between them need not be arbitrary. Defend the further view that such choices can be rational because the act of choosing, when it is wholehearted, creates new agent-relative reasons that were not available before the choice. In making the argument, anticipate and answer the strongest objection: that treating post-hoc commitment as a source of reasons merely rationalizes a decision that, at the time it was made, had no rational basis. Keep the piece's target audience — thoughtful non-specialists — in mind, but don't flatten the concepts; explain the differences among incommensurability, incomparability, and equality where those distinctions matter. If your argument depends on a contested reading of any of those terms, say so in a sentence rather than presenting it as settled. I'd rather have a strong, opinionated case than a balanced overview.
