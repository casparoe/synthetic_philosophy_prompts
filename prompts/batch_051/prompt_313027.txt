I am a PhD student working on philosophical methodology, specifically on the question of when case-specific intuitions should count as evidence and when they should be overridden by general principles. I have been recording my own verdicts on this, and the pattern is messier than I expected. I would like to know whether a single coherent principle captures it, and, if not, which of my verdicts I should revise. If the request itself rests on a confusion—for example, if the contrast between case intuitions and principles is too crude to do the work I am asking it to do—please say so plainly rather than trying to force a reconciliation.

Here are the verdicts.

1. The trolley cases. I am strongly inclined to treat the switch case and the push case as morally different, and I take that difference as evidence against a maximizing principle that treats the two as equivalent. I would not give up the case-level difference merely because a simpler theory says there is none.

2. The barely-better chain. Consider a long sequence of alternatives, each of which I judge only negligibly worse than the one before, where the endpoint of the chain is clearly worse than the starting point (a spectrum from a mild annoyance to serious pain, say). My adjacent-pair judgments would, taken together, violate the transitivity of "better than." Here I keep transitivity and overturn some of my small-step judgments. I am more confident in the structural principle than in my fine-grained comparative verdicts.

3. Grue and simplicity. I treat simplicity as genuine though defeasible evidence in choosing among empirically adequate theories, even though I cannot point to any particular case intuition that grounds this. And in Goodman's grue case, I side with "green" over "grue," but I suspect this is really my preference for simplicity doing the work rather than an independent datum about the case.

4. Set theory and mereology. In set theory I accept the iterative conception—sets are built up in stages from a starting point—and therefore accept that there is no set of all sets, even though the idea of such a set feels natural. Here the principle governs. But in mereology I judge that a statue and the lump of clay constituting it are distinct, and I let that case intuition govern even though the principle of extensionality (same parts, same whole) would identify them. I cannot yet say why the two domains differ in how I weigh case intuition against principle.

5. Framing an innocent person. If a maximizing theory tells me to frame an innocent person in order to prevent a riot, I abandon the theory, not the case intuition. The case verdict wins over the principle, even though the principle is simple and has other virtues.

6. Fission in personal identity. In a fission case where a person's two hemispheres are transplanted into two new bodies, my immediate intuition is unstable and feels contaminated by the fantastical set-up. I let a theoretical commitment—that identity must be a one-to-one relation across time—settle the case rather than my wavering case-level verdict.

My tentative rule is that a case intuition should be trusted to the extent it is stable, vivid, and drawn from a domain in which I have had repeated practical feedback, while a principle should be trusted to the extent the domain is formal or stipulated. But that rule seems to misfire twice: it would count my vivid intuition that there is a set of all sets in its favor even though I reject that intuition, and it would make my trolley intuitions look suspect merely because the scenarios are contrived, even though I treat them as central data. So the rule is at least incomplete, and probably wrong.

I would like you to state a precise principle—or a small set of candidate principles—that best captures these verdicts, explain which feature of each case is doing the work, and tell me what the resulting account predicts for cases I have not listed, such as a proposed counterexample to a definition of knowledge, the moral difference between doing and allowing harm, or an intransitive-looking chain of preferences under risk. Be explicit about which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether the cleanest principle requires me to overturn one or more of them.
