During my years working as a physical chemist, I was continually aware of a quiet slippage in the papers and advisory reports my colleagues and I produced: conclusions about what ought to be done often arrived dressed as factual findings, with evaluative choices buried in the selection of measurements, in thresholds for "significance," and in words like "clean," "risk," or "acceptable exposure." Since retiring I have been reading moral philosophy, especially the is-ought distinction and the naturalistic fallacy, and I have come to think of this old slippage as partly an institutional problem: the people who write advisory reports are rewarded for producing actionable conclusions, not for keeping descriptive and normative claims visibly apart.

So allow me to propose a rule and ask you to analyze how it would actually operate. Suppose a national research council required every publicly funded report or policy advisory to be divided into two explicitly labeled sections: one containing only descriptive claims and one containing only evaluative or normative claims. Any practical recommendation would then have to be accompanied by a third "derivation" section that displayed the passage from the stated descriptive premises to the recommendation, with each step tagged as deductive, statistical, or normative premise. A standing panel--statisticians, philosophers, and a rotating group of citizens--would review only this derivation section. If a step tagged deductive or statistical is found to depend on an unstated evaluative premise, the authors must either move that premise into the normative section or withdraw the recommendation. I am not asking whether this is desirable in principle. I want to know where it breaks, where it gets gamed, and what it does to the content of scientific advice in practice. When the evaluative content is squeezed out of the findings, where does it go--into the choice of measurements, the definition of key terms, the selection of studies, the menu of policy options considered? Would the panel itself become a locus of unacknowledged normativity, and would its rulings simply relocate the authority to frame the question? How would readers and policymakers interpret the public display of "normative premise" tags: as honesty, as weakness, as a reason to ignore the recommendation? And are the failure modes fixable--by rotating panel membership, by rewarding clean derivations, by requiring referees to flag missing premises rather than only fallacious inferences--or does the mechanism ultimately presuppose the very distinction it is trying to enforce?
