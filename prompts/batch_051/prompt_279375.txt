I find myself pulled in two directions about rational uncertainty. On one side, I am convinced by the line of thought behind Cox's theorem: if the plausibility of a proposition on given evidence is represented by a real number, and if the plausibility of a negation is fixed by the plausibility of the proposition while the plausibility of a conjunction is fixed by the plausibility of one conjunct and the plausibility of the other given the first, then a few consistency requirements force those plausibilities to obey the ordinary product and sum rules of probability, up to an arbitrary recalibration. That is an elegant result. It seems to show that any coherent scheme of plausible reasoning just is probability theory, and that anyone who reasons with partial beliefs is already committed to the probability axioms. The view has the virtues of definiteness, unity, and a clear decision procedure; I see why it attracts such loyalty.

On the other side, I have an intuition that some states of uncertainty are not naturally shaped like a single number. When I say I have no idea whether a proposition is true, I do not feel that I have selected any particular degree of belief, and I resist being told that a number was there all along waiting to be elicited. With evidence that arrives in fragments of different quality — a stale memory, a possibly biased test, an expert who will not be pinned down — I want to describe my state as irreducibly imprecise, or to say that the plausibility of one proposition and the plausibility of another are simply not comparable rather than that one ranks above the other or they tie. The standard reply, that this is just uncertainty about one's own credence or a refusal to make up one's mind, feels as though it wins the argument by stipulating that every epistemic state must already be a distribution over hypotheses. I cannot tell whether that stipulation is a discovery about rationality or a modelling convenience.

I am not asking for a verdict that simply awards the match to one side. I want you to lay out what the Cox argument really forces and what it leaves open: which of its postulates are rationally mandatory and which are harmless simplifications, and whether real-valuedness and global comparability are among the things one may coherently refuse. Then tell me what it would take for the single-number picture to survive my intuition intact — for the intuition to be exposed as an artefact of confusing a credence with a description of evidence, or of treating a decision rule under ambiguity as if it were a mental state. Tell me equally what it would take for the single-number picture to give way, and what would occupy its place: interval-valued credences, sets of distributions, comparative plausibility orders, or something else. And say whether there is a stable reading on which both sides survive, for instance one that reserves precise probability for decisions with well-defined stakes while allowing the underlying epistemic state to be richer. Please write in continuous prose, in British English, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables; and be explicit about which parts of your answer are robust and which depend on controversial assumptions.
