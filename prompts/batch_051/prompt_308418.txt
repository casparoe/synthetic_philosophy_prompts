I was arguing with a friend who does legal theory, and she kept insisting that in a liberal legal order rights are not just especially heavy weights in a utilitarian calculus—they're trumps, or at least side constraints: when a right conflicts with aggregate welfare, the right is supposed to block the welfare calculation rather than being traded off against it. I pushed back that this is mostly how judges and politicians talk, and that in practice everyone balances, they just do it quietly so the system can keep the rhetoric intact. She says that's an empirical cop-out, and that if I looked at the right places I'd see the side-constraint structure showing up in actual institutional behavior. So I want to know whether this disagreement is even testable, and how.

Suppose we frame the claim as: "this legal system (say the US, or Norway, whichever is easier to test cleanly) treats at least some core rights as genuine side constraints, not as defeasible instruments for promoting welfare." How would you set up the strongest possible test of that claim? I can imagine looking at emergency derogations, torture ticking-bomb cases, property takings and compensation, vaccine mandates, hate speech, or how courts handle rare cases where a right protects someone whose protection clearly lowers aggregate welfare. But I don't want a list of interesting examples—I want the one best test design, with the kind of evidence that would favor the trump/side-constraint reading and the kind that would favor the instrumental reading, and a clear statement of what each result would and would not establish. In particular, how much does the test depend on operationalizing "takes a right as a side constraint" in a way one side of the debate could reasonably reject, and what residue—normative, conceptual, or both—would remain unsettled even if the test came out cleanly one way or the other?
