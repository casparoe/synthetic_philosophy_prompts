I am preparing a seminar for a philosophy of language course on speech acts and performative utterances, and I want to use Arthur Miller's The Crucible as our extended literary case study. The play seems to me to run almost entirely on utterances that change legal, social, and moral standing simply by being said in the right setting: the girls' accusations, the judges' rulings, the confessions, the recantations, and finally the signing of a written confession. But I am unsure how far the standard Austinian framework—felicity conditions, illocutionary force, uptake, and the distinction between misfires and abuses—can be pressed before it begins to distort the play. The girls' accusations are false, yet the court treats them as effective; does Austin's framework say those acts succeed but are insincere abuses, or should they fail because something about the procedure is corrupted from the start? And what, exactly, is John Proctor doing at the end when he is willing to let his spoken words confess but refuses to sign the document that would publish them: is the signed confession a distinct illocutionary act from the spoken one, a change in its perlocutionary effects, or something Austin's categories handle poorly?

What I would like is a reading that moves in both directions. First, read the play with the theory: identify the main performatives in the trial scenes, state the felicity conditions each one presupposes, and show how the play satisfies or violates them. Second, read the theory with the play: point out where Miller's drama exposes something Austin's examples, drawn mostly from ordinary interpersonal ceremonies and promises, do not capture—for instance, the way an institution can assign force to utterances by speakers who lack the appropriate standing, or the moral weight of a durable public record as opposed to a spoken act. End by saying what the play adds to the philosophical debate about performative utterance that an argument alone would miss.
