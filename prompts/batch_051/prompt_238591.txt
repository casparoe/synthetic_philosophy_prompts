A mathematics professor is teaching a transition-to-proofs course, and I have been tracking how she grades one exam question: "Prove that there is no rational number whose square is 2." The intended proof runs as follows. Suppose, for contradiction, that the square root of 2 equals p/q, where p and q are integers with no common factor. Squaring gives p² = 2q², so p² is even. Since the square of an odd integer is odd, p must be even, so write p = 2k. Substituting gives 2q² = (2k)² = 4k², hence q² = 2k², so q² is even, and therefore q is even. But then p and q share a factor of 2, contradicting the assumption that they had no common factor.

Her verdicts on past students:

- Mina reproduced the proof exactly. When asked, she proved the lemma that the square of an odd integer is odd, explained that the "no common factor" assumption is doing real work because it makes "p and q are both even" a contradiction rather than a harmless observation, and then adapted the argument to show that the square root of 3 is irrational by replacing parity with divisibility by 3. Verdict: understands.
- Omar could not reconstruct the proof past the first line, and when asked why one assumes p and q have no common factor, he said he did not know. Verdict: does not understand.
- Priya reproduced every step correctly, verified each inference when prompted, proved the odd-square lemma, and explained the role of the "no common factor" assumption in the original proof; but when asked to adapt the argument to the square root of 3, she could not get started. Verdict: understands.
- Tom reproduced the proof accurately but could not justify the step from "p² is even" to "p is even"; he said he had memorized it as a rule. Verdict: does not understand.
- Yuki wrote the proof without first putting p/q in lowest terms and concluded "p and q are both even"; when asked whether this proved the result, she said yes. Verdict: does not understand.

Next week's student is Dara. She reproduces the proof perfectly and, when asked, verifies every inference, including giving a correct proof of the odd-square lemma. When asked why the argument assumes p and q have no common factor, she says, "So that p and q being even is a contradiction." When the professor asks what the proof would establish without that assumption — whether it would still prove the same result, establish something weaker, or break somewhere else — Dara says, "It wouldn't work," but cannot say anything more specific and cannot repair the argument.

Predict the professor's verdict on Dara. I am less interested in the verdict itself than in the rule it reveals. Lay out the candidate rules about what it is to understand a proof that fit her track record so far; candidates might include: (a) understanding is just accurate reproduction; (b) it requires verifying each step and each invoked lemma; (c) it additionally requires explaining the role each assumption plays in the proof as a whole, including what would go wrong if it were removed; (d) it additionally requires transferring the argument to new cases. Say which of these each of her earlier verdicts rules out, what each surviving rule predicts about Dara, and give your best guess about her verdict with the reasons for it. Finally, specify a further case that would settle the matter if Dara's own case does not.
