I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I co-organize a once-a-month philosophy discussion group at a mid-sized software company. The group is 12–16 people — mostly engineers and product managers, also without formal philosophical training — and we meet for 75 minutes in a conference room. We are planning a session on the line between manipulation and legitimate persuasion, a question that comes up constantly in our work on onboarding flows, retention features, notification design, and A/B testing. I would like you to design a runnable in-person activity for this session, not a lecture and not a discussion guide built around reading.

The activity should be arranged so that participants run into the problem themselves before anyone names the theme. I want it to involve real doing: small teams drafting and defending a short persuasive sequence for a fictional product decision, and a rotating review board that must rule on individual moves under some non-monetary scoring or approval scheme with real stakes in the room. Printouts, index cards, and a whiteboard are fine; no internet and no money. Please provide explicit rules, roles, the goals of each role, a timeline, and the materials needed, including concrete examples of case briefs and move cards.

One constraint matters more than any other. The game must not sort influence techniques into a clean list of legitimate moves (ordinary reasons, logic, transparent disclosure) and manipulative ones (defaults, framing, urgency cues, social proof). I suspect that distinction collapses, because the same technique can be legitimate in one context and manipulative in another. The rules and scoring should therefore force the hard cases — defaults, ordering effects, emotionally valenced framing, selective disclosure, scarcity and social-proof cues — to surface early and get contested, while the easy cases (an outright lie versus a plain argument for a genuine benefit) are disposed of quickly. That way the group has to sit with what makes the middle cases difficult instead of congratulating itself on the easy ones.

I also have a working hunch I want the activity to test rather than state up front. It is roughly this: a move becomes manipulative when the person being influenced could not reconstruct how their decision was changed without losing trust in their own judgment. On this view the defect is not in the technique but in the fact that the influence works by making the target's own reflective capacities unreliable. I do not insist the hunch is right, and I would rather see it pressure-tested. Please design the debrief so that it maps concrete moments from the game onto this idea, and also flags the moments where the idea fails to capture what participants actually objected to.

The main takeaway I want participants to leave with is that the manipulation/persuasion line cannot be read off a list of techniques, but depends on how a technique interacts with a person's ability to notice, reconstruct, and endorse the way their mind was changed. Please build the activity so that takeaway is earned in the game, and save the terminology until the debrief.

Finally, include a note on what will predictably go wrong the first time this is run and how to handle it, and an honest note on which feature of real-world manipulative design a 75-minute conference-room game cannot reproduce and which feature the facilitator will have to supply in the debrief. There should be no lecture up front, and the name of the theme should not be used until after the activity.
