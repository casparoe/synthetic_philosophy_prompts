Consider a dollar auction with the following rules. A one-dollar bill is auctioned to two bidders. Bids must rise by a fixed quarter-dollar increment. When bidding ends, the highest bidder pays their final bid and receives the dollar, while the second-highest bidder also pays their final bid and receives nothing. The two largest bids are therefore both collected.

I built the following argument for a seminar, and I now suspect it proves too much.

(1) Once both players have bid, the player who no longer holds the high bid — call them the trailing bidder — must either raise by one increment or stop.
(2) If the trailing bidder stops, they lose their last bid, whatever it was, for certain.
(3) If they raise instead, the extra amount put at risk is only one increment; and if the other player then stops, the trailing bidder wins the dollar, which either yields a net gain or, later on, sharply cuts the loss they were about to accept.
(4) When one option locks in a loss and the other spends only a little more for a genuine chance to avoid that loss and perhaps end with a gain, the second option is rationally preferable.
(5) Therefore, at every point after both players have bid, the trailing bidder should raise rather than stop.
(6) Each player is trailing in turn, and each will reason the same way; so, so long as both can pay the next increment, neither will stop.
(7) A rational player, foreseeing all of this, will never make the first bid; and a rational player who somehow found themselves already in the auction would be committed to keeping the bidding alive until spending limits, rather than the value of the dollar, bring it to an end.

I can accept that the opening bid may simply be a mistake. But the argument also appears to show that a single trivial bid rationally dooms both players to a runaway escalation, in which the attempt to avoid taking a loss rolls the loss higher and higher with no internal stopping point. That is the part I cannot accept. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? Identify the exact premise or step to reject, explain why it struck me as plausible, and say what rejecting it commits one to. Please answer in American English.
