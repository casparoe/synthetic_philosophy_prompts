Someone in my local policy nerd group floated this: a city fire department adopts a rescue dispatch rule inspired by Taurek's claim that the numbers shouldn't count. Whenever an incident commander has to choose between two groups of victims and can't reach both, they run a lottery that gives each group an equal chance of being rescued, no matter how many people are in it. So one person versus five people is a straight coin flip. The stated rationale is that each person has an equal claim to rescue, and letting the numbers count treats the five as if their claims add up and outweigh the one's.

Treat this as a mechanism, not a moral stance. What does it actually do? Walk me through the incentives for incident commanders (how do they define 'a group,' and when would they rather not invoke the lottery?), for victims and bysanders in an emergency (splitting up, gaming the group boundaries, how they describe things to 911), and for institutions around it (building layouts, insurers, employers). Where does it get gamed first, and are those failure modes fixable without just abandoning the equal-claims idea? And tell me what would change your mind about whichever failure you think is worst, or where your reasoning is most likely to go wrong.
