ok so ive been using a distinction i keep calling 'semantic range' and i want it stress-tested, not flattered. here's the idea: the semantic range of a word is the whole distribution of things it has actually, successfully been used to do—pick out, refuse, index, flatter, whatever. its 'meaning' is not a thing over and above that range; it's just the densest patch of the range, sampled at some particular time. so 'bank' doesn't have two meanings, it has one range with two clumps big enough that we draw circles around them; 'run' doesn't have one meaning plus dozens of distinct senses, it has one range with a dominant clump and lots of satellite uses. polysemy and monosemy are the same phenomenon at different zoom levels, and a dictionary entry is basically a census of recent high-frequency uses with circles drawn around visible clumps. when a word 'changes meaning,' that's not an event that happens to the word, it's the center of mass of its range drifting as the dense patch moves. i know this rhymes with wittgenstein's meaning-as-use, prototype effects, and distributional semantics, so the real question is whether 'range' does any work beyond those. is it a real construct or just meaning-as-use with a histogram taped on? where's the first place it gives the wrong verdict—give me the strongest counterexample (ambiguity, synonymy, semantic change, whatever) and tell me whether the concept dies there or just needs a patch.
