I am a doctoral student in economic history, and one of the things that most frustrates me about my field is the way the origins of industrialization get framed as a two-horse race. The Needham question asks why modern science did not emerge in China despite its earlier technical sophistication, and the Great Divergence debate asks why the Yangzi Delta, which by many measures looked comparable to England in the eighteenth century, did not industrialize first. Recently I have been reading some philosophy of biology on the side, and I cannot shake the sense that these questions have the same logical shape as contrastive questions in evolutionary biology: why did eusociality evolve in some insect lineages but not in others, or why did one lineage radiate while its sister lineage went nowhere. In both fields an actual outcome is set against one or more non-outcomes; in both the foil is chosen after the fact, usually because it looked like a plausible candidate at the time; and in both the explanation seems sensitive to how the contrast class is specified, so that changing the foil changes the causal story.

I would like to know whether this parallel is deep or superficial. On the one hand, it seems deep: both fields face the problem that explaining a contrast presupposes some common causal field, and if England and the Yangzi Delta were not genuinely comparable units evolving within the same kind of process, the question may be ill-posed rather than merely unanswered. On the other hand, it may be superficial: evolutionary biology has a well-defined variational mechanism, natural selection acting on heritable variation in populations, and a reasonably clear notion of what a trait is, whereas economic history deals with intentional agents, institutions, and path-dependent singular events that may not combine into anything like a population-level process.

I would like you to work through the analogy systematically rather than give a yes-or-no verdict. What exactly would have to be true of the historical cases for a contrastive question about them to be as well-posed as a contrastive question in evolutionary biology can be? Does the comparison help diagnose what is conceptually troubled about the Great Divergence debate, or does it mislead by smuggling a population-thinking framework into history? And can you identify something the parallel would predict about how well-posed contrastive questions in history should behave that turns out false, so that I can see where the analogy gives out?
