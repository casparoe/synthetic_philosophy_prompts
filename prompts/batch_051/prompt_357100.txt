Here's one I've been turning over. In the Second Treatise of Government, chapter "Of Prerogative," John Locke writes: "nay, it is fit that the laws themselves should in some cases give way to the executive power, or rather to this fundamental law of nature and government, viz. That as much as may be, all the members of the society are to be preserved: for since many accidents may happen, wherein a strict and rigid observation of the laws may do harm; (as not to pull down an innocent man's house to stop the fire, when the next to it is burning)." Now take Lincoln's suspension of habeas corpus in Maryland in April 1861. Congress wasn't in session; Lincoln acted unilaterally; Taney held in Ex parte Merryman that only Congress could suspend the writ; and Congress passed the Habeas Corpus Suspension Act in 1863, after the fact. Skip the standard constitutional history—I know it. Run the episode through that passage in both directions. What would Locke say Lincoln, Taney, and the later Congress each should have done? Does the fire example actually map onto detaining suspected saboteurs, or does it pull in a different direction? A little later in the chapter Locke says that prerogative used for the public good never gets questioned—so what do we make of the fact that this one was challenged immediately? Then say where his theory is forced or silent when applied to the case, and whether the episode ends up counting for or against the idea that law sometimes has to give way.
