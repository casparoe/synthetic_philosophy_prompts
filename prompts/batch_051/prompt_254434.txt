In the Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, right after introducing the universal law formula, Kant has a footnote in the second section where he more or less dismisses the golden rule as trivial. He says it can be derived from his principle only with various limitations, and that it cannot serve as a universal law because it supplies the basis neither for duties to oneself, nor for duties of love to others, nor for strict duties to others. I've read that passage a bunch of times, and I need a deeper explanation of what he means by that claim. Or is it that the rule is structurally incapable of grounding obligations where no reciprocal second-person relation is in view, like duties to oneself? And why exactly does he think a criminal could appeal to the golden rule against a judge? I'm particularly interested in whether the standard fixes actually solve the conceptual problem. If you build in a notion of what a rational or reasonable person would want, or universalize over preferences, or add something like Scanlon's justifiability condition, do those fixes address Kant's three gaps or just quietly import his universal law formula? Please give an intuitive explanation of the underlying conceptual issue, not just a summary of the footnote. Also, please write your answer in American English.
