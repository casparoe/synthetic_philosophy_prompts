I'm drafting a paper on whether Harsanyi's aggregation theorem earns its reputation as a derivation of utilitarianism from individually rational preferences plus a Pareto condition. In the course of reading I've collected six objections, and they strike me as cutting at very different depths, but I keep going back and forth about which ones genuinely threaten the theorem and which are repairable or misdirected. I'd like you to rank them from most damaging to least damaging, considered specifically as objections to the claim that the theorem establishes utilitarianism on weak premises. If two seem tied, tie them, but otherwise give a strict ordering. For each, add a sentence or two explaining what it actually shows and why it lands where it does.

Here are the six:

(1) Arbitrary weights. The theorem delivers only that social welfare is some weighted sum of individual von Neumann–Morgenstern utilities. Since each individual's utility function is unique only up to a positive affine transformation, the relative weights can be shifted by rescaling individual utility functions; nothing in the theorem fixes them. It therefore stops short of selecting a social welfare function and leaves the substantive interpersonal comparisons unearned.

(2) Questionable social independence. The proof requires society's preferences over lotteries to obey the same independence axiom that is rational for a single agent. A society concerned with fairness in outcomes may refuse: it might be indifferent between an indivisible benefit going to person A and its going to person B, yet strictly prefer a fair lottery over either certainty, which independence rules out.

(3) Wrong input. The utilities being aggregated are von Neumann–Morgenstern risk preferences, not a measure of how well each person's life goes. Risk attitudes can reflect caution, fear, or error, which looks like the wrong object for a moral theory to maximize.

(4) Weak Pareto. The Pareto condition used is deliberately thin—it merely preserves unanimous indifference and unanimous weak preference—which is what gives the theorem its sweep. One might worry, though, that the ethical weight has been quietly shifted into the requirement that social preferences satisfy the expected utility axioms, so the theorem isn't deriving utilitarianism from Pareto at all but from a contestable formal constraint.

(5) Veil detour. To turn the social utility function into an impartial ethical judgment, the aggregation theorem is usually supplemented by an observer behind a veil of ignorance with equal chance of being any member of the population. That equal-probability assumption is an additional egalitarian premise, not something the theorem itself delivers.

(6) Fixed population. The whole setup presupposes a fixed list of individuals with well-defined utility functions and says nothing about adding new people. Since many of the distinctive uses of utilitarianism—population policy, extinction risk, obligations to future generations—are cases where the set of people changes across alternatives, the theorem cannot ground utilitarianism where it matters most.

After the ranking, tell me whether any of these objections are better understood as objections to an interpretation of the theorem rather than to the theorem itself, and whether two or more of them work in combination even if each alone is weaker than it looks.
