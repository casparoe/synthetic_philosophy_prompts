I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, so I may be misusing these labels. I have been trying to understand the debate between mathematical Platonism and nominalism, and I keep getting stuck on what looks like a practical consequence for my own field. In software engineering, we treat correctness as objective: an algorithm either computes the function it is supposed to compute, or it fails to. A primality-testing routine is simply wrong if it reports 2 as composite, and no amount of convention, consensus, or corporate policy could make it right. That seems to commit me to a realm of mathematical facts—numbers, properties like primality, and functions—that exists independently of any implementation or human judgment. If the nominalist denies that such objects exist, then in what sense can a correct program be objectively distinct from an incorrect one, rather than merely correct-relative-to-our-current-conventions? How do nominalist philosophers of mathematics account for the objectivity of algorithmic correctness without quietly reintroducing abstract objects?
