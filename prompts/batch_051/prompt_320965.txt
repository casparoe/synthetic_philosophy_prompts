I recently joined the four-person review board of a small quantitative trading firm that runs a self-modifying system. Each evening the system generates small patches to its own code—a change to how it featurizes market data, a faster parser for an exchange feed, occasionally an adjustment to the standing criteria by which it decides whether a proposed patch is worth trying. Before any patch is deployed it must pass through an automated gatekeeper: a formal checker that proves specified safety properties about the patch against an abstract model of the system. The problem is that the gatekeeper is itself part of the system, so nothing structural prevents a patch from touching the gatekeeper's own code, the specification it checks against, or the proof rules it relies on. Our logician keeps calling this the Löbian obstacle: roughly, a system cannot prove the soundness of the very reasoning it would use to approve a change that could alter that reasoning, without already trusting itself. Last quarter a patch to the gatekeeper's internal ranking function shipped, and in the audit that followed we could not fully reconstruct what the gatekeeper had effectively assumed about itself in approving it. The board meets daily, the system trades our own capital and clients' money, nobody can hand-audit every line of a patch, and the person who signs off is personally accountable. As the newest member, I have been asked to write the operating policy.

Please give me a decision procedure the board can actually run for deciding, for each proposed patch, whether the automated gatekeeper may evaluate and approve it, whether it must be escalated for full human verification, or whether it must be rejected outright. The procedure should be an ordered list of questions with explicit stop rules, and it should say what counts as a sufficient answer to each, including what to do when a question cannot be answered to the required standard in the half hour before the board must rule. It should flag every step that is really a judgment call wearing the clothes of a check. Then apply the procedure to three patches: one to the data pipeline that formally cannot affect the gatekeeper or the specification; one that rewrites the proof checker's own parser while allegedly preserving exactly what it proves; and one that loosens the acceptance criteria so the system may explore a wider class of strategies at lower confidence. I want at least one case where the procedure gives the board an answer it will not like, and an honest statement of what the procedure cannot guarantee.
