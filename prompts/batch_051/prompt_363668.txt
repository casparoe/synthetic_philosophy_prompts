Build me a thought experiment to a spec. I'm playing with a simplified Abelardian view of sin: all the guilt lives in the act of consent — the moment you say yes, inwardly, to doing something you take to be wrong — and the outward deed, and even the initial impulse or the pleasure you take in the thought, add nothing to the sin's seriousness. Contrast that with a plain modern act-plus-intent view: culpability attaches mostly to the wrongful deed, and a completed act is, other things equal, worse than an attempt, which is worse than a mere intention, though intent can still aggravate within a category. Treat both views as stipulations, not as something you need to get right about the historical Abelard.

I want a single realistic scenario with two people, call them A an B. Requirements:
- A actually goes through with a wrongful act. B consents to a very similar wrongful act but is stopped before anything outward happens.
- Ordinary-life stuff, not murder or anything monstrous, no clergy, no weird knowledge asymmetry.
- Both are competent adults who know the acts are wrong.
- The scenario should be one paragraph.

The case has to make these two verdicts come out:
1. On the Abelardian criterion, B is more culpable than A.
2. On the act-plus-intent criterion, A is more culpable than B.

After the scenario, walk through the verdicts: spell out exactly what each person consented to and did, why Abelard's criterion ranks them B over A, and why the act-plus-intent view ranks them A over B. Tell me which feature of the scenario is doing the real work, and whether removing it collapses the case into the boring one where Abelard treats the doer and the mere-consenter as equally guilty. Finish with the best objection a defender of the act-plus-intent view could make to the Abelardian verdict.
