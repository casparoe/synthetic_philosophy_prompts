As someone who has spent years reading rationalist-blog treatments of decision theory, I keep running into the same move: someone invokes a Dutch book or representation-theorem argument to show that any coherent agent must maximize expected utility. The move is usually made quickly, as if the hard work is already settled. I think there is still a live question underneath it, and I want to turn it into a one-semester philosophy decision theory paper proposal.

Write a proposal for a roughly 6,000-word term paper on whether coherence arguments for expected utility maximization extend to agents who are uncertain about the space of possible outcomes or about the stability of their own future preferences.

The standard arguments, from Dutch books to money pumps to Savage-style representation theorems, treat the set of acts and outcomes as fixed and known to the agent. They show that violating certain structural axioms exposes the agent to a sure loss, or that any preference ordering satisfying the axioms can be represented as expected utility maximization relative to some probability and utility function. But in realistic reasoning, an agent may not know in advance what the full set of possible outcomes is, and she may also be unsure whether the preferences she has now will survive reflection or new evidence. I suspect that in such settings the relevant notion of coherence is not a one-shot constraint on a static preference ordering but a constraint on the agent’s policy for revising preferences and acquiring information over time.

The proposal should do the following: state this as a precise question, for example whether coherence arguments can be reformulated for agents with incomplete beliefs about outcome spaces, and what they would then show about sequential preference revision; explain why the existing arguments leave this open rather than settling it; propose a method for making progress, such as defining a generalized Dutch book that allows bets on the agent’s own future decisions, or examining which axioms of the standard representation theorems fail once the outcome space is open-ended; say what a positive result would look like, for example a coherence theorem for sequential preference revision, or a concrete counterexample showing that the standard arguments are incomplete; and say what would count as failure, such as the problem dissolving into a bookkeeping issue or turning out to have no tractable formalization. The paper should engage with the conceptual issues rather than the technical details of proving representation theorems, but it should be precise enough that a formal follow-up would be possible.
