I cover science and ideas for a living, which mostly means listening to very sure people disagree with each other and then deciding which of them knows what they're talking about. But the other week I genuinely got stuck.

I was reporting a piece about a criminal case in which a parent was convicted after their infant starved to death; the parent had stopped feeding the child for days. After the interview, two researchers I'd asked to help me think it through started arguing in the hallway. One is a philosopher who works on causation; the other is a sociologist who studies how legal systems talk about responsibility. I took notes, and thier back-and-forth went something like this.

Philosopher: The conviction doesn't bother me, because the parent caused the death as surely as if they'd done something active. If they'd fed the kid, the kid would have lived. That's the standard counterfactual test for causation, and the absence of food passes it. Saying they "just didn't do anything" misses everything: withholding food from a dependent is doing something.

Sociologist: But that test has no brakes. If "if you'd done X, Y wouldn't have happened" makes you a cause, then anyone who didn't send their last paycheck to a famine charity caused those deaths, and everyone who didn't skip a coffee to buy mosquito nets caused malaria deaths. Everyone becomes a cause of everything. As a single criterion, that's useless.

Philosopher: It's promiscuous, not useless. Maybe all those people are, in a thin causal sense, causes of those deaths. What's absurd is treating them all as equally blameworthy. Blame depends on duty, proximity, special relationships — and none of those are causal concepts. So the counterfactual test is doing its job; people keep smuggling moral judgments into it and then complaining that it looks moral.

Sociologist: But the smuggling happens before you identify the omission at all. The charity donor's not-sending-money counts as an omission only against a background idea of what they should have done. No norm, no omission, no thing to plug into the counterfactual. So in omission cases "cause" isn't a neutral factual relation; it's shorthand for a normative verdict about which absences stand out.

Philosopher: That's true of every causal attribution. When we say the spark caused the forest fire, we ignore the oxygen and the dry grass, because the spark is the abnormal thing against our normal background. Actions are no less interest-relative; you've just shown why we're selective, not that omissions are a special case.

Sociologist: There's still a real asymmetry. With the spark, once you've picked it out, there's an actual event that set a physical process running to the fire. With the parent, there's no event and no physical process connecting them to the death. The child died of physiological starvation; the parent merely didn't interrupt a process they didn't start. Calling them a cause says they failed a duty, not that they made something happen.

Philosopher: Then you end up committed to saying the parent didn't cause the child's death, but is morally just as bad as if they'd poisoned the child. Can you really hold those two thoughts together?

Sociologist: Easily. They're just as culpable — for allowing a death they had a duty to prevent, not for causing it. The law can convict without a cooked-up theory of causation.

Philosopher: But "allowing" and "preventing" are causal notions themselves. Preventing is causing something not to happen. You haven't escaped causation; you've just renamed it.

That's where they stalled. I had to file my story and I still can't tell you whose overall account is better. Walk me through the exchange move by move: which points are actually right, which are wrong, and what are they both missing? And if you had to pick, which of them is more right about whether omissions can be causes?
