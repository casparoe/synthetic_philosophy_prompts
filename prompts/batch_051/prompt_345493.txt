I've got an odd favour to ask. I'm helping a friend who teaches a legal philosophy course put together a problem set on sovereign immunity — the old chestnut that the king can do no wrong, and whether that means the state can't commit a legal wrong or just can't be haled into court for one. He's invented a fictional jurisdiction (let's call it the Commonwealth) whose supreme court has produced a small string of sovereign immunity precedents, and he wants students to reconstruct the rule the court is actually following and then apply it to a new dispute. I'm not convinced the precedents hang together as cleanly as he thinks, so I want to stress-test the exercise before he hands it out.

Here are the four cases the students get. In Northgate Hospital, the public hospital authority's charter said it could "sue and be sued," a patient sued over a sponge left in after surgery, and the court allowed it, saying the authority operated like a private hospital and the charter itself contemplated litigation. In State Police Pursuit, a trooper on an emergency call ran a red light and hit a motorist; the suit was dismissed because policing is a core governmental function and no statute waived immunity. In Flood Control District, a dam failure drowned a farmer's fields; the district charged some fees, but its enabling act said nothing about being sued, and the court dismissed, reasoning that charging fees for a public flood-control service doesn't make the state a commercial actor. In Public Marina, the Port Development Corporation ran a pleasure-boat marina, its statute said it "may contract, sue, and be sued," and a boater hurt by a collapsed dock was allowed to sue; the court pointed to both the clause and the obviously commercial character of a marina.

The new case he wants them to decide: the Commonwealth Broadband Authority sells rural broadband at market rates and competes directly with private internet providers; its enabling act allows it to set prices and enter contracts but says nothing about litigation. A customer's house burns down after one of its linemen does sloppy wiring, and she sues. What should the court decide if it wants to be consistent with these precedents? Which of the four old cases is the closest analog, and which candidate principle does it commit the Commonwealth to? And if the past cases genuinely underdetermine the answer, I'd like the students to see that too: what principle would the court be adopting with each possible ruling, and what further case would cleanly separate those two principles?
