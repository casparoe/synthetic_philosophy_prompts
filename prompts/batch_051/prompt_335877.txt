I am a novelist, and I have drifted into a view I would now defend if pressed. It is this: the possible and the probable are not two different kinds of judgment, but two points on a single scale. Both are judgments about the same underlying thing — a setup, plus an assignment of weight over its outcomes. To call an event possible is to say that the setup gives it nonzero weight; to call it probable is to say the setup gives it weight above a threshold that we usually leave unstated. On this view, no possibility outruns probability: any modal claim can in principle be rewritten as a probabilistic claim relative to an explicit or implicit setup, and any apparent categorical line between "could" and "likely" is the residue of thresholds, ignorance, or an unarticulated setup. I take the same view into my fiction: a plot event is possible in a story exactly to the degree that a competent reader's running model of the story-world assigns it nonzero weight, and a twist feels impossible when it violates the setup the reader has inferred, not when it violates some separate modal rule.

In my editing I keep noticing something that makes the view feel right and also seems to test it. I call it silent re-thresholding. When I revise a scene by adding an ordinary detail — a character's commute, the weather, a delay — I do not merely change how likely an outcome is; I can change whether a reader will call the outcome possible at all, and the reader cannot say where the threshold was. The threshold floats as the setup thickens. That made me suspect that "possible" is merely low probability plus an unstated threshold, "probable" is high probability plus an unstated threshold, and the threshold itself does no independent modal work.
