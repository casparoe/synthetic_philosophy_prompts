You are a political philosopher who works on distributive justice, especially Nozick's entitlement theory. I'm drafting a seminar handout on the Wilt Chamberlain argument and need one more entry for the objections section. As I present the argument, you begin from any distribution D1 you regard as just. Wilt Chamberlain, a hugely popular basketball player, signs a contract giving him a 25-cent surcharge on each home-game ticket. A million fans cheerfully buy tickets and pay the surcharge; Wilt ends up $250,000 richer, and the resulting D2 is highly unequal. Nozick concludes that D2 must also be just, because each fan was entitled to spend 25 cents and Wilt was entitled to his labor; therefore no "patterned" principle of justice (equal shares, distribution by merit or need) can be maintained without forbidding exchanges people want to make.

The objections I already have are these:

(1) The initial-holdings objection: actual holdings are tainted by past force and fraud, so the argument never reaches real-world inequality but only an idealized baseline.

(2) The voluntariness objection: the fans' choices are shaped by background inequality and by Wilt's market position, so calling the transfers "free" in a justice-relevant sense presupposes what has to be shown.

(3) The self-ownership objection, associated with G.A. Cohen: the argument appears to rest on liberty, but the load-bearing premise is full self-ownership of one's talents and labor; deny that, and the inference from voluntary exchange to a just outcome no longer goes through.

(4) The post-hoc taxation objection: a patterned principle need not ban the small transfers; it can allow them and then tax to restore or maintain the pattern, so the argument at most shows that patterns conflict with the freedom to keep every cent of an exchange, not with liberty straightforwardly.

(5) The difference-principle objection: the example assumes the only pattern worth considering is strict equality at every moment; a patterned principle such as Rawls's difference principle could declare D2 just if the prospect of such gains improves the position of the worst off, so Nozick has not refuted patterned principles as a class.

Give me one more objection to the Wilt Chamberlain argument that is not on this list and is not a restatement, special case, or combination of these five. It should attack a premise or inference step that the five leave untouched. Start by brainstorming several candidate objections and discarding the ones that are variants of the five; then present your best candidate, and then say honestly whether it succeeds against Nozick's actual stated conclusion or only against an uncharitable version of the argument.
