I'm in an epistemology seminar on Nozick's tracking theory, and I've noticed the view has leaked into how I actually run my own intellectual life. The version we read says knowledge requires sensitivity: roughly, you don't know that p if you would still have believed p even if p had been false. That already blocks closure under known entailment—you can know you have hands but not know you're not a handless brain in a vat, because the hand-belief tracks the truth while the not-a-brain-in-a-vat belief doesn't. Fine as an abstract claim. But I've started turning this into a personal policy: in discussion I won't grant that a valid deduction from something I know automatically gives me knowledge of the conclusion, and I keep asserting ordinary knowledge claims while denying their obvious anti-skeptical entailments. Last week my study partner accused me of running an "epistemic diet" and said I only wheel out the sensitivity requirement against conclusions I already don't want, which stung because it's partly true.

What I'd like from you is an honest policy evaluation, not a survey of the literature. Is this habit defensible, or have I turned a controversial theory of knowledge into a license to be arbitrary? Can the policy be restated so I keep whatever is good in it—say, a healthy resistance to letting deduction do all my epistemic work—without the weirdness and the selective skepticism? I want you to actually commit: either defend a version of my rule, or tell me to drop it and say what I should do instead, with the strongest case against yourself acknowledged.
