One of my courses assigned a general-audience book on the philosophy of language, and the chapter on interpretation closes with a summary of Davidson that I want to check before I rely on it in an essay. The summary, paraphrased rather than quoted, runs as follows:

Davidson's radical interpretation is an extension of Quine's radical translation. A radical interpreter faces a speaker of an unknown language and must work out what her sentences mean using only what she says, when she says it, and what is publicly going on around her. Since belief and meaning cannot be fixed independently, Davidson's answer is the principle of charity, which says the interpreter must assume the speaker's beliefs are mostly true and mostly agree with the interpreter's own. The procedure is to maximize agreement: when a speaker says something as snow is falling, translate it as "snow is falling" unless that attribution would conflict with too many others. On this basis the interpreter can construct, for any speaker, a Tarski-style truth theory whose T-sentences have the form "S is true if and only if p"; because p gives the sentence's truth conditions, it gives the sentence's meaning. A familiar objection is that this writes real disagreement out of the picture: if beliefs can only be attributed on the assumption of agreement, the interpreter could never discover that the speaker has deeply false beliefs, and Davidson's position collapses into saying that divergent outlooks are just different languages.

How fair is this as an account of radical interpretation?
