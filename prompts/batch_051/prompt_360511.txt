I recently listened to an introductory philosophy lecture in which the instructor summarized Descartes' cogito in a way that struck me as both elegant and possibly too strong. As I remember the summary: Descartes starts by doubting the senses, the existence of the body, and even mathematical certainties under the hypothesis of an evil demon. But he cannot doubt that he is thinking, because the act of doubting is itself a form of thinking. From this he concludes that he is a thinking substance—an immaterial mind whose essential nature is thought—and that this conclusion is known with certainty on its own, before any proof of God or the external world. Therefore, the cogito alone proves that I exist as a nonphysical soul.

I do not know enough Cartesian scholarship to tell where the summary is fair and where it has compressed or overreached. When I read part of the Second Meditation, Descartes seemed to settle for the weaker claim that he is a thing that thinks, and he explicitly postpones whether this thinking thing might be identical with something bodily or material. But perhaps I am missing an implicit argument from thought to substance.

Please go through the summary sentence by sentence and say what a careful Cartesian would accept, what is a common simplification, and what is a genuine misreading. In particular, address whether the move from the certainty that thinking is occurring to the conclusion that I am an immaterial thinking substance is legitimate at that point in the Meditations, whether the phrase thinking substance smuggles in assumptions Descartes has not yet earned, and what the cogito does and does not establish before the later Meditations.
