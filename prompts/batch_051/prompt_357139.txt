I have been defending a particular view about how consciousness and happiness combine in the value of a mental state: the value of an experience is the product of its degree of consciousness and its amount of happiness, with suffering treated as negative happiness. My standard defence is an analogy. Consciousness, I say, is like the volume knob on a stereo, and happiness is like the music coming through the speakers. With the knob at zero there is silence no matter how fine the music is; turning the knob up does not add music of its own, it only amplifies or dampens whatever is there. So consciousness contributes nothing by itself, and the welfare of a being with no consciousness is exactly zero even if we can measure happiness-like responses in it. I lean on this analogy to license three claims: a barely conscious creature's mild pleasure counts for almost nothing; a fully conscious creature's pain counts for a great deal; and no amount of mood-enhancing drug applied to a non-conscious system creates welfare. Audit the analogy for me. Give the table of correspondences between the stereo and the value theory, identify where the mapping breaks, separate the disanalogies that matter for those three conclusions from the ones that do not, and say whether the multiplicative formula value equals consciousness times happiness survives once the disanalogies are on the table or whether the analogy was carrying the conclusion. Finally, say how confident you are in your overall assessment and single out the point you are least sure about.
