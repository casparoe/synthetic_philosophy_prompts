I'm auditing a course on vagueness and I keep getting stuck comparing epistemicism and supervaluationism, so maybe you can help me think it through. The standard contrast I've got is this: the epistemicist says that for a sorites series of color patches running from clearly red to clearly orange, there is a sharp last red patch, but we're constitutively unable to know where it is; the supervaluationist says there is no single sharp boundary until you pick an admissible precisification of "red," and a sentence like "patch 47 is red" is neither true nor false if the precisifications disagree. But the part that doesn't get enough attention is higher-order vagueness. You get patches that aren't definitely red and aren't definitely not-red, and then patches that aren't even definitely definitely red, and so on. Which of the two theories has the less awkward story there? Does the supervaluationist end up needing a sharply bounded set of admissible precisifications, or a sharply bounded class of "definitely" iterations, which would be doing the very thing they criticized? And is the epistemicist's claim that we're simply ignorant of a hierarchy of higher-order boundaries any less mysterious? I'd like a direct comparison on this point, and then a verdict on which theory you'd defend if higher-order vagueness is the main target.
