A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about whether there is any rigorous sense in which moving first is an advantage. He kept reaching for the strategy-stealing argument used for Hex. Suppose the second player had a winning strategy. Then the first player could make an arbitrary opening move and afterward simply follow that supposed winning strategy herself; if the strategy ever called for playing the cell already occupied by her opening stone, she would play another arbitrary move and treat that stone as the new opening move. Since an extra stone of one's own color can never hurt in Hex, and since Hex has no draws, the first player would win, contradicting the assumption that the second player had a winning strategy; therefore the first player has one. As I understand it, the argument rests on perfect play in the sense of complete strategies that guarantee an outcome, absence of draws, symmetry between the players, and the monotonicity property that extra moves never hurt. The proof is also nonconstructive: it never identifies which opening move or which strategy wins.

The thing I cannot stop turning over is whether this argument survives the shift to how actual players and systems now play games. Chess and Go are far from solved; AlphaZero-style systems evaluate positions with a learned value function and limited search rather than constructing or following a game-theoretic winning strategy, and even in Hex the known existence of a first-player win does not hand any actual player the winning strategy. Does strategy-stealing still license first-mover advantage claims in that setting, or does it collapse into a purely existence result with no bearing on real play?
