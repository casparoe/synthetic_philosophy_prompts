I am a retired physical scientist, and I have only recently found my way into the literature on the ethics of armed intervention. I have been trying to discover whether my judgments about particular cases actually hang together, and I would like you to do for me what I used to ask my students to do with a scatter of data: find the curve that best fits it, and tell me which points are outliers.

Here are my verdicts, in the plainest form I can manage.

Kosovo, 1999. NATO conducted an air campaign against Serbia to stop Milošević's campaign of ethnic cleansing in Kosovo, without Security Council authorization. I think this was the right thing to do. I concede it was unlawful as a matter of international procedure, but I do not think that changes the verdict.

Iraq, 2003. The United States and Britain invaded Iraq to disarm it and remove Saddam Hussein. I think the war was unjustified. There was no ongoing atrocity to halt, the humanitarian rationale was supplied after the fact, and the result was disastrous. The absence of Security Council authorization was not even the main problem.

Libya, 2011. The Security Council authorized the protection of civilians, and NATO's initial strikes stopped Gaddafi's forces from taking Benghazi. I endorsed that phase. I now think the later phase—arming the opposition, bombing regime forces, and prosecuting the war until Gaddafi was killed—was a mistake. The fault was not the intention but the outcome: it traded a dictator for a failed state and a long civil war. What I cannot do is explain why the later phase was wrong while Kosovo remains right, since in Kosovo the protection of civilians also ended only when the regime gave way.

Rwanda, 1994. The international community stood by during the genocide. I consider this a plain failure. A rescue operation would have been justified, and I would not have insisted on Security Council authorization before acting.

Sierra Leone, 2000. British forces intervened with the consent of the government, prevented Freetown from falling to the RUF, and gave the UN mission room to regroup. I think this was justified, and it is the cleanest of my cases.

The rule I keep reaching for is this. External military force may be used across borders to stop an ongoing or imminent atrocity; the operation must do no more than that task requires; and the consent of the recognized government or Security Council authorization counts in favor but is not an absolute prerequisite. That rule gets Rwanda, Sierra Leone, and the first phase of Libya right, and it condemns Iraq. But it seems to condemn the later phase of Libya and yet not Kosovo, which cannot be right if Kosovo and the later phase of Libya are, in relevant respects, the same case. I have been trying to distinguish them by authorization, consent, prospects of success, or the scale of the threat, and none of these candidate distinctions feels as though it does the moral work.

Two further cases are testing me. The first is Darfur: at the height of the killing, roughly 2003 to 2005, a serious intervention to protect civilians would probably have required sustained force against the Sudanese government, and none came. My instinct is that non-intervention was wrong, but I cannot tell whether the intervention I would have wanted would have ended as Kosovo or as late Libya. The second is hypothetical. Suppose a regime is on the brink of massacring a minority; the only coalition able to prevent it has no Security Council authorization and no prospect of local consent; and stopping the killing requires removing the leadership, but removal will probably leave a power vacuum. Would your principle authorize that intervention? Does the vacuum retroactively make it the wrong decision?

I would like the following from you. First, state the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that best captures the pattern in my verdicts, with the clause each case motivates. Be precise about the conditions under which force is permissible. Then say which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether the better repair is to modify the principle or overturn the verdict. I would rather overturn a verdict than accept a principle fabricated solely to save every point. Then apply the account to the Darfur case and to my hypothetical, and to any sixth real or constructed case you regard as a useful test that I have not named.

After your analysis, please supply a JSON object with the following fields:

- "principle_or_candidates": a string or array of strings stating each candidate principle in one or two sentences.
- "status_of_my_tentative_rule": one of "retained", "repaired", or "replaced".
- "status_note": a one-sentence explanation of that status.
- "clauses": an array of objects, each with "clause" (the condition or exception) and "motivating_case" (the case that forces it).
- "case_verdicts": an array of objects, each with "case", "my_verdict", "principles_verdict", and "preserved" (a boolean indicating whether my original verdict is kept).
- "verdicts_overturned": an array of cases in which my verdict should be given up.
- "further_applications": an array of objects, each with "case" (for example Darfur, the hypothetical, or your own test case), "verdict", and "reason".
- "remaining_tension": a string stating the strongest unresolved tension in the resulting account.
