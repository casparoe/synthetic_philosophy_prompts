I am trying to anticipate a colleague's judgments about when it is legitimate to describe pre-modern mathematics and physics in modern terms. Her record so far is as follows.

She approves of rewriting a Greek exhaustion proof in modern algebraic notation, provided the algebraic steps mirror the original reasoning and the result is presented as a translation. She approves of the sentence 'Kepler's laws were an indispensable step toward Newton's celestial mechanics,' but rejects 'Kepler discovered universal gravitation.' She rejects 'Aristotle had a concept of inertia,' and she regards 'Aristotle denied Newton's first law' as equally anachronistic. She accepts that Newton's three laws of motion can be restated in modern vector notation without distortion, but rejects the claim that Newton proved the principle of conservation of momentum in its modern form. She rejects both 'phlogiston chemists had a concept of oxygen' and 'the ancient atomists had a concept of the chemical element,' while accepting that phlogiston theory was a rational and empirically responsive research programme. She accepts 'Galileo established that freely falling bodies accelerate uniformly,' but rejects 'Galileo possessed the modern concept of kinetic energy.'

The next case I expect her to be asked about is whether to accept or reject this sentence: 'Descartes's principle of the conservation of quantity of motion — quantity of motion being measured by size times speed — is an early, scalar version of the law of conservation of momentum.' I want to know how she should rule if she rules consistently with her existing record, not merely what you think about Descartes. Which of her earlier verdicts is the closest analog? What two or three distinct rules about anachronism fit her record equally well, what does each predict about the Descartes sentence, and which rule gives the best account of her record as a whole? Finally, specify one further case that would most sharply separate those rules, in case I need to test her with it.
