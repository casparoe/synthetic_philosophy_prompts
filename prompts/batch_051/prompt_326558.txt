I am giving a departmental seminar next term on the ethics of blame, and I want a Q&A prep sheet tailored to the particular room I will be in.

The thesis I am defending is this: hypocrisy does not remove a person's standing to blame in the sense of their permission to judge the wrongdoer; it removes only their standing to call the wrongdoer to account. I distinguish verdictive blame—a true or false judgment that an act was wrong—from hortatory blame—a demand that the wrongdoer answer for it. Hypocrisy, I claim, defeats only hortatory standing, and it does so not by changing anything about the blamer's rights but by changing the target's normative situation: the target may reasonably set the demand aside, though that licenses no doubt about the verdict itself.

My argument, in outline: (1) blame in its fullest form is a second-personal claim on the wrongdoer, not a bare negative evaluation; (2) the legitimacy of that claim depends on whether the blamer's own conduct repudiates the norm she invokes; (3) this is a condition on the uptake of the demand rather than on the truth of the judgment; so (4) a hypocrite can blame truly but not authoritatively. The moves I expect to be pressed on are the inference from (2) to (3), and the charge that my view lets wrongdoers deflect criticism by pointing at the messenger.

The audience is a philosophy department of about fifteen, dominated by moral psychologists working on the reactive attitudes; two members work in philosophy of language on assertion and presupposition, and one political philosopher works on blame as a mechanism of social norm enforcement. No one specializes in the standing-to-blame literature, though several teach it in surveys. They will be sympathetic to the thought that blaming is a norm-governed practice; I expect them to resist treating blame as a speech act, and to worry about compromised blamers whose blame is socially valuable—repeat offenders who recognize an offense from the inside, whistleblowers, activists.

Please draft the prep sheet: the eight or so objections I will actually get from this audience, not the objections an online debate would produce, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way a member of this department would put it. For each, give a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, say whether I should concede or hold firm, and mark the place where the reply is softest. End with the question I should be most afraid someone will ask, and an honest note on why I do not yet have a good answer to it.
