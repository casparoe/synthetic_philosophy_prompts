He concentrated on one virtue per week, cycling through the thirteen over thirteen weeks, with four such courses planned for each year. In his account, order gave him the most trouble, and he eventually gave up the attempt to make himself perfectly orderly, letting himself off with an anecdote about a man who decides he prefers a speckled axe to one polished free of every flaw. Later, after a Quaker friend told him he was generally thought proud, Franklin added humility to the list. His final verdict was that he never achieved perfection but judged that the attempt had made him better and happier than he would otherwise have been.

On this model, improving one's character is a matter of (a) specifying checkable failure conditions, (b) keeping a running record of violations, and (c) periodically reviewing that record so the information feeds back into future conduct, the way a defect log works in engineering. The mechanism of change is information, not habituation.
