Take Newton's bucket, but add this wrinkle. The water has settled into spinning at some angular speed ω and it's climbing the sides like normal. Now surround the whole setup with two enormous rings of matter, equal in mass and radius, both centered on the bucket's axis, one just above the plane the water rotates in and one just below it. Forget their gravity—their only job is to stand in for the rest of the universe's matter, the stuff a relationist would compare the water against. Ring A co-rotates with the water, so their relative rotation is zero. Ring B spins just as fast in the opposite direction, so relative to the water it's going at 2ω. Run the substantivalist analysis and the relationist analysis of this exact case. The substantivalist presumably says the surface is still concave, because the water still has absolute rotation ω and the rings are irrelevant. But what should the relationist say? The notion of rotation relative to the rest of the universe doesn't pick out one quantity anymore, since the surrounding matter is split into two camps with opposite spins. If the relationist answers by averaging the rings' motions, the pair's mean rotation is zero and the water comes out concave at ω after all—but where does that averaging rule come from, and doesn't it quietly smuggle back in a privileged standard of rest that relationism was supposed to get rid of? Or is there a genuinely relational reading that gives a different result? I want the two analyses side by side, with attention to exactly what extra machinery relationism has to posit to get a determinate prediction at all.
