I'm writing a paper for a course on the moral psychology of politics, and I keep coming back to Occupy Wall Street, maybe because I was in college then and remember being both moved by it and slightly put of by it. The obvious way to read it is as a story about economic inequality, but I want to run it through a more specific distinction from Rawls instead. As I understand him, Rawls treats envy as a vice — the pain we feel at someone else's advantage, where we'd rather bring them down than raise ourselves up — but he says envy can be excusable when inequalities get so deep that they start to undermine people's self-respect. Resentment, by contrast, is a properly moral emotion: it responds to someone breaking the rules that everyone is supposed to play by. Those feel like very different diagnoses of what was going on in Zuccotti Park, and I want to know which one does more work.

Analyze the Occupy movement through that Rawlsian distinction. Which features — the 99 percent framing, the anger at the bank bailouts, the focus on Citizens United and money in politics, the refusal to issue a single list of demands — look like excusable envy, and which look like resentment at broken rules, and where would the distinction say the anger was justified and where was it just envy? Where does the distinction have to be stretched or where does it go silent? And then run it in the other direction: does Occupy show that the envy/resentment split is doing useful work in separating sour grapes from a genuine sense of injustice, or does it show that in real political life the two are too tangled to keep apart? I'm less interested in a verdict on the movement itself than in a careful mapping, including the places where Rawls's categories just don't fit.
