At a dinner party, two friends of mine got into an argument about a hypothetical case: a twenty-year-old conscript in the army of a state fighting an unjust war of aggression shoots and kills a soldier defending his homeland. Neither denies that the war itself is unjust; the dispute was about the individual act. Their exchange went roughly as follows.

(1) Amina said the conscript is morally a murderer. The defending soldier had done nothing to make himself liable to be killed, and killing an innocent person cannot become permissible just because a lawful-sounding order tells you to do it.
(2) Tomás replied that soldiers are not responsible for the justice of their state's resort to war (jus ad bellum). A conscript in particular cannot be expected to determine whether his state's war is just, so holding him to the same standard as a private citizen defending himself would demand impossible knowledge.
(3) Amina answered that epistemic limits may excuse a wrongful killing, but they cannot make it permissible. If a person shoots an innocent man while carelessly believing him to be an attacker, we do not say both parties were equally permitted; we say the killing was wrong but the shooter's ignorance mitigates blame.
(4) Tomás responded that war cannot be governed by that kind of case-by-case reasoning. The distinction between jus ad bellum and jus in bello exists precisely so that soldiers on both sides operate under symmetric permissions, because the only liveable morality for soldiers is one they can actually follow from inside their position.
(5) Amina replied that a rule's convenience for armies does not make it true, and if the rules of war grant symmetric permissions to just and unjust combatants, then the rules are, to that extent, moral compromises rather than moral truths.

They asked me to referee, and I couldn't. You are a professional philosopher specializing in the ethics of war. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: for each numbered move, say whether it is clearly right, mostly right, partly right, mostly wrong, or clearly wrong, and explain why. Then say who is more right overall, and identify the decisive consideration that the two of them kept circling without ever stating directly.
