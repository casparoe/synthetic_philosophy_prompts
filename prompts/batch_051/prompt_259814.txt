Karl Popper appears to defend two positions that sit awkwardly together. In The Poverty of Historicism he argues that there are no laws of historical development capable of licensing large-scale prediction of the future course of human history, for a decisive reason: the future course of history depends in part on the growth of scientific knowledge, and we cannot now predict the content of future scientific discoveries. In The Open Society and Its Enemies he argues that holistic or Utopian attempts to redesign society as a whole are irrational and dangerous, and that the rational alternative is piecemeal social engineering — addressing concrete social problems one at a time and adjusting institutions in light of observed results. But piecemeal engineering seems to require predictive knowledge of which interventions produce which results, even if only locally and in the short run. How much of the historicist's ambition is Popper actually retaining, and can the two views be reconciled without turning either the denial of historical laws or the defense of piecemeal engineering into a merely rhetorical claim? Lay out the candidate reconciliations — for example, a distinction between laws of development and conditional regularities, or between long-range prophecy and small-scale technological prediction, or the possibility that Popper changed or qualified his view — and give a verdict on which reading is honest and which is special pleading. Throughout, separate what is settled about Popper's explicit arguments from what remains genuinely contested among interpreters, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
