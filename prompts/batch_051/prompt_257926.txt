I've been keeping a list of how a friend of mine judges whether a pain is bad, and I want to know what she should say about a case I recently read about. Her verdicts so far: a throbbing toothache is bad; the burn from touching a hot pan is bad; the deep ache of a broken arm is bad; the jab of a needle at a doctor's office is bad, though only slightly; jerking back from hot water before the pain arrives is not bad; and the pleasant ache of a deep stretch, which she says is still pain, is not bad.

The new case is pain asymbolia. A patient with this condition feels the sensory side of pain normally: he can tell you exactly where a pin is pressing, that the feeling is sharp, and how intense it is, and the pin really is breaking the skin. But he reports that it does not hurt in the everyday sense — it is not unpleasant, it does not bother him, and he feels no urge to pull away; he stays calm and unconcerned while the pin pricks him.

What would my friend say about the asymbolia patient's pain if she wants to be consistent with her earlier list? More important, which rule is she implicitly running? The candidates I can make out are that pain is bad when it feels unpleasant, when the person wants it to stop, when it signals real bodily damage, or something else. Say which of her earlier verdicts each candidate rule handles well, which rule best fits them overall, and what it predicts for the asymbolia case. Then name one further case whose verdict would most cleanly separate the leading candidate rules from each other.

Please write the answer in American English. At the very end, on its own line, state your prediction in this form: Verdict: the asymbolia pain is bad / the asymbolia pain is not bad.
