I'm a software engineer on a team that runs a customer-service chatbot, and there's a weird mismatch in how we treat the thing that I can't shake. Our training pipeline literally uses files with "intent" in the name; when the bot gives a bad answer we write up a bug like "the model believed the shipping date was the order date" or "the agent intended to issue a refund but described the wrong policy"; we review its outputs and annotate them as if they're representations of the business rules. On the other side, the same engineers, if you ask them, will flatly say the model doesn't understand anything, has no beliefs, doesn't intend anything — it's just next-token prediction. And yet, when an output goes to a customer, the company treats it as tho the system made a claim: QA checks whether "what the bot said" is true, and we file incidents when it contradicts our policy.

What theory of meaning — like, where aboutness or content comes from — does this whole practice presuppose? Does it commit us to saying the bot's outputs have meaning in their own right, or that the meaning is borrowed from the people who trained it and the people who read it, or something else? Is the practice even coherent given what the team says they believe? And what would concretely have to change in how we build, log, test, and ship the thing if we swapped out whatever view the practice presupposes for a different one?
