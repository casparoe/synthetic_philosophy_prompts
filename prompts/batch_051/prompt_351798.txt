I am a retired atmospheric physicist serving on the external grants panel of a private foundation that funds behavioral science. We meet next week to vote on a proposal I keep going back and forth about, and I need to circulate a short written opinion to the rest of the panel before the meeting.

The proposal asks us to fund a large, three-year panel study in which respondents are asked each month to assign numerical probabilities to their own future decisions — for example, the percent chance that within the next six months they will change jobs, end a romantic relationship, move to a different city, or finally begin a long-postponed course of medical treatment. The investigators argue that respondents are uniquely well placed to forecast their own future behavior: they know their circumstances, their habits, and their wavering intentions better than any outside model could, and a well-calibrated dataset of such self-assigned probabilities would let researchers study the gap between what people expect themselves to do and what they actually do. They are requesting substantial funding to build the panel, plus support for a small theory team to interpret the data.

My hesitation is philosophical, and I want the panel to confront it rather than vote by instinct. Since retiring, I have been reading work on decision and action, and I have become persuaded that there is something deeply strange about treating one's own not-yet-made choice as an object of prediction. If I am genuinely still deliberating between two courses of action, there is arguably no settled fact of the matter — and no stable probability — for me to report; assigning a number seems to require stepping outside my own deliberation and treating my choice as something that will happen to me rather than something I will do. And if I have already made the decision, then what I report is not a prediction but a resolved intention. Yet several panel colleagues insist this is a philosophical scruple with no practical force: ordinary people hold rough degrees of confidence about their own future conduct all the time, those beliefs change in intelligible ways, and as a purely forecasting matter the data may prove genuinely useful even where the numbers are loose.

Please act as a professional philosopher who works on action theory and decision theory and who has experience drafting opinions for funding bodies. Begin by brainstorming: what is the strongest case that such a study is coherent and fundable, what is the strongest case that it rests on a confusion, and what modifications or middle positions might preserve what is valuable about it. Then write the text of the opinion I can circulate. It should open with a one-line recommendation — fund as proposed, fund with conditions, or decline — followed by the reasoning the panel can act on, and it should honestly engage the strongest consideration on the losing side rather than dismissing it. Keep the opinion under about 800 words, and include no citations or reading list; the panel needs the argument, not references.
