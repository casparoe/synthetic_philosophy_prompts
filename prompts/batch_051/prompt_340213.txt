Right, bit of setup: I don't have any formal philosophy background—I work as a software engineer—but I've been reading Machiavelli's The Prince lately, and chapter eighteen, where he discusses how a prince should keep faith, has sort of lodged in my brain. As I understand him, he argues there that a ruler who always acts virtuously will come to ruin, because the world is full of people who don't play by those rules, and so a prudent ruler has to learn how to enter into evil when necessary. He also says it is useful to appear merciful, faithful, humane and religious, but that a ruler must be ready to switch to the opposite when circumstances demand it. This is what's got me thinking about the dirty hands problem in politics.

The position I'm half-convinced by is roughly Machiavelli's: sometimes a political leader must do things that would normally be wrong—deceive, break promises, use cruelty—because doing otherwise would bring about much worse outcomes for the state, and we might actually judge them worse for not doing those things. What I'd like to know is what would count against this view. Not the shallow stuff like 'it just feels wrong' or 'Machiavelli was a cynic', but the serious defeaters. For instance, is there a plausible case where the view commits you to something unacceptable, or where it collapses into thinking morality is just optional for anyone in power? Which objections would a careful defender of the dirty-hands position themselves concede as genuine costs or counter-evidence, and which would they dismiss as beside the point? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and an honest judgement on whether the view has insulated itself so thoroughly that nothing could ever really count against it.

If it helps orient the answer: I'm not interested in a reading list; I want the reasoning. And I'd rather you tell me if the view is actually well insulatd and whether that's a bug or a feature.
