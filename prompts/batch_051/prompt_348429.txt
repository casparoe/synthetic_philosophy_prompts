I have been struck by a resemblance between one corner of public law and one corner of the debate over artificial intelligence, and I want to know whether it amounts to a structural parallel or only a loose metaphor.

In several legal systems, an administrative decision can be defective not because the outcome is wrong, on the reviewer's independent view, but because the reasons offered for it are too thin, too conclusory, or fail to engage with the relevant facts and arguments. A reviewing body may decline to overturn the substance while still faulting the decision for being unreasoned. The underlying idea seems to be that intelligibility is a constraint with standing of its own: a decision that binds others must be one whose reasoning can be laid open, assessed, and contested, and this requirement is allowed to bite even when the outcome looks acceptable.

A structurally similar demand appears in the proposal that machine learning systems should be trained for legibility rather than merely for task performance. On this view, a system is acceptable for high-stakes use only if a human overseer can understand, track, and contest what the system is doing or why it produced what it did, even if the legible system performs somewhat worse than an opaque competitor. Here too, acceptability is imagined to rest partly on something other than getting the answer right.

The mapping I have in mind is this: a model output corresponds to an administrative decision; an explanation or legibility property corresponds to the reasons for a decision; the human overseer or affected user corresponds to the person or court receiving those reasons; and raw task performance corresponds to substantive correctness. In both domains, the imperative to be right is tempered by an imperative to be understandable.

I would like you to assess whether this analogy is deep or superficial. In particular: state precisely what would have to be true on both sides for arguments to transfer in either direction — what would the considerations that justify reason-giving in law have to be true of in a machine learning system before they carried over, and what would a concern for legibility imported from machine learning have to be true of in law before it transferred back; identify where the mapping comes apart, and distinguish the disanalogies that matter from the ones that are merely presentational; and name at least one thing the analogy would predict about AI legibility that you think is false, along with the disanalogy that explains the failure.

One candidate disanalogy I keep turning over is this: the legal requirement to give reasons is often defended by the interest people have in contesting decisions that bind them, and not solely by the prospect of better outcomes, whereas the demand for legible AI is usually defended as an instrument of better oversight. If that difference is real, does the analogy run deeper than its surface, or does it only match the features that make the two domains look alike? I am less interested in a final verdict than in a precise map of where the two structures align and where they come apart.
