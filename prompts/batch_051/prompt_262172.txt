I'm toying with a short essay about inheritance taxation and I'd be curious what you'd push back on. The core idea is this: people usually defend estate taxes on equal opportunity grounds, but the actual tax in many systems is levied on the estate as a lump before it's divided up, with a single exemption. If the real worry is that some kids start out with way more unearned advantage than others, then the natural unit isn't the dead person's pile, it's the windfall each recipient receives. An $8m estate split among five kids may be less of a dynastic problem than a $4m estate going to one kid, but an estate-type tax is basically insensitive to that split. So I want to argue the equal-opportunity rationale points toward a progressive tax on what each person receives in inheritances and major gifts over a lifetime—an accessions tax—rather than a tax on the estate per se. Is that distinction actually doing work, or am I just rearranging deck chairs?

Then I complicate it for myself. If we go down this road, why stop at money? Rich kids also inherit networks, expectations, private schooling, an absence of financial precarity, and so on. A tax on financial inheritances obviously can't reach those. Does that make the accessions-tax move ad hoc, or can you say money is just the part we can feasibly tax without interfering in how parents raise their children? I also have a half-baked line about dynastic compounding: a first-generation transfer might be covered by the parents' right to give away what they earned, but by the second or third transfer the wealth is unearned by everyone still involved, so maybe trusts and generation-skipping transfers should be taxed more heavily. Im not sure whether that's original or just confused.

For comments, I mostly want to know: where's the argument weakest; does the move from estate tax to accessions tax actually follow from an equal-opportunity premise, or am I smuggling in a different principle; and should the social-capital tangent be cut or developed. If the generation-skipping point is a known dead end, just say so.
