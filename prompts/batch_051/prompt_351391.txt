I'm taking a medieval philosophy class and we've been working through Boethius's Consolation, book five, the section where Lady Philosophy tries to sort out how divine foreknowledge doesn't turn future contingents into necessities. The passage I keep coming back to is where she distinguishes two kinds of necessity: one simple, like the fact that all humans are mortal, and another conditional, like when you know someone is walking, it is necessary that they are walking, but only in that conditional way and not with the same kind of compulsion as simple necessity. She says God sees all things as present, and that this doesn't force a voluntary act into being a simple necessity. I get the basic move, but I've been building a list of later attempts to solve the underlying problem, and I want to see if I'm missing one.

So far I have four responses to the apparent conflict between infallible foreknowledge and genuine alternatives:

1. Boethius's own solution, where God is outside time and sees all times as present, combined with the simple/conditional necessity distinction.

2. The Ockhamist solution, which says only facts that are strictly about the past are necessary; divine foreknowledge may be past, but it is a soft fact about the future, not a hard fact about the past.

3. The Molinist middle-knowledge response, where God knows what every possible free creature would do in every possible circumstance, and actualizes a world accordingly without causally determining the creature.

4. Open theism's solution, which just denies that God has definitive exhaustive foreknowledge of future free actions, so there are genuine open possibilities.

I want one more response to the problem that has a genuinely different structure from these four, not a restatement of one of them and not a mixture. Then tell me honestly whether it actually closes the gap or just moves it somewhere else. Please write in American English.
