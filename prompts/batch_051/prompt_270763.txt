so I found out earlier this year that I have pain asymbolia, which is the thing where you can tell a sensation is pain — you can locate it, grade it, say "that's a sharp one" — but it doesn't bother you. I walked on a broken foot for three days because it just read as information, not as a reason to stop. The doctors find me fascinating, my roommate thinks I'm a superhero, and I've realized I'm a walking counterexample to how almost everyone talks about pain.

I don't need the medical story of why I'm like this; I need the philosophical spec first. If someone wanted a theory of what makes pain bad — why it matters, why it's aversive, why it seems to give other people a reason to help — what would it actually have to get right to count as adequate? I want the requirements list with the case or consideration behind each: the ordinary person flinching from a burn; my case, where the sensation shows up and the badness is simply missing; the fact that some miseries (grief, a kidney stone, a needle phobia) are horrible in ways that don't track sharpness or intensity; the difference between feeling pain and suffering from it, and between something's being bad for a person and the person minding it. Which distinctions can't be collapsed? Which requirements are non-negotiable, and which are just what people assume because they've never met anyone like me? Flag any pair of requirements that can't be jointly satisfied. And tell me straight: can a view that says pain is bad in itself, bad in virtue of the sensation, survive my case at all, or does it force a two-layer story where the unpleasantness is a separate thing my brain apparently doesn't add? Please don't give me a reading list or citations — I want the spec itself, because I'm tired of reading people who treat pain as if it's obviously its own aversion and footnote asymbolia away.
