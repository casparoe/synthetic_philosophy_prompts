Since retiring from a career in physics — most of it spent on the safety of complex engineered systems — I have been reading my way into moral and legal philosophy, and one parallel keeps bothering me. I would like a careful assessment of whether it is deep or superficial.

In my old field, a standard lesson was that certain serious accidents are system accidents, not component failures. A cascading blackout can happen even though every protective relay tripped exactly as it was designed to trip; a chemical plant can fail even though no valve was defective and no operator violated a written procedure. There is, in a precise sense, no faulty part: each element obeyed its local rule, and the disaster emerged from the way the elements were coupled and from the tightness of their interactions. The practical conclusion we drew was that hunting for the culpable component is usually pointless and can be actively harmful. The right response is to redesign the couplings — loosen tightly coupled feedback loops, add redundancy, widen the operating envelope — so that the system can no longer propagate a normal collection of small local failures into a catastrophe. Blame, in that world, was close to a category mistake.

The philosophical literature that set this off is the discussion of responsibility gaps for autonomous systems. A self-driving vehicle or a diagnostic algorithm causes serious harm, but the designers were not negligent with respect to that particular case, the operator behaved reasonably under the circumstances, and the system itself is not a fit object of blame in any ordinary sense. The worry is that we are left with bad outcomes and no one to hold accountable.

To me the two structures look identical. In each, an unwanted outcome arises from many individually acceptable or correct contributions; in each, the retributive reflex — locate the bad element and hold it responsible — sits awkwardly with the causal structure; and in each, the productive-looking response appears to be forward-looking redesign rather than backward-looking assignment of fault. Before I build anything on this, I want to know whether it is a genuine identity of structure, a loose analogy, or a confusion. Please state the mapping as precisely as you can (what corresponds to the component, the local rule, the coupling, the system accident, and the redesign on the normative side), identify the points where the two cases come apart, and say what would have to be true of moral and legal responsibility for the engineering lesson to transfer. In particular, I want at least one thing this parallel predicts that turns out false — and your judgment as to whether its falsity is a repairable detail or a sign that the analogy is doing more rhetorical than argumentative work.
