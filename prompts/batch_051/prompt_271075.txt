I'm currently reporting a piece on the gap between the categories institutions use and the unruly phenomena they classify, and I keep coming back to Wittgenstein's later philosophy of language. In particular, I'm interested in his idea that meaning is bound up with the practices or 'language-games' in which expressions are used, and that many concepts — 'game' being the example — are not defined by a common essence but hang together by overlapping similarities he called family resemblance. In an interview for the same story, a cognitive psychologist went further: she claimed that the classical view of concepts, according to which a concept is a mental definition, a set of necessary and sufficient features that every instance must satisfy, has effectively been refuted by empirical work, and that family-resemblance structure is simply a better description of what people do when they categorize.

I left that interview unpersuaded about what exactly had been shown. So: treat the following as a claim about one ordinary concept, say 'game' or, better, a messier one such as 'sport' or 'documentary': that its members are not united by any shared definition and are instead connected only by a network of overlapping similarities. What is the strongest empirical test of that claim? Specify what observations, judgments, or measurements you would collect, what pattern of results would count in favor of family resemblance, and what pattern would count in favor of recovering a definition. Then be precise about what even a clean result would not settle: whether we have merely failed to find the right definition, whether the finding records only degrees of typicality rather than the structure of the category, whether it concerns the concept itself or only how speakers describe it, and whether any amount of descriptive data could tell us how such a category ought to be drawn.
