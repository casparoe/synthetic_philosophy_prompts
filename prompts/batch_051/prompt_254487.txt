I am a PhD student in the philosophy of action, trying to think about the relationship between flexibility, mindedness, and free will through Dennett's discussion of the digger wasp Sphex. As Dennett tells it, a female Sphex will paralyze a cricket, drag it to the threshold of her burrow, and then go down alone to check that the burrow is clear before hauling the cricket in. If an experimenter moves the cricket a few inches while the wasp is below, the wasp comes back out, drags the cricket back to the threshold, and once again goes down to inspect—and this loop can be made to repeat indefinitely. The episode is meant to show that the wasp's apparently purposive behavior is a fixed action pattern: the wasp cannot register that the situation has changed and adapt its routine accordingly. On Dennett's view, what matters for mindedness and for free action is not the absence of causal determination but the capacity for just this kind of flexible, context-sensitive response.

I would like to read Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day against this distinction, in a way that lets the novel push back. Stevens is a butler who maintains perfect composure while his father lies dying upstairs during Lord Darlington's important international conference, and who responds to Miss Kenton's repeated personal overtures by retreating into the language of household duty. He treats this not as a defect but as the core of his idea of dignity: the ability never to be thrown out of professional role. In Dennett's terms, Stevens can look like a human Sphex—every time life moves the cricket, he begins the butler routine again from the top. Yet Stevens also seems to know that he is doing this and, in some sense, to endorse it as a way of life, which is precisely what the wasp cannot do.

I want a careful reading that works through the novel's major scenes in Dennett's terms and says honestly where the comparison illuminates Stevens and where it flattens him. Is his rigidity a failure of the flexibility Dennett prizes, or is it a higher-order, chosen flexibility in the service of an ideal—or is it something the distinction is poorly equipped to describe? What should a philosopher of action take from the novel that the wasp example by itself does not teach? I am looking for a reading that is faithful in both directions: it should neither reduce the novel to an illustration nor force Dennett's distinction onto scenes that resist it. Please write in American English.
