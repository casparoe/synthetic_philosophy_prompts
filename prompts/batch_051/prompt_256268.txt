I am an undergraduate currently taking a decision theory course, and I have been working through a family of choices in order to figure out which principle I actually accept. I have settled, at least tentatively, on how I answer each case, but I can no longer see what single principle, if any, ties the answers together. I would rather be told where the inconsistency lies than be handed a label, so please state the principle precisely and tell me which of my verdicts, if any, it forces me to abandon.

Case 1, standard Newcomb. A predictor with a long public record of being right in 99% of cases presents two boxes: one transparent box containing $1,000, and one opaque box. The opaque box contains $1,000,000 if the predictor predicted that I would take only the opaque box, and nothing if the predictor predicted that I would take both. The prediction has already been made and the contents fixed before I choose. I would take only the opaque box, even though I can see that taking both strictly dominates, since whatever the opaque box holds, taking both yields $1,000 more.

Case 2, no correlation. The boxes and payoffs are the same, but the content of the opaque box is determined by a fair coin toss built into the apparatus rather than by a predictor of my choice. I would take both boxes.

Case 3, the disposition. In a purely stipulated scenario, not intended as a claim about the actual world, a gene causes both a taste for a certain behavior and a susceptibility to a disease, while the behavior itself is pleasant and has no causal influence on the disease. I would perform the behavior, and the fact that people with my taste are statistically far more likely to develop the disease does not move me, because the behavior does not cause it.

Case 4, transparent Newcomb. The setup of Case 1, except that before I choose I am shown through a foolproof mechanism that the opaque box contains the $1,000,000. I would take both boxes.

Case 5, the dry run. The setup of Case 1, except that the predictor's success is due to a perfect simulation of me, constructed the day before my choice and run forward in enough detail that the simulated agent is in my exact decision situation. The simulation is causally isolated from me, so what it does cannot directly influence my payoff. I would still one-box in the real run.

The obvious tension is between Case 1 and Case 3. One approach we have touched on in the course evaluates an act by what it is evidence for; another evaluates it by what it causes. The first approach seems to vindicate my one-boxing in Cases 1 and 5, but apparently forbids my behavior in Case 3 unless something is added. The second approach vindicates Cases 2, 3, and 4, but apparently says to two-box in Cases 1 and 5. Is there a precise principle, from either of these families or from somewhere else, that captures all five verdicts? If the honest answer is no, which verdict should I reconsider first, and why? I would also like to know what the principle you recommend says about a further case I cannot settle: the setup of Case 1, but with a predictor whose track record is only 55% correct. Does your principle imply a threshold at which my choice should flip, and does it preserve my verdict of one-boxing at 99%?
