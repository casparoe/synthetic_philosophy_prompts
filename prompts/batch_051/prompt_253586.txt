I recently agreed to help a small film society choose its winter program, and the discussions have made me realize how unsure I am about something I used to take for granted: what it is to judge a work of art for oneself rather than to hold an opinion about it at second hand. One of my fellow selectors, a critic I genuinely admire, will assert without any embarrassment that a new film is overpraised though he has not seen it. When pressed, he explains that he is reasoning from a review he trusts, from the director's earlier work, and from a friend's description. He insists these are aesthetic judgments, not guesses, and I do not think he is being dishonest. But I no longer know how to tell a judgment that is mine from one I have borrowed, or whether that difference even tracks what matters.

I want a working definition of judging for oneself in aesthetic matters. I have in mind roughly what the principle some philosophers call the acquaintance principle is meant to capture: the thought that one's verdict on a work must be earned by first-hand experience of the work itself and cannot simply be transferred from another person's say-so. I want the definition to classify the following cases correctly. I am fairly confident about the first two; on the rest I have only leanings, and I expect a sound definition to force me to revise something.

1. A listener with no musical training hears a symphony once and says afterward only that it was beautiful, unable to say why. I take this to be judging for herself, and I would resist a definition that rules her out merely because she cannot articulate the grounds.

2. A student who has not read a novel faithfully reconstructs the critical consensus that it is structurally brilliant, states that verdict when asked, and adds that he himself has no view. I take this not to be judging for himself; he is reporting.

3. Someone is moved to tears by a friend's detailed, scene-by-scene retelling of a tragedy and declares the play devastating, though she has never seen or read it. Her response is real, but I am unsure whether her verdict is hers.

4. A reader works through a play only in translation, never sees it performed, and concludes that it is dramatically effective. Is he judging the play, the translation, or only the script he read?

5. A curator stands before a painting, is entirely unmoved, and nonetheless calls it excellent because she recognizes features that art history associates with importance. If the definition requires a felt response, she is not judging for herself; I am not sure I accept that consequence.

6. A listener begins by calling a song great solely on a trusted friend's say-so, later listens to it, finds it boring, and continues to call it great because he trusts the friend's ear more than his own boredom. It seems to me he has not judged the song for himself, but I cannot say at which moment the failure occurs.

7. Someone who has never heard an album confidently calls it mediocre, having read three reviews and knowing from long experience that he dislikes the genre. This strikes me as a prediction about his own response rather than a judgment of the work, but I should not build that conclusion into the definition before testing it.

Propose the definition, run it through all seven cases without skipping the awkward ones, say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer at all, and then either revise the definition or defend the surprising classifications. If you think the phrase judging for oneself does two jobs at once—something about the evidence on which the judgment rests and something about the judger's involvement or endorsement—say so explicitly and tell me which cases turn on which job.
