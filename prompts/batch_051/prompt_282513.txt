I'm drafting a short paper on the paradox of blackmail for an upper-level seminar in philosophy of criminal law, and I'd like a critical reader's comments on the argument before I revise it. The paper pushes back on the standard way of setting up the paradox—that blackmail illicitly combines two acts that are each permissible on their own—and argues that the real work is done by a distinction between two kinds of conditional proposal: ordinary bargaining that improves the other party's baseline, and extortionate leverage that threatens to make the other party worse off relative to their present position. My current draft is below.

---

The standard formulation of the paradox of blackmail invites us to notice that a person may permissibly disclose an embarrassing truth about someone, and may permissibly ask that person for money, yet may not permissibly say "pay me or I will disclose it." A conjunction of two individually permissible acts is, it seems, impermissible. I think this formulation is defective. The two acts that get combined in blackmail are misdescribed: a disclosure announced as the penalty for non-payment is not the same action as a bare disclosure, and a demand backed by that threat is not the same action as an open request. The appearance of paradox comes from individuating actions too coarsely.

The obvious way to explain what changes is to say that blackmail is coercive. But coercion is too coarse in the other direction. A merchant who says "pay the listed price or I will not transfer the goods" issues a conditional proposal that puts real pressure on a buyer who urgently needs the goods, yet it is not extortion. The difference is not the presence of pressure but the baseline the proposal posits. The merchant withholds a benefit: absent the proposal, the buyer simply does not have the goods, and the merchant offers to improve that situation. The blackmailer, by contrast, threatens to set back something the victim already has—the practical good of not having reputationally damaging information spread to people who matter to them. The proposal is "make me better off or I will make you worse off relative to your present position," and that describes leverage over another person's standing, not ordinary bargaining.

One might object that if the disclosure is permissible, the victim has no entitlement to continued secrecy, so the baseline I invoke is illusory. But permissibility and baseline come apart. I may be within my rights to publish a harsh but honest review of a colleague's book; still, my choosing not to publish leaves the colleague in their present position, while my announcing "pay me or the review goes out" does not merely withhold a benefit from them—it wields, as a bargaining tool, a harm over which I have discretion. The relevant question is not whether I would wrong them by publishing the review, but whether the law should license my converting my discretion over that harm into an instrument of extraction. I submit that it should not, and that the paradox of blackmail is really the line between using a liberty and selling one's power to harm.

---

I'd appreciate comments on three points in particular. First, is my claim that blackmail is not helpfully explained by a generic appeal to coercion fair to the strongest coercion-based accounts, or have I dismissed them too quickly? Second, does the baseline proposal actually dissolve the paradox, or does it covertly assume the very thing it needs to establish—that the victim has something like a protected interest in continued secrecy? Third, where is the weakest step in the argument, and how would a defender of the standard formulation press on it? In your reply, please also say how confident you are in each of your main judgments and single out the point you are least sure about.
