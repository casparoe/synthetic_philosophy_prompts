I have recently joined my city's climate resilience task force, and next month we have to design and run a public engagement session before the city council votes on whether to allow a research consortium to use the municipal airport as the base for high-altitude flights in a small stratospheric aerosol injection field trial, a way of loading reflective particles into the upper atmosphere to cool the planet. This is no longer an abstraction for me: the outcome will shape how my neighbors and I live with whatever the council decides.

The session will have 24 participants: neighborhood representatives, small-business owners, members of an environmental-justice coalition, high school civics students, and city infrastructure staff. We have 75 minutes in one large room with movable chairs, and no materials beyond index cards and poster paper. By the end, I want participants to come away seeing that even a local gateway decision is already entangled with three larger problems. First, moral hazard: if people begin to treat the prospect of a technical climate fix as a fallback, they may slacken on cutting emissions now. Second, termination shock: if solar geoengineering were maintained at a level that meaningfully cools the planet and then suddenly stopped, the result would be an extremely rapid catch-up warming, so starting it creates an open-ended, intergenerational commitment with no safe off-ramp. Third, legitimacy: the climate effects would cross every border and reach future people, so it is unclear who could have standing to decide that this should begin, yet here is a city council facing a smaller but real gateway vote.

Design the activity we should run: a structured deliberation or role-based simulation in which participants encounter these three problems through their own choices and incentives before the facilitator names them. Give me the roles or teams, the rules and materials, a timed sequence, and any payoff, token, or commitment structure needed, without relying on real money. Then provide a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto moral hazard, termination shock, and the standing-to-decide problem. Finally, tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time we run it and how to adjust, and be explicit about which feature of the real-world situation the activity cannot reproduce and that I will need to supply directly as facilitator. Please write the entire plan in American English.
