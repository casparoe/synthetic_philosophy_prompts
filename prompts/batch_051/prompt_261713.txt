I've been teaching a graduate seminar on the demandingness debate, and there is one tension I keep returning to without being able to settle it to my own satisfaction. On reflection I am drawn to what I will call the maximizing picture of moral goodness: moral reasons are not simply one class of reasons to be weighed against others in the usual way, because once you grant that you may permissibly stop short of doing the most good you can, you appear to be drawing an arbitrary line between what you owe and what you merely could have done. If some act would make things morally better than its alternatives, and the cost to you is not itself of overriding moral importance, the only non-arbitrary stopping point seems to be to maximize. This gives me a clean, principled standard: more moral value is always better, and the best choice is the one that realizes the most of it. But I also have a firm intuitive resistance to the ideal that this yields. The person who is maximally morally good, not merely generous or conscientious but organized around moral improvement at every turn, strikes me as missing something that is not just self-indulgence: particular attachments, irreverent humor, play, aesthetic absorption, the capacity to waste an afternoon on something useless and love it. The portrait of the moral saint as a somewhat bland and humorless figure haunts me even where I do not accept every step of the argument that produces it, and I find myself thinking that a life so dominated by the moral point of view would be in some all-things-considered sense less admirable, perhaps even less valuable, than a life that leaves room for forms of excellence and love that answer to no moral mandate.

I do not want to be told which side wins. I want an honest accounting of what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that gives way, what it would take for the theory to be the thing that gives way, and whether there is a stable reading on which both survive. For the intuition to give way, one would presumably need to show that it tracks an unrepresentative caricature of the maximally good person, or that it conflates maximal moral goodness with a particular personality type, or that it takes features that need not co-occur and bundles them into one unattractive figure. For the theory to give way, one would need a principled account of why moral reasons, though weighty, are satiable or domain-limited rather than maximizing, or why the correct standard is to meet a threshold while honoring non-moral values that do not themselves get their authority from morality. For both to survive, one might need to distinguish the claim that moral considerations govern a certain domain of duty from the much stronger claim that every choice should be an exercise in moral optimization, and then explain whether that distinction can be drawn without collapsing back into the arbitrariness that pushed me toward maximization in the first place. Walk me through the costs of each move and tell me which concession you would ultimately accept, rather than assuming from the outset that one of them is free.
