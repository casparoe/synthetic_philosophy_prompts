I keep noticing a move in arguments about property — mostly in online libertarian defences of enclosure, but sometimes in the early modern texts I work on — and I want to know whether it has an established name. It goes like this. Someone claims a farmer owns a field because he cleared and ploughed it; the labour is what makes it his. When somebody objects that the enclosure cut off the neighbouring villagers' path to the river, the defender shifts register: under a system of private property everyone is better off than they would have been in the commons, so the villagers have no complaint. But push on that — point out that these particular villagers are not better off, that they've ended up landless and worse off — and the defender goes straight back to "well, it's his because he worked it." I saw the same thing with a mining claim: the claim was justified by the effort of prospecting and extracting, then when the mine blocked a grazing route the justification became "the mine creates jobs and the country needs the revenue," and when the jobs claim looked shaky it was back to "we dug it up, so it's ours."

The odd thing is that the two justifications — labour-ground and everyone-benefits-ground — are not being combined into one argument. They take turns. Each one is treated as decisive until an objection to it is raised, at which point the other is treated as decisive, and when that one is attacked the first returns. They are seperately conclusive, never jointly tested. Is there a name for that specific alternation? It isn't exactly motte-and-bailey, because there isn't an obvious more-defensible half and a less-defensible half; the everyone-benefits claim is often the most empirically exposed part of the argument, and the labour claim is no clearer a candidate for the "bailey." It's also not plain equivocation, since each premise, were it true, would license the conclusion on its own; the problem is how the speaker switches between them whenever one fails. I'd like to know the nearest concepts, how this differs from each of them, and which fits best. If nothing established fits, name it yourself and give it a definition with boundary cases. Please be committed about this: pick a label and defend it, rather than laying out the options and leaving me to choose.
