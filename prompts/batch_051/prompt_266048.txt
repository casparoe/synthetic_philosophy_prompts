I have been trying to formalize an argument that came up in a reading group on verificationism and map–territory talk, and I am left with the feeling that a very plausible epistemic principle proves too much. Let \(K\) be a knowledge operator and \(\Diamond\) a possibility operator. Start with the knowability principle: for every true proposition \(p\), \(p \to \Diamond Kp\). Now suppose there is at least one truth nobody in fact knows. Make it concrete: say \(q\) is the proposition that a particular coin landed heads on a table that no one ever inspected and no one will ever inspect. Then the conjunction \(q \land \neg Kq\) is true. Could anyone know \(q \land \neg Kq\)? Knowledge distributes over conjunction, so someone who knew the conjunction would know \(q\). But they would also know \(\neg Kq\), and since knowledge is factive, \(\neg Kq\) would be true. Yet their knowing \(q\) makes \(Kq\) true, contradicting \(\neg Kq\). So \(q \land \neg Kq\) is a truth that cannot possibly be known. But if any unknown truth \(q\) exists, this true-but-unknowable conjunction exists. Contraposing: if no truth is unknowable—that is, if the knowability principle holds for all truths—then no truth is unknown. From \(\forall p(p \to \Diamond Kp)\) we would have to accept \(\forall p(p \to Kp)\): all truths are actually known. That conclusion is absurd, unless the argument has somehow slipped into modeling our actual epistemic situation as God’s. Where exactly is the first bad step, and which premise is least costly to surrender?

I have seen the standard moves—rejecting knowability outright, restricting it to a privileged class of basic propositions, or denying that knowledge distributes over conjunction—and none feels right. Rejection gives up too much for a verificationist; restriction looks ad hoc unless you can state a non-circular criterion for which propositions are eligible; denying distribution over conjunction seems to require a much more radical departure from normal modal logic than the original problem warrants. I would especially like to know whether constructing \(q \land \neg Kq\) is the real culprit, since it builds a negation of knowledge into the content of what would have to be known, and whether that construction is legitimate for ordinary propositions rather than self-referential sentences. If the knowability principle is the thing to weaken, can it be weakened in a way that preserves the anti-realist thought that truth is constrained by what can in principle be verified, without collapsing into the claim that every truth is in fact verified? Or is the correct diagnosis that the proof is valid and full-strength knowability was never tenable in the first place?
