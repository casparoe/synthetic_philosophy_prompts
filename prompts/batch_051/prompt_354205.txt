I am teaching a course on collective action and public goods, and one analogy keeps doing almost all of the argumentative work in class discussion. In his 1968 article, Garrett Hardin frames the problem through a passage about a pasture open to all: a herder who adds one more animal receives the entire private benefit from that animal, while the cost of the resulting overgrazing is spread thinly across every user of the pasture, so any individual who restrains himself bears the full cost of restraint while others continue to add stock. Hardin generalizes from the pasture to pollution, population, and other resources, and concludes that the logic of open access drives rational individuals toward ruin. My students now apply this pasture analogy to nearly everything: the atmosphere is a commons, ocean fisheries are a commons, groundwater is a commons, even urban parking is a commons.

I want an audit of how far Hardin’s pasture analogy actually holds for modern collective-action problems. Please act as an expert on commons institutions and collective action, and start by brainstorming candidate correspondences before settling on your analysis. Give me the correspondence table for at least three of the cases I listed: what plays the role of the pasture, the herdsman, the cattle, the private benefit, the shared cost, the fencing, and the carrying capacity. Then identify the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion—that individual rationality plus open access leads inevitably to depletion. Where does the pasture picture quietly import assumptions that fail for a global pollutant with delayed and diffuse harms, a fugitive resource like fish that move across boundaries, or a congestible good like street parking where exclusion is technically possible but institutionally costly? Which disanalogies are merely cosmetic and do not threaten the conclusion? Finally, say whether Hardin’s conclusion—that some restriction, enclosure, or coordination is necessary—survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying the argument. If you think the request itself rests on a confused premise—for example, that the tragedy of the commons is one mechanism rather than a family of distinct collective-action problems—say so directly instead of politely complying, and then answer the version you can defend.
