I co-run a small game-theory reading group — about a dozen undergrads, mostly philosophy and econ majors, 90 minutes a week. Last night I got into a long argument with a friend about iterated elimination, and I want to turn it into a session. The thing I want them comming away seeing is that eliminating a strategy only when it's strictly dominated by another pure strategy is too weak: a strategy can be strictly dominated by a mixed strategy even when no pure strategy beats it against everything the other side could do. My friend's whole point was that showing people a payoff matrix won't make them feel this — they'll nod and then go back to checking pure strategies one by one. So I want to build something where the group runs into it themselves before it's named.

Ideally an actual scored game where one option looks like the sensible compromise or safe pick, enough of them take it, and then someone realizes that a coin flip between the two extreme options beats that compromise no matter what the other side did. The reveal should come out of the scores they experienced, not from me explaining the matrix afterwards. Design the activity for exactly that group: concrete rules and payoffs (points are fine, small stakes okay), how the time breaks down inside 90 minutes, and a debrief afterward. The debrief should map what actually happened in the game onto the distinction between pure-strategy dominance and mixed-strategy dominance, say what will predictably go wrong when it's run (people not noticing the mixed dominance at all, or thinking the point is just risk aversion, or someone confusing dominated with not optimal), and how to handle each of those. Also be frank about which part of the idea the game can't get them to feel — the thing the facilitator will just have to say out loud.

And don't use the phrase iterated elimination with the participants until the debrief, if you can. In your answer, tell me how confident you are in the design overall and which single piece you're least sure would survive contact with real participants.
