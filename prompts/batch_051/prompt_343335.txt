I've been working through Stephen Wolfram's *A New Kind of Science* after a friend who does computer science told me it was either the most overrated or most underrated book about science. A science blog I follow summarized the argument like this: Wolfram showed that tiny cellular automata such as Rule 110 can generate patterns as complex as anything in nature, and from this he supposedly draws two conclusions. First, the complexity we see in nature doesn't require complex rules; it comes from simple rules run for many steps. Second, the Principle of Computational Equivalence says that almost any process that isn't obviously simple is computationally just as sophisticated as any other, so a growing crystal, a brain, a weather system, and a universal computer are all doing the same kind of thing computationally. The blogger then said this means traditional mathematical science, with its differential equations and closed-form predictions, is mostly obsolete for complex systems, and that NKS is a new way of doing science by systematically enumerating possible simple programs.

I've now got the actual book open to chapter 12, sections 2 and 3, where he introduces the principle. My memory of his formulation is something like: once a process is not obviously simple, it is capable of computations that are just as sophisticated as those of any other process, so almost all processes are either clearly simple or computationally equivalent. But I know I'm paraphrasing from the book rather than quoting exactly. My question is whether the blog's summary is fair to what Wolfram actually says. Could you go through the accurate parts, the overstatements or conflations, and whether the blanket dismissal of traditional science follows from the Principle of Computational Equivalence as he actually states it? I'm trying to decide whether the book is genuinely making a claim about the practice of science or a much weaker, maybe more defensible, claim about computation.
