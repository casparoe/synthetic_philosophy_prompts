I run a small conservation outfits and we've been trying to get an all-female, parthenogenetic desert lizard listed under our state's species-protection law. The definitional section reads:

"'Species' means any group of actually or potentially interbreeding natural populations that are reproductively isolated from other such groups."

The lizard population reproduces by cloning; there are no males, and so far as anyone can tell there never have been any. Individuals are nearly genetically identical to one another. The state wildlife agency denied the petition on the grounds that, under the statutory words, the lizards are not a "species": no interbreeding occurs, and none is possible, so the definition simply fails to reach them. But the same act says, in its statement of purpose, that it is meant to protect "the state's distinct forms of life," and pretty much every herpetologist who has looked at these lizards treats them as a species (some under the older name, some under a new one).

I'm less interested in the immediate listing decision than in what it exposes. Walk me through the main approaches to interpreting a clause like this (plain meaning, the legislature's purpose, what the drafters would have said if you'd asked them about asexual vertebrates, what a reasonable reader would understand) and tell me which reading should control and why. Then say what it shows about the risks of writing a particular species concept into law — is the lesson that the interbreeding definition is broken, that the courts should defer to taxonomists, or that statutory definitions of "species" always lag biology? Would the analysis change if the taxonomists were split about whether asexual lineages count as species at all, or would that just push the problem up a level?
