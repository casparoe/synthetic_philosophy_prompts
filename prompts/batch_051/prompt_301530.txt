I keep running into the line "Malthus was right, just early," and it seems to mean something different every time I meet it. First time was in a comments section under a video about vertical farming: someone used it to brush off the idea that we'd ever run out of food, but also to insist we should keep worrying anyway, because the mass starvation Malthus predicted in the 1800s just got postponed by artificial fertilizer and later the Green Revolution (I had to look those up). Then a friend used it about housing costs, like when a city gets too full, prices and quality of life do the "checking" instead of famine. And my uncle, who reads a lot of econ blogs, says the whole saying is a dodge: a theory you rescue every time it fails by quietly moving the date back isn't a theory anymore, it's unfalsifiable. They can't all be right, but they're all quoting the same five words like it settles the argument.

What I want is for you to pull apart the different precise claims "Malthus was right, just early" could be making. For example: right that scarcity eventually bites somewhere; right about the specific mechanism, that food supply grows slower than population; right that any check on population has to show up in a grim form — famine, disease, war, poverty — rather than people just choosing smaller families; or right only in the loose sense that any exponential growth eventually hits any finite planet. For each reading, tell me whether it's trivially true, interesting but unproven, unfalsifiable, or outright false, and which one a person is actually leaning on when they say "right but early" instead of just admitting he was wrong. Then give me your committed view — don't just hand me the menu and tell me to pick. Which reading, if any, is worth holding onto, and what was the vertical-farming-comments person's use of the line actually doing in that argument?
