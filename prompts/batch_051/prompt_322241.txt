I'm outlining a novel where the central marriage is a particle physicist and a developmental biologist who can't stop arguing about whether the universe is beautiful, and writing them has got me fixated on a strange asymmetry in how different sciences treat elegance. If two physical theories fit the data equally well, physicists will generally take the more elegant one more seriously; inelegance gets treated as a real intellectual strike against a theory, not just a matter of taste. But try saying the equivalent about a biological mechanism—that this metabolic pathway or embryonic signaling network is to inelegant to be the real one—and a biologist will look at you like you've never met a living system. Organisms are full of kluges, redundancies, and historical accidents, so ugliness is basically the default and not evidence against a hypothesis.

So elegance seems to be functioning as evidence in physics and as almost anti-evidence in biology. Is there a principled distinction here, or is the physicist's aesthetic heuristic just a lucky habit that's payed off so far? Maybe it matters that physics aims at a small set of fundamental laws while biology studies path-dependent, contingent structures; maybe elegance is only a good guide when you have independent reason to think the target is simple. I want the strongest defense of the asymmetry you can build, and an honest verdict on whether it survives.
