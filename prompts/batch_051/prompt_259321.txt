Having recently retired from a career in the natural sciences, I have taken up the study of philosophy and find myself returning repeatedly to Searle's Chinese Room. I would be grateful for a careful analysis of a variation that I have not seen discussed. In the original argument, a monolingual English speaker in a room follows a rulebook, written in English, for manipulating Chinese symbols, and in this way produces Chinese answers to Chinese questions without understanding a word of Chinese. Suppose now that we remove the rulebook and put in its place two things: (i) a printed list of the trained parameters of a large language model, of the kind produced by gradient descent on a large corpus of Chinese text and containing no hand-coded grammar or lexicon; and (ii) an English-language procedure for performing, one step at a time with pencil and paper, the arithmetic of a forward pass across those parameters whenever a Chinese sentence is presented. The speaker follows the procedure mechanically and returns the Chinese characters that it outputs, and the room performs indistinguishably from a native speaker of Chinese.

My question is this: does this alteration strengthen Searle's original argument, weaken it, or leave its force unchanged? In particular, does the syntax-versus-semantics distinction that carries the original argument apply in the same way when the programme is a trained set of numerical weights rather than a set of explicit rules? How should the systems reply be reformulated for the variant, and what exactly would a defender of strong AI have to show in order to locate genuine understanding anywhere in this arrangement? I would be grateful for an answer written in British English.
