I've been thinking about how a service could infer what someone really wants from what they actually do rather than from what they say, and I'd like you to walk through how my proposed version would behave if it were built. I'm not asking whether it is a good idea; I want the mechanisms, the incentives, and the downstream effects.

The service would maintain a private model of each user's preferences. It would ignore all stated preferences: no likes, no stars, no surveys, no interest boxes. It would train only on choices. But not all choices would count equally. Actions that cost the user nothing, such as a one-click like, a click that is instantly abandoned, autoplay, or hovering, would be recorded but not used for training. Actions that cost the user something real, such as finishing a long piece, paying money, spending scarce time, recommending something to a friend, risking embarrassment, deleting an account, or paying to remove ads, would be used for training, with a weight proportional to how much the choice cost relative to the next-best option the user could realistically have taken. The system would never optimize for engagement or attention; it would only try to present the items that its inferred preference model ranks highest for that user.

There would be one twist. When a later action contradicts an earlier one, such as someone undoing a purchase, unsubscribing from something they have used daily, or deleting something they once shared, the system would not average the two signals or reweight the old one. It would delete the earlier observation and record the later one as fresh evidence, weighted by the later action's own cost. The idea is that later actions express the preference the person would rather stand behind.

Please trace what happens over time once everyone has adapted. Once users understand how their behavior is being weighted, how does their behavior change, including behavior meant to shape the model rather than to pursue the underlying interest? What do content creators and advertisers do once they notice what the system counts as expensive evidence? Who games it first, and how? Does the model converge on something like the user's sincere, stable preferences, or on something manufactured by the system and by the parties trying to feed it? Where does the first serious failure appear, and could small patches to the rule fix it, or would any fix undermine the core idea that cost reveals caring?

As the last line of your answer, give a one-sentence summary of the main failure mode you identify.
