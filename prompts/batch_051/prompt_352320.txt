I've been arguing with a friend about whether human extinction is bad for reasons over and above the sheer number of deaths it involves, and I keep landing on a view I picked up somewhere — I think it's called the afterlife conjecture, from Scheffler. The idea is that a huge amount of what gives point to the things I'm doing right now depends on an unspoken assumption that humanity will still be around after I'm gone. Not because my own life gets longer or because I'll be remembered, but because the projects I care about — teaching someone something, doing research, trying to build something that outlasts me, even just tending a friendship — only seem to make sense against a background of some larger human future they feed into. The stock case is the cancer researcher who would be devestated to learn the species will go extinct the day after she dies, even though her own death was already certain and the research was never going to save her anyway. Lately I've been using this as my answer to why extinction would be bad beyond the deaths: the deaths aren't the only loss, because the meaning of the lives being lived now would be retrospectively hollowed out.

But honestly it's been functioning as an intuition pump for me, and I want to know how exposed it actually is. What would count against it — not in the sense of "what's an alternative view" but in the sense of which observations, arguments, or cases ought to move me, and by how much? I'd like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward. In particular, I'm unsure whether this is an empirical claim about human psychology, a claim about what value or meaning is, or a claim about the internal logic of goal-directed action, and the kind of evidence that would refute it probably depends a lot on which of those it is. What would a person or a whole culture have to be like for the conjecture to be false as a claim about them? Are there actually existing cases that already put pressure on it, and are those counterexamples or just outliers? What does the conjecture predict that a rival account of extinction's badness doesn't, and has any of that been tested?

One request about how you answer. Don't quietly fix the view up so it survives; if the only defensible version is a much weaker claim than the intuitive one, say so. I'd also like to know what someone who accepts the conjecture should concede counts against it, not just what a critic would say. And if I've set the question up badly — for example if I'm conflating a psychological fact about what I'd feel with a normative claim about what's actually bad — tell me that plainly rather than answering anyway. What I'm after is a map of where the position risks being wrong, whether it's the kind of claim that can even be wrong, and whether it's been insulated in a way that ought to bother me.
