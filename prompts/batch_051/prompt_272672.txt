You're a value theorist who works on the ethics of human extinction, and I want you to think this through slowly and brainstorm several candidate diagnoses before you settle on one; don't just pattern-match to a canned objection.

Here's an argument I've been toying with, and I can't find the exit. I'm not sure wether it's a sound argument with a wild conclusion or a slick bit of smuggling. It starts from something I took to be obviously true, and it ends somewhere I refuse to live.

(1) The badness of human extinction is not just the sum of the individual deaths. Imagine we learn that a meteor will wipe out everyone in six weeks, painlessly. The deaths are bad. But there's an extra loss: a cancer researcher who would keep running trials during those six weeks wouldn't just be grieving the deaths; her research itself would seem to lose its point, even though it would still extend some of those remaining lives. The point of the research depended on there being someone after the catastrophe to benefit.

(2) So a lot of what we do has its point or value only on the assumption that humanity continues after us. This isn't about personal survival — the researcher may never see the cure — it's about successors, in Scheffler's sense of an "afterlife" that's just the ongoing life of the species after one's own death.

(3) But successors are in the same boat. Their projects will equally depend on there being people after them. There's no generation whose activities are self-supporting in a way that could stop the chain with them.

(4) So the point of my current projects is hostage to whether the human line continues indefinitely. There's no principled cutoff: if the chain can't stop at the researcher's generation, it can't stop at the 10,000th generation either, because by (3) each generation depends on the next.

(5) Therefore I should care violently about the deep, deep future. A tiny chance that humanity goes extinct in, say, 10,000 years is a tiny chance that everything I'm doing right now loses its point, and the expected value at stake is enormous. By this logic I should be willing to live substantially worse now to shave small risks of extinction in the 13th millennium.

(6) But that seems unhinged. Playing in a band, teaching someone to read, breeding a better tomato — the point of these things doesn't become more or less real depending on what happens in the year 12024. So (5) must be false, and the argument must fail somewhere.

I'd love to keep (1) and (2); they feel true. And I'd cheerfully ditch (5), but I can't see how to keep (1) through (4) while giving up (5) without special pleading.
