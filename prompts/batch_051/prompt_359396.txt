ok so I just got out of a long arguement with a friend about whether a perfect simulation of a brain counts morally, and I blurted out a distinction Ive been turning over for a while. I want it checked before I use it again, because I'm not sure if it's a real idea or just a metaphor with a moral ribbon on it.

I call it owned computation vs. merely enacted computation. The claim is that moral status attaches to computations that are owned by the system running them. A computation is owned when the system has a model of its own state and uses that model to pick among its possible next states—the output feeds back into the process that produced it. If there's no self-model in the loop, the computation is just enacted: someone designed or described it, and the credit (or blame) should go to them, not to the system.

Examples to make it grip. A mouse in pain owns its computation: the nociceptive signal updates a body-state/self model and that biases future action. A termostat doesn't: it computes an error between setpoint and current temp, but it has no model of its own condition as a thermostat, so the "computation" belongs to the engineer. A chess engine that can print a verbal evaluation of its own position still owns nothing, because it never uses that report to revise the process doing the evaluating. My friend pushed back that this would grant moral status to any self-monitoring AI and deny it to a person under deep anesthesia or a newborn, which he thought was absurd. I thought it at least draws the line where attributions feel right.

Is this a real distinction, or is it just "self-model" + "moral relevance" stitched together? What's the nearest thing in existing debates (I dimly remember higher-order thought and global workspace and that class of view, but I don't want to strawman them), and does my version say anything they don't? And where does it break—what does it wrongly count in, what does it wrongly count out? Give me the strongest version of the idea and then the most damaging cases against it. And answer in American English, please.
