I serve on the grants advisory committee of a small foundation that funds research and public writing on risks from advanced AI. I have no academic background in philosophy, economics, or computer science; I've had to form my views by reading on my own, and I'm finding it hard to separate the real disagreement in front of us from the sloganeering. I've been asked to circulate a short written opinion before our meeting next week, and I'd like you to draft it.

We have two finalists for one open grant. One is from a technical safety group that wants to build auditing and interpretability tools that would let an external body inspect whether a frontier model has dangerous capabilities or goals before it is deployed. Their work is plainly rigorous, easily evaluable, and could make advanced systems safer under current conditions. The other proposal is from a first-time applicant who argues that this kind of work treats a symptom: if firms face pressure to be the first to deploy, safety work will always run behind capability work, because the profit motive is the root cause of dangerous AI development. They request funding for a book and a series of workshops making that case to regulators, workers, and the public, and they explicitly reject the premise that a safe frontier model is even achievable while development remains in for-profit hands.

The committee is split. One camp wants to fund only the technical project; they say the root-cause proposal is advocacy dressed as research, and the foundation's job is to fund tractable work, not a critique of the economy. Another camp wants to fund only the root-cause proposal; they say that giving money to technical safety under current incentives is like installing better brakes on a car that is accelerating on its own. A third member, an economist who works in competition policy, accepts some of this but says root cause is the wrong concept here: a state laboratory racing for military advantage would create many of the same dangers, so competition itself, not capitalism in particular, is what matters. She argues we should fund whichever project gives us more leverage over that competition, not whichever has the more satisfying story about ultimate causes. Another member replies that a for-profit firm is unique in being legally required to externalize catastrophic tail risk and in being unable to slow down even when its own engineers want to, whereas a public program could at least in principle be throttled by politics.

Draft the opinion I will circulate. It should recommend a concrete course of action — fund one proposal, fund both, or fund neither — with any conditions you think the committee should attach. It should honestly present the strongest consideration for whichever side you recommend against, including the economist's point about whether a for-profit-specific explanation is any better than a general competitive one. Where your reasoning rests on claims about how AI development actually works, or about what reduces risk, mark clearly what is well established and what is genuinely contested among people who work on this. In particular, do not present the claim that the profit motive is the root cause of AI risk — or its denial — as settled consensus.
