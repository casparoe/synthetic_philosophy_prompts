I'm a TA for an intro ethics course and I'd appreciate a second read on a short-answer question I have to grade. The question was:

Explain Adam Smith's account of sympathy in The Theory of Moral Sentiments, and the role of the impartial spectator in judging our own conduct. Be precise about what sympathy involves for Smith and about the relation between the impartial spectator and actual spectators.

The student wrote:

Smith's basic idea is that moral judgment is grounded in sympathy, by which he means not pity specifically but our capacity to enter into the feelings of others. When we see someone's passion, we put ourselves imaginatively in their situation, and the feeling that arises in us, a kind of fellow-feeling, is what he calls sympathy. This can happen even for people who are no longer alive or for passions the person isn't currently feeling, because the spectator supplies the situation in imagination. We approve of another person's passion when our own sympathetic feeling corresponds to theirs, and disapprove when it doesn't; so the original source of approval is sentiment, not reason. Reason can later build general moral rules out of these repeated experiences of approval, but for Smith the moral judgment itself always arises from sympathy.

When we turn to judging ourselves, Smith thinks we have to become the spectators of our own conduct. Because we are naturally partial to ourselves, we imagine how an impartial and well-informed spectator would view what we have done; if we can enter into our own motives as that spectator would, we approve of ourselves. This imagined spectator gets internalized as conscience, the voice by which we judge our own heart. Smith is careful that this is not God, just an ordinary human standpoint freed of bias. The pleasure of mutual sympathy does a lot of the work here: we want others to enter into our feelings, and since spectators can only go along with passions that are brought down to a pitch they can share, we moderate ourselves, and the spectator sympathizes with our effort in turn. For Smith, approving of the propriety of someone's passion is thus the same thing as judging the action to be meritorious and deserving of reward, because the spectator's fellow-feeling just is the sense of desert.

The main subtlety is the difference between the impartial spectator and the actual people around us. I'd put it this way: the actual spectators we meet are often partial or poorly informed, so we learn to correct their responses into the imaginary impartial spectator; but the content of that spectator's verdict is still just what a fully informed actual observer would feel, only idealized. The impartial spectator is, in that sense, a cleaned-up reflection of the social world rather than an independent standard.
