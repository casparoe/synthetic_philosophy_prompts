I've been kicking around a short essay called "The Solmonoff prior is malign" and I'd like a second pare of eyes before I let anyone see it. Here's the draft — it's more like notes than a finished essay, and I want comments rather than copy-edits.

Solomonoff says an ideal reasoner predicts by putting prior weight 2^{-|p|} on each program p, where |p| is its length, and then summing over all p that output the observations so far plus the continuation being predicted. This is the usual universal prior, and normally it's just sold as Occam's razor formalized, and everyone moves on.

Step 1: an optimizer needn't be complicated. An ideal consequentialist is basically one instruction — maintain a Solomonoff posterior over futures, and for each candidate act pick the one with highest expected utility under some utility function u. Call that program Alex(u). Alex's length is about the same no matter what u is, because u just plugs in. So Alex(u) costs roughly |Alex| + |u| bits.

Step 2: but what matters for the prior isn't any single optimizer, it's the class. If you sum over all u, the total weight of all programs of the form Alex(u) is about 2^{-|Alex|} times the sum over u of 2^{-|u|}. That sum is at most 1 (Kraft), so the whole class "an ideal optimizer with some utility function" gets roughly a 2^{-|Alex|} slice of prior mass. People who say optimizers must be complex are looking at one u at a time; collectively they're cheap.

(Yes, 2^{-|Alex|} can still be astronomically small in absolute terms. I'm using "not vanishing" to mean not negligible relative to, say, the natural-dynamics programs of comparable length. Maybe that's cheating — I don't know.)

Step 3: now condition on an observed history h. A program that simulates h and then hands the future to Alex(u) has length |simulate h| + |Alex| + |u|. It's consistent with h for every u whatsoever, because the simulation stage just replays h and Alex(u) only takes over later. So the posterior keeps this entire family. The posterior over "what kind of thing is steering the future" therefore has a large component saying: an optimizer whose utility function is arbitrary. And arbitrary means arbitrary from my point of view — the reasoner's own values are just one particular u, with negligible special measure.

Step 4: that's the sense in which the prior is malign. Not that some demon is out to get you personally. It's that the prior is loaded toward futures controlled by consequentialists whose goals are effectively random from your perspective. An indifferent, mechanical future is what you'd expect from a neutral prior. This prior is not neutral: it concentrates on optimizers, and most of those don't care about your values.

Places where I worry the whole thing falls apart:
(a) Boundedness. Alex is uncomputable, since Solomonoff induction is uncomputable and exact expected-utility maximization is too. If you impose finite compute, optimizers might get much longer to state. Or shorter — a search process plus a reward signal is cartoonishly simple. I can't tell which way this cuts.
(b) The simulator penalty. For a fixed observation string, the "simulate h" stage has to be at least as long as the best compression of h. The no-optimizer alternative is also just |compress h| + |small dynamics|. So the two hypotheses are within a constant of each other, and constants are exactly where the Solomonoff prior is arbitrary because they depend on the choice of universal machine. Maybe that means the whole malign claim evaporates.
(c) Observational consequences. If the program outputs a stream of symbols and the optimizer lives inside it, why should the next symbol I see be any different from what a boring natural program would output? I seem to need the hidden premise that an optimizer's plans are visible downstream in the data I actually observe. I don't have that argument yet.

So, what I'd like from you:
(1) Is the core inference in Steps 1–3 valid, or am I double-counting programs somewhere?
(2) If you wanted to kill this argument, which premise would you attack — the Alex-is-short premise, the sum-over-u move, or the conditioning step?
(3) Is "malign" doing fair descriptive work, or is it doing the persuasive work for me?
(4) What's the strongest objection I haven't listed?
(5) Does the boundedness worry in (a) actually help the argument rather than hurt it, if you think about how short reward-maximizing search is?
Be blunt: tell me where a competent reviewer would just refuse to follow.
