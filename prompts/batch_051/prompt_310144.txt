I'm preparing a podcast episode built around a long-form interview with David Hume on Of Suicide, and I want to avoid the usual format where the guest just restates the essay's conclusion. I've been rereading the part where he tries to disarm the claim that suicide usurps God's providence, and I keep getting stuck on one of his analogies. He writes: "A house, which falls by its own weight, is not brought to ruin by his providence more than one destroyed by the hands of men; nor are the human faculties less his workmanship than the laws of motion and gravitation." I'd like you to draft eight to ten interview questions that treat this passage as the load-bearing move in his argument and force him to say exactly what work the analogy does. Each question should include an anticipated reply and a follow-up keyed to that reply. At least one question should press on whether falling houses and deliberate human acts are really on a par in his system; if he says yes, follow up with the person who chooses to pull someone out from under a collapsing building; if he says no, ask for the principle that marks human agency as special without reintroducing the special providence he has just rejected. Include a question about whether his reasoning commits him to holding that preventing a suicide violates providence just as much as committing one, and a question about whether it rules out criticizing any deliberate alteration of the course of nature, from damming a river to altering an ecosystem's reproductive cycle. End with a question that tries to force him to say what residual content duty to oneself can have once suicide is classed as a neutral part of animal economy. I want the sequence to make his standard reply progressively harder to use.
