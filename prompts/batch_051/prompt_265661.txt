I am a novelist by trade, and I have spent my working life studying how a shapely structure can be false to life. That background has left me holding two commitments about the role of elegance in mathematics and physics that pull against each other, and I do not know which one ought to give way.

On one side is what I will call the evidential view of elegance. Its claim, put strongly, is that beauty in a theory—economy of postulates, symmetry, compactness of formulation, the surprising unification of apparently unrelated phenomena—is genuinely evidence that the theory is true or at least on the right track. The argument is not merely that elegant theories are nicer to work with. It is that a theory which assumes very little and yet predicts a great deal, with constraints sharp enough that the prediction could easily have failed, would be an extraordinary coincidence if the theory were false; the survival of such a constrained prediction is exactly the kind of event that should increase our confidence. There are canonical cases where the elegant theory won and won decisively. And there is a formal gloss one can put on this: simpler hypotheses, or hypotheses with higher internal symmetry, can carry higher prior probability or sharper confirmatory power, so elegance need not be a mystical sign of truth—it can be folded into an account of confirmation.

On the other side is a firm intuition I cannot shake, and it comes directly from my craft. A novel can have impeccable symmetry of plot, a motif that returns at exactly the right moments, a final chapter that resolves every thread—and still be false about how human beings actually are. Beauty of structure and fidelity to the world are not the same excellence, and I have learned to distrust the kind of neatness that flatters the maker. When I carry that suspicion into the history of science, I see elegance doing ambiguous work: theories that were once held with aesthetic loyalty long after the data strained against them, and beautiful dead ends remembered as objects of admiration rather than instruments of discovery. My intuition is that we systematically overcount the successes because the failures are forgotten or reclassified as mere formalism. More deeply, I suspect that elegance is a property of the presentation, or a fact about what our minds find compressible, memorable, and teachable—not a channel of information about the world. The universe, so far as I can tell, is not obliged to be beautiful to us.

I am not looking for a verdict that splits the difference. I want a negotiated account: under what precise conditions, if any, does elegance constitute evidence, and under what conditions is it decoration, a mnemonic for scientists, or even a source of error? Should the intuition be debunked as the occupational bias of a novelist who professionally distrusts neatness, or should the evidential view be amended with a distinction that preserves its force while conceding the intuition's point? I have a half-formed suspicion that the argument collapses because "elegance" is doing at least two jobs at once—elegance as a compact encoding of data already in hand, and elegance as a risky prediction about structure not yet seen. Tell me whether that distinction can bear the weight, or whether I am merely carving the concept to save both sides. What would it take for the theory to be the thing that yields, what would it take for the intuition to be the thing that yields, and is there a reading on which both survive without equivocation?
