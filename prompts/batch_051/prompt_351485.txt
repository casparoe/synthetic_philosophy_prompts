Ok here's one that's been rattling around since I fell down a Mencius-versus-the-Mohists rabbit hole. Suppose that in 136 BCE or thereabouts, when Emperor Wu of Han is setting up the official curriculum and making mastery of a textual canon the main road into state office, he backs the Mohist canons instead of the Confucian Five Classics. From then on the state selects and promotes officials on a curriculum built around jian'ai — impartial care, or universal love depending on the translation — along with the Mohist insistence on benefiting everyone, frugality, and promotion by merit rather than birth. Everything else about the Han situation stays the same: the great landed families, the household as the basic tax and corvée unit, the frontier wars, the huge Qin-era legal machinery.

Give me your best reasoned prediction for how the next two centuries of Chinese political and intellectural history actually go. I want a chain of reasoning, not a story. In particular: (1) what, if anything, does an officialdom selected for impartial care do differently in practice about kin-based patronage, tax collection, and the slow accumulation of power by big families? (2) Does jian'ai as a state ideology scale to running a huge empire, or does it get hollowed out into something like impartiality toward everyone below and graded loyalty upward, the way Legalism's harsh uniformity often got softened in practice? (3) Where does Confucian graded love — care starts with parents and children and extends outward — survive once the state stops promoting it: in village religion, in the family, as an opposition ideology? Does it come back, and through which institutions? (4) What is the strongest argument that the change would matter less than the ideology suggests, i.e., that the Han state would just absorb Mohist slogans and keep operating like a state? For each step in the chain, be explicit about what you're confident in and where you're guessing, and say which fact about Han society would have to be different to overturn that prediction.

Oh, and write the answer in American English.
