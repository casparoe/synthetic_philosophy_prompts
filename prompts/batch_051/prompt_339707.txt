I have been trying to get a precise grip on when deference to another agent's judgment is a virtue rather than a vice, and I keep failing to settle a real-feeling case. Here are the criteria I currently use. On my view, an act of deference is virtuous only if all five are met:

1. Comparative competence: the deferrer has good reason to think the authority is better placed on this particular decision, not merely of higher rank.
2. Endorsable role: the authority's standing is one the deferrer can reflectively endorse, rather than one they merely fear or are habituated to.
3. Scope: the particular matter falls inside the legitimate scope of the authority's role.
4. Monitoring: the deferrer retains an active disposition to notice and interrogate signs that the authority is mistaken.
5. Non-outsourcing: the deference does not amount to a standing policy of surrendering a domain of judgment the deferrer has a professional duty eventually to exercise themselves.

Now the case that is giving me trouble.

An emergency-medicine resident is working a mass-casualty incident under a hospital protocol that incorporates a machine-learning triage system. The system assigns patients a priority score meant to allocate scarce life-saving resources. The protocol refers to the system's score as having the standing of a senior consultant: residents are expected to follow it, and overrides are permitted only with a written justification. The resident is a mediocre-to-decent clinician with two years of experience.

A patient arrives with a rare endocrine condition the resident has some familiarity with. The system rates the patient as low priority. The resident's own clinical impression is that the patient is higher risk than the score suggests, but the resident cannot articulate a crisp pathophysiological explanation on the spot; it is partly a sense that "this presentation doesn't fit the usual rules." The resident knows two relevant facts: the system was validated mainly on ordinary adult emergency-department populations, and the protocol was adopted after a safety review the resident has never read. The resident follows the score, documents a mild concern in the chart, and moves on. The patient has a bad outcome of the kind proper prioritization might have prevented.
