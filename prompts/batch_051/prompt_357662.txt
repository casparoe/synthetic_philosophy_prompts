You are an expert on institutional design and political philosophy, with a working knowledge of law and economics and public choice theory. Think carefully and work through the question step by step; before committing to a conclusion, brainstorm the incentives facing each category of actor.

A host country proposes the following charter-city arrangement, which its advocates present as a genuine way to opt out of the civil side of the state while remaining on land. The city's territory is governed by a special statute with these provisions:

1. Exit from civil law. Each adult resident must annually contract with exactly one licensed legal services provider, a private firm licensed by the city, for a package that includes formation and enforcement of contracts, a property registry, tort adjudication, and family law. Residents pay providers directly and owe no civil-law-related taxes to the city or the host state. Residents may switch providers once a year; providers may not refuse an applicant, but may charge different prices within a regulated band.

2. Retained state functions. The host state retains a minimal criminal code binding on everyone, an environmental code, and a land-value tax on every parcel. Residents may not contract around these.

3. Cross-provider disputes. When two residents with different providers disagree, the case goes to a standing arbitration panel. The panel's members are appointed by the licensed providers; an appeal lies to a joint city–state conflict-of-laws court, which applies a code written by the host state and the city at founding.

4. Spillover fee. Every resident also pays the host state a monthly spillover fee, set by a formula that estimates the resident's use of roads, public health surveillance, port facilities, and national defense. The formula can be revised every five years by a joint city–state board.

Walk through how this arrangement would actually behave, treating it as a mechanism rather than taking a position for or against it. Be concrete about who the actors are, what each is incentivized to do, and in what order the predictable pathologies would appear. In particular: how would providers compete, and would competition in law drive legal packages toward quality, toward the cheapest enforceability, or toward something else? How would residents use the right to switch providers, and how would that affect the content of the packages on offer? How would the spillover-fee formula and the retained criminal and environmental codes become bargaining sites, and who would capture them? Where would the system be gamed first, and which failure modes could be fixed by amendment without turning the city back into an ordinary state? Finally, offer a verdict on the deeper conceptual issue: in this design, has a resident genuinely opted out of the state, or has the state been re-created at one remove through mandatory licensing, the retained criminal monopoly, the tax power, and the conflict-of-laws court?
