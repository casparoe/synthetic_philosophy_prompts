I'm leading a two-hour seminar for final-year law students and philosophy undergraduates on naked statistical evidence, built around the blue bus case (the plaintiff can prove the defendant ran most of the buses on the route but cannot identify the particular bus) and the gatecrasher case (a spectator is selected at random from a crowd where it is more likely than not that any given spectator entered without paying). The students will arrive thinking that evidence which makes a fact 80 percent likely is evidence like any other; I want the session to make the real puzzle vivid rather than survey case law. Plan the explanatory arc: the distinction that must be in place before the cases bite; which case should carry the session and what its presentation must guard against; the objections students will raise in roughly the order they will raise them — that statistical evidence is unreliable, that a sufficiently high probability should be sufficient, that expected-loss or error-cost reasoning already explains the asymmetry, that the real problem is jury psychology or fairness to the parties — and how each stage of the sequence should absorb the objection before the next one arises. Also specify what should be deliberately set aside at this level, and end with a check that would show they have grasped why a bare statistic can justify full personal belief without licensing a legal finding, rather than merely showing they have learned that courts reject it.
