In The Social Contract, Book I, Chapter 5, Rousseau interrupts the question of how a people selects its leaders. He argues that before one can sensibly ask about a government, one must explain how a scattered multitude becomes a people in the first place; that prior act, he says, cannot itself be an act of the already-constituted people without circularity, and he treats it as the true foundation of society.

Now consider an asymmetry in ordinary democratic judgments. If a subset of an existing electorate were disenfranchised—say, all naturalized citizens or all residents under twenty-five—we would regard that as a profound injustice, not merely one policy choice among others. But when a demos simply leaves outsiders out of its decision-making, even outsiders whose lives its decisions plainly shape—immigrants, neighboring communities, future generations—we tend to treat that exclusion as unremarkable, almost as a background condition of self-government. One might think the difference is obvious: the first group is already inside the demos, while the second is not. But Rousseau’s point is that this inside/outside line is exactly what needs to be justified, not an administrative fact to be taken for granted.

Is the asymmetry between wrongfully excluding current members and permissibly not including nonmembers defensible? Or does the boundary problem show that both are acts of constituting the demos, differing only in how comfortable we are with the status quo? Take a stand.
