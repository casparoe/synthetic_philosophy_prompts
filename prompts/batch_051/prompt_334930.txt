Me and a friend stayed up way too late arguing about wether the dark forest hypothesis in Liu Cixin's books is anything more than a one-shot prisoner's dilemma with doomsday payoffs. His version: two civilisations detect each other, and each can stay quiet or strike first. If both stay quiet, both survive (worth 1 each). If one strikes and the other stays quiet, the striker wins the galaxy alone (worth 3) and the quiet one is wiped out (worth 0). If both strike, both homeworlds are lost, but each side's already-launched offworld seed ships give it a 0.2 chance of carrying on. Since 3 beats 1 and 0.2 beats 0, striking looks dominant, which is why the galaxy goes silent. I thought this was way too clean, because in the story the first strike is basically clean and decisive. So I changed exactly one thing: make the weapon dirty. After you launch, there's a probability p that the launch signature, the gravitational lens of the relativistic projectile, or the target's last-second warning gets you traced by some third civilisation, which destroys you before you can go dark again. The target you shot still dies; the extra risk falls entirely on the shooter.

So a clean unilateral strike is worth 3, but a dirty one (you strike, they stay quiet) has expected value 3(1-p). Everything else stays as above.

Write out the payoff matrix and run the calculation. I keep going back and forth on three things: (1) for what range of p does striking still dominate staying quiet? (2) once p passes that threshold, does the game turn into a stag hunt — mutual quiet and mutual strike as two equilibria, with trust as the real problem — and if so, which equilibrium is payoff-dominant and which is risk-dominant? (3) does the chain-of-suspicion argument from the books still have any force in that high-p regime, i.e. can it push rational players into the bad equilibrium even after the payoffs stop favouring a first strike on its own?
