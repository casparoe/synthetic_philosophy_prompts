Okay so I just had a long arguement with a friend about the void-for-vagueness doctrine and I'm trying to figure out what actually underpins it. He kept saying the whole thing is about fair notice—people should be able to know what's illegal before they act. But then we looked at this passage from Justice Marshall's opinion in Grayned v. City of Rockford, 408 U.S. 104 (1972), where the Court upheld a Rockford anti-noise ordinance that said:

"(N)o person, while on public or private grounds adjacent to any building in which a school or any class thereof is in session, shall willfully make or assist in the making of any noise or diversion which disturbs or tends to disturb the peace or good order of such school session or class thereof. . . ."

The phrase 'disturbs or tends to disturb' is not exactly a model of precision, but the Court still didn't strike it down. My friend thinks that proves notice can't be the whole story, because no one can know in advance exactly what noise level crosses the line. So now I'm trying to inventory the real principles behind the doctrine.

The rationales I already know:
1. Fair notice: people should be able to know what is forbidden ahead of time.
2. Preventing arbitrary or discriminatory enforcement: vague laws invite police and prosecutors to apply them unevenly.
3. First Amendment chilling: vague statutes make people self-censor rather than risk punishment.

I want one more rationale, not a rewording, special case, or combination of those three. It should be the kind of thing that could explain why the Grayned ordinance survives despite its imprecision, or at least something a judge or legal philosopher could coherently endorse. Then tell me honestly whether it holds up.
