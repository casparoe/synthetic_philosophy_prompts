I got into an argument with a friend who's always going on about how we should give the competent and well-informed more political power, and how it's absurd that a random retiree's vote counts exactly as much as a policy analyst's. I didn't know what to do with the conversation, but it made me notice something odd about institutions we already have. In the legal system I'm most familiar with, the most consequential epistemic task ordinary citizens are ever given is deciding guilt beyond a reasonable doubt; and we hand that to twelve amateurs selected basically at random. The people deciding arn't required to know anything, and in many places the lawyers are actually forbidden from using cognitive traits as a basis for keeping someone off or putting someone the jury; they can strike for all sorts of other reasons, but not for being bad at reasoning. Meanwhile the same society won't let a person cut hair, teach fifth-grade science, or prescribe antibiotics without years of vetted training and some kind of license, even though the stakes of any individual one of those decisions are usually way lower then a criminal conviction. So there's a split: at the very top of the stakes scale we are strongly anti-expert, and near the bottom we are strongly pro-expert. What theory of knowledge and democratic equality does this pattern presuppose? Is there an implicit view that makes it coherent, or is it two different theories stapled together, with different parts doing different jobs? And what would have to change at the institutional level if we actually adopted my friend's view, or instead the view that ordinary people should always have the final say on everything?

Please write your answer in the following sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths:

1. The pattern, sharpened (about 150 words). Restate the two sides more precisely than I did, including the jury-side design features that make the split genuinely strange (random selection, no competence screening, the demanding standard of proof).
2. What the jury side strictly presupposes (about 250 words). Pull apart the reading on which juries exist to maximize accurate verdicts from the reading on which they exist as a check on state power and a right of defendants to be judged by their peers, and say which of these the actual design features commit us to.
3. What the licensing side strictly presupposes (about 200 words). Is it an epistemic principle about competence, or is it mostly consumer protection with an expertise-shaped justification bolted on?
4. The implicit theory, if any, that holds the split together (about 300 words). Give your best candidate unified view, then your best case for there being no single view at all.
5. Where the practice is underdetermined (about 200 words). Which rival theories would license exactly the same institutions, and what would distinguish them?
6. What changes under a swapped presupposition (about 250 words). Run it in both directions: full tilt toward the view that the knowledgeable should rule, and full tilt toward ordinary people having the final say.
7. Bottom line (about 100 words).
