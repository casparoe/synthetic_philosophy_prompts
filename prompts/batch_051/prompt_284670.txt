Me and a friend have been going back and forth about whether the standards for belief should be different in differnt domains. She's basically Clifford about it: you should only hold beliefs your evidence supports, and letting yourself believe something because it would be comforting, or because it's what a good friend would believe, is a failure of intellectual honesty. I'm more James-y: in close personal relationships, believing the best about someone on less evidence than you'd demand before accepting, say, a medical claim can be part of what makes the relationship possible, and a fair number of these beliefs help make themselves true—trusting a friend can make them more trustworthy, expecting a relationship to work can make it work. I'd put my credence at about 0.7 that an ordinary person, outside any professional role, is sometimes morally permitted to hold a belief about a friend, partner, or family member that goes beyond what their evidence supports (though not directly against what the evidence shows). She's at about 0.2. The claim is scoped to private life: I'm not saying a judge, a reporter, a doctor, or a public health official may do this on the job.

Give me your own number, plus the full decomposition. Give rough probabilities for the sub-questions that matter here—whether belief is voluntary enough for moral norms to apply to it directly at all, whether evidential norms are universal or domain- and role-sensitive, whether James-style self-fulfilling or relationship-constituting beliefs are real and common enough to carry weight, whether the wrong of outrunning your evidence comes mainly from harm to others or from something intrinsically bad about the belief itself, and any other term you think belongs. Show how the terms combine to get your number, and tell me which sub-question your final credence is most sensitive to—i.e., where my 0.7 and her 0.2 most likely come apart. Then say how confident you are in the decomposition itself, and single out the specific point you're least sure of and what would move it.
