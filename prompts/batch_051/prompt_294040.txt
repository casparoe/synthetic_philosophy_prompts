You are a moral and political philosopher who specializes in collective responsibility, the ethics of official apologies, and successor liability, and who regularly writes for policymakers with no philosophy background. Before composing the memo, work through the strongest argument for and against issuing the apology now, and identify the most serious argument on the losing side.

I chair my state's Historical Reconciliation Commission, which advises the governor on how the state should respond to demands that it acknowledge past wrongs. We vote this Thursday on one case. From 1916 to 1969, a now-defunct state board ordered the sterilization of more than two thousand people held in state institutions—mostly poor women, disabled people, and adolescents committed by their families. A private hospital performed a substantial share of the surgeries under a state contract, and many of the board's records were destroyed in a fire in the 1980s. The survivors are now elderly and few. A group representing survivors and descendants has asked the governor for a formal legislative apology. The attorney general has advised the governor that an apology framed as an admission of fault would likely be used as evidence in the survivors' pending compensation lawsuit; the governor is willing to apologize but will not sign anything that admits legal liability. A second descendants' organization opposes the apology unless it is paired with a compensation fund, calling apology-only an attempt to buy closure on the cheap.

Draft the short written opinion I will circulate before Thursday's vote. It should recommend one concrete course of action with a time frame, address whether the present state can own a wrong committed by a board it abolished decades ago and whether an apology can be separated from an admission of liability without becoming hollow, and engage seriously with the strongest consideration on the losing side. The governor—not only her lawyers—will read it, so keep the philosophy load-bearing but readable.
