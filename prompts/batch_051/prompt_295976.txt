Heres a weird asymmetry in how we talk about forgiveness. On the one hand people say forgiveness is a gift the victim is free to give or withhold — nobody has to forgive, and if someone forgives instantly we tend to think theyre a pushover or not taking the wrong seriously. On the other hand, those same people will say that if the offender has apologized sincerely, made real amends, and changed, then at some point the victim is being petty or even cruel for not forgiving them. So forgiving is optional for the victim, yet the victim can also be morally blamed for not doing it. Is that a genuine inconsistency, or is there a principle that makes both judgments come out true? If you think forgiveness can become obligatory once the offender has done enough, tell me how that squares with it being a gift — does an obligation to forgive destroy the thing that made forgiveness valuable in the first place? And then the harder version: do you ever owe forgiveness when the offender hasn't apologized at all? Why does our practice make repentance do so much work if the victim's act is the one that's supposed to matter? I want the strongest account you can give of this pair of judgments, and an honest verdict on whether you'd keep both.
