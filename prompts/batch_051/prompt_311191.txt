I cover science and ideas for a general-interest outlet, and I'm drafting a long-form explainer on the argument from illusion and the disjunctivist reply. Instead of opening with the usual back-and-forth, I want to open from a neutral specification: what would any adequate theory of perceptual experience have to deliver, regardless of which side it ends up on? Write that specification for me.

Produce the desiderata list: the phenomena a theory must account for, the cases it must get right, and the distinctions it must not collapse, with the motivating case or consideration behind each. Work through at least these, and add anything else you regard as a genuine requirement: veridical perception; illusion (a straight stick half-submerged in water; the Müller-Lyer lines, which never stop looking unequal even when one knows the trick); hallucination (a voice no one else in the room hears; a vivid scene in a fever dream); subjectively indistinguishable pairs of good and bad cases; the fact that perception is causally mediated by light, sound, and neural processing; and the epistemic role of perception in justifying beliefs about the world.

For each requirement, mark it as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp, and say why. Keep three things apart as you do: (i) constraints any theory must meet; (ii) contested premises that one camp treats as data, such as the common kind assumption or the transparency of experience; and (iii) mere methodological tastes, such as parsimony or a unified account of good and bad cases.

Then give me the part I most want. Identify which subsets of these desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied, and state the trilemma as cleanly as you can. I suspect the sharpest version is: (a) a veridical experience and a subjectively indistinguishable hallucination have the same phenomenal character; (b) the phenomenal character of a veridical experience is constituted by the mind-independent objects one perceives; and (c) a hallucination contains no object, mind-independent or mind-dependent, that could constitute that character. No theory can hold all three. Tell me whether that is right, and qualify it where it needs qualifying — for instance, over whether sameness of phenomenal character amounts to sameness of fundamental kind of state, and over how a disjunctivist can grant subjective indistinguishability while denying the common kind assumption. Then say which contender sacrifices which horn: sense-datum theory, representationalism or intentionalism, and naive realism in its disjunctivist form.

Keep the scorecard brief: no more than 300 words on how the three contenders fare against the list; the criteria themselves are the deliverable. Do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; the piece has to carry the structure of the problem on its own.

Hard length limit: 1,200 words total.
