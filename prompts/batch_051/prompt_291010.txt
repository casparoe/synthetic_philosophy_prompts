Here's a parallel I keep noticing and can't decide about. When people reach for Munich, Vietnam, or a supposed 'Thucydides trap' to justify or attack a policy, they take a handfull of unusually vivid cases and turn them into an all-purpose rule — appeasement invites aggression, interventions become quagmires, rising powers make war near-inevitable. That looks to me a lot like overfitting in machine learning: you fit a high-variance rule to a small sample that was selected for salience rather than representativeness, then confidently apply it to new cases that differ along many dimensions you never explicitly modeled. Both even share the same blind spot: the many similar-looking cases that didn't become famous get silently left out of the dataset, so the rule is never checked against anything like a base rate. But maybe the analogy breaks because a policymaker can notice disanalogies, argue about whether a case really resembles Munich, and hold a principle independently of the examples that suggested it, which a fitted model can't do. Give me a committed verdict: is this a deep structural point about induction from salient examples, a superficial analogy, or something specific in between? Lay out the mapping, say what would have to be true for results to transfer, and name at least one thing the parallel seems to predict that turns out false.
