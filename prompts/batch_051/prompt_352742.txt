I am a junior at a public high school, and I serve on the student council. Last week we held a meeting to decide whether to propose a new attendance policy to the administration. The policy would penalize students who miss more than a certain number of unexcused absences by barring them from after-school clubs and sports. The council president, who is well liked and a strong speaker, opened the meeting by saying he had already discussed the matter with several team captains and that they supported it. He then allowed about twenty minutes of open discussion, but whenever anyone raised a concern—for example, that students with part-time jobs or family responsibilities would be most affected—he would respond quickly and move the conversation forward. Several quieter members never spoke. At one point, a sophomore asked whether students who would be affected had been consulted, and the president replied that there was no time to survey everyone and that the council had to make a responsible decision. The advisor then said we should not let the discussion drag on and called for a vote. The proposal passed easily.

Afterward, a friend of mine who voted against it said the whole process felt like a formality, as if the outcome had been decided before anyone walked into the room. Another friend defended the outcome, saying the debate was open, everyone had a chance to speak, and the majority ruled. I keep going back and forth. On the one hand, nothing overtly unfair happened; no one was threatened or silenced. On the other hand, I suspect that the way the discussion was structured made it impossible for weaker arguments to be exposed as weak and stronger ones to carry the day on their own merits. Is my unease justified? What would it actually mean for a decision like this to be reached through genuine deliberation rather than just a vote, and does a formally open discussion fall short in ways I could point to at our next meeting?
