(1) Corporations are legal persons: they can hold property, enter contracts, and sue or be sued in their own name.
(2) Corporations have no consciousness, biology, or mind, so whatever makes them legal persons can't be a matter of consciousness, biology, or mind.
(3) So legal personhood is a purely formal status: an entity is a legal person just in case the legal system treats it as a unit that can bear rights and duties.
(4) If legal personhood is a purely formal status, then the only real barrier to AI legal personhood is whether the law can assign AI systems the same bundle of formal capacities (owning assets, being a contracting party, being liable, etc.).
(5) There's no in-principle reason the law can't assign AI systems that bundle, e.g. by letting an AI-operated entity own assets or register as a contracting party.
(6) Therefore, if corporate personhood is justifiable, then personhood for suitably structured AI systems is justifiable too, and someone who accepts the one but rejects the other is just being inconsistently speciesist.

I suspect the weak spot is somewhere around (3)-(4), something like "legal personhood" is being used as a merely formal status in one premise and then quietly carrying normative weight in the next, but I can't make the charge stick. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and on what reading of it? And if you read it charitably so it does come out valid, what substantive claim has been smuggled in?
