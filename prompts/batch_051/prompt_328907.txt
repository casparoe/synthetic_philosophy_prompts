i do incident reviews for a mid-size logistics company, and the recurring fight on our team is whether two seperate anomalies are 'the same mechanism' or just happen to look alike. i've started pushing a rule i call fix-equivalence: two anomalies count as the same mechanism just in case the same intervention would have prevented both of them, with the background held as it actually was. So a batch of late deliveries all caused by a broken dock-door motor and a batch of late deliveries caused by drivers following the same bad turn-by-turn route are different mechanisms, because fixing the motor does nothing for the route problem and fixing the route does nothing for the motor. But a damaged pallet in dallas and a damaged pallet in memphis that both trace to one faulty clamp on a forklift are the same mechanism, because the one repair prevents both. Two things about the rule bother people. First, it says two anomalies can share the exact same chain of upstream events and still be different mechanisms, if preventing them would require different interventions. Second, it says two anomalies with almost nothing upstream in common can be the same mechanism, as long as their chains converge on the same fixable node. It also seems weirdly sensitive to how you describe the intervention: 'replace the forklift' and 'replace the worn clamp on the forklift' pick out different things, and one might match a second anomaly while the other doesn't. i know about root-cause analysis and common-cause talk, but they strike me as too loose to settle whether we're chasing one mechanism or two. is fix-equivalence actually capturing a real distinction? does something like it already exist under a better name? and where does it break? give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion, covering: the strongest version of my idea, the nearest existing concept, the best case it handles, the hardest counterexample you can think of, and your honest verdict on wether the rule survives.
