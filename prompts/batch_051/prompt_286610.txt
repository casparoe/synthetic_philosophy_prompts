So last night my friend and I went back and forth for hours about his pet idea — that we should eventually use biotech to abolish suffering outright, not just relieve it but engineer away the capacity for it, in humans and maybe animals too. I kept landing on the obvious objection: pain is an alarm system, and without it you get people quietly destroying their bodies, like people born unable to feel pain who don't notice a broken arm. His reply was that you keep the signal and lose the sting; there are patients who insist they can feel the pain but it doesn't bother them, so the alarm can stay without the suffering. Around 2am I walked away with something i've been calling the sentinel problem.

My claim is this. An alarm only works if it can't be ignored; a notification you can snooze will get snoozed exactly when the organism is exhausted, overloaded, or has a local reason to look away. And the only 'can't be ignored' state we've actually encountered is one that hurts. So a finished abolitionist civilization faces a choice: keep a small, quarantined pool of real felt suffering as an invigilator — a few beings, or simulated minds, kept genuinely miserable so some 'something is wrong' signal can't be dismissed — or accept that, after abolition, the civilization as a whole slowly walks into walls. And my suspicion is that people in this debate quietly assume the first horn, because their examples of alarm-without-hurt are always someone calmly noticing a signal in a lab, not someone acting on it when acting matters.

Is this an actual dilemma, or am i just restating the old 'pain is useful' argument in a costume? If it has any content, give me the strongest abolitionist reply, and tell me where the sentinel idea itself breaks before we even get to the ethics of making one being suffer so the rest can be fine. I want it checked, not flattered.
