Robert Nozick’s Wilt Chamberlain example in *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* is usually read as showing that any patterned principle of distributive justice is incompatible with liberty, because people will use their legitimate holdings to make voluntary transfers that disrupt the pattern. Nozick’s version depends on spectators each freely paying an additional amount to watch Chamberlain play, so that the final unequal distribution arises from a long chain of individually unforced choices.

Now consider the same example run through a twenty-first-century digital platform rather than a turnstile. The platform sets personalised prices for each spectator, designs the checkout flow to maximise conversion, uses behavioural data to decide which seats to show at which moments, A/B tests whether to call the fee a booking charge or a service charge, and takes its own cut before any money reaches the athlete. The underlying transfer is still, in some loose sense, chosen: no one is legally coerced into buying a ticket.

Does Nozick’s argument survive this shift? Or does the presence of algorithmic price discrimination and manipulative choice architecture mean the sequence of transfers is no longer sufficiently voluntary to count as just steps in Nozick’s own framework, so the example loses its force against patterned principles of justice? Which premise of the original argument is actually under pressure, and can Nozick’s argument be repaired to do the same work without simply ignoring what the platform has changed?

I want a committed response: take a definite position on whether the argument survives, needs repair, or fails in this setting, and give the reasons rather than laying out both sides.
