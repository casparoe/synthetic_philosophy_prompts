For my extended essay, I am working with a view I have been calling tranquilism: the thesis that only the absence of craving and suffering has final value, and that everything else—pleasure, achievement, knowledge, friendship—matters only as a means to producing, or as a threat to, that absence. I have been compiling objections to this view, and I have five so far:

(1) The neglected-positive-goods objection: if two lives contain the same absence of craving and suffering but one is rich in joy, friendship, and achievement, the view must call the two lives equal in value, which seems wrong.

(2) The paradox of desiring desirelessness: the view appears to tell me to want the absence of all wanting. If the final good includes having no craving, then pursuing it is either impossible, because I must crave the very state I aim to eliminate, or self-defeating, because once I succeed there is no remaining motivation to sustain the state.

(3) The experience-machine objection: a chemically induced state of serene detachment, wholly disconnected from reality, would count as just as good as a hard-won and clear-sighted tranquillity, because the view treats only the inner state as valuable and ignores whether that state answers to the world.

(4) The instrumental-value objection: absence of craving and suffering is not good for its own sake but only because it makes clear thought, free action, and relationship possible; if tranquillity never enabled anything else—if someone were tranquil but inert—it would be worthless, which the view cannot say.

(5) The nonexistence objection: if the only final good is the absence of craving and suffering, then nonexistence achieves that good perfectly, so the view seems committed to saying that death is no worse than a tranquil life, or even that it is just as good, which is hard to accept.

I would like another objection to tranquilism that is genuinely different from these five—not a restatement of one of them, not a special case of one of them, and not a combination of two or more of them. It may attack the view's account of what has value, its account of how one could pursue that value, or an implication the view is committed to.
