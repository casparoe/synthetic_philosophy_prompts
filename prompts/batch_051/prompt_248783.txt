I help organize a monthly discussion group at our local library for adults who enjoy puzzles and good-faith arguments. We usually get between eight and fourteen people, nobody is required to prepare anything in advance, and most of us have never taken a philosophy course. We meet for about ninety minutes. Lately I have found myself going in circles over a sentence I cannot stop thinking about: "This sentence is false." If I decide it is true, then what it says is the case, so it must be false; if I decide it is false, then what it says is not the case, so it must be true. I have no formal training in any of this, but from my own reading I have gathered that the trouble comes from sentences that talk about their own truth, and that people have tried to escape it by forbidding that kind of self-reference, by denying that every sentence has to be either true or false, or by saying some sentences fall into the cracks or land in both boxes at once. What I would like is an activity that lets our group feel the force of this puzzle before any of those fixes are named.

Please design a ninety-minute activity for this group. It should be a runnable game, role-play, or structured deliberation using only simple materials such as index cards, pens, a timer, and a whiteboard—no money, no prior reading, and no devices. The key requirement is that participants run into the paradox themselves: they should get stuck, flip a verdict back and forth, and start inventing their own repairs out of frustration, before any name or theory is introduced. The phrase "Liar paradox" should not be spoken until the debrief. I want people to leave with three things: a felt sense of why self-referential talk about truth is unstable; an appreciation that the obvious repairs, such as just picking one verdict, just banning such sentences, or just allowing neither or both, each come with a cost; and some respect for why reflective people have kept arguing about this for so long.

Please structure your response in titled sections, in this order and roughly at these lengths: "Setup and goals" (one short paragraph: who the activity is for, the time available, and what success looks like); "Materials and rules" (one or two paragraphs, stated plainly enough for a newcomer to run it); "The activity stage by stage" (two or three paragraphs with a rough minute-by-minute timeline, including what the facilitator should and should not say at each point); "Debrief" (two or three paragraphs mapping specific moments from the activity onto the Liar paradox and the main families of response—blocking self-referential truth-talk, rejecting the assumption that every sentence must be either true or false, allowing sentences to be neither true nor false, and allowing sentences to be both—introduced only here and in plain language); "Likely failure points and how to handle them" (one or two paragraphs, candid about what will predictably go wrong the first time and what to do when it does); and "Limits of the activity" (one short paragraph on which part of the idea the exercise cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply).
