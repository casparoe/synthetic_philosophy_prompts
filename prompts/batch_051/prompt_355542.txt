I'm a novelist about four months out from starting a book that hinges on what printing money actually does — the difference between money, liquidity, and wealth, and whether a government printing a lot of currency into existence creates anything real or just shuffles claims around. The book follows a finance minister in a small country who starts monetizing a basic-income program, and I want the economics under the plot to be sound enough that an economist reading it would say, okay, she got the important things right, but not so fussy it turns into a lecture.

I keep hitting the same unresolved-sounding disputes: whether money is just a claim on real output so printing it creates nothing; whether it can create wealth when the economy has idle people and machines; whether the effect is mostly about who touches the new money first before prices adjust; whether inflation just eats the gain and when it tips from manageable to self-reinforcing; whether liquidity and wealth are actually doing different jobs in all this. I have maybe three months of reading time before I draft, and I'd rather spend it on the questions that genuinely change what a scene or a theme can honestly say than on things only a macroeconomist would notice.

So: for that project, which of these questions — or others I'm not listing — are most worth getting clear on first, and which are overated or fine to fudge? Rank them and defend the ranking. For each, say what hangs on it narratively or thematically, and where you'd tell me to deliberately simplify or get something a bit wrong because it won't hurt the book. I don't want a reading list; I want a defended agenda.

Also, tell me how confident you are in the underlying economics, and single out the point you're least sure of. I'd rather know where the account might wobble than have a false sense that it's settled.
