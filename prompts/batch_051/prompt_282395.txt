I have been turning over a question that I put to my seminar in the metaphysics of mind, and I have found that my own verdicts across cases are more idiosyncratic than I would like. I set them out below and ask you to extract the principle that would make sense of them—or to tell me which of them must go.

The question is this: under what conditions, if any, does a composite system count as having a mind when none of its parts individually does?

Let me fix the target with six cases. Grant the stipulations in each case, and concentrate on the philosophical difference my verdicts are trying to mark.

First, an ant colony of the kind Hofstadter imagines in the Ant Fugue dialogue—the kind that, in his story, appears as a conversationalist under the name Aunt Hillary. Its individual ants follow local pheromone rules, and none represents the colony's overall state, yet the colony as a whole reallocates labour, adjusts to shifting food supplies, defends itself, and can be interpreted as representing features of its environment across generations. My verdict: the colony does not have a mind, and I am reluctant to call its behaviour goal-directed except metaphorically.

Second, a human brain. Its neurons are very numerous, none represents a whole scene or a considered intention, there is no central controller, and yet the system as a whole perceives, deliberates, and is conscious. My verdict: this is the clearest case of mindedness there is.

Third, a slime mould of the sort that solves mazes, habituates to repeated stimuli, anticipates periodic changes, and produces efficient network structures. It is a single multinucleate cell with no neurons at all. My verdict: no mind, though I find the case embarrassingly close, and I suspect I may be protecting the word rather than applying a principle.

Fourth, a thermostat. It senses temperature, computes an error signal against a set point, and acts to reduce the discrepancy. My verdict: no mind; the teleological language is merely useful shorthand.

Fifth, a large multinational corporation. It owns property, enters contracts, maintains a corporate memory in files and procedures, forms aims that survive complete turnover of personnel, and reaches decisions through committees in which no single person represents the whole. My verdict: it has beliefs, intentions, and perhaps responsibilities in a genuine, non-metaphorical sense, and it is rationally assessable; but I deny that it is conscious, and I doubt there is anything it is like to be it. I want to say it has a mind without a point of view.

Sixth, a market or price system. Buyers and sellers acting on local information give rise to prices that aggregate dispersed knowledge, adjust to shortages, and are routinely described as expecting inflation or learning from shocks, though no participant represents the whole. My verdict: no mind; it is a causal aggregate that we find it convenient to describe in mental terms.

My rough guess at the principle behind these verdicts runs as follows. A composite has a mind to the extent that its parts implement a sufficiently integrated and self-modifying model of the system's own states and of its environment, which the system can use to revise its own priorities flexibly. The brain satisfies the requirement fully; the corporation satisfies it in the domain of propositional attitudes but lacks the unity needed for consciousness; the ant colony, slime mould, thermostat, and market fail on integration or on self-modelling. But I worry that the phrases 'sufficiently integrated' and 'self-modifying model' are vague enough to be doing no work beyond restating my verdicts.

What I would like from you is the principle—or a small set of candidate principles—that makes the best sense of these judgements. State each candidate precisely; identify the case that forces each of its clauses; test it against further cases I have deliberately left open, such as a honeybee swarm selecting a nest site through quorum sensing, a scientific community revising a consensus, and a distributed artificial system whose components are separate learning agents sharing a global objective; and then say candidly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and whether I should abandon a verdict rather than save it with epicycles. Please answer in British English.
