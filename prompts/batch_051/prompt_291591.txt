I've been chewing on whether ideas can really be owned, and I keep coming back to Thomas Jefferson's 1813 letter to Isaac McPherson. He says an idea is like a flame: the person who lights their candle from mine gets light without leaving me in the dark, so ideas can't be exclusive property in the way a field or a horse can be. He does allow that a society might grant inventors a temporary monopoly as a special reward, but he treats that as law-made, not natural. I can't shake the feeling that this solves something and ignores something at th same time. Write me an essay testing whether Jefferson's flame argument actually settles anything about modern patents, copyrights, or trade secrets. Take the argument seriously, don't just wave it off as a pretty metaphor, and face the obvious objection that if I spend ten years on an invention and someone copies it in a weekend, I've lost something real. Reach a verdict and defend it. Please write in British English, and at the end say how confident you are in the main claim and point out the one bit you're least sure about.
