I have been trying to make sense of a line of argument about the foundations of arithmetic, and I cannot find the weak point, though I suspect one must exist. It runs as follows.

(1) We are entitled to trust the standard axioms of arithmetic—say, Peano arithmetic—only if we can demonstrate that they are consistent.
(2) Any demonstration of their consistency would have to be given within some formal system.
(3) By Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, no consistent formal system strong enough to express elementary arithmetic can demonstrate its own consistency.
(4) So if some formal system T were to demonstrate that Peano arithmetic is consistent, we would be entitled to rely on that demonstration only if we could demonstrate T's own consistency, which by (3) cannot be done within T and so would require a further system.
(5) The same applies to that further system, and so on without end.
(6) Therefore the consistency of the basic axioms of arithmetic can never be demonstrated, and the possibility that they are inconsistent remains a live one.

Is this argument valid? If not, exactly which step fails, and on what reading of "demonstrate" or "entitled to rely"? I am especially unsure whether the move from the regress in (5) to the conclusion in (6) is a genuine inference or a slide between two senses of "possible". I have a background worry that the hidden work is being done by a very demanding picture of justification, one on which trust always requires a further demonstration; but I would like you to assess the numbered steps as written before turning to that.
