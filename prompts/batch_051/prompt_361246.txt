i've been poking at the hinge of history hypothesis—the idea that we're living at a unusually influential moment—and i keep wanting to split it into two things, but i can't tell if the split is real or if i've just relabelled stuff in the literature. i've been calling them hinge elasticity and hinge width. a moment has high hinge elasticity when a small amount of effort, information, or luck at that moment produces a big difference in which far future we end up in. a moment has high hinge width when the range of seriously possible far futures is unusually large. my worry is that hinge-of-history people run them together: they say the range of possible futures is huge (AI, synthetic biology, nuclear war, etc.) and then conclude that small things now could matter a lot. but those seem like different claims. a moment could have huge width—the futures we might get are wildly different—while being low-elasticity, because nothing anyone does right now is actually steering which of those futures happens; the outcome is being settled by forces no one controls. or the reverse: the summer of 1914 might have had narrow width but very high elasticity, where tiny choices by a few people changed everything. so i'd like to know: is this a real distinction, or have i just rediscovered something like contingency versus variance under a worse name? if it's real, which property should the hinge-of-history hypothesis actually require? and where does the distinction break—what does it paper over or get wrong? give me the three strongest objections and tell me if any of them is fatal.
