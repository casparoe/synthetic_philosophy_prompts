i teach a small applied ethics seminar — 16 undergrads, one 80-minute session. i want an in-class game that makes them feel the difference between importance, tractability, and neglectedness in cause prioritization, not just have me define the words. the idea should come out of what goes wrong for them during the game, and only get named in the debrief.

what im picturing: four foundations of four students, each with a budget of chips to spend each round on a menu of maybe six cause cards (mix of global health, animal welfare, longtermist stuff, whatever makes the point). cards have public descriptions and public 'importance' scores, but true tractability and how crowded a cause already is are hidden or noisy. during a due-diligence phase groups can spend chips to get private analyst memos — noisy clues about one or two causes' true tractability and how crowded they are. scoring should be a simple formula combining importance, tractability, and a crowding term so a cause's return per chip shrinks as everyone piles on. round one should mostly punish the groups that just fund whatever sounds most important; round two should reward any group that goes looking for the unglamorous, cheap, and ignored cause. winning is bragging rights or picking next week's discussion topic — no real money.

give me: the full rules, a materials list, the exact scoring formula (something a person with a calculator can run, no spreadsheet), an 80-minute timeline, a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the three concepts, what will predictably go wrong the first time i run it and how to handle it, and an honest note on what the game cant reproduce about real cause prioritization so i know what i have to supply verbally at the end.
