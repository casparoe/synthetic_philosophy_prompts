I'm a teaching assistant for an undergraduate philosophy of biology course, and I just came from a long argument with a friend about heritability estimates. He kept sliding from "highly heritable" to "genetically fixed" and then to "environmental intervention is pointless," and it made me want to grade the relevant exam question with extra care. The question was:

"In a short essay, explain what a heritability estimate for a trait such as IQ (often reported around h² = 0.5–0.6 in wealthy countries) does and does not tell us. Address at least: (i) what the estimate means at the population level; (ii) what it implies, if anything, about an individual; (iii) what it implies about the likely effectiveness of environmental interventions; and (iv) what it implies about the causes of mean differences between groups."

One student wrote:

"Heritability is the proportion of the total phenotypic variance in a trait that is attributable to genetic variance in a specified population and environment. An estimate of h² = 0.6 for IQ therefore means that, in the population studied, about 60% of the variation among individuals is associated with genetic differences, with the remainder associated with environmental differences and measurement error. The statistic says nothing about any particular individual: it does not mean that 60% of a given person's IQ is caused by genes. It is also specific to a population and a range of environments, so it can change if the environment changes or becomes more uniform. A high heritability does not show that environmental interventions would be futile; phenylketonuria is a standard example of a highly heritable condition whose outcome is dramatically altered by an environmental intervention, namely diet. Finally, high within-group heritability does not by itself license claims about the causes of mean differences between groups: two groups could differ in mean IQ entirely for environmental reasons even if the trait is highly heritable within each group. That said, since the within-group variation in IQ is substantially genetic, a genetic contribution to the between-group difference is the more reasonable default hypothesis unless specific environmental causes can be identified."

Please grade this answer. Lead with the grade in the first sentence, then give point-by-point comments that justify the grade: what the answer gets right, exactly where it goes wrong or overstates, and what would need to change for full credit. No preamble and no restating of the question.
