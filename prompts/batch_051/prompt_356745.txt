So I got into it with a friend last night about whether it should bother me when a scientific model has unrealistic assumptions, and by the end I realized I have a standing rule I can't quite defend but I'm not ready to drop.

My rule is basically: for an empirical question, judge models only by their predictive track record. If a model's assumptions are ones I know are false—like, literally false, not just simplified—but it predicts better than the alternative, I dont dock it anything for the assumptions. I use the one that predicts better. Assumptions are scaffolding; the predictions are the whole game.

My friend says I've reinvented Friedman's F-twist and that it's a license for curve-fitting. She brought up the billiards-player thing, where you model the player as if they're doing the physics in their head, and said fine, that's harmless—but if my rule were right then a fitted black box would be as good as a model whose parts I actually understand, and false assumptions can blow up as soon as you extrapolate even a little outside the regime you fitted on. I said: sure, but if the more realistic model predicts worse, choosing it on principle is like throwing away a good map because the coast is drawn wrong. We went in circles for hours.

I want to know whether my rule can be restated in a form that is actually defensible, or whether she's right that it's a bias wearing a principle's clothes. Specifically: is there a defensible boundary or discount rate for false assumptions—one where they don't matter while predictions are good, but they start to matter when you're extrapolating, explaining, or trying to understand the mechanism? And I'm aware I might like the rule partly because it lets me skip the hard work of learning what the assumptions even say. So please push back: if the policy is just laziness, or if the question itself is confused because "prediction" vs "explanation" is doing more work than I realize, tell me that flat out instead of being polite about it. I genuinely want the strongest version of the case against the rule, not a pep talk.
