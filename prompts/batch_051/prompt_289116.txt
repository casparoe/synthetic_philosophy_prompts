Suppose the major frontier AI labs adopt the following arrangement to target the competitiveness constraint — the pressure to skip alignment work because any alignment tax a lab pays (the compute, staff, and schedule a less careful competitor can avoid) leaves it behind. The idea is to tax the capability jump rather than the safety work. Under the scheme, a member that deploys a model whose score on a public, independently maintained capability benchmark exceeds the prior frontier score by more than a margin δ must pay a levy into a common pool, set as a fixed fraction of that training run's reported compute cost for each point by which the model clears the threshold. At the end of each quarter the pool is paid out as an equal 'restraint dividend' to every member that deployed no model above the threshold. Membership is voluntary, but non-members cannot buy compute on the consortium's shared training clusters, which together account for a large share of frontier training capacity. The benchmark and δ are maintained by an independent foundation whose methods are public but whose actual test items are secret and refreshed quarterly.

Treat this as a mechanism, not as a position on AI risk. Walk through how it would actually play out: what it incentivizes among members, non-members, and the foundation; the first places it gets gamed; what it does to the competitiveness constraint and the alignment tax; and whether its failure modes can be fixed without gutting the scheme.
