Many Protestant churches officially teach some variety of cessationism: the position that the New Testament's sign gifts—tongues, prophecy, miraculous healing—were given for the apostolic era and have since been withdrawn. Yet those same churches routinely practice intercessory prayer for the sick, and when a recovery follows they often speak of it as an answer to prayer. That looks like an inconsistency: if the gift of healing has ceased, what is the congregation asking God to do? I would like you to reconstruct the philosophy of divine action that this pairing of doctrine and practice presupposes. In particular, does a coherent cessationism require distinguishing between a standing human gift, which has ceased, and a singular divine act in response to petition, which has not? What does that distinction commit a church to about natural law and secondary causes—should an answered prayer be understood as a suspension of the laws of nature, as God acting through the ordinary causal order, or as an outcome God foreordained to coincide with the prayer? Say which of these views the practice strictly presupposes, which are merely compatible with it, and where the practice is philosophically underdetermined. Finally, what would have to change in the practice if the church replaced this presupposition with a thoroughgoing continuationism, or with the view that God answers prayer only by shaping the dispositions and circumstances of creatures and never by acting on physical outcomes themselves?
