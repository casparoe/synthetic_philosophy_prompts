A colleague in my reading group has offered the following argument. He thinks it shows that the standard account of imaginative resistance, taken seriously, makes moral fiction impossible as a vehicle of moral thought. The conclusion cannot be right, but I cannot find the step at which it slips.

(1) Fiction can stipulate the most extravagant descriptive facts, and readers accept them as fictional truths. Tell them that there is a golden mountain, or that cats do arithmetic, and they nod; tell them that a mother's killing of her newborn was the right thing to do, and they refuse in a way that feels principled rather than unimaginative. Call this asymmetry imaginative resistance.

(2) The standard explanation is that the resistance marks a conceptual limit. To accept the killing as right, a reader would have to deploy her own concept of an innocent child's being killed, a concept that classifies the act as wrong, while simultaneously deploying a concept that classifies the same act as right. That is more like being asked to accept a round square than being asked to accept a golden mountain.

(3) If this explanation is right, the difficulty is not a contingent failure of some readers' sympathy. It belongs to what a moral concept can be used to represent, for a thinker with the concepts we actually have.

(4) But a fiction can serve as a moral thought experiment only if it can make the reader imagine a moral order different from her own, one in which what her own concepts condemn is right. Otherwise fiction merely rehearses our actual judgments and never tests them.

(5) So it follows from (2) and (3) that readers cannot do what (4) requires; moral fiction, as a thought experiment, is impossible.

(6) Yet moral fiction manifestly does what (4) requires. Dystopias and satires make readers grasp a world in which informing on one's family, exterminating a class of people, or torturing for the state is right, and this grasping is precisely what allows the fiction to turn that world into an object of moral criticism rather than mere description.

(7) Since (5) conflicts with (6), and (6) is a datum we would not surrender, the standard explanation in (2) must be false. The remaining option is that imaginative resistance is only a psychological reluctance, a mood or a failure of sympathy, and reveals nothing about the structure of moral thought.

Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? I would like to keep both the standard explanation and the datum about dystopian fiction, but I cannot see how to make them fit together. Which step must be given up, why did it look as though it had to be true, and what position am I left in if I reject it?
