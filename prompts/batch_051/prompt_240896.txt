So I keep running into people who cite Harsanyi's aggregation theorem as if it basically proves utilitarianism, or at least weighted utilitarianism, from almost nothing. As I understand the setup: each individual has preferences over lotteries that satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, the social planner's preferences over the same lotteries also satisfy those axioms, and a Pareto condition connects them (everyone indifferent between two lotteries means society is indifferent; everyone weakly prefers one and at least one strictly prefers it means society strictly prefers it). Then the theorem says social preference can be represented as a weighted sum of the individual utility functions, and the usual takeaway is that rationality plus Pareto gets you utilitarianism.

What I want is a map of how exposed that takeaway actually is. What would have to be true for the leap from the formal result to utilitarianism-is-forced to fail, or for the theorem itself to give out? I'm not after generic hand-waving about expected utility theory being descriptively false; I want the specific pressure points. Which premise is doing the real work, what would the world have to look like for each to fail, and which failures are live options rather than merely logically possible? For instance I've got a vague sense that the interesting action is in whether the weights mean anything without interpersonal comparability, and in whether social preferences over ex ante lotteries should be assumed to satisfy independence at all, because society might reasonably care about how uncertainty resolves across people rather than just the expected welfare, but I can't tell how those map back onto the theorem's assumptions.

Also, the honest part: what would a defender of the Harsanyi reading themselves concede counts against them when pushed, as apposed to just what the critics say? Distinguish stuff that would be decisive from stuff that would just be awkward. And give me an actual bottom-line verdict on whether the standard conclusion is well insulated, and whether that insulation is itself a problem — not a both-sides hedge. I'd be most persuaded by a critique that follows from the theorem's own formalism rather than one that imports outside moral intuitions, but don't let that constrain you. I'd rather have a sharp, possibly wrong map than a blurry fair one.
