I am a PhD student working on the ethics of immortality and philosophical theology, and I have been developing a position I call the Replenishment Account of eternal life. It begins from Bernard Williams's Makropulos case: a person who has lived for centuries finds that the desires which once gave her reason to continue have all been satisfied or worn out, so that further life strikes her as flat and pointless, and she chooses death over more of it. Williams took this to show that endless life would eventually go that way for any of us, because the categorical desires that make continued life worth wanting would be exhausted and only conditional desires—desires whose objects presuppose that one is alive—would remain. I think that conclusion is too strong, and that Williams has mislocated the problem. I distinguish two kinds of agent. A finitely generated agent is one whose standing sources of value, forms of engagement, and possible projects form a finite or repeating set; however long she lives, she is only recombining the same few goods. An open-ended agent is one whose living generates categorical desires that are new to her, not merely new occasions for old ones: she can come to want goods she could not have specified before living the life that reveals them. My Replenishment Account says that the force of the Makropulos case comes entirely from its heroine being finitely generated; unending time entails tedium only given that condition. It follows that heaven, or eternal bliss more generally, is coherent just in case the blessed are, or become, open-ended agents, so that desire is continually replenished and time never runs it dry. I am aware that 'open-ended' may be doing suspicious work here, and that I may have either renamed Williams's problem or saved eternal life only by changing the subject into something no longer recognizably the finite person who died. Please give me an honest assessment in exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. The five points should cover: whether the finitely generated/open-ended distinction is coherent; what the account gets right; whether the position already exists under another name; the strongest objection to it; and whether that objection is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable.
