I keep going back and forth on a parallel I noticed, and I want someone to check it before I start treating it as serious.

The St. Petersburg game: you pay a fee, then a fair coin is flipped until it lands heads; if the first heads comes on flip n, you get 2^n dollars. The expected dollar payout is (1/2)(2) + (1/4)(4) + (1/8)(8) + ..., which is 1 + 1 + 1 + ..., so it diverges. Yet an actual person wont pay more than a few dollars for the ticket. The usual fixes say the linear-in-money model is wrong, the house has finite funds, or tiny probabilities shouldn't be taken at face value.

Now the repugnant conclusion in population ethics: for any population of, say, a billion people with excellent lives, there is some much bigger population of people with lives barely worth living whose existence is supposed to be better overall once you just add up welfare. Keep stacking barely-positive lives and the total outruns any fixed number of wonderful ones. Maybe I'm just pattern-matching on the word "infinite," but the engine feels the same: an unbounded index set of positive contributions — coin-toss states on one side, possible extra people on the other — and in both cases the sum gets dominated by sheer quantity rather than quality.

So my question is whether this is a deep structural sameness (both being failures of some boundedness or Archimedean assumption in additive aggregation) or whether the analogy falls apart for a specific reason, e.g. because in St. Petersburg the states are mutually exclusive and weighted by probabilities that sum to one, while in population ethics the people all coexist and each counts fully. And if the analogy is real, does anything transfer? Would bounding utility or discounting tiny probabilities in the gamble correspond to something coherent on the population-ethics side, or would it just collapse into the usual "leveling down" and critical-level debates? I want to know exactly where the two structures stop matching.
