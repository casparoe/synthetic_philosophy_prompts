At happy hour the other week I listened to two grad students go at it about whether science is rational during a scientific revolution, and it's been bothering me because neither one landed a clear knockout. I left before they finished, so I only caught the middle of it. It went something like:

Ana: "Think of it the Popperian way. A revolution is just conjecture and refutation at a bigger scale. The new framework makes predictions the old one can't; the old one starts needing ad hoc patches to protect it from anomalies; and scientists eventually switch because the new theory has survived harsher tests. That's rational by science's own standards, no special story needed."

Ben: "You're treating 'harsher test' and 'ad hoc patch' as if they're revolution-neutral, but they aren't. Which anomalies matter, which tests are severe, and which adjustments count as ad hoc all depend on the paradigm. Defenders of the old theory genuinely see their patches as principled. Popper's rules can't referee a situation where the rules themselves are what's in dispute — that's Kuhn's point about incommensurability."

Ana: "Fine, no neutral algorithm. But there are shared observational results and long-standing values like accuracy, consistency, scope, simplicity, and fruitfulness. The new theory often beats the old on exactly those values, and you can show the old guard that their moves would have looked ad hoc by their own earlier standards. Rational comparison doesn't require an algorithm; it requires responsiveness to criticism."

Ben: "Shared values won't help, because the two sides weight them differently, and they weight them differently because of their paradigms. And the 'their own earlier standards' move cuts both ways: from inside the old paradigm, the new theory is the ad hoc one, since it hasn't yet solved whole areas the old one handles fine. The successes aren't commensurable, so the evidence underdetermines the choice. That's what's special about a revolution."

Ana: "Underdetermination doesn't make a choice irrational, otherwise normal science would be irrational too, since theory choice there is already a judgment call among underdetermined options. If both staying and switching can be reasonable, that's fallibilism, not conversion. A rational revolution is just the community gradually being persuaded that the new framework solves more of what it cares about."

I want you to referee this the way I couldn't. Go through the actual moves and tell me who is right about which specific point, where each overstates or misapplies the view they're invoking, and what both of them are missing. If it helps, say who ends up closer on the central question. And please be explicit about what's settled versus genuinely contested here — Kuhn and Popper get flattened into cartoon versions a lot, and I don't want one contested reading passed off as the consensus.
