I've got to run a two-hour session on the discursive dilemma for a reading group of sharp non-academics — mostly lawyers, two economists, nobody who knows what social choice theory is. I'm not worried about them finding it arcane; I'm worried they'll file it under 'groups are dumb' and move on. I've got the standard three-judge example: each judge votes on two premises (there was a valid contract, there was a breach) and on liability, where liability follows only if both premises hold. Judge 1 says yes on contract, no on breach, so no liability. Judge 2 says no on contract, yes on breach, so no liability. Judge 3 says yes on both, so liability. Majority says contract, majority says breach, majority says no liability. Plan the teaching sequence for me: what has to be on the table before this example can even bite (individual consistency? what a proposition set is? the difference between a judgment and a preference?), whether it has to stay at three judges with two conjunctively necessary premises, and what would break if I added a judge, made the premises independent, or let a judge's votes not determine there conclusion. Then the order for introducing the premise-based and conclusion-based procedures, and the confusions this crowd will hit in the order they'll hit them. I already know one: they'll confuse the whole thing with the Condorcet paradox, and another will propose the 'obvious fix' that we should just vote on premises. Where do I head each off? What do I leave out entirely — no full-blown impossibility theorems, right? And what counts as a real check at the end that they've understood its not the judges who are inconsistent but the procedure, rather than them just finding the case clever?
