You are a professional philosopher working on promising, oaths, and self-binding speech, and you are equally at home in contemporary social ontology and in the treatment of oaths in ancient myth and religion. Work through the question carefully and in stages: before committing to a resolution, sketch at least two distinct ways the tension below might be dissolved.

I am a novelist, and the book I am writing turns on an oath sworn on the river Styx — an oath by which even the gods bind themselves, and whose violation would be not merely shameful but a genuine wrong; the plot does not work unless that binding has real force. At the same time, I find myself convinced by a deflationary theory of oaths. On that theory, an oath binds because of what it does to the swearer and to those who witness it — the expectations it licenses, the honor it stakes, the convention it invokes — and the object sworn on is only staging. No river can contribute a force that promising itself does not already have, and beings who stand behind all conventions could not be bound by one more convention. Yet I cannot shake the intuition that a Styx-oath differs in kind from an ordinary vow, and that the river is not incidental to that difference. I am not asking for a verdict between theory and intuition. I am asking what it would take for the theory to survive the intuition, what it would take for the intuition to survive the theory, and whether there is a reading of binding force on which both are true — on which the oath's mechanics remain entirely convention while its gravity is something more.
