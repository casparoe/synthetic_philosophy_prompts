I have recently been appointed to a continuity-of-government working group, and the work has forced me to articulate a set of convictions about the ethics of designated survivors that I apparently already held. Here is where I find myself:

1. If an attack kills the governor and the ranking legislators at once, the next official in the statutory line of succession holds office with full legal authority; no fresh democratic authorization is needed at that moment.
2. Secrecy about who has been moved to a secure location is justified, because announcing it would make the continuity plan self-defeating.
3. Democratic legitimacy requires the public to know, in advance, who would actually govern them in a catastrophe; a succession that would come as news to most citizens is a genuine moral defect, not merely a public-relations problem.
4. Nevertheless, if that successor begins issuing lawful orders, the public and lower officials should comply, because the only alternative is a struggle over who speaks for the state, and that would be worse.
5. An official who voluntarily remains in a succession-designated office has consented to inherit the role later, including being removed to safety without notice.
6. That kind of standing consent is too thin to authorize the successor's first acts if those acts are of the highest consequence—deploying the National Guard, detaining people, or repudiating standing agreements—which would need fresh, visible authorization.

I would like an audit rather than a lecture. Are these commitments jointly tenable? Please identify which pairs genuinely pull against each other and which are only apparently in tension, rank the genuine tensions by severity, say what would have to give in each case, and tell me which single revision would do the most to restore coherence and what that revision would cost.
