The topic has recently become more than academic for me, and I want your help making sure the view I have landed on is internally consistent before I present it to others. Earlier this year my brother had a stroke in the brainstem and spent several weeks unable to move or speak. He later told us that he had been conscious for much of that period — he heard voices, felt the sheets, followed conversations — but could not make his body respond. The neurologist remarked that his conscious experience was real but that it did not appear to be doing any of the causal work in producing movement or speech. I have not been able to stop thinking about that remark, and I have ended up with the following set of commitments about the causal status of consciousness.

Please answer as a professional philosopher specializing in philosophy of mind and epistemology. Before issuing your audit, work through what each commitment commits me to and how each pair of commitments interacts; do not simply flag everything as in tension. Then deliver the audit in the form I describe below.

Here is where I currently stand:

1. Epiphenomenalism about phenomenal states. Phenomenal states — the raw feel of a pain, the look of red, what it is like to hear a familiar voice — are caused by physical processes in the brain, but they themselves have no causal effects. They do not move my body, and they do not even cause my judgments or beliefs that I have them.

2. First-person evidence. My own first-person case is the strongest reason I have for thinking phenomenal states exist at all. I know about pain, color, and the like from the inside.

3. Introspective reliability. In favorable conditions — attentive, undistracted, no relevant pathology — my judgments about my current phenomenal states are highly reliable, reliable enough that I can know whether I am, for instance, still feeling a pain.

4. Reliabilism about introspective justification. A judgment that I am in pain is justified when it is produced by a process that reliably tracks whether I am in pain. It is not required that the pain itself cause the judgment.

5. A common-cause story. The same neural process that causes the pain also causes my judgment that I am in pain. The judgment and the phenomenal state are lawfully correlated through this shared cause. This, together with (4), is what is supposed to explain how introspection can be reliable despite (1).

6. Zombie possibility. A creature microphysically identical to me but with no phenomenal states at all is not just conceivable; I see no contradiction in such a being. It would say all the same things I say, with the same conviction.

7. Aboutness. My introspective judgments are genuinely about my phenomenal states — about the felt pain itself, not merely about its neural basis.

8. The paradox is overstated. The familiar paradox of phenomenal judgment — the objection that epiphenomenalism would make my beliefs about my own experiences unjustified or self-undermining — is defused by (4) and (5), so it does not force any revision in my view.

Please now audit this set of commitments. Tell me which commitments, if any, are actually in tension with one another and which only appear to be in tension. For each real tension, rank it by severity, say exactly what the conflict consists in, and say what I would have to revise and what the revision would cost me. For apparent tensions, say briefly why they dissolve. Then tell me which single revision would do the most to make the overall package defensible. Be precise about the reasoning that leads from one commitment to the next, since I will need to defend or revise these views in a critical discussion group.
