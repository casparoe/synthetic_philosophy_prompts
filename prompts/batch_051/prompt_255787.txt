I spent most of last night in a long, circling argument with a friend about whether doubt is something we owe one another. I kept defending the Cliffordian line that holding a belief on insufficient evidence is not merely an epistemic slip but a moral failing, while my friend pressed the familiar counterpoints: that we cannot switch convictions on and off at will, that a rule against insufficient evidence seems to require evidence for itself, and that many of the commitments that hold a life together—trusting a partner, backing a friend on thin testimony, acting decisively when the evidence is genuinely open—cannot wait for an audit. We never settled it, and I came away wanting to see the position pushed to its logical, slightly monstrous conclusion by someone who fully inhabits it.

Write me a sustained modest proposal in the arch, reasonable, mildly bureaucratic voice of an official policy document, around 700 words. The conceit: a society persuaded that doubt is a civic duty establishes a Ministry of Doxastic Rectitude, which requires citizens to file an annual belief return listing their high-confidence convictions, and empowers a tribunal to impose remedial doubt on anyone holding a belief on evidence the Ministry deems insufficient. The proposal should keep a perfectly straight face and let the standard objections surface through the machinery of the scheme rather than stating them outright—the who-audits-the-auditors problem, the ought-implies-can objection, the regress of demanding evidence for the evidence, and the social damage of treating belief as a public-health hazard when trust, testimony, and decisive action all run ahead of what we can prove. It should be legible to a reader who has never read Clifford or James, but precise enough that someone who knows the ethics of belief recognizes the ship-owner example and James's point about forced, momentous options handled quietly and fairly.
