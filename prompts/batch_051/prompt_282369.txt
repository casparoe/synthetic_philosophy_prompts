I'm trying to locate where a disagreement about rescued-animal byproducts actually lives, and I'd like you to help me by giving a probability with its derivation.

Consider the following case. A small city maintains a quasi-sanctuary flock of roughly forty retired laying hens on public land. The hens are fed and housed until they die naturally; the city keeps no rooster and raises no chicks, so keeping the hens alive does not increase the population. The hens nonetheless lay unfertilized eggs, which the city currently collects and incinerates as biological waste, because municipal policy forbids selling or otherwise using animal products. A local freegan group proposes that the eggs instead be given to a shelter kitchen that currently serves plant-based meals. The eggs would not be sold, bartered, advertised, or displayed; they would simply be eaten by people who would otherwise be fed adequate plant-based food at similar cost. Assume throughout that taking the eggs does not alter the hens' care, and that the shelter's existing meals are nutritionally sufficient, so the eggs are not needed to prevent malnutrition.

The proposition I want you to put a probability on is this: it is morally permissible for the city to donate, and for the shelter to serve, these eggs. By permissible I mean not merely excusable, and not merely something one may tolerate in others, but that there is no moral objection sufficient to make the practice wrong.

My credence in that proposition is about 0.85; a strict vegan friend of mine puts it at about 0.15. Give your own number, but more importantly, decompose it. Please assign a rough probability to each of the following sub-propositions, state whether it pushes your overall credence up or down, and say how they combine:

1. The hens have no welfare interest in what happens to their eggs once laid, so nothing done with the eggs wrongs the hens themselves.
2. Because the eggs would otherwise be destroyed and the arrangement creates no market demand, the standard harm-based objections to using animal products are fully neutralized here.
3. Even with no demand created, using the eggs still constitutes a wrong of commodification or objectification of the hens: treating their bodies as a standing resource rather than as something owed a different kind of regard.
4. The city's involvement adds a distinct moral problem, because an institution donating eggs expresses official endorsement of the idea that animal products are an appropriate human food source, and that endorsement is itself objectionable.
5. Destroying edible food when it could be eaten is a genuine moral loss, so the waste-avoidance argument carries real independent weight in favor of donation rather than being mere rationalization.
6. The arrangement is likely to produce indirect effects that outweigh the direct benefits, such as weakening a social norm against animal use or giving some future actor a reason to maintain or expand a laying flock.

Finally, tell me which of these sub-questions most likely explains the gap between someone at 0.85 and someone at 0.15, and what concrete evidence, if it could be obtained, would move your own number the most.
