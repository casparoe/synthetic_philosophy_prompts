Last evening I spent several hours arguing with two close friends, and I came away unsure whether they were contradicting each other or describing two halves of one truth. The argument was about a third friend, Leah, who recently went through something she calls a conversion. In her telling, she has stopped trying to earn her standing and has learned to trust that she is accepted without having to prove anything. The change is in some ways real and admirable: she is noticeably calmer, less preoccupied with other people's opinions, less defensive. But she has also quietly given up nearly everything she used to do for others — the volunteer work, the habit of showing up when someone was sick or in crisis, the ordinary practical help. When this was raised, her answer was roughly that she no longer has to justify herself by doing enough. Our other friend, Marcus, was angry about exactly this. He insisted that a conviction which never issues in action is not a conviction at all, only a mood or a story one tells oneself. He also kept saying he was not asking her to earn anything — only to let her trust show itself in action. Leah replied that she cannot see the difference between letting trust show itself and proving herself, and that Marcus's scorekeeping — who did what, who gave enough, who may feel at peace — is precisely the old law from which she has been set free. Each seemed to me to be right about the other's error, while also talking past the other.

I would like a considered judgment of this quarrel. Is Marcus closer to the truth, or Leah, or are they caught in the same mistake from opposite directions? Is there a coherent way to hold that deeds matter without making them the currency in which a person's standing is bought? And because I am less interested in a confident-sounding verdict than in a well-founded one, please say what would change your mind: which consideration, if it turned out to hold, would push you toward the opposite judgment, and where is your reasoning on this most likely to fail?
