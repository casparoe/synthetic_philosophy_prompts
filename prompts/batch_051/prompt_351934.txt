I'm a novelist researching a book about physicists, and Ive picked up a rule from the Weinberg-Hawking-Feynman side of the old debate about whether philosophy is useful to science. When Im reading philosophy of science, I only keep going if the philosopher can point to something a scientist would actually do differently — a measurement, an experiment, an observation, a number in a notebook. If the best they can offer is that they'd change how we interpret the result, or clarify a concept, or cure a confusion, I close the tab and move on. Recently I applied it to a philosopher on whether time is real: okay, I thought, but what would this change about what a physicist measures tomorrow morning? Nothing? Then I'm out. The rule saves me enormous time, and I have a deadline, but I also know I find this material hard and it's very convenient that my rule tells me I don't have to learn it. So: is this rule defensible on the merits, or is it avoidance wearing a principle's clothes? If it's partly defensible, what is the version of it a serious person could actually keep?
