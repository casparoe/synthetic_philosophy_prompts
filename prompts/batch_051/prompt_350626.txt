In many American jurisdictions, a physician or laboratory that negligently fails to detect or disclose a fetal anomaly before birth can be sued by the parents for what the law calls wrongful birth. The parents recover at least some of the costs of raising the child — commonly including the extra medical, therapeutic, and educational costs attributable to the condition — on the theory that, had they been informed, they would have ended or avoided the pregnancy. Yet the same negligent act, producing the same child, does not ground a claim by the child for what the law calls wrongful life. Courts have nearly always held that the child cannot recover for the bare fact of being born into the life she has; in the few places where a child's claim is recognized at all, recovery is limited to the incremental costs of the condition and never extends to general damages for existing with it. The parents, in other words, are treated as having been harmed by the child's existence, while the child is treated as not having been harmed by that same existence.

Reconstruct the theory of harm, welfare, and identity that this practice presupposes. Does it commit the law to a person-affecting view of wrongful harm, on which an act wrongs a person only if that same person would have been better off in some alternative — and on which never having existed is not an alternative for the person at all? Must it also hold the stronger comparative thesis that a life with this condition is not worse for the child than nonexistence, or that the comparison is simply unintelligible? And is the practice internally consistent when it compensates parents for the disability-related costs of the child's life while denying that the disability is a harm to the child whose disability it is? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and mark where it is philosophically underdetermined. Finally, say what a court would have to change about both claims if it instead accepted an impersonal, total-welfare view on which the negligent act is wrong just insofar as it leaves the world as a whole worse than some available alternative.
