At the pub on Thursday, two of my flatmates got properly stuck into an argument about moral language, and neither of them would let it go. I'll recount it as faithfully as I can.

Dan started it. He said he'd been reading around the idea that saying "bullfighting is wrong" isn't a description of some spooky moral fact out in the world; it's more like a stance or a plan — "boo to bullfighting" — and that's why moral judgments get a grip on you in a way ordinary factual claims don't. It explains, he said, why two people can agree on every non-moral fact about bullfighting and still disagree: they're not detecting different facts, they've got different attitudes.

Kat wasnt having it. She said: fine, but then what's going on in "If bullfighting is wrong, then paying to watch it is wrong"? You're not booing bullfighting when you say the antecedent, because you're not asserting it at all. You might think bullfighting is perfectly fine and still accept the conditional. And then there's the argument:

1. Bullfighting is wrong.
2. If bullfighting is wrong, then paying to watch it is wrong.
3. So, paying to watch it is wrong.

She said that's obviously valid; anyone who accepts the premises but rejects the conclusion is making a logical mistake. But if the first premise expresses a stance and the conditional expresses something else entirely, she can't see how the pieces fit together to force a mistake. She called this the Frege–Geach problem, and said attitudes just don't have the right shape to be premises.

Dan came back with what he said was the standard fix. The conditional, he said, expresses a higher-order policy: a kind of disapproval of the combination of states (booing bullfighting while not booing paying to watch it). So if you accept the first premise and the conditional but refuse the conclusion, you're not being formally inconsistent; you're in a state that clashes with your own higher-order policy — more like intending to get to the library and intending to catch the only bus that gets you there, while refusing to get on the bus. "Logical validity" in this domain is really a constraint on coherent combinations of attitudes, not on truth-preservation.

Kat said that's clever but she has two problems with it. First, it gets the direction backwards: we understand the conditional before we understand whatever exotic higher-order attitude supposedly goes with it, and she finds it hard to believe ordinary speakers are forming attitudes about their own attitudes every time they think a conditional moral thought. Second, Dan hasn't said anything about all the other connectives, quantifiers, and tense operators. What's the attitude expressed by "It's not wrong", or "Most bullfights are wrong", or "If it had been wrong, I wouldn't have gone"? Until he has a systematic story, she said, he's just patching one case.

Dan said that's a demand for a full compositional semantics, and it can in principle be met — "wrong" expresses disapproval, sentence mood expresses acceptance, and the connectives are builders that take attitudes and output more complex attitudes. The fact that he can't do the whole thing on a napkin doesn't mean it can't be done. The real constraint, he said, is just that the meanings have to compose, and attitudes can compose.

Kat granted that, but said the harder case is mixed sentences: "Either bullfighting is wrong or the trains are running late." There's no natural attitude that's the disjunction of a disapproval and a belief. You'd have to say the sentence expresses a sort of hybrid pair-state, and then you're not doing pure expressivism anymore; you're admitting that moral words do their logical work partly by expressing something belief-like after all.

Dan paused and said maybe expressivism does have to be a bit hybrid — the view can say moral judgments express both a desire-like state and a factual belief, with the desire-like part doing the action-guiding work. That, he said, isn't a retreat; it's the sensible version of the view, and it can still deny that there are robust moral facts.

Kat said that hybrid views just move the bump in the carpet: if the belief part is doing all the logical work in the embedded contexts, what's left of the original idea that moral language is fundamentally non-descriptive? And if the desire-like part is what makes moral language special, it's now a tagalong in exactly the cases that introduced the problem.

I got called away to get a round in at that point, so I didn't hear how it ended. Could you go through this exchange move by move and say who's right about which specific point, and what both of them are missing? Overall, who do you think has the stronger side of the argument?
