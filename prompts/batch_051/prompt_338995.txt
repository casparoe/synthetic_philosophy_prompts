I am a safety analyst at a regional transport authority. Every few weeks I must recommend for or against a proposed road-safety change — a signalized crossing, a lower speed limit, a roundabout conversion, a protected cycle lane, an automated enforcement camera — to a board of elected officials, who frequently overturn recommendations on public-opinion grounds. I need a reusable decision procedure, not a one-off judgment, for deciding when a scheme's expected effect on road deaths is worth its financial cost, delay, and other burdens.

Ground the procedure in the micromort (a one-in-a-million chance of death) and translate whatever is genuinely usable from the theory of risk and decision-making into it: the value of a statistical life, quality-adjusted life years, discounting, the distinction between voluntarily and involuntarily accepted risk, distributive fairness across groups, and how a scheme's risk changes compare with the background risks of ordinary travel. The result should be a step-by-step checklist I can run in a day or two per scheme, with the steps ordered, the inputs each step requires, fallbacks for when a number is unavailable, and explicit flags wherever a step is a judgment call rather than arithmetic.

The procedure must handle at least these hard cases: risk shifted from one group to another rather than eliminated; very small per-person risk reductions spread across very large populations; measures aimed chiefly at people who knowingly accept a risky activity; and measures that reduce deaths but increase serious injuries.

Apply the procedure to three worked cases: (1) a junction redesign expected to save 0.3 micromorts per daily user per year at a capital cost of $400,000; (2) a 20 mph zone expected to prevent one death every eight years while adding two minutes to a thousand journeys each day; and (3) a motorcyclist-protection measure expected to save lives but opposed by the riders it would protect. Ensure that at least one application yields a recommendation the board will not like. Conclude by stating plainly what the procedure cannot decide — the residual moral and political choices that no checklist can settle.
