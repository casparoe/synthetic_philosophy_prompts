My advisor and I have been going back and forth about this and I want a second opinion. Suppose it's 2075 and fundamental physics has actually finished: there's one stunningly well-confirmed theory that covers everything from the standard model to gravity, no experimental anomolies, and it's formulated in terms that don't map onto our older pictures—no billiard-ball particles, no nineteenth-century fields, just states of some high-dimensional structure evolving by a deterministic rule. Everything observable supervenes on those states.

At that point some philosophers are clearly going to declare victory for physicalism. Others (my advisor among them, I think) will say this is exactly when physicalism stops saying anything: if 'physical' just means 'whatever the completed theory describes,' then the doctrine has no exclusion left, because a panpsychist could wait for a completed psychology, point at the irreducible feels in it, and relabel them 'physical' too. That's Hempel's dilemma, or close to it. My question is: in this scenario, where the finished physics is actually on the table and not merely 'ideal,' does physicalism get settled, does it get re-formulated, or does the debate move somewhere else? Predict how the two horns play out once a completed physics exists, and be explicit about your confidence: which moves are forced, which are just likely, and what assumptions would have to fail for your prediction to be wrong.
