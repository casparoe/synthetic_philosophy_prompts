I serve on the allocation committee of a regional health authority, and because our recorded votes end up being cited in later disputes, our past decisions have become a kind of precedent among the people who appeal to us. Over five years we have built a remarkably consistent pattern. We funded a hypertension management program expected to prevent roughly 40 deaths a year, while declining a proposal to purchase surgical equipment that would have substantially improved outcomes for a named group of 15 existing patients. We approved a mass immunization drive expected to avert 90 deaths a year, rather than an expensive diagnostic and treatment program for a rare condition that would clearly benefit about 12 identified patients. We also chose a tobacco-cessation outreach program estimated to prevent 150 statistical deaths over a hospital unit that would not have saved more lives but would have made the last months of a small number of terminally ill patients considerably gentler. In each instance the majority reasoned in terms of expected benefit per dollar and cost per quality-adjusted life-year, and one member regularly insists that an identified death and a statistical death should count exactly the same. Yet we have never had to decide against treating an identifiable patient who faces imminent death with no alternative.

Now we do. A proposal asks us to fund a treatment for nine identifiable patients with a rapidly progressive disease; without it, all nine are expected to die within a year, and no other available intervention would help them. The same funds would otherwise support a vaccination outreach program in an underserved region, expected to prevent 55 statistical deaths a year at a substantially lower cost per life saved. If we want to be consistent with the principles our record actually embodies, should we fund the treatment, fund the outreach program, or adopt some split? Identify the closest precedent, say what principle each option would commit us to, and if our past rulings leave the matter open, describe the further case that would distinguish the candidate principles.
