I've been reading about murder cases and something keeps nagging at me. When a defendant had a brain tumour that made them loose it, or they were genuinely out of their mind, the court usually says they weren't really in control and sends them to a secure hospital rather than prison. But a "normal" defendant who does the same act gets a long sentence, and the judge talks about what they deserve. So the law seems to be assuming that normal people have some special thing — real control over their actions, being the true source of what they do — that makes punishment deserved, whilst the others lack it. But then the same court will treat a horrible childhood or poverty as mitigating factors, which feels like it's admitting that our actions have causes after all. What view of free will and moral responsibility does this practice of punishing people "because they deserve it" actually presuppose? Is it coherent, or is it caught between two pictures? And there's a stronger view I've come across that says nobody has that kind of control, because everything we do comes from genes, upbringing and luck, so nobody truly deserves punishment, and prisons should just restrain dangerous people and stop pretending. If that stronger view were right, what would have to change in how courts talk and sentence? Please also say how confident you are in each main claim and single out the point you're least sure of.
