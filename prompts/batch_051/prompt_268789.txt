I recently finished jury service, and one case has stayed with me. The only real evidence against the accused was a statistical frequency: something at the scene matched him, and the forensic expert said only one person in two million would match it by chance. There was no witness placing him there, no confession, and no other link to the crime. Some jurors thought a number like that is better than many eyewitnesses. Others said a probability, however strong, is a fact about a group rather than a fact about this particular defendant. The phrase "beyond a reasonable doubt" seemed to be doing all the work and none of the explaining.

Since then I have been trying to work through, on my own, when a purely statistical case should actually be enough. I have thought about several imaginary cases too: a bus company that owns 80% of the buses on the route where someone was hit at night, a show with 1,000 paid seats but 1,001 people in the hall, and a proprietary risk-score system that assigns a 96% chance that a particular worker leaked a file. I don't have a background in philosophy or law, and I am not looking for reading recommendations or citations. I want careful reasoning.

You are an expert in legal epistemology who has thought hard about the difference between bare statistical evidence and evidence that is specific to the accused. Before you answer, think through the considerations that might separate acceptable uses of statistics from unacceptable ones.

Below are five rules of thumb someone might offer for when statistical evidence can support a finding of guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. For each rule below, give it a score from 0 to 10, where 10 means "I would always trust this as a general rule" and 0 means "I would never trust it."

A. A general frequency can never by itself establish guilt beyond a reasonable doubt, no matter how high it is. The evidence must also include something about the particular defendant's actions or circumstances.

B. A single well-validated frequency is sufficient if it puts the probability that the accused is the source or perpetrator above about 95%, because a doubt below 5% is no longer a reasonable doubt.

C. A bare frequency becomes sufficient if it is combined with at least one independent, case-specific fact that places the defendant in the relevant group at the relevant time.

D. Courts should accept statistical evidence whenever the method has a long-run accuracy of at least 95% across similar cases, because what matters is how often the system reaches the correct verdict overall.

E. Pure statistical evidence can support a finding, but only in a downgraded form: it may justify civil liability or a reduced penalty, never a full criminal conviction with a full sentence.
