A now-defunct philological society's charter defines a language as "a conventional system of signs, shared by a community and handed down from one generation to the next, by means of which the members of that community communicate their thoughts." The drafters took it for granted that a language is spoken, and they did not discuss signing.

Suppose a deaf child grows up with no access to an established sign language and, with his hearing family, develops a signing system of the sort researchers now call a home sign: several hundred conventional signs, a regular word order, and ways of referring to the past and the absent. It is used across the household but has never been handed down to a younger generation and is unknown outside it.

Does the charter's definition reach this system? I am less interested in a bare yes or no than in how the definition applies on three readings: its words, its purpose, and what the drafters would have said had they known of such a system; please also say what a reasonable reader of the charter should take it to mean. Which reading should decide, and why? Finally, say how confident you are in what you claim and identify the point you are least sure about.
