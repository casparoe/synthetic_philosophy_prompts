I have spent the past year learning Arabic, and at the same time my daughter has been learning to read English. An asymmetry in how people characterize the two writing systems has begun to bother me, and I want to know whether it can be defended.

Arabic script is almost always introduced to me with a warning: it does not write the short vowels. The written word كتب gives me only the consonants k-t-b, and my knowledge of grammar and context must fill in whether it is kataba (he wrote) or kutub (books), among other readings. The implication in course materials and casual explanation alike tends to be that the script is missing something: an incomplete alphabet, a system with a hole in it. Yet nobody describes the English my daughter is learning as defective, even though its five or six vowel letters underdetermine a system of a dozen or more distinct vowel sounds in our own speech, and even though standard written English leaves word stress entirely unmarked. Reading the words tough, though, and through aloud requires me to know things about each word that the letters do not transparently encode. The same kind of gap between letters and sound is treated as a structural omission in one script and as historical character or depth in the other.

I would like you to find the most defensible principle that could justify this asymmetry, stress-test it against nearby cases, and then say plainly whether the asymmetry should be kept, revised, or abandoned. Candidates I can imagine: Arabic omits whole vowel graphemes rather than writing them inconsistently; Arabic short vowels are largely recoverable from root-and-pattern morphology, so readers need less vowel information than English readers do; English orthography buys morphological and etymological stability at the price of sound-letter transparency, as with the shared spelling in sign and signature or electric and electricity. But I am less interested in a catalogue of possible defenses than in the strongest one, honestly tested. Would a proposal to write English as an abjad — consonants written, short vowels left to context — strike us as obviously worse than a proposal to write Arabic with a fully phonemic alphabet? If a community whose language had never been written asked for advice on a new script, should I reassure them that the vowels can safely be left to grammar, and how would that advice sit with what I accept about my own writing system?

If the comparison is mis-set — if I have misdescribed either orthography, or the two cases are not genuinely parallel, or I am assuming something naive about what a script is for — tell me that directly rather than working inside my framing. And if the asymmetry is only parochialism, a familiar script naturalized and another exoticized, I would rather be told so than given a diplomatic account of both sides.
