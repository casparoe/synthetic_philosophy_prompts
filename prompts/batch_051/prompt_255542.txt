I keep noticing a pattern in how blame gets distributed when a target is gamed, and I can't tell whether it's defensible. When a school is caught teaching to the test—cutting history and recess to drill students on past papers—we treat the teachers and administrators as having done something wrong, and the episode gets reported as a scandal. When a salesperson hits quota by pushing products customers don't need, we call it misconduct and fire the salesperson. When a customer-service worker gets high satisfaction scores by hanging up on difficult callers, we call it gaming the system and punish the worker. But the people who set those targets—the officials who made test scores the measure of a school, the managers who made quotas the measure of sales, the executives who made survey scores the measure of service—are rarely blamed for the predictable gaming. At worst they are called naive or careless; often they are praised for bringing accountability to a fuzzy activity. Yet the designers wrote the rule, they had more information about how it would be used than any single worker did, and the entire point of the rule was to change behavior. So the asymmetry is this: we blame the lower-power person who responds to a bad measure by optimizing it, while treating the higher-power person who created the bad measure as essentially innocent, or even as a reformer. Is there anything that justifies that asymmetry? Maybe the target-chaser knowingly violates the spirit of the rule while the designer intended the spirit—but the designer also knew the measure was only a proxy, and proxies get chased. Maybe we just need a responsible individual and the worker is the easier one to replace—but that sounds like an explanation, not a justification. Or maybe the asymmetry should be reversed, with the designer treated as the main wrongdoer, or given up altogether because the whole setup is what is broken. I want to know which possible justification is strongest, whether it survives being applied to the school, the salesperson, and the service worker at once, and whether we should keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or abandon it.
