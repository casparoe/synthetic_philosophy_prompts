I'm taking a class on the ethics of doing good, and the semester-long argument is whether you should do the small, verifiable thing that predictably helps a few people—deworming, bednets, whatever—or whether those marginal fixes just make a broken structure tolerable, so the only real change is structural and high-risk. I've been rewatching The Good Place, and it struck me that the show basically runs this exact argument as a supernatural sitcom. The afterlife is literally a point system that tots up individual actions one at a time, which is marginalist moral bookkeeping made cosmic; the structure never gets questioned until the show reveals that the structure itself is broken, because modern life (supply chains, investments, all the invisible ways a purchase hurts someone) makes it impossible to keep your hands clean. So far so systems-thinking.

But I'm not sure the ending actually commits to that. The solution the show lands on is at least partly structural—they get the point system replaced with a series of tests—but the show's energy is still one-soul-at-a-time, and the four main characters' slow moral improvement is what makes it feel earned. I cant tell whether The Good Place is arguing that systems change is necessary and everything else is a patch, or whether it quietly undercuts that by centering the individual redemption of four dead people. Or maybe it's pointing at something the marginal-vs-systems debate misses, like the need for structures that let small local improvements feed back into the system instead of just buying it another year. I'd like a careful reading that doesn't use the show as a mere illustration, that says where it complicates or resists both positions, and that maybe says what the show adds to the debate that argument alone can't.
