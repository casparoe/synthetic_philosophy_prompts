I began reading the philosophy of science after I retired, and I have found myself revisiting a working habit from my career as an experimental physicist that I would now like assessed. The habit was this. I treated the central theoretical framework of my field as, in practice, beyond the reach of any single experiment. When a careful measurement conflicted with that framework, my first move was never to doubt the framework itself; I asked instead whether the detector had been properly calibrated, whether the background had been modelled correctly, whether our auxiliary assumptions were sound, or whether some unconsidered effect had disturbed the result. In most cases the trouble was found in one of those peripheral places and the framework stood. When it was not found, I still did not treat the result as a refutation. I set the anomaly aside as a problem for further work, or I adjusted some subsidiary assumption to bring theory and observation back into agreement. I allowed doubt to reach the core only when anomalies had accumulated, when the subsidiary adjustments had become ad hoc and had produced no novel prediction that survived testing, and when a rival framework was already available that handled the old and new results more naturally.

I now want to know whether this policy was rationally defensible or merely a sophisticated form of stubbornness. A younger colleague who has read Popper puts the objection sharply: a theory shielded in this way, he says, cannot really be falsified, and a claim that cannot be falsified is not scientific. He may be right, and I no longer trust my own judgment here. I am not asking for an exegesis of Popper; I am asking about the practice itself. Was my habit defensible? If so, what distinguishes a principled version of it from dogmatic refusal to let evidence count, and what sort of developments should finally be enough to make one abandon the core?
