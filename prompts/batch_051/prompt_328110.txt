I've been going back and forth with someone about whether pure time preference is ever rational, and they keep falling back on the standard argument that probably goes back to Pigou and Ramsey. The version I know runs something like this: the mere fact that a pleasure or pain is located later in time is not a rational ground for caring less about it, so if you do care less about it for that reason, you're exhibiting a cognitive defect, the old Pigou line about our telescopic faculty being defective. Rationalists usually add that this is a bias like present bias or the planning fallacy, and that the right response is commitment devices, longtermism, and generally trying to debias yourself into weighting the future more.

But I recently got into the stuff on hyperbolic discounting as an intrapersonal bargaining problem, Ainslie's picoeconomics, Parfit-style reductionism about identity, and the idea that your present self and your future self are best modeled as different agents whose preferences are only imperfectly aligned. If you accept that picture, the old argument seems to me to assume the very thing it's trying to establish. The eighty-year-old who gets the benefit of my current saving is, in a real sense, a different person. Me discounting his utility isn't a defect in my epistemic telescope; it's just ordinary self/other partiality, no more irrational than caring more about my friends than about strangers. Does the classical anti-pure-time-preference argument survive that shift? Which premise of the argument does the reductionist picture actually break, and can the argument be rebuilt without leaning on the claim that all your time-slices are literally the same agential self? I'd especially like to know whether the remaining argument collapses into a moral claim, that you should be impartial between all your time-slices, rather than a rationality claim, that pure time preference is a bias, and whether that matters for the usual rationalist project of debiasing.
