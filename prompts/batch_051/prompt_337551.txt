In a seminar on design arguments, a participant cut off a debate about whether a multiverse could explain the constants of nature by saying: "Look, the universe is fine-tuned for life — that much is just a fact, and the only real question is what explains it." I think that sentence slides between several different claims, and a design conclusion is being smuggled in before anyone argues for it. I'd like you to take the slogan apart and lay out a menu of the precise claims it could be making. Include at least these candidate readings, and add any I've missed: (i) a physical claim that the life-permitting range of various constants and initial conditions is narrow; (ii) a probabilistic claim that such a universe is in some sense improbable or surprising absent a further explanation; (iii) an explanatory claim that this cannot be treated as a brute fact and calls for some further account; (iv) an inferential claim that design is the best explanation; and (v) a teleological claim that the universe was in some way aimed at, or intended for, life. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a defensible but contestable thesis, or probably false, and explain how the word "for" in "fine-tuned for life" affects it — in particular, which readings survive if we switch to the more neutral phrase "life-permitting." Finally, say which minimal reading the seminar participant actually needed to support the move from "the constants of nature are life-permitting" to "the only real question is what explains that," and whether the slogan, read charitably, actually supplies it.
