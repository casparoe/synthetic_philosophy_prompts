At a philosophy club meeting, we read the part of Aristotle's On Interpretation, chapter 9, where he argues that if every statement about future events is already true or false, the sea battle tomorrow would be necessary. Two members, Lena and Marcus, got into a back-and-forth afterward.

Lena said the argument exposes a real problem: if the sentence there will be a sea battle tomorrow is true today, then something about today makes it true, and the alternate outcome is already ruled out. So to keep the future genuinely open, we should deny that future contingent sentences are true or false yet. Marcus replied that this gets the direction backwards. The sentence is true today because the battle will happen tomorrow; truth tracks the event, it does not force it. Lena came back: if the sentence is true today, then there is already a fact of the matter, so the non-battle is not a real possibility. Marcus answered that it is not a real possibility in the absolute sense, only possible relative to our ignorance; that is all the open future could mean. Lena said that is exactly her point: on your view, an infallible predictor could know the truth in advance, which shows the future was never open—so you have given up openness, and the only disagreement left is whether to call the fixed future open. Marcus replied that this is not just wordplay; the future can be fixed in the sense that there is a true answer while our choices still matter because we do not know the answer.

Neither would budge, and they asked me to referee. Please go through their exchange move by move and say who is right about which specific point, where each argument fails, and whether there is a coherent position that captures what is right on both sides. Also say what, if anything, they are both missing.
