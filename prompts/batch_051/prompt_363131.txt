I just had a long argument with a friend about whether determinism guarantees predictability, and we kept returning to a passage in Henri Poincaré’s Science and Method, in the chapter on chance. He claims, as I understand it, that a very small cause that escapes our notice can determine a considerable effect that we cannot fail to see, and that we then call the effect chance. I want to test that idea with a counterfactual. Suppose a world physically identical to ours in its laws and constants, except that a finite Laplacean demon exists inside that world: at some initial moment it possesses exact positions and velocities of all particles, but its memory and its calculations are themselves physical processes governed by the same laws, including the kind of sensitivity Poincaré describes. Give me a reasoned prediction of how this demon’s predictive power degrades as it tries to predict farther into the future. Distinguish the robust consequences of chaotic dynamics from the contingent consequences of the demon’s own size, precision, and computational speed. In particular, address whether the demon can ever know that its own future states are included in its predictions, and whether there is any way for it to avoid becoming part of the problem. I want the chain of mechanisms, not a narrative, and at each link say whether your prediction is robust or a guess, and what would have to be so for it to fail.
