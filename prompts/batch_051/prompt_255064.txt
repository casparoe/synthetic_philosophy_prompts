Below are notes for a short essay, working title 'Forbidden Colours as Boundary Effects, Not New Dimensions'. I would be grateful for a critical reading of these notes before I expand them into a full essay; in particular, I want to know whether the central distinction can carry the weight the argument places on it.

The notes outline an essay defending a distinction between two senses in which an experience can be new in type. Call them recombination and expansion. A recombined experience is one that has not occurred before but is composed of elements already in the subject's qualitative repertoire, arranged along dimensions already present. An expanded experience is one that adds an element, or a whole dimension, not already in that repertoire.

The essay's target is the tendency to describe successful inductions of 'forbidden colours' — hues such as reddish-green and yellowish-blue, which combine opposite poles of a single chromatic opponent channel and are normally unavailable to ordinary viewing — as evidence that a brain interface or other technique has given a subject a genuinely new kind of colour experience. The essay argues that even a stable, carefully reported reddish-green would be recombination, not expansion. The reason is that reddish-green is defined entirely in terms of the two opponent axes already present in human colour vision, red–green and yellow–blue. It would be a previously unvisited state of an existing space, not an additional axis. The intended analogy is a chord that nobody has ever played before: a novel chord is not a new note.

What would count as expansion? A third chromatic opponent channel, or a new class of receptors, whose outputs cannot be matched to any mixture of the familiar hue primitives. The essay claims that stimulating existing colour pathways — however exotically — could only drive the existing network into unusual states of the space it already instantiates. The interface is in this respect like a pianist: no sequence of key presses, however unprecedented, introduces new notes. Expansion would require altering the hardware that defines the space, not merely varying its inputs.

The essay then floats a criterion: a reported quale should count as expansion only if the subject cannot match it to any mixture of previously instantiated qualia. It concedes that this criterion is hostage to suggestion, imperfect introspection, and demand characteristics, and concludes that the distinction is real but forensic: interface reports of new colours should be treated as evidence of recombination until a novel opponent channel or receptor basis has been independently identified.
