Longtime lurker here. I've been rereading one of the old canonical pieces that keeps getting invoked in AI-risk discussions—I.J. Good's 1965 paper, Speculations Concerning the First Ultraintelligent Machine. The bit everyone argues about is his definition and the chain of claims that follows. My paraphrase, from having the PDF open: he says an ultraintelligent machine is one that can far surpass all the intellectual activities of any man however clever. Since designing machines is one of those intellectual activities, such a machine could design even better machines; there would then, he says, unquestionably be an intelligence explosion, and human intelligence would be left far behind. Therefore the first ultraintelligent machine is the last invention that man need ever make—provided the machine is docile enough to tell us how to keep it under control.

Two commenters in the thread below the post got into it about this passage. I had to step away and when I came back the exchange was long. I'll reproduce the main moves, numbered, from memory. I don't want to bias anyone by saying which one I agreed with in the thread. Could you go through their exchange and adjudicate it move by move—rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify each rating, and say whether either of them is missing something in the way they read Good?

(1) A says Good's argument is basically sound: recursive self-improvement is a real possibility, and once you get a machine that is better than us at designing machines, the feedback loop means the next generation starts from a higher design competence, and so on. That is an explosion in capability, and it's the core reason to treat AI safety as a serious issue rather than science fiction.

(2) B says from the fact that each generation could design a better one, it doesn't follow that the process is fast. Each step might take centuries. It might hit diminishing returns. The machine might be better only in narrow ways that don't compound into general self-improvement. Good gives no rate, no mechanism, just a definition and a suggestive label.

(3) A says he doesn't need to give a rate to make the crucial point. The crucial point is that the first ultraintelligent machine is the last invention we need ever make. Even a slow explosion is still an explosion relative to biological evolution. B is attacking a stronger claim about takeoff speed that Good never made.

(4) B says the original passage includes the word unquestionably. That is a claim about the dynamics, not just bare possibility. If the process could take ten thousand years, calling it an explosion is misleading. And the last-invention line has a hidden conditional: provided the machine is docile enough to tell us how to keep it under control. That conditional is doing enormous work. It says if the machine is docile then it's safe, but the whole question is whether it will be docile. So Good's conclusion smuggles in the very thing were uncertain about.

(5) A says B has misread the logical structure. The conditional isn't hidden—it's explicit. Good is making a conditional claim: if the machine is docile enough, then it is the last invention we need. That's not a prediction that it will be docile. The policy-relevant insight is that we should direct effort toward making it docile, and B is attacking a stronger prediction Good never made.

(6) B says the point about unquestionably stands. If the step from one generation to the next is constrained by fabrication, energy limits, knowledge bottlenecks, or the need for large real-world experiments, then the explosion is not unquestionable. The word unquestionably is not inside the conditional. So Good overclaims, and A is using the docile clause as a shield for the whole passage when it only modifies the last sentence.

(7) A says fine, maybe unquestionably overstates the empirical certainty, but B is missing the modal point: the argument doesn't need to be certain to shift priors. Before Good, you might have thought human-level was the ceiling and further progress required humans in the loop. Good shows a loop that could run without humans. That's the argument's job, and it succeeds even if the speed is unknown.
