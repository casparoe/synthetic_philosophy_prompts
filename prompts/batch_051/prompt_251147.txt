Me and a friend have been going back and forth about whether moral claims are ever really true or false, or whether they're more like expressions of attitude — the whole "is 'torture is wrong' more like 'torture, boo'?" thing. We keep talking in circles, and he thinks I'm overreading everything. Then it hit me that there's a legal practice that seems to take a pretty definite stand on this, and I want to know whether it actually does.

Here's the practice. For a long time, if a former dictator had been granted an amnesty in his own country for atrocities committed while he was in power, that was basically the end of the matter: no other country would prosecute him, because you can't convict someone for something that wasn't a crime under the law that applied to him at the time. But over the last few decades, courts in third countries have started arresting such people anyway, under doctrines like crimes against humanity or universal jurisdiction, on the ground that torture and mass killing are wrong regardless of what local law said — and that an amnesty can wipe out the legal liability without touching the underlying wrongness. So the practice looks like it presupposes that there are moral facts that stand above any legal system's say-so, there to be discovered rather than created.

I'm not convinced it needs the full metaphysical package, though. Couldn't someone who thinks moral claims just express attitudes also support universal jurisdiction, because they too have the attitude that torturers should be locked up no matter what their local law says? Or does the practice quietly commit the legal system to something stronger? I'd like you to work through that.
