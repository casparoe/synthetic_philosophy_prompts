I've got a standing rule with animals, and I'm starting to wonder if it's actually a good epistemic policy or just a bias wearing a lab coat. When I see an animal do something that looks clever, I make myself start from the least mentally fancy explanation that fits, and I only move up if that really can't handle the behavior. Like, my dog sometimes nudges me toward the treat cupboard when I get home late—fine, association, he's learned that sound of my keys means food is likely. My friend once saw a crow drop walnuts onto a crosswalk and wait for cars to crack them, and she was convinced it had solved a problem about how to use traffic. My first reaction was that the crow could have stumbled onto the effect and then repeated whatever preceded it. I can almost always construct some lower-rung story if I try hard enough. My friend says I've turned Morgan's canon into a personal rule—don't attribute a higher mental faculty when a lower one will do—and that it's just anthropomorphism in the other direction: I treat "association" as the always-available cheap explanation, so no amount of flexible behavior ever gets counted as thought. She also says the canon was written in a period when we barely knew what animals could do, and using it as a default now is outdated. But I think I'm just refusing to over-interpret, especially since animals can't correct our story about them. Is my rule defensible? And is there a version of it that keeps me from fooling myself without leaving me permanently unable to notice when an animal genuinely is doing something smarter?
