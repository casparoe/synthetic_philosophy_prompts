Just had a long, exhausting argument with a friend about whether randomized trials are circular, and I can't shake it. Her line was: a trial is supposed to tell us whether X causes Y, but to know we ran it right we have to know our manipulation was a proper intervention — that it was surgical, changing only X and reaching Y only through X, not through a bundle of other things or a placebo back-channel. But "reaching Y only through X" is itself causal talk, so we're using causal knowledge to set up the very test that's supposed to discover causal knowledge. I said that's just the usual circularity worry for manipulation-and-invariance accounts of causation (roughly: causes are what can be wiggled to make a difference, and the difference holds up under interventions), and it's not fatal. She says it makes the whole "learn causes by intervening" picture bankrupt.

So here's what I want: what theory of causation does experimental practice — RCTs, A/B tests, natural experiments, instrumental variables if they're relevant — actually presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory: manipulationist, invariance-based, something else? Then tell me whether the circularity is real or only apparent, and what would concretely change in how experiments are designed and interpreted if that implicit theory were swapped for a pure regularity view (causes are just constant conjunctions) or a plain possible-worlds counterfactual view. The "what would change" part matters most, becuase my friend would only concede if the circularity turns out to be a feature that's doing real work, not a bug.
