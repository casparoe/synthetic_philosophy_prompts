I keep going back and forth on something I noticed and I can't tell if its a real parallel or just me pattern-matching. In English we were doing vocabulary and someone said decimate really means to kill exactly one in ten because of the Latin root for ten, and that using it to mean destroy a large portion of something is a mistake. My teacher called that the etymological fallacy: the idea that a word's oldest or root meaning is its true meaning and any later meaning is a corruption. Then in government we covered originalism, the view that a law or the Constitution means what it meant at the time it was written and ratified, and that later changes in how we'd use those same words shouldn't count, so judges who read it differently are basically editing it. It struck me that both positions do the same thing, but I'm not sure the same thing goes all the way down.

The shared structure seems to be: there is a word or text that had some meaning at an earlier time, ordinary usage or interpretation drifts, and the view says the earlier meaning is the real one and the later drift is misuse. Both also have to answer which original meaning counts (the root? the meaning at enactment? whose understanding?) and why later people should be stuck with the old one. But there are obvious differences too: a word like decimate wasn't enacted by anyone and nobody has authority over its meaning, whereas a law was deliberately made to bind people, so democratic legitimacy might make its original meaning special in a way that word origins aren't. My actual question is whether originalism is just the etymological fallacy applied to legal language, or if not, what exactly is the difference that makes original meanings authoritative for a statute but not for a vocabulary word? And if the analogy holds, should the usual linguists' debunking of the etymological fallacy transfer over and debunk originalism, or does the transfer fail somewhere specific?
