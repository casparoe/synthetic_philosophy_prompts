I am preparing a seminar on strategic reasoning and keep returning to an argument whose status I cannot settle, and I want to be able to present it to the class with an exact diagnosis. Consider a four-move centipede game between two players, A and B. At the first node, A chooses Take or Pass: Take ends the game with payoffs (4, 1) to A and B respectively, while Pass moves to the second node. At the second node, B chooses Take, ending with (2, 8), or Pass, moving to the third node. At the third node, A chooses Take, ending with (16, 4), or Pass, moving to the fourth node. At the fourth node, B chooses Take, ending with (8, 32), or Pass, ending with (64, 16). The payoffs are utilities, each player cares only about maximizing her own payoff, and the entire tree is known to both players.

The argument runs as follows. (1) At the fourth node, a rational B takes, because 32 is better for B than 16. (2) At the third node, A knows (1), so A knows that passing would end with B taking and leave A with 8; since taking gives A 16, a rational A takes. (3) At the second node, B knows (2), so B knows that passing would end with A taking and leave B with 4; since taking gives B 8, a rational B takes. (4) At the first node, A knows (3), so A knows that passing would end with B taking and leave A with 2; since taking gives A 4, a rational A takes. Therefore (5) rational play stops immediately at (4, 1), even though the continuation path on which A passes, B passes, A passes, and B then takes gives A 8 and B 32 — strictly more for both than the stopping point.

I cannot tell whether this is a piece of impeccable reasoning with an unwelcome conclusion or whether something shifts between the concrete reasoning at the last node and the conditional reasoning at the earlier ones. Is the argument valid? If not, at which step does it first fail, and under what exact reading of the relevant "knows" or "rational"? If it is valid, then say which premise must be given up to resist the conclusion, and what exactly we are committed to about the beliefs players would hold at nodes that, according to the argument itself, should never be reached.
