I'm a doctoral student who has to lead a two-hour session for advanced undergraduates who have already worked through the justified-true-belief analysis, Gettier cases, and the internalism/externalism split. I want the session to make pragmatic encroachment feel like a live option rather than a slogan — the idea that whether someone knows can depend partly on what is practically at stake for them — and to leave students able to distinguish that view cleanly from epistemic contextualism. I found the standard low-stakes/high-stakes pairs compelling when I first met them, but I suspect that if I simply present such a pair, a good half of my students will shrug or feel they have been tricked. Don't explain the two views to me; plan the order in which I should teach them. What has to be in place before the high-stakes version of the case has any force — in particular, how much of the classical picture of knowledge as fixed by truth, belief, and evidence should I lay out first, and what bridge principle connecting knowledge to action has to be introduced so that practical stakes can even look relevant? Which single example should carry the whole session, and what must it have and avoid? I imagine it must hold the evidence and the truth of the proposition fixed across versions, and it must not let the high-stakes subject merely become more cautious or more anxious; the only thing that should vary is what the subject stands to lose.

Give me the predictable misconceptions in the order students will form them — I would guess at least these: that raising the stakes lowers the probability of the proposition; that knowledge requires certainty; that the cases obviously concern practical rationality and could not possibly reveal anything about knowledge; and that contextualism is a relativism about truth, or is just the same view as pragmatic encroachment with different branding. For each, tell me at which point in the sequence I should head it off rather than wait to correct it afterward. End the plan with one check for understanding — ideally one that varies the evaluator's practical stakes while holding the subject's situation fixed — that would show they have genuinely got the difference between standards shifting for the word "knows" and stakes changing whether the knowledge relation itself obtains, and say explicitly which nearby topics — the formal semantics of epistemic vocabulary, the norms of assertion, the social functions of knowledge ascriptions — I should announce as out of scope at this level.
