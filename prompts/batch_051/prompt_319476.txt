I'm a reporter on the science and ideas beat, but I've been stuck on a legal puzzle about dead-letter laws. The same officials who'll tell you that the rule of law is all about generality — nobody above the law, no punishment except under pre-existing rules, the same rules for everyone — will also leave a nineteenth-century ban on adultery sitting on the books forever, unrepealed and unenforced, because writing the repeal bill just isn't worth the political cost. And it's not only adultery: blue laws against Sunday sales, fortune-telling bans, old morality statutes. They're not repealed, they're not enforced, and yet they remain "valid law," sometimes still capable of doing real legal work — a predicate offense, a bargaining chip in a plea negotiation, a ground for voiding a contract. Some legal traditions even have a doctrine for this, desuetude, where a law can lapse through long non-use. The system I'm interested in mostly rejects that, or half-accepts it.

Here's what I want. Reconstruct the implicit theory of legal validity, official role, and the rule of law that makes this practice coherent — not just excusable but principled. Does the practice presuppose that a law's validity comes from its source (it was enacted) rather than from it's actual grip on anyone's conduct? Does it presuppose a sharp split between identifying law and enforcing it, where enforcement is the executive's optional instrument rather than part of what the law is? Does it quietly treat the law as a menu of possible charges rather than a set of standing rules that actually stand? Then tell me whether the practice strictly needs that theory or is compatible with several rival theories — and which rival theory it most clearly rules out. Finally, flip it: if a robust desuetude doctrine were adopted and non-use could actually kill a statute, what else in the system's self-understanding would have to give? I care less about the reform proposal than about what the reform would reveal about what the current practice has to believe.
