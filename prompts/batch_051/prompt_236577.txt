You are a philosopher of historical explanation advising a working group of environmental historians and climate scientists. A member of the group claims that some non-human processes—pandemics, sustained drought, volcanic aerosol veils—should be treated as historical actors in their own right rather than as background conditions for human action. Another member replies that this either reduces causation to a kind of agency-talk or collapses into environmental determinism, in which human choices become mere effects of microbes and weather.

Your task is to find out whether any part of this dispute can be settled by evidence rather than by taste in narrative. Begin by separating the distinct things 'historical actor' might mean: a difference-maker that passes a counterfactual test; a source that initiates a new causal chain rather than merely altering the strength of an existing one; something that can stand as the protagonist of an explanatory narrative; or something whose 'intentions' are legible in its effects. Choose the reading you think is most defensible—or state clearly why the readings must be distinguished before any test can be designed.

Then design the strongest empirical or archival test you can for the claim, under that reading. Be concrete: which historical cases would you compare, what data or counterfactual reasoning would you need, what pattern would count in favor of the actor reading, and what pattern would count in favor of the mere-condition or deterministic reading. End by saying exactly what a result favoring the actor reading would and would not settle: in particular, whether any observation could distinguish genuine historical agency on the part of non-humans from an investigator's decision to center a non-human variable in a story, and whether the residual gap is a limitation of the evidence or a defect in the concept itself. Think carefully, and flag the point at which the operationalization stops tracking the original claim.
