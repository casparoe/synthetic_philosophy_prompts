I cover philosophy and the behavioral sciences for a general-interest magazine, and I am trying to characterize the decades-long stalemate over moral luck for an article. The standard cases are familiar: two equally drunk drivers run a red light, one hits and kills a pedestrian while the other is stopped a minute later; two people form identical bad intentions, and circumstances alone intervene for one. Observers blame the unlucky driver far more, and all sides concede both facts: our reactions differ, and the difference in outcome was outside either agent's control. What has not moved since the debate took shape in the mid-1970s is whether that asymmetry is defensible.

The positions have hardened into a small menu. One camp holds that the asymmetry is indefensible and that blame should track only what an agent controlled. A second holds that the asymmetry is defensible because outcomes can reveal something about the agent, or because our reactive emotions are not and need not be perfectly fair instruments. A third tries to dissolve the puzzle by separating blameworthiness from punishment, or from the warranted expression of reactive attitudes. Reading the back-and-forth, I am struck by how often each side concedes the others' local points — the controllability point, the point about what outcomes reveal, the point about the expressive function of blame — and how little anyone moves.

My working hypothesis is that this is not a factual dispute and not merely a dispute about the word "blame." It looks like a collision between two different jobs moral assessment is being asked to do: a ledger function, which records what is properly attributable to an agent, and a signaling function, which regulates how hard people should try and how much they should care. The ledger pushes toward symmetry of blame because control does not differ; the signaling function pushes toward outcome-sensitivity because outcome-sensitivity calibrates incentives and emotional attention.

I would like a diagnosis rather than a verdict. Is the disagreement verbal or substantive? Are the camps answering different questions under one heading? Is my ledger-versus-signal hypothesis the right way to characterize it, or is something else — a hidden empirical claim, a difference over which cases count as data, an unargued starting intuition — doing the real work? And given the diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, a moral-psychology finding, a formal result, a change of question, or the frank conclusion that no progress should be expected. Keep the diagnosis to 400 words or fewer.
