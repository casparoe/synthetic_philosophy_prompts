In the experimental philosophy unit of my mind-and-language course, two people in my study group got into a long argument about the Knobe effect, and I got stuck trying to referee them. You probably know the setup: an executive says he does not care whether a new project damages the environment, he only cares about profit, and subjects tend to say he damaged the environment intentionally; in the matched case where the side effect helps the environment, and he also says he does not care, subjects mostly say he did not help the environment intentionally. Mara and Jordan agreed on that result but not on what it means.

Mara said the asymmetry shows the ordinary concept of doing something intentionally is not a purely descriptive notion: whether an outcome is good or bad shifts whether people count it as intentional, so the folk's core action-concepts are soaked in evaluation. Jordan answered that this was too fast — in the harm case, saying he did it "intentionally" is doing a blaming job rather than describing a mental state, and participants hear the question as "should he be held responsible?" and answer that; the underlying concept could be perfectly neutral. Mara came back that if it were just pragmatics of the question, the effect should shift or vanish when you change the wording, the response format, or the scenario — for example, ask whether he did it "on purpose" in a case with no one obviously to blame — and she thought that if the effect survives all of that, robustness is evidence about the concept rather than the questionnaire. Jordan said fine, robustness would be real, but it doesnt pick between her view and his: in the classic harm case the agent accepts a side effect that conflicts with his main goal, while in the help case the side effect costs him nothing, so maybe people call side effects intentional when they are a cost against a goal, not because they are bad. Mara admitted that confound was real, but said Jordan's version has its own problem: imagine an evacuation that saves many lives but as an unavoidable side effect destroys a beloved mural, where the good main goal does not trade against the damage at all, or a good side effect that actively frustrates the agent's goal. She said she honestly did not know what the folk would say about those cases, and both of them agreed that by the time you design them, the original effect alone can't tell the two stories apart.

I couldnt decide who won the exchange, so I'm asking you to adjudicate it move by move: say what each of them is right about at each turn, where each overclaims, and what the Knobe effect actually does and doesn't show given just their exchange. Then be explicit about what would change your verdict — which pattern of results in the cases they floated (or a case you'd design) would flip you, and which step in your adjudication you're least sure about.
