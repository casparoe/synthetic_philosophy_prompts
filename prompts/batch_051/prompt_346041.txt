Here's an asymmetry I keep noticing in how people (me included) talk about population and quality of life. Take a city of 100,000 people with an average wellbeing of 8/10. Nearly everyone says: if we could make those same 100,000 people a bit better off, moving the average to 8.2, that would be a clear improvement, a good thing to do. Now suppose instead the city grows to 200,000 people, all of them genuinely happy, just with an average of 7.9. Total happiness has shot up, but the average has ticked down. A lot of people won't call that an improvement; some will even call it a decline, because the average dropped. Yet if you press them, they also don't want to say that a city of a million mildly happy people is no better than a city of ten thousand somewhat happier ones. So which is it? Is there a defensible principle that treats making existing people better off as a real improvement while staying neutral (or negative) about adding more happy people, without forcing you to say a bigger happy family, city, or species doesn't matter in itself? Or is the asymmetry just a mental shortcut that tracks averages when it should track totals, or the other way around? Give me the strongest principle you can find and an honest verdict on whether it survives.
