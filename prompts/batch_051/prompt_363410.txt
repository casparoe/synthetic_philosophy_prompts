At a workshop I chaired last term on distributive justice, two of my colleagues spent the closing hour in a running argument about what an egalitarian society should equalize in the first place; I stayed out of it at the time, and I want a cold adjudication of the arguments they actually made.

Fonseca defended the position that justice should equalize people's resources, not their satisfaction or their capabilities. His central move was the familiar expensive-tastes case: if one person is content with modest pleasures while another has cultivated tastes that require opera tickets and fine wine, the second person is not owed a larger share on that account. Equalizing welfare would make the rest of us pay for other people's preferences, Fonseca argued, and that mistakes the whole object of equalization; justice should equalize the external circumstances within which people pursue their own ends, and then let each person bear the consequences — good or bad — of how their tastes and ambitions convert those resources into happiness. Ellison replied that resources are the wrong currency because people differ sharply in their power to convert resources into actual doings and beings. A person with a serious mobility impairment may need far more than equal money to move about town, work, or take part in public life. Fonseca answered that an impairment is an unchosen circumstance, not a chosen taste, so on his view it is precisely what a fair system of social insurance should compensate. Ellison said this concession gives the game away: to know how much compensation is enough, you have to ask what the person can actually do and be at various levels of funding — and that is already to measure capabilities, not resources. Fonseca countered that capabilities are too amorphous to serve as a public standard: there is no uncontroversial list of which capabilities matter, and once the state equalizes capabilities it will have to make substantive judgments about the good life that a liberal society should leave to individuals. Ellison replied that this worry is overstated, because capabilities are freedoms rather than achieved outcomes — the state need not require anyone to use their options — but she granted that Fonseca's point shows why a capability metric must be justified rather than assumed.

Toward the end someone asked each of them to say, in one sentence, what the other would have to establish to win. Fonseca said Ellison would need a general principle for weighing different capabilities against one another and against income, without smuggling in a controversial ranking of ways of life. Ellison said Fonseca would need the distinction between a person's choices and her unchosen circumstances to be sharp enough to do the work his view requires — and she doubted it could be, once one notices that health, talent, social expectations, and even the preferences one finds available to adopt are all, to varying degrees, unchosen.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each main claim, indicate whether it is clearly right, clearly wrong, or a genuinely open issue; note what both parties are missing; and say which of the two is more right overall, if either. Throughout, separate what is settled among people working on this topic from what remains genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
