I have been trying to work out what a large language model can actually do when it produces text that looks like a performative utterance: a routine message reading that a company accepts an offer, a chatbot's apology, an automated notice that a payment has been authorised. J. L. Austin's original account treated such utterances as actions whose success depends on felicity conditions rather than truth: there must be an accepted conventional procedure, the persons and circumstances must be appropriate, the procedure must be carried out correctly, the speaker must have whatever intentions or feelings the procedure calls for, and the utterance must in the relevant sense be taken up by an audience. The standard examples implicitly assumed a single human speaker who decides to perform the act on the occasion.

Suppose that a large and increasing share of the performative-looking utterances that matter are now assembled by models that have no intentions themselves, but are deployed under an operator's standing authorisation: the model may be allowed to accept, apologise, warn, or confirm within parameters, without a human composing or even reviewing the exact sentence. Does Austin's account survive this development intact, does it need repair (for example, treating the operator or the deploying organisation as the speaker and the model as a kind of delegated utterance), or does it lapse until a human ratifies the act? Which felicity condition is actually under pressure, and is there a repaired version that preserves the original insight that saying can be doing? I am interested in what happens to the speech-act account itself, not in the policy question of how such systems ought to be regulated.
