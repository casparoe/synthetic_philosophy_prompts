so in my philosophy of language and misinformation seminar we've been on Frankfurt's bullshit/lying distinction, and I've talked myself into a view I want stress-tested. My position is that LLM hallucination should be classified as bullshit in the relevant sense, not lying and not straightforward error. Lying requires something like an intention to deceive, and calling something an error presupposes the system was at least trying to get at the truth; LLMs don't seem to have either. But they do have a training objective that's basically "produce the most plausible next token," which is indifferent to truth. So when a model confidently invents a legal case or a study that doesn't exist, it doesn't look like it's failing at a truth-aiming task—it's succeeding at the task it actually has, which is exactly the bullshit profile.

What I want is a map of what would count against this. Assume I'm already committed to the bullshit classification: tell me what evidence, arguments, or future developments should actually move me, sorted from decisive to merely awkward. Would it count as evidence for "error" if models hedge more when you tell them truth matters more than fluency, or if scaling and RLHF reduce hallucinations in a way that looks like better truth-tracking rather than just more memorization? What would mechanistic interpretability have to find, or fail to find, for me to give the view up—a world model that gets consulted and then overridden, an explicit truth-checking step, what? Is there a version of the "lying" classification that becomes live once you drop full-blown first-person intent and talk about the system including its designers instead? And I'm also worried I've set things up so that any inaccurate output just counts as more evidence for me, which would make the view unfalsifiable. Tell me honestly if that's what I've done, and whether that's fatal or just kind of fine for a thesis like this one.
