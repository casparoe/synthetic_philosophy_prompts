I'm a PhD student in the ethics of AI and international security, and a chapter of my dissertation is on lethal autonomous weapons. The problem is that everyone in the field uses the term meaningful human control, but nobody quite means the same thing by it. My supervisor has been pushing me to stop just surveying the options and actually commit to a ranking, so I wanted an outside take.

Could you sort the following five accounts into tiers (S/A/B/C) by how plausible you'd take them to be as a general account of when a human has enough control over a lethal autonomous system? Please give a sentence or two for each about why it lands where it lands.

1. Target-level control: a human must select and approve each specific target before the system may engage it. 2. Last-moment veto: the crucial thing is that a human operator can intervene and abort an attack up to the point of no return. 3. Tracking-and-failing conditions: the system's capabilities, and the situation it's used in, must let the operator track what the weapon is doing and foresee the relevant outcomes, and the system should fail in ways that don't undermine that awareness. 4. Deployment-level control: it's enough that a human decides whether, when, and where to deploy the system, even if the system then picks and engages targets on its own. 5. Scalar view: there's no single yes/no requirement; control is a matter of degree and has to be judge case by case against the mission and the environment.

I know these aren't fully mutually exclusive, but I'd like your sense of which ones are doing the real normative work. I don't need citations or a lit review, just your honest ranking and the reasoning, even if it means biteing some bullets.
