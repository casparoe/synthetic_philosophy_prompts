For the past few weeks I have been arguing with a colleague about a provision in the enabling statute of a central bank — a fictional provision, but drafted in the register of the real statutes of the 1930s. Section 4(a) reads:

'The Bank may issue notes and may credit the accounts of member banks. Every such issue or credit shall be matched, at the time it is made, by the delivery to the Bank of assets of equivalent value, of such classes and subject to such discounts as the Board shall from time to time determine. The Bank shall not otherwise create money or credit. The Bank shall not lend directly to, or purchase securities directly from, the Government, but may buy and sell Government securities already issued in the open market.'

We have been applying it to a case its drafters plainly did not foresee. In a recent financial crisis, the Bank credited the account of a special-purpose vehicle established by the Government — not a member bank, and not itself a government department in the ordinary sense — and the vehicle used the credit to buy newly issued government bonds directly from the Treasury. The only matching asset delivered to the Bank in exchange was a certificate from the Treasury stating that the Bank may at any time require the Treasury to pay it the face amount of the bonds; the certificate bears no interest, has no maturity date, cannot be sold, and the Bank has announced it will never present it for payment. The Bank's lawyers say the certificate is an asset of equivalent value and that the section's prohibition on direct purchases was addressed to the Bank itself, not to vehicles it funds. My colleague says the transaction is exactly the government financing the section was written to prevent, laundered through an intermediary.

What I want is less a prediction about how a court would rule than an interpretation that is honest about what the clause was doing. Argue the case from the words, from the purpose of the section, and from what the drafters would most plausibly have said if the transaction had been described to them. Then say which of these approaches should control here, and what that answer commits you to about the underlying question: when the Bank credits an account against paper of this kind, has it created money, created wealth, or merely exchanged one set of claims for another — and does the clause's picture of 'assets of equivalent value' survive contact with this case?
