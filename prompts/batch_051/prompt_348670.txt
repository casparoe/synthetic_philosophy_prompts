For an essay on the role of appeals to nature in legal argument, I want to work through a concrete case. Under the common-law doctrine of coverture as it operated in England, still in force through much of the nineteenth century, a married woman's legal personality was absorbed into her husband's: her property and earnings came under his control, she could not sue or contract in her own name, and a husband held broad legal authority over her person. Defenders of this arrangement were not content to defend it as mere custom or administrative convenience; they defended it as a match for women's nature, arguing that women's emotional volatility, weaker reason, and domestic vocation made their subordination fitting. Yet the same legal system allowed unmarried women and widows to hold property, make contracts, and sue, which suggests the alleged incapacity arrived with marriage rather than with womanhood. What view of the relation between nature and social arrangements does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory, and say which rival views about nature and social order it quietly rules out. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it is merely compatible with, and assess whether the appeal to nature is doing independent argumentative work or only decorating a prior commitment to male authority. Finally, say what would have to change in the law and in its defense if we replaced that assumption with the view John Stuart Mill presses in The Subjection of Women: that the traits attributed to women's nature are largely the artificial product of custom and unequal institutions rather than their cause.
