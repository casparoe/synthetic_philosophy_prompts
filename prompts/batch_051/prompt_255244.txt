Something I've never been able to get comfortable with is that basically every activity imposes some risk on people who never consented, and the two standard answers both feel wrong to me: cost-benefit regulation (which seems to assume a consent nobody was actually asked for) and requiring actual informed consent from everyone affected (which is unworkable the moment the "affected population" is a city or the whole internet). So I've been tinkering with a mechanism in between, and I want it stress-tested as a mechanism, not evaluated as a moral stance.

Call it sample consent with a universal price. When someone wants to impose a new risk on a non-consenting population—pollution from a plant, an AI system deployed in a public space, a drug with side effects—they have to name the exposed group and state a price per person their willing to pay. A neutral body draws a random panel of, say, 400 people from that group. The panel gets the proposer's own risk description plus an independant summary, and votes. If 60% or more vote yes, the project goes ahead, and everyone in the exposed group, not just the panel, receives the exact same per-person payment. If the vote fails, the proposer can rerun it once at a higher price; if it fails again, the project is blocked for five years. As part of the proposal, being outvoted or taking the payment extinguishes ordinary tort claims over that specific risk, but gross negligence and criminal liability remain.

The failure modes I can already half-see: the proposer sets the price to buy the median panelist while the people in the tail of the distribution, who'd get hit much harder by the same risk, get the same check and lose their day in court. The population definition is gameable—declare the affected group to be people who won't actually mind the risk. The panel might approve because they're being paid to decide for other people, not because they'd personally accept the risk. Are these bugs or fatal flaws? And is there a tweak—say, pricing by individual actuarial risk instead of a flat rate, or giving a veto to the most-exposed decile—that fixes the worst of it without just reintroducing the original consent problem under a new name?
