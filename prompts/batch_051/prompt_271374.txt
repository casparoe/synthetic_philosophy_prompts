I am a novelist, not a mathematician or a political scientist, and I have agreed to run a fifty-minute seminar for a circle of fellow fiction writers. The group is collectively sketching a novel in which a small town attempts to redesign the way it elects a local council, and one of the characters, a retired social choice theorist as it happens, keeps insisting that the reform is doomed for a structural reason the other characters cannot see. The writers are intelligent about psychology and motive, but none of them has any formal training in economics, mathematics, or formal political theory. They arrive, I expect, with an unexamined folk picture in which elections go wrong because people are dishonest or apathetic, and in which a good voting system is simply one that makes cheating harder. I would like the session to leave them with something more precise than that.

That something more precise is the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem. As I understand it, roughly: when there are at least three possible candidates and every voter submits a complete strict ranking of them, any rule that converts those rankings into a single winner is either dictatorial in a precise technical sense, or susceptible to strategic voting, meaning some voter can sometimes do better by reporting a ranking other than her true one. If that rough statement is misleading or missing needed conditions, I would like the plan to correct me before it uses me.
