Spent my career in physics and only recently started reading the decision-theory side of philosophy properly, instead of just skimming the fun puzzles. I've built myself a little argument that I half-believe, and I can't find the fault, so I want it checked step by step.

(1) Rational action is choosing the act with the highest expected utility.
(2) Expected utility is calculated with your current, fully updated probabilities: the posterior you have after conditioning on everything you've observed.
(3) In Newcomb's problem, a very reliable predictor has already put $1,000,000 in box A if and only if it predicted you'll take only box A; box B is transparent and holds $1,000. Since the money is already placed, your posterior treats the contents of box A as fixed no matter what you now do.
(4) So taking both boxes pays $1,000 more than taking only A whichever prediction was made. On (1) and (2), two-boxing is required.
(5) But in this setup the one-boxers reliably come away with the million, while two-boxers reliably come away with a mere $1,000.
(6) So the fully updated, posterior-based calculation is systematically recommending the act with the worse payoff in exactly the case where the pre-prediction correlation between your choice and the prediction mattered.
(7) Therefore the correct rule cannot always be 'use your current posterior.' It should sometimes be updateless: evaluate your act with the probabilities you had before you learned anything that is correlated with your own decision.

I suspect (2) and (7) are carrying alot of the weight, but I can't say exactly what each would mean on its strongest reading, or whether the argument survives once you distinguish a couple of things that are both being called 'probabilities you have.' Which numbered step is the first one that fails? Or is the argument fine and I should just bite the bullet?
