I have noticed an asymmetry in my own reactions to environmental explanations of history, and I want to know whether it rests on a defensible principle or only on a temperamental prejudice.

I accept without hesitation that the 1815 eruption of Mount Tambora caused the year without a summer in 1816, and that the resulting failed harvests caused food shortages and hunger riots in several countries. Each link in the chain is traceable, and the claim seems to me like ordinary causal history, not environmental determinism.

Yet I reject, with real resistance, the claim that the cooling of the Little Ice Age caused the early modern European witch-hunts; and I would likewise reject the claim that climate change caused the fall of the Roman Empire. Those claims feel monocausal and deterministic, even though they share their broad form with the Tambora case: a non-human environmental process is credited with a mass human catastrophe.

At first I want to distinguish the cases by saying that Tambora explains a bounded, short-term, material event through an inspectable mechanism, while the Little Ice Age and Roman claims explain large-scale cultural or civilizational outcomes. But that could collapse into the difference between claims I find well-evidenced and claims I find under-evidenced, which is not yet a theoretical account of when non-human factors may act as historical causes.

Please state precisely the strongest candidate principle that licenses my first verdict without licensing the second. Then test it against these cases: the Black Death of 1347–1351 being credited with weakening serfdom in Western Europe; prolonged drought being credited with the ninth-century collapse of the Classic Maya; the potato blight of the 1840s causing the Irish famine and the demographic collapse that followed; and the Lisbon earthquake of 1755 being credited with undermining European confidence in divine providence.

Finally, apply the principle to the case that most unsettles me. I am half-inclined to accept that the cold, wet weather of 1816 shaped the circumstances in which Mary Shelley began Frankenstein — a climate-to-culture claim at a much smaller scale than the witch-hunts, but with a documented chain. If the principle rejects the witch-hunt claim, what does it say about this one? And if it cannot deliver stable verdicts across all of these cases, tell me which of my starting judgments I should overturn and why.
