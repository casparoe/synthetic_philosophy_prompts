I keep coming back to Maxwell's demon, probabaly because I spent forty years doing thermodynamics and only now started reading the philosophy of physics around it. There's an argument I've seen in a few places, and as I reconstruct it, it's meant to show that the information-theoretic treatment of the demon saves the second law. I half-suspect it's too quick, but I can't quite locate the sleight of hand. Here it is:

(1) The second law says the entropy of an isolated system never decreases.

(2) The demon, the box of gas, and the shutter together form an isolated system, so the total entropy of that composite never decreases.

(3) When the demon sorts the fast and slow molecules onto opposite sides, the thermodynamic entropy of the gas itself goes down.

(4) To do the sorting, the demon has to take in information about the molecules' speeds and hold it in a physical record. A stored bit is physical, and there's a famous result that erasing one dissipates at least kT ln 2 of heat, so building up a record of all those sorting decisions adds back at least as much entropy as the gas loses.

(5) Therefore the apparent drop in the gas's entropy is offset by the demon's record-keeping, total entropy doesn't decrease, and the second law is safe.

Is the argument valid, and is each premise true? I especially suspect (4) is doing double duty with the word 'information' or 'entropy' — treating a Shannon-style measure of the record's contents as if it were the thermodynamic entropy of the combined system. But maybe I'm wrong, and the argument just goes through. If (4) has a stronger reading on which it is true, tell me what it is and check whether the argument still goes through. Give me a definite verdict: either the argument is fine, or it fails, and if it fails I want the first step that fails and why. Don't lay out a menu of options for me to pick from; commit to one and defend it.
