In my civics class, we studied the U.S. Foreign Corrupt Practices Act, which generally forbids American companies from bribing foreign officials to obtain or keep business. But the same law contains a narrow exception for small payments made to a foreign official to expedite or secure performance of a routine governmental action the official is already required to perform, such as issuing a permit, processing a visa, or providing police protection. These are often called facilitation payments or grease payments. I am trying to figure out what theory of corruption that exception actually presupposes. Does the distinction between paying for a favorable discretionary decision and paying for the nondiscretionary performance of an owed action commit the law to the view that corruption is essentially wrongful influence over judgment, rather than, for example, the sale of official action itself or the use of public office as a source of private income? Is that distinction coherent once you acknowledge that low-level officials usually retain some control over timing, order, and whose paperwork moves first, so that even seemingly routine action can become a choke point? And if one instead held that any private payment to a public official for an official act is corrupt, even when the act is already owed, what would have to change about the law and about the category of routine facilitation payments? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
