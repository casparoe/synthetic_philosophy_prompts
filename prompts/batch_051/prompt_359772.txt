I am a PhD student in philosophy working on formal accounts of the betterness relation, and I have found myself applying a methodological policy in seminars and in my own reading that I cannot fully underwrite. I want to know whether it is a defensible epistemic rule or a bias dressed up as methodology.

The policy governs conflicts between case-level intuitions and principle-level commitments. I work with principles that are simple, that impose transitivity, and that earn their keep by bundling many pairwise judgments into a few constraints. When I encounter a single imagined case—often a purported counterexample offered by a colleague, or an informal set of pairwise choices—in which my intuitive verdicts form an intransitive cycle, I do not treat the case as a counterexample unless at least one of three conditions is met. First, the troubling verdict survives when I redescribe the case in affectively neutral terms and present its components in a different order. Second, the same verdict appears in at least two structurally dissimilar cases that I did not design myself. Third, I can identify a mechanism that explains why cases of this sort fall outside the principle's intended range of application, rather than merely restating that the principle gets the case wrong. If none of these conditions is met, I set the case aside as an unexplained anomaly and keep the principle.

I am aware that this looks like a machine for converting every recalcitrant case into an anomaly, and that the more I value simplicity and transitivity, the more the policy will protect principles that have those features. I am also unsure why simplicity or transitivity should count as evidence of truth at all, rather than as pragmatic features I find tractable. If they are only pragmatic virtues, then giving them an evidential advantage over prima facie counterexamples begins to look circular.

So I have several questions. Is this policy a rational response to the known fragility of case intuitions, or is it a form of question-begging? Under what conditions, if any, should a principle-level intuition or a formal property such as transitivity outweigh a conflicting case-level intuition? Can the policy be restated so that it keeps whatever debiasing value it has without becoming a shield for cherished principles? And how could someone tell from the inside whether they are using the policy as a reliability filter or as a protected way of never losing an argument with recalcitrant cases?
