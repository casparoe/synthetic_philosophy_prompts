Write a dialogue, maybe 700 words, between two philosophers at a conference bar who disagree about whether the nonidentity problem should push us away from a strictly person-affecting ethic. Call one Mira; she defends the narrow person-affecting principle: an outcome can be worse only if it makes some particular person worse off than they would otherwise have been. Call the other Sam; he thinks that principle falls apart on the classic depletion-versus-conservation case, and he should spell the case out: a government can choose a depletion policy that gives the current generation a small benefit while leaving future people with lives that are still worth living but much worse than they would have been under a conservation policy. Because the two policies change who meets whom over the generations, the particular future people under depletion are different from the ones who would have existed under conservation, and each of them has a life worth living. So, Sam argues, depletion isn't worse for anyone—yet it's clearly the worse choice. The case is same-number (both policies yield the same number of future people) but different-people. Sam should argue for a wide person-affecting principle (compare the lives led by whoever exists under each policy, without requiring that it be the same people) and maybe fall back to an impersonal principle about the quality of lives. Don't make Mira a strawman: she should push back hard, for example by charging that the wide principle is just an impersonal ranking wearing a person-affecting disguise, or that once you give up the idea that wrongness requires someone to be worse off, you can't explain what makes morality about persons in the first place; she might even bite the bullet and defend the no-difference conclusion. Make them actually respond to each other's points rather than taking turns monologuing. Let the dialogue end with the disagreement sharper than it started, and don't include citations or book titles. At some point, one of them should crystalize the puzzle as the thought that a choice can be bad for nobody in particular and still be wrong.
