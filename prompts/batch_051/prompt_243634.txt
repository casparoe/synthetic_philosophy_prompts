A friend and I have been going around in circles about Chesterton's fence, and I want to see what someone else makes of it. The principle as I always see it glossed in the rationalist-ish parts of the internet is the citable little cousin of Burkean conservatism: you shouldn't remove an institution, rule, or social practice until you understand why someone put it there, because the reason is often non-obvious and the thing may be doing hidden load-bearing work. The deep version isn't really about loving tradition; it's about the fence encoding information about the world that you don't have yet, and your model of the system being almost certainly simpler than the system itself.

What I keep getting stuck on is that when this argument was made, removing the fence was basically a one-shot act, and the only way to know what it did was to already have a theory of it. But now a huge fraction of the fences people actually argue about are cheaply and reversibly removable, and we can just measure how they work. A platform can take a moderation rule that everyone treats as a mysterious legacy fence, remove it for a random subset of users, watch downstream metrics for a couple months, and roll it back. A city can pilot removing a parking rule. A codebase can delete a weird legacy check and let a canary tell them what breaks. In that setting, do we still need the original reason before touching anything? Or does Chesterton's fence turn into an empirical claim about old institutions that assumed a pre-experimental world, and it's simply dead now that experiment is cheap?

I want a verdict, not a shrug. If you think the argument survives, tell me which premise is actually doing the work. My prior guess is that the surviving version is something like: the most important effects of removing a social rule are slow, second-order, and show up in metrics nobody thought to instrument, so the experiment gives false confidence unless you already have a theory about what to measure and for how long. If that's the real argument, then cheap A/B tests don't refute it at all; they just move the hard part from guessing the function to guessing the right measurement, and a repaired version of the fence still has force. If you think its mostly dead, say precisely what cheap experimentation kills, and what if anything should replace the principle.
