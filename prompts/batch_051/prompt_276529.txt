I am giving a talk next month at an interdisciplinary workshop on the epistemology of misinformation, and I want to be prepared for the question period rather than surprised by it. The thesis I will defend is that the most serious epistemic harm of deepfakes has been mislocated: it is not primarily that fabricated videos will convince people that particular false events occurred, but that the cheap availability of plausible fake recordings turns every genuine and damning recording into something that can be peremptorily dismissed as probably synthetic. Audiovisual recordings have long functioned as an epistemic backstop. When testimony about what a public figure said or did was disputed, the recording served as a shared, hard-to-impugn court of appeal, and a bare assertion that it could have been faked was not enough to neutralize it. Deepfakes remove that backstop by making the dismissal of any recording cheap and socially respectable: one no longer has to show that this recording was doctored, only that recordings of this kind can now be produced. My further claim is that once the backstop dissolves, the damage is done whether or not very many people are actually fooled by any particular fake, and that individual epistemic hygiene — checking provenance, raising evidential standards, attending to sources — cannot rebuild a shared background of credibility once it has been withdrawn.

The audience is not a generalist crowd. Most of the room will be analytic epistemologists who work on testimony, defeaters, and disagreement; they are sympathetic to the problem but are disposed to think the remedy lies in better individual evidential practices, not in a story about shared background conditions. A smaller but vocal bloc consists of computer vision researchers who build synthetic-media detectors and who think of the whole problem as a technical arms race that better detection will eventually win. I expect different objections from those two camps, and I want them in the form they will actually take, in the vocabulary each camp already uses.

Write me a prep sheet, not a polished defense. Order the objections by how likely they are and how damaging they would be, phrase them as these participants would phrase them, say which objections have real force and what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and how to reply in the two or three minutes available for each. Also identify the objection I should be quietly hoping no one raises: the weakest point in my position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss.
