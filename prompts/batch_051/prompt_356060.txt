I keep trying to figure out why the Kuhn vs. Popper debate hasn't budged in fifty years, and I've started to think the real conflict is hidden a level down. The way I hear it, the Popperian says a scientist facing a choice between paradigms can still be rational because the new theory makes successful novel predictions the old one couldn't; the Kuhnian says that's cheating, because inside a revolution the old theory's anomalies get reclassified as things the new theory explains, and the standards for what counts as a good explanation shift. Then the Popperian says that just collapses discovery into justification, and the Kuhnian says justification is only ever relative to a paradigm, so the Popperian is helping herself to post-revolution standards. Everyone agrees on the obvious cases — Copernicus, Lavoisier, Einstein — and nobody moves. My working theory is that they're using 'rational' in two different ways, ex ante and ex post: one is asking whether the switch was rational by the standards available before the revolution, the other whether it was rational by the standards that survived it. But maybe it's really a descriptive-versus-normative fight, or a disagreement about whether rationality attaches to individual scientists or to the community over a generation, or an argument about whether incommensurability is total or only partial. Can you diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is, based on the moves each side makes — and then say what would honestly count as progress? A distinction, a formal result, a historical study, or is the question just ill-posed the way it's usualy framed?
