I've been chewing on the Malcolm-vs-Dennett are dreams experiences thing for a while now, and I keep ending up thinking the real issue is that the word experience is doing an lot of hidden work. This started from a LessWrong rabbit hole, if anyone asks. So, give me a working definition of experience, specifically having an experience during sleep, that gets these cases right:

1. A lucid dream where the person knows they're dreaming and can steer the dream.
2. A normal REM dream with a clear narrative and strong emotion, reported on waking.
3. Dennett's cassette case: the brain writes a dream narrative into memory at the moment of waking, so no dream content was actually run during sleep, but the person sincerely reports a dream afterward.
4. Sleepwalking, where the person navigates around furniture, maybe mumbles, and remembers nothing.
5. A false awakening, where the person dreams they've woken up and starts their usual morning routine.
6. Deep slow-wave sleep, no recall, nothing dramatic on EEG.
7. Someone woken repeatedly from REM who reports no dream, but when the experimenter says a word like house, a dream image suddenly loads and they say it felt like it had been there.
8. A dog's paws twitching and whimpering during REM sleep.
9. A person under general anesthesia with no recall afterward.
10. A hypnagogic image at sleep onset that the person half thinks they saw.

I think 1 and 2 are clearly experiences, 6 and 9 are clearly not, and I don't have a stable view on the rest. Give me the definition, run it through all ten, and tell me which verdict is least secure. Also, if the definition makes case 3 come out different from case 2, defend that a little. Does the definition end up dissolving the original Malcolm/Dennett disagreement, or just relocating it?
