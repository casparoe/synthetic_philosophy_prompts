I have, after oscillating, come to hold a deflationary position about the Sleeping Beauty problem. The protocol, for concreteness: on Sunday, Beauty is told that a fair coin will be flipped. If it lands heads, she will be woken once, on Monday, and the experiment ends. If it lands tails, she will be woken on Monday, given a drug that erases the memory of that waking, and woken again on Tuesday. All wakings are indistinguishable from the inside. When woken, she is asked for her credence that the coin landed heads. Halfers answer 1/2; thirders answer 1/3.

My position is that the protocol, as stated, is underdescribed: there is no single credence the protocol alone mandates. Which answer Beauty should give depends on which question the number is being asked to answer — a one-off assessment of a fair coin, a fair betting price quoted at each waking, a fair betting price quoted once per run, or the expected proportion of heads among wakings in repeated experiments. Halfers and thirders, on my view, are mostly not contradicting one another; each side has silently supplied one of these interpretations, and the appearance of paradox comes from the problem's refusal to specify which interpretation is in force. I would put the point more sharply: self-locating credence is only well-defined relative to a sampling rule and a decision problem, and the original problem launders that choice through the word credence.

Treat this as a substantive position about self-locating belief, not as a refusal to engage, and give me a ranked map of what would count against it. In particular, I want to know:

Which variants of the protocol — for example, a single run rather than many runs, tails producing a million wakings, a clock in the room, a bet offered once per waking rather than once per run, or an outside observer who samples a waking rather than a run — would either force the deflationist to take sides in the halfer–thirder dispute or reveal that there is a fact of the matter after all? Which formal considerations — Dutch-book constructions, dominance arguments over repeated experiments, branching or duplication cases, or Bayesian constraints on how self-locating evidence should shift credence — would individually pressure the claim that the protocol alone is underdetermined? What does the deflationary view predict about these variants that the standard halfer and thirder views do not, and where do those predictions become liabilities rather than advantages? Which of these defeaters should a committed deflationist themselves concede as genuine pressure, and which can they dismiss as merely re-importing an answer-specific framing? And is this a view that has been, or would easily become, insulated from falsification by its own flexibility — and if so, is that a problem for this kind of view in a way it would not be for an ordinary claim about a coin?

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and indicate which ones should actually move a deflationist.
