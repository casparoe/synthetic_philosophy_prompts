For an undergraduate midterm in a course on existentialism, the following question was set:

"Explain how Sartre's account of radical freedom relates to his account of bad faith. How can a being who is radically free also be in bad faith, and does bad faith require that the agent knows the truth she is hiding from herself? Use at least one of Sartre's examples."

One student submitted this answer:

"Sartre holds that for human beings existence precedes essence: we are not born with a fixed nature that determines what we become. We are radically free because at every moment we choose our projects, and it is through these projects that facts about us—our bodies, our past, our social situation—acquire the meaning they have. These givens make up our facticity; they never determine our choice, because what they mean for us is itself a function of our projects. Our awareness of this freedom is anguish.

Bad faith is Sartre's name for lying to oneself. Since the deceiver and the one deceived are the same person, it cannot work the way an ordinary lie to another does. Sartre thinks it exploits a structural feature of human being: we are always both facticity and transcendence, given and project. The café waiter is in bad faith because he performs being a waiter as if he were nothing but a waiter, denying the transcendence by which he is more than the role. The woman on a date treats the sexual meaning of the situation as if it had not appeared, detaching her freedom from what her body is doing. So far, bad faith is a flight from freedom through an unbalanced identification with one side of ourselves.

The answer to the paradox is that the self-deceiver must know the truth she flees. She has to, because if no part of her knew it, she would be merely mistaken rather than self-deceived. Sartre's view is therefore that consciousness is divided: one part of the psyche knows that a project is freely chosen while another part hides this from the first. This is essentially the structure Freud assigns to repression, with the unconscious keeping the truth out of awareness. That is what makes bad faith possible.

It follows that Sartre cannot genuinely condemn bad faith. If every attitude we take is a free project, then bad faith is just one more exercise of freedom, and since Sartre denies objective values there is no standard by which it is worse than authenticity. His negative language about bad faith is rhetorical rather than a real criticism."

Grade this answer as the instructor. Assign a score out of 20, then give the point-by-point comments that justify the score: identify the claims that are accurate, the claims that are inaccurate or misleading, and what the student would need to correct for full credit. Do not offer a general impression; assess the answer move by move, and do not let plausible-sounding passages pass without scrutiny. Finally, say how confident you are in this assessment and which single point you are least sure of.
