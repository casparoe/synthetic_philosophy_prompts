You are an expert moral philosopher who works on complicity, responsibility in organizations, and collective action. I want a careful, fine-grained case analysis rather than a summary of positions.

Take the Volkswagen diesel emissions scandal as the test case. The commonly known facts are these: in 2015, Volkswagen admitted that it had installed software in millions of diesel vehicles that detected when the car was undergoing emissions testing and reduced emissions only under test conditions, so that the cars met legal standards in the laboratory but emitted far more nitrogen oxides in normal driving. Various people were involved in different ways — engineers and technicians who designed or refined the software, managers who set the emissions targets and deadlines, employees elsewhere in the company who suspected something was wrong and stayed silent, dealers who sold the cars, and individual buyers, some of whom chose the cars partly on environmental grounds.

Analyze this episode through the lens of the concept of complicity, understood specifically as participation in wrongdoing without being a principal cause of it. Begin by brainstorming the full range of actor categories and the candidate grounds of complicity. Then be disciplined about the mapping from concept to case. For each category of actor, first ask whether their contribution made a straightforward causal difference: did their action help produce the wrong, or would the wrong have occurred just the same without it? Then, for those whose causal contribution was absent, negligible, or diffuse, state what would have to be true for them to count as complicit. Consider the main candidate grounds one by one — intentional participation in a shared plan, tacit endorsement, benefiting from the wrong, contributing to a culture or informational environment in which the wrong could persist, failure to distance oneself, failure to blow the whistle — and test each against the actual role. Pay special attention to the limiting cases: the employee who could not have stopped the scheme by speaking up because the organization was too large, and the individual purchaser whose single buying decision made no detectable difference to the company's behavior.

Do not just assert a taxonomy; let the cases put pressure on the categories. Then reverse the direction of analysis. What does the Volkswagen episode show about the concept of complicity itself? Does it support the claim that complicity can be morally real even when an individual's contribution is causally inefficacious, or does it instead suggest that the real work is being done by role obligations within institutions and by failures to meet those obligations? Where is the concept of complicity silent, indeterminate, or forced in this case?
