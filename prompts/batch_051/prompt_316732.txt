In my teaching and in my own reasoning I follow a policy I call the untrained-move rule. The policy is this: I do not permit an inferential move, heuristic, or pattern-recognition judgment to influence my settled view on a live question unless I have first trained that move on simplified, self-contained exercises with known answers, drilling until the move fires automatically and its measured error rate is stable. I keep an inventory of certified moves: a base-rate kata, a sunk-cost kata, a calibration kata, a pre-mortem kata, an inside-versus-outside-view kata, and so on. When a pattern fires in a genuine case, I ask which item of the inventory it instantiates. If I cannot identify the kata behind it, I set the impulse aside as noise, however much it feels like insight. If I can identify the kata, I admit the conclusion only with the discount that the drill's measured error rate requires. I adopted this policy after noticing that my untrained insights were mostly fluent confabulation, and that the drills improved my performance within the domains they covered. I now worry that I have built an epistemic virtue but also possibly a costly ritual: that I am mistaking fluency under training conditions for reliability in the wild, and mistaking a training effect for a reason for belief. Is a policy of admitting only drilled moves defensible? Under what conditions is it rational to withhold assent from an undrilled but carefully considered judgment, and under what conditions would a rational person trust that judgment over a drilled heuristic? Can the useful core of the policy be restated without treating one's own training log as the criterion of truth?
