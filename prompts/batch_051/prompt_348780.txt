I've been reading Cesare Beccaria's On Crimes and Punishments for a class, and the chapter on torture keeps nagging at me. The way I understand his argument, he says that how much pain someone can withstand mostly tells you about their constitution—how robust or delicate they are—not whether they're actually guilty. So an innocent but timid person might confess just to stop the pain, while a hardened guilty person resists, which means torture is a test of strength, not truth. Fine.

But yesterday a friend sent me a short article that summarizes Beccaria, and I'm not sure it's fair. Here's what the article says: "Beccaria proved that torture is completely useless for getting information, because the guilty always resist and the innocent always break under pain, so it can't tell them apart. Since the only possible justification for torture would be reliable information, and Beccaria showed it gives none, his argument settles the ticking time bomb case once and for all. If you think torture is ever justified, you either haven't read Beccaria or you're ignoring the fact that he refuted the information argument."

I'm inclined to push back on that, because the ticking time bomb isn't about getting a confession out of the suspect; it's about the fact that he knows where the bomb is, and even unreliable methods have some probability of producing the location. But I also don't want to be one of those people who thinks Beccaria is only talking about his own century. How much of this summary is actually right? Go through it and tell me what Beccaria argues, what the article gets subtly wrong, and whether his argument lands differently against the ticking time bomb than against ordinary torture used to extract confessions.
