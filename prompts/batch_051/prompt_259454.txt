I chair the Campus Food Policy Committee at a mid-sized research university. We are revising the default dinner menu in the residential dining halls, and the university's Sustainable Procurement Policy contains the following clause:

"The University shall, in all food procurement and dining service decisions, select the option reasonably expected to minimize harm to sentient animals, taking into account the number of animals affected and the intensity and duration of any suffering caused."

Three options are before us. First, we could retain the current default, a beef entree sourced from feedlot-finished cattle. Second, we could switch the default to a chicken entree from conventionally raised broiler chickens. Third, we could switch the default to a plant-based patty made primarily from field-grown chickpeas, lentils, and rice, with no animal-derived ingredients.

The clause was adopted when the policy's drafters had only farmed mammals and poultry in mind; its drafting history contains no discussion of wild animals or invertebrates.

The committee's staff report has made the decision harder than expected. Because one beef carcass yields many more servings than one broiler carcass, the chicken default would kill on the order of two hundred times as many individual animals over an academic year as the beef default would. At the same time, the staff's aggregate welfare scoring, which gives each animal a duration-weighted score for the intensity of its suffering, ranks the chicken default as causing less total expected suffering than the beef default: the cattle spend many months with chronic lameness, heat stress, and liver abscesses before slaughter, while the broilers live only about six weeks under conditions the scoring treats as poor but comparatively brief. The plant-based default causes no direct farmed-animal deaths, but it would enlarge the row-crop acreage in the university's supply chain, and the staff estimates that this would add substantial harvest-related mortality among wild rodents and ground-nesting birds, together with a far larger but unquantified number of insect deaths that the scoring excludes because insect sentience is disputed.

I need to prepare the committee's interpretive opinion before next week's meeting. Under the clause quoted above, which of the three options does the policy require, which does it permit, and which does it rule out? Please work through four approaches: the plain words of the clause; the purpose of the clause; what its drafters would most likely have said if asked about wild animals and invertebrates; and what a reasonable reader on campus would now understand the clause to require. Then say which approach should control here and why, and give a bottom-line recommendation for the default dinner menu. Please draft this as the short opinion I will circulate before the meeting.
