A friend in my metaphysics reading group has put together an arguement about origin essentialism that's been doing my head in all week. Each step looks right on its own, but the conclusion seems mad, and he refuses to treat it as a reductio. Could you tell me which step to give up, or whether I should just accept the whole chain?

(1) Necessity of origin: if someone in another possible world grew from a different zygote than the one you actually grew from, then that person isn't you. So you couldn't have come from a different zygote.

(2) Which zygote gets started is massively path-dependent. Conception is a race between millions of sperm, and tiny differences — half an hour's timing, the position of your parents' bodies, the exact conditions in the reproductive tract — would very likely have let a different sperm win. A different sperm means a different zygote.

(3) So there's no possible world where you exist and your parents conceived you at a slightly different time. In any world where they did, a numerically different child exists instead.

(4) But now run the same style of reasoning on the sperm itself. The particular sperm that made you was produced by a particular meiosis in your father's body, and that process is just as path-dependent: had your father's temperature, hormonal state, or movements in the weeks before your conception been minutely different, a different gamete would have been produced (or none). So, by parity with (1)–(3), the particular sperm that made you couldn't have existed under those slightly different conditions.

(5) If you couldn't have existed without that zygote, and the zygote couldn't have existed without that sperm, and that sperm couldn't have existed without that exact history, then you couldn't have existed without that exact history either. Essentiality of origin seems to transmit along the causal chain.

(6) And there's nothing special about the sperm: chain the same move through your father's own origin, or your mother's cycle, or whichever thread you pull on, and it looks like your existence requires a huge swathe of the actual causal history to be exactly as it was. Had your dad had a different breakfast on one particular morning a couple of months before your conception, you might never have existed.

(7) That's where I part company with him. It seems to prove too much: it turns nearly all of the deep past into part of your individual essence, and it makes a mundane counterfactual like "if Gran hadn't missed that train, Mum wouldn't have met Dad" secretly a claim about a numerically different grandchild. I'd definately rather keep (1) than accept (7), but I can't tell whether (4) or (5) is the weak link or what it would cost to reject them. Where does this go wrong, if it does?
