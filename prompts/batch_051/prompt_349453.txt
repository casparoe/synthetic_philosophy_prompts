I've been trying to settle when two rational people can agree to disagree, and I have a pile of gut verdicts I can't unify. Two forecasters share the same prior and each privately sees a different honestly-sampled signal about whether a coin is biased; if they just take turns announcing their updated probabilities, I think they're forced to converge, no way around it. If they start from different priors because one trusts a certain polling firm and the other doesn't, then after seeing the same public polling releases they can drift closer and still disagree forever, and I don't think either one is being irrational. If they share a prior but one of them barely moves on the other's announcements because he's sure the other guy is bluffing or reasoning sloppily, disagreement can survive, but that feels like common knowledge of rationality has failed, so it's not a counterexample. The case that keeps me up: same prior, both honest and Bayesian, but each has his own private black-box way of turning raw evidence into a probability — different partitions, if you want to be formal about it — and neither knows exactly how the other's model weights evidence. They announce only their bottom-line probabilities. I want to say they still have to converge eventually, but I also feel like this is exactly where the theorem's conditions quietly stop applying. Is there a single principle, something like Aumann's agreement theorem with the actual conditions made explicit, that fits all my verdicts? And if not, which verdict do I have to drop, and what does the principle you'd defend say about the black-box case?
