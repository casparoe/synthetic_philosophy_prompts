I am a high school student taking an elective philosophy course, and recently the class read a chapter on mystical traditions — the kind that describe God or ultimate reality only by saying what it is not, on the grounds that anything you could actually state about it would miss it. A friend of mine left class convinced that the entire position is self-defeating, and the argument he laid out has been bothering me since. He was careful enough to number the steps:

(1) To know something is to know at least one truth about it; if there is nothing you can correctly say about a thing, you do not know that thing, you merely have a feeling. (2) A truth is, by its nature, the kind of thing that can be said: a proposition, a content that could in principle be expressed. (3) Therefore, if a thing can be known, at least one proposition about it is true and expressible. (4) But if a thing is ineffable, then by definition no proposition about it can be stated without falsifying it. (5) Therefore, nothing ineffable can be known. (6) The trouble is that this conclusion is not confined to mysticism: people routinely say that the character of an experience — what it is like to see red, or to feel pain — cannot be put into words at all, and yet you plainly know what those experiences are like. (7) So the argument seems to prove too much, which suggests something is wrong with it, but neither of us can locate the exact place where it goes wrong.

I would very much like to hold on to the idea that the mystic and the ordinary person staring at the color red both possess some kind of genuine knowledge, and I would also prefer not to pay for that by draining the word ineffable of all meaning. Which step in the argument should be rejected, why did that step look so secure, and what exactly is the price of rejecting it?
