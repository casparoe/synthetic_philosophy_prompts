Something that's been nagging me since I started reading about probability. The standard Dutch book story is roughly this: if a person's degrees of belief are incoherent, then there's a set of bets, each of which they'd accept on its own, that together guarantee they lose money no matter how things turn out; and the fact that such a book exists is meant to show they're irrational, not merely that they've been unlucky. So far so good. But try turning it into an actual scene. Suppose you meet someone like that -- a man at the bookies whose been losing all afternoon and has stopped keeping track of his own odds -- and you quietly sell him the whole set of bets and pocket the certain profit. Most of us would say the man has been swindled and you are the one in the wrong, not him. So the very transaction used to prove the buyer is irrational is one we'd morally condemn someone for actually carrying out. Is there a principle that lets us keep both judgments, a way of separating the Dutch book as a proof from the Dutch book as a practice? Or does the argument only work by presupposing exactly the kind of predatory dealer we otherwise think shouldn't exist? I want the strongest account of the difference, and an honest verdict on whether the two reactions really can live together.
