Two people I know have been arguing for months about the Sleeping Beauty problem — one is a committed thirder, the other a committed halfer — and they have agreed, partly out of exhaustion, to write an adversarial-collaboration document both can sign. The document should open with a numbered list of claims both camps accept about the protocol and about the uncontroversial parts of the problem: for example, Beauty's credence before she goes to sleep, and what the two sides are even disagreeing about when one says the answer is one half and the other one third. But the agreed list should not stop at platitudes; it should push up to the strongest substantive claims that survive the disagreement, stated in wording neither side would wince at signing. Then a disagreements table should set out the precise formulations of where they part ways, including the contested question of what Beauty should say if she is told that today is Monday before giving her answer, and the contested question of whether her waking state itself constitutes new evidence. Each position should be phrased so that its own side would accept it as a fair statement of their view, while the other side would accept it as a fair characterization of the view they reject.

After that comes the crux section. I suspect the real pressure points are these: (i) whether the fact that Beauty is woken twice under tails changes the probability of a single toss of a fair coin, or only her share of awakenings; (ii) whether the dispute is fundamentally about the coin, about self-locating belief, or about fair betting odds; and (iii) whether a repeated version of the experiment in which Beauty bets on every waking is a legitimate test of the original single-shot question. For each crux, record the concrete case, variant, or argument both sides would accept as bearing on it — such as a variant with only one waking under tails, a version in which Beauty is told the day before answering, or a betting scheme run over many iterations — and have each side commit in advance to what it predicts that case will show, along with how much each side would update if the prediction failed. Close by identifying any part of the dispute that both sides would, on reflection, accept is merely verbal. Format the whole thing as a numbered agreed-claims section, followed by a disagreements table, followed by the crux record with each side's predictions written down.
