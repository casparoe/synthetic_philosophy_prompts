I teach a second-year undergrad political philosophy seminar, about 20 students, one 80-minute session. I want an activity that gets them to feel the tension in the "does the market corrupt or civilize?" debate, not just argue about it. The reading for the week is Adam Smith, and I want them to see something specific: the same Adam Smith who says the butcher and baker give us dinner out of self-interest also says, later in the book, that a person whose whole working life is one tiny repetitive operation loses the habit of exercising his mind and becomes, on Smith's own description, as stupid and ignorant as a person can become. I want the activity to make them live that tension themselves.

Here's my idea so far, but I'd like you to develop into a runnable plan with rules, materials, a timeline, and a debrief. I'm imagining a two-phase game. In phase one, students are randomly assigned secret valuations for a set of tokens, and they trade anonymously through written slips to maximize their own point totals. Some students are secretly brokers who can see the distribution of valuations and can use that information to make better trades. In phase two, the same students sit in a circle, all valuations become public, and they have to collectively allocate the same tokens by deliberation. The brokers from phase one are revealed but must now justify whatever they gained. The idea is that the exact same people, with the exact same underlying preferences, might act or feel quite differently depending on whether they are in a market frame or a common frame. But I'm not sure how to structure the scoring so that the contrast becomes visible and uncomfortable, and I don't want the brokers to feel personally attacked in the debrief.

Please give me concrete rules, a payoff scheme that doesn't need real money (maybe points that convert to a small grade bonus, or just bragging rights), a precise timeline for 80 minutes, and a debrief that maps what happened to the Smith tension and to the broader question about whether markets corrupt or civilize character. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and be honest about what the activity can't show—for example, it probably can't show long-term character effects, only situational ones. And I'd like the debrief to end by naming the idea explicitly so students leave with the concept clearly labeled.
