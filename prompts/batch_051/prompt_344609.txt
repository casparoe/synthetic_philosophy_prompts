Had a long argument with my friend last night about whether it even makes sense to ask for "the" cause of a big historical event. He kept saying that once you've got more than one necessary condition you've got more than one cause, full stop, and singling one out is just storytelling or politics. I agree with him in the abstract but my gut doesn't cooperate, and now I'm trying to figure out what I actually think. Here are my raw reactions to some cases; I'd like to know if there's a principle behind them, and if not, which ones I should give up.

1. A train derails. The rails were badly weakened by an overnight storm and the engineer was going eight miles over the speed limit. I'd say the speeding caused the derailment, and I'd also say the storm caused it, but if someone asked "what caused it" I'd say the speeding. The storm feels like it's in second place and I don't know why.

2. The outbreak of WWI. I'm fine saying "the assassination of Franz Ferdinand caused the war" when I'm narrating the summer of 1914. I'm also fine saying "the alliance system caused the war." But I wince at "the existence of rifles caused the war," even tho without rifles it wouldn't have went the way it did. So not every but-for condition is equal.

3. The collapse of the Roman Empire. If someone claims lead pipes caused it, I think that's basically a parlor trick, or at least comically incomplete. But I don't feel the same about "slavery caused the American Civil War," even though I know plenty of other things were causally in play — Lincoln's election, secession, the constitutional structure. Why does one big event get to have a cause and the other doesn't?

4. A bridge collapses under a heavy truck. The official inquiry says the cause was a cracked, rusted girder that had been failing for years; a newspaper says the cause was the truck being ten tons over the limit. I sided with the inquiry: the girder was the cause and the truck was just the occasion. But I can't honestly say why, since without the truck the bridge would've lasted at least until the next inspection.

5. The 2008 financial crisis. "Low interest rates caused the crisis" feels too pat to me, but "the housing bubble caused the crisis" feels fine, and "subprime lending caused the crisis" feels even better. Something about proximity to the mechanism, or specificity, seems to matter.

6. The French Revolution. I'll happily say "the fiscal crisis triggered the revolution" but I'd never say it caused it. Except in the derailment case I was fine calling the speeding the cause even though it was also kind of a trigger on top of the storm. So maybe I'm just being sloppy with "trigger."

The case I keep going back and forth on: a patient with severe emphysema dies of a heart attack three days after surgery. The surgeon says the operation caused the death; the internist says the emphysema caused it. I want to say both are right and their disagreement isn't just verbal, because each answer points to a different thing you'd do differently next time.

Is there one principle that gets all of these, or close? Please state it precisely (or give me a small set of candidates), say which of my verdicts it can't accommodate and which ones I'd have to drop, and tell me what it says about the patient case and maybe one further case I haven't considered. Also: can my friend's rule be patched with some extra clause, or is it just wrong?
