I've been roped into leading a discussion section for an undergraduate epistemology course this term — I'm a science journalist by trade, and honestly the deparment needed a warm body, but I did work on philosophy of perception back in grad school, so it's only mild impostor syndrome. This week was Sellars and the myth of the given. The short-answer prompt the students got read:

"Explain what Sellars thinks the 'myth of the given' is. Be specific about the exact mistake he attributes to the foundationalist, and then say whether Sellars's argument, if successful, shows that sensory experience cannot justify empirical beliefs."

One student submitted this:

"Sellars's target is the foundationalist picture on which empirical knowledge ultimately rests on a layer of the immediately given: raw feels, sense-data, or bare 'this is simply how things appear to me' items that are supposed to justify everything else without themselves needing justification. The myth, he argues, is the idea that any state could play that double role. Conceived as a non-conceptual, non-epistemic item, a bare sensation belongs to the causal order — it's something that happens to us — and causes don't justify. But if a state does justify, it's already conceptual and answerable to norms, in which case it isn't the unproblematic given either. That's why Sellars holds that observation reports like 'this is red' aren't reports of an unmediated given: applying the concept red already places one in the space of reasons. His treatment of 'looks' talk makes the same point from the other direction — 'looks red' isn't more basic than 'is red'; it's more sophisticated, because one can say how a thing appears while withholding endorsement of how it is. So the conclusion he draws is that nothing non-conceptual survives this argument. Sense experience collapses into purely causal input, and perceptual experience by itself never justifies a belief: perception can cause beliefs, but it can't be a reason for one."

Could you grade this? I want an actual letter grade, and then a point-by-point breakdown — what the answer gets right, what it gets wrong or overstates, and the comments you'd write to justify the grade.
