I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am currently reporting a piece about how controversies over marginal scientific claims are adjudicated when decisive data do not yet exist. In one interview, a researcher who defends a widely criticized hypothesis about the causes of a poorly understood medical condition objected when I noted that the published literature contains no positive support for his view. "Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence," he said, and then elaborated that the existing studies were too small and too poorly designed to have detected the effect even if it were real, so their null results should not count against the hypothesis and the rational posture is agnosticism rather than rejection. I have heard the line used this way before — as a deflection of a demand for positive evidence — and I would like to handle it with precision before I quote or challenge it.

Could you separate the distinct claims that the sentence might be used to express, and assess each? I am especially interested in how the reading changes depending on whether the absence arises because nobody has looked, because people have looked with weak instruments, or because people have looked carefully and found nothing; whether a null result can ever constitute evidence of absence and under what conditions; and whether the slogan is best understood as a point about logic, about what one may rationally believe, or about where the burden of proof should sit. For each reading, I would like to know whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or an overstatement, and which reading the researcher's argument actually needed in order to succeed. Please write the answer in American English.
