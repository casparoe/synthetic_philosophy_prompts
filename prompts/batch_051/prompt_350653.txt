A friend and I spent an evening arguing about multipolar traps — situations in which each player's individually rational move pushes everyone toward an outcome all of them would pay to avoid — and we ended up inventing a mechanism that neither of us could fully talk through. I would like it analyzed rather than praised.

Imagine an industry in which all firms currently maintain a costly standard: say, a safety practice that protects their workforce, or a restraint that preserves a common resource. Any single firm could quietly drop the standard, cut costs, and gain market share; once one does, the rest must follow or shrink, and in the end the entire industry is worse off. To stop this, the firms form an association with one rule. Before any member may reduce the standard, it must announce the smallest payment it would accept to refrain — its stated price for not defecting. The other members then submit sealed bids toward buying it off. If the bids sum to at least the stated price, the defection is cancelled and the collected sum is paid to the would-be defector, who remains bound by the standard. If the bids fall short, no one pays, and the defection goes ahead.

How would this mechanism actually behave? Work through the incentives for the would-be defector — how it sets its price, and whether the availability of a buyout begins to manufacture threats — and for the bidding members, including how much each chooses to contribute rather than free-ride on the others. Then follow it over repeated rounds. Identify the main failure modes, including at least strategic hold-ups, free-riding among bidders, collusion, information problems about gains, and defections by firms outside the association. Which of those failure modes, if any, can be fixed by amending the rule, and which are symptoms of something the mechanism cannot solve?
