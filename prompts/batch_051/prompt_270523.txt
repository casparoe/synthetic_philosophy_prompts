I’m a features journalist covering science and ideas, and I’m working on a piece about a small town in the north of England that’s debating whether to demolish a Victorian sluice gate. Nobody seems to know what it was built for. Some local records suggest it might have been for flood control, but the river has been rechannelled since then. The council’s engineers say it now does nothing except collect debris and cost money to maintain. A local preservationist has been arguing that we should not remove something unless we know why it was put there in the first place; she also keeps bringing up Edmund Burke’s Reflections on the Revolution in France, where he warns against treating a nation as a blank sheet of paper and describes society as a partnership among the living, the dead, and the unborn. A young tech entrepreneur on the council says this is just status quo bias dressed up as wisdom and wants to trial removing the gate for a year.

I definitly don’t want the piece to be a he-said-she-said rundown. Could you help me think through the philosophical structure here? Is there a principled way to distinguish legitimate caution about inherited institutions from mere status quo bias? What would Burke’s actual argument imply about a case where nobody knows the original purpose and the maintenance costs are real? And when, if ever, would a temporary removal count as responsible reform rather than the kind of vandalism the preservationist worries about?
