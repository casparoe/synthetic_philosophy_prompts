A friend has walked me into an argument that seems to generalize the statue-and-lump puzzle into a proof that ordinary counting is massively wrong, and I cannot find the faulty step.

(1) A sculptor forms a statue from a lump of clay; once the statue exists, the statue and the lump occupy exactly the same region and share all their parts.
(2) The lump can survive being squashed into a ball; the statue cannot.
(3) If two things differ in any property, they are not identical.
(4) Therefore the statue and the lump are two distinct material objects that coincide.
(5) The same style of reasoning applies to any pair of sortals with different persistence conditions: the piece of clay, the portion of clay, the artwork, the thing-that-cannot-survive-squashing, and indefinitely many others each yield a distinct coincident object.
(6) So wherever common sense says there is one object, there are indefinitely many, and ordinary counting of objects is massively false.
(7) Ordinary counting of objects is not massively false.

Each step looked plausible to me, but (4), (5), and (7) cannot all be true. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? If it is valid, tell me which premise to reject and what rejecting it costs. I would least like to give up (7); I would rather deny that a mere difference in sortal profile buys a new object, but I do not see how to do that without making what there is depend on our concepts. Name the step and price the alternatives.
