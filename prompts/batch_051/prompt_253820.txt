I am currently taking a philosophy module on collective intentionality, and after a recent seminar two people on my course got into an argument that none of the rest of us could settle. We had been discussing a simple case: two people carrying a heavy table out of a room together. The tutor asked whether the togetherness of what they are doing amounts to anything over and above the sum of what each individually intends and does. Afterwards, one coursemate — who was defending something close to Bratman's planning theory — and another — who was defending something close to Gilbert's account of joint commitment — went back and forth as follows.

(1) Maya said that once you specify that each agent intends to do their own part, intends the joint activity of carrying the table, expects the other to play their part, and that all of this is common knowledge between them, you have said everything there is to say; 'collective intention' is just a convenient label for this mesh of individual attitudes, with no further ingredient. (2) Tom replied that this omits the normativity of the case. If one of the two simply sets down their end and walks off midway, the other is entitled to object in a distinctive way — not merely 'I am surprised' or 'you have let me down', but 'you cannot unilaterally end what we are doing.' That standing to rebuke is evidence of a joint commitment: something the parties have created together that neither can revoke on their own. A mesh of individual intentions plus common knowledge carries no such obligations. (3) Maya answered that the obligation can be explained without any joint commitment. If I knowingly let you rely on my contribution, I am answerable for disappointing a reliance I invited; that is a matter of ordinary moral principles governing trust, underwritten by how individual intentions interact, and it needs no 'plural subject' or collective mind. (4) Tom said the reliance story is still too weak. If I merely predict that you will lift your end, my walking away might be rude, but it is not the violation of a right. In genuinely joint action each party does not just predict the other's contribution; each accepts it as part of what we are doing. Moreover, if we tried to analyse this as a pair of conditional intentions — each willing to carry only if the other carries — the conditions would never discharge and nobody would lift. What is needed is a single 'we' content, an intention to carry the table together, in which each shares. (5) Maya countered that this 'we-intention' is either redundant or mysterious. If it is an attitude located in each individual's psychology, then the reductive account can absorb it: on Bratman's picture each already intends the joint activity, not merely her own bit. If it is meant to belong to some collective subject over and above the individuals, that is a dubious entity being asked to do explanatory work no one has shown it can perform. So talk of a shared 'we-intention' either collapses into reduction or becomes spooky.

I could not tell whether this was a genuine theoretical disagreement or whether they were talking past one another, and I would like a sharper verdict than my seminar group produced. Could you go through the exchange move by move and adjudicate it? For each move, say which parts are sound and which are overstated or mistaken, and indicate how damaging the reply from the other side is; then say who has the better overall position, and whether there is anything important that both sides are missing.
