My theology reading group is wrapping up a term on the problem of evil, and the last session is on the version I find way more stubborn than the usual human free will one: how anyone could sin in a state of original, created goodness. Not Adam and Eve, but the angels. The setup says they were made good, with clear knowledge and no bodily appetites or bad upbringing, and yet one of them still fell. All the standard moves—pride, the in via/in patria distinction, privation—feel like theyre just restating the problem instead of solving it, because a good will making itself the center is exactly the thing that needs explaining. I want to end the session with a Petrarchan sonnet in the voice of such an angel, and I want the form to carry the argument. The octave should lay out the case for why sin is impossible in that state. The volta shouldnt be a logical conclusion but the act of falling itself, mid-thought, as if the turn in line 9 is the defection. And the sestet should refuse to tidy it up into something that makes sense. It should be a good poem first, but someone who knows the theology should recognize the problem is being rendered accurately, not just vaguely gestured at.
