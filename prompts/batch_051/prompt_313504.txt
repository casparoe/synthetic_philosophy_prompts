An algorithmic-fairness working group has spent a year with two factions talking past each other about what the impossibility results in fair classification actually show. The incompatibility camp takes those results — for instance, that outside degenerate cases a calibrated risk score cannot simultaneously satisfy balance for the positive class and balance for the negative class, and that with unequal base rates calibration and equalized error rates pull against each other — to show that cross-group fairness is not one coherent target but a bundle of criteria that no fixed decision rule can meet at once. On their view, any algorithm presented as fair across groups is systematically misleading, and much of the field's work amounts to documenting this incoherence. The tradeoff camp takes the same results to show that fairness theory has matured: we now know with precision which properties can and cannot be jointly realized, so decision-makers should stop invoking a single definition and instead set explicit, domain-specific priorities, treating each deployment as a choice among criteria rather than as compliance with a standard.

You are the professional drafter hired to produce the joint statement this adversarial collaboration would issue. The document you write must be one both camps could put their names to. Structure it as follows:

1. A short preamble stating what the collaboration is and what each camp grants about the other.
2. A numbered list of claims both camps accept. These should be substantive rather than vacuous: they should include the settled mathematics, the claim that the choice among criteria is value-laden rather than merely technical, and the claim that the disagreement is not about whether the tradeoffs exist.
3. A table of residual disagreements. For each row give: a neutral formulation of the exact question; the incompatibility camp's own wording of its view; the tradeoff camp's own wording of its view; and a judgment about whether the disagreement is substantive or partly verbal.
4. A list of cruxes: sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one camp. For each crux, specify a concrete case, dataset feature, or piece of evidence both camps agree would bear on it, and record in advance what each camp predicts that evidence will show. Draw the cases from at least two settings — for example, pretrial risk assessment and credit or lending decisions — and make the predictions specific enough to be right or wrong.
5. A closing note stating what would change each camp's mind, and which of their positions no evidence of the kind described could settle.

Commit to the definite formulations, choices, and predictions you judge the two camps would actually sign. Do not hedge by offering menus of alternative wordings or leaving the cruxes disjunctive; where the record would leave a gap, fill it with the strongest version consistent with each camp's stated view and say briefly why that version is the right one.
