Consider the following argument, offered as a reconstruction of the reasoning behind Cox's theorem, for the conclusion that the probability calculus is the logic that any rational reasoning under uncertainty must obey.

Let pl(A | E) denote a rational agent's plausibility of proposition A given evidence E.

(1) Real-valued comparability. Plausibilities are real numbers, and any two are comparable: for all A, B, E, exactly one of pl(A | E) < pl(B | E), pl(A | E) = pl(B | E), or pl(A | E) > pl(B | E) holds.

(2) Conjunction locality. The plausibility of a conjunction is fixed by the plausibilities of its parts taken in conditional order. There is a single function F such that, for all A, B, E,
pl(A ∧ B | E) = F(pl(A | E), pl(B | A ∧ E)).
Nothing about what A and B are about can make a difference beyond these two numbers.

(3) Negation locality. The plausibility of a negation is fixed by the plausibility of the negated proposition. There is a single function G such that, for all A, E,
pl(¬A | E) = G(pl(A | E)).

(4) From associativity to the product rule. Since (A ∧ B) ∧ C is logically the same proposition as A ∧ (B ∧ C), their plausibilities must coincide. Substituting (2) into this identity gives, for x = pl(A | E), y = pl(B | A ∧ E), and z = pl(C | A ∧ B ∧ E),
F(F(x, y), z) = F(x, F(y, z)).
Under the standard regularity assumptions of the derivation — that F is continuous, strictly increasing in each argument, and twice differentiable — every solution of this associativity equation is ordinary multiplication after a monotone rescaling of the plausibility scale. So the scale may be chosen so that
pl(A ∧ B | E) = pl(A | B ∧ E) · pl(B | E).

(5) From negation locality to the complement rule. With (3), the logical facts that ¬¬A is equivalent to A and that A ∧ ¬A is contradictory while A ∨ ¬A is tautologous, and the product rule from (4), the remaining freedom in the scale is fixed so that
pl(¬A | E) = 1 − pl(A | E).

(6) Recovery of the probability axioms. The identities in (4) and (5) are the multiplication and complement rules for conditional probability. Therefore any plausibility calculus satisfying (1)–(3) and the regularity assumptions in (4) is, up to a choice of scale, a probability assignment.

(7) Probability as the logic of plausible reasoning. So any rational agent must, on pain of incoherence, assign degrees of belief that obey the probability calculus. Probability is not one instrument of uncertain reasoning among others; it is the unique consistent logic of plausible reasoning.

Question: Is the argument valid? If it is not valid, identify the first step that fails, distinguish the reading of that step on which it is true from the reading on which it is false, and state what remains of the conclusion once the step is corrected. If it is valid, identify the first place at which the argument assumes something that minimal rationality does not itself require, state the weakest form of that assumption, and explain what the argument proves once that assumption is restricted.
