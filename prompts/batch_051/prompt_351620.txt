Suppose someone runs the classic dollar auction in an economics lab, but with one rule changed. Quick reminder of the setup: an auctioneer sells a real one-dollar bill. The highest bidder pays their bid and gets the dollar. The second-highest bidder also pays their most recent bid and gets nothing. Bids rise in 5-cent increments, no jumping, and once you've made a bid you can't just walk away. The usual story is that once two people have bid past roughly 50 cents, each keeps raising so as not to be the one stuck in second place paying for nothing, and the total can climb well past a dollar.

The twist: after each new top bid, the person now in second place gets ten seconds to either raise or take an exit option. If they exit, they pay x percent of their most recent bid to the auctioneer instead of the full amount, the auction ends right there, and the top bidder pays their bid and collects the dollar. If they raise instead, the roles swap and the other person gets the same exit option. The auctioneer keeps all exit fees.

Walk me through how this plays out as x goes from 0 up to 100. I want the reasoning to be explicit about incentives at each stage: when does the exit option kill the escalation trap, when does it just shift the numbers, and is there a range where it actually makes outcomes worse? Which of your predictions are robust, and which turn on assumptions about risk aversion, loss aversion, or spite? What would have to be true about the bidders for your main claim to be wrong? And if I had to pick one value of x that most reliably keeps total bids from passing a dollar while still making the auctioneer some money, what would you pick and why?
