As a novelist, I have been trying to diagnose a specific failure in my current draft, and the shape of the problem keeps reminding me of something I half-remember from moral philosophy. The novel is narrated in the third person by a voice that is broadly omniscient and openly judgmental. In one chapter, this narrator condemns a minor character's vanity at some length, and the condemnation is, textually, accurate: the character really is vain. Yet my writing group reports that the judgment does not take hold. The reason, they say, is that the prose itself is preening. It calls attention to its own cleverness, flatters its own cadences, and commits, at the level of style, precisely the fault it accuses the character of. The interesting thing is that nobody thinks the narrator has gotten the character wrong. The defect is not factual unreliability; it is something more like a failure of authority.

What this resembles, to me, is the idea in the ethics of blame that a person can utter a true accusation and still lack the standing to make it. A hypocrite who condemns someone else's infidelity may say something entirely accurate, but the act of blame is nonetheless compromised by the blamer's own conduct. If I lay out the mapping as I see it, it runs as follows.

The blamer corresponds to the narrator, and the blamed agent to the character under judgment. The truth of the blame corresponds to the textual accuracy of the narratorial assessment: the character really does have the flaw. Hypocrisy or complicity, which defeats standing on the ethical side, corresponds to the narrator's stylistic or structural complicity in the very flaw being condemned. And the distinction between a defect in the content of a charge and a defect in the position from which the charge is made corresponds to the intuition that the novel's problem is not unreliability in the usual sense; the narrator knows the facts and applies the right predicate, yet the evaluation still misfires.

But I can see how this might be only pattern-matching. In the philosophical case, standing is usually understood as a relation between agents: the wrongdoer can answer the hypocrite by pointing out that the hypocrite does the same thing, and this matters because there is a demand for reciprocity or equal treatment between persons. In the literary case there is no dialogue in any literal sense. The character never answers, and the sense that the judgment has failed arises in the reader, not in a second party to the exchange. That disanalogy might be shallow, though, since the same split arguably reappears inside the philosophy, where some accounts place standing in the conversational relation between blamer and blamed and others locate it in the blame itself.

So my question is whether this parallel is deep or superficial, and I would like a philosopher to take it seriously enough to tell me exactly where the structures match and where they come apart. If the parallel is deep, can anything transfer across it? Does narrative theory gain something by borrowing the idea that a narrator's assessment can be evaluated along two independent dimensions, one epistemic and one standing-based, with unreliability tracking the first and hypocrisy tracking the second? And in the other direction, does the novel suggest that standing need not be second-personal at all, since the reader's uneasy response requires no actual answer from the character? I can imagine that carrying that idea back into ethics would be a category error rather than a discovery, but I cannot articulate, from inside the analogy, what would make it one. Where exactly does the mapping break, and which side should admit that its phenomenon is the less fundamental?
