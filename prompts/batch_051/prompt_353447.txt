Put yourself in the role of a professional decision theorist who works on expected utility and the value of information. I'm trying to collect the various ways I. J. Good's theorem—that a rational agent with fixed acts, states, and utilities should never turn down costless information—can fail or appear to fail. Quick version of the theorem, just to have it on the table: for any fixed decision problem, the optimal way to use a free signal can always mimic the act you would have picked without the signal, so the signal can't lower your ex ante expected utility.

I already have five families of exceptions or apparent exceptions on my list:

1. Strategic settings: if the fact that you aquired the information (or will acquire it) is known to other agents, their behavior can change to your detriment. Information can have negative value in games and bargaining even when it would be fine against Nature.

2. Belief-dependent and emotional utility: information can cause dread, anxiety, or loss of hope, and if the utility function was specified over "external" outcomes only, the theorem looks violated. This one is usually a modeling failure rather than a real counterexample.

3. Preference change: information can alter what you fundamentally want, not just what you believe, so the fixed-utility premise silently stops holding.

4. Bounded rationality: the theorem assumes you'll choose the optimal act after each possible signal. If the information overloads you, or you can't compute the posterior optimum, that's no longer true.

5. Act-set changes: some information doesn't change your beliefs about the world so much as it changes which acts are available or permissable—it can create a legal duty, destroy plausible deniability, or turn a tolerable act into a culpable one, so the fixed-set-of-acts premise fails.

I have the feeling I'm missing a sixth family that isn't a special case or combination of those five. Think carefully: brainstorm four or five candidates, discard any that are really variants of the ones I listed, and then present the strongest genuinely distinct one. Tell me what the family is, how it differs from the five above, and then give me your honest verdict on whether it is an actual failure of the theorem or just another case where a premise quietly gives out.
