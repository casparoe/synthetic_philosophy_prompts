I am a novelist planning a historical novel set in early eighteenth-century England, narrated by a maidservant in a great house. I chose her deliberately, wanting a life of no public consequence; I did not want another novel of statesmen, battles, and the marriage market of the landed classes. To make her world credible, I have been reading the kind of scholarship that reconstructs ordinary lives from inventories, court depositions, account books, wills, and parish records. The reading has taught me a great deal, but it has also confronted me with a tangle I do not have a word for.

Two examples may show what I mean. A townswoman becomes visible to me because a dispute over her late husband's estate left depositions describing the contents of her rooms, how she ran her household, and what the neighbours thought of her; yet the very dispute that preserves her also marks her household as unusually fractious, and I cannot know how far her household resembled the quiet ones that left no such papers. A rural miller speaks in a trial transcript only because an ecclesiastical court examined him for unorthodox opinions; a miller whose beliefs and habits were wholly ordinary would never have been interrogated, so the record that gives me his voice is itself evidence of his exceptionality. The problem is not merely the familiar one that poverty and illiteracy leave few documents. It is the sharper point that the documents which do survive tend to be produced by a rupture — a suit, a crime, a scandal, an unusual testament — with the result that the more thickly documented an ordinary life is, the more likely it is to be extraordinary in just the ways that make me unsure whether the person stands for anyone beyond herself.

I can imagine a resolution: perhaps an exceptional case can nonetheless reveal the norms and limits of its world. But a path out is not a name for the trap, and I want the trap labelled before it is escaped. What I am asking, then, is whether this configuration has an established name in historical scholarship — this inverse relation between the granularity of a single ordinary person's traces and that person's usefulness as a representative case. If it is discussed under more than one name, I would like the names kept distinct: what each term covers, how each differs from the phenomenon I have described, and which fits best. If nothing established fits exactly, I would welcome a coined term, defined carefully and with its boundary cases marked — for example, does the tangle disappear when the person is exceptional only in having been documented, rather than in being powerful, criminal, or remarkable? I want the carving, not merely a label.

Please give your answer in prose first, and after the prose append a structured summary as a JSON object with exactly these fields:

- "nearest_concepts": an array of objects, each with "name" (the term), "covers" (a short statement of what the concept denotes), "differs_from_my_case" (a short statement of how the concept departs from the phenomenon I described), and "fit" (one of "exact", "partial", or "adjacent").
- "exact_match_exists": true or false.
- "best_fit": the name of the nearest concept, or null.
- "coined_term": a proposed term if no established one fits exactly, otherwise null.
- "definition": a one-sentence definition of the coined term, or of the best-fitting established concept if no coinage is needed.
- "boundary_cases": a short note on where the phenomenon begins and ends, including the case of a person who is exceptional only in having been documented.
