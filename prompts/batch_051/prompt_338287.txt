I'm giving a talk next month to a room of union organisers, labor journalists, and a few policy people who work on tech and employment. My thesis is that the famous stretch in Marx's Grundrisse, the Fragment on Machines, where he says that under large-scale industry the worker steps aside from the immediate production process and becomes its supervisor and regulator, and that real wealth depends less on direct labour time than on the general state of science and technology, is almost always misread today: its read either as a prediction of mass unemployment or as a liberation story where automation frees everyone, and both readings miss that Marx's point is about the contradiction between a system still organised around abstract labour time and a productive apparatus that makes living labour marginal. I want to defend using that passage to diagnose current automation discourse, not to predict anything, and especially not to say automation is inherently good or bad. I'll have maybe fifteen minutes of Q&A. The audience is not academic; they're sympathetic-ish but suspicious of theory, and a couple of the union people have already told me they're tired of leftists talking about robots as if that's the real enemy instead of the boss. Draft me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections I'll actually get from this crowd, in roughly the order they'll come and phrased the way they'd put them, with what I should concede, what I should hold, and the weakest part of my reading I need to defend rather than hope they miss.
