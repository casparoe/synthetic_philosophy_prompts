In the volunteer community where I help moderate a technical Q&A site, we are redesigning our karma-based reputation system, and one faction on the design committee keeps settling disputes with the same analogy: a karma score is a currency. It is earned through contributions the way money is earned through work; it can be debased if the platform issues upvotes too freely, much as a government debases money by printing it; early users hoard it the way early investors accumulate capital; and a high score gets "spent" whenever its holder throws their weight behind a moderation decision. The practical conclusion this faction draws is that we should import the standard economic toolbox wholesale: constrain the supply of karma to control inflation, correct hoarding through redistribution or a carrying charge on dormant balances, and let trusted users stake or spend their reputation on decisions, since that is what you do with a currency.

I am not convinced the analogy is doing the work it appears to do, and I want an audit of it before we build policy on it. Start with the full correspondence table: what maps onto the mint, the money supply, the transaction ledger, inflation, savings, spending, counterfeiting, and the exchange rate. Then identify the points where the mapping breaks, and for each disanalogy say whether it matters for the specific conclusion being defended or is merely a harmless mismatch. I already suspect several fault lines: money is fungible and transferable, while karma is issued for, and attached to, a particular history of actions by a particular person; a monetary expansion has roughly uniform effects, while upvote inflation may selectively degrade the signal carried by the scores of more and less reliable contributors; and money's value is mostly a matter of what others will accept in exchange, while karma's value may be nearly exhausted by what it lets the platform and other users know about someone. But I do not know which of these, if any, are decisive. Finally, say whether the conclusion—constrain the supply and allow staking or spending—survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and propose a better guiding analogy if the currency one misleads. Aim for something around 900 words that I can circulate to the rest of the committee.

One ground rule for the piece: as you lay out the correspondences and disanalogies, separate what is genuinely settled in the study of reputation systems from what is actively contested among people who work on them, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
