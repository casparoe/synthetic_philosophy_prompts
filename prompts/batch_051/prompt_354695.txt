One of my friends insists that a state and a business corporation are fundamentally different kinds of thing: the state is a public, territorial institution claiming the exclusive right of coercion, while a corporation is a private, profit-seeking association that exists only because some state's law creates it. Reading about early modern chartered companies makes that distinction look less like a discovery and more like a retrospective tidying-up, and I am trying to figure out how much of it survives scrutiny. Consider the English East India Company in the later eighteenth century. It was chartered as a joint-stock trading company, yet it collected land revenue in Bengal, maintained a large standing army, ran civil and criminal courts for the people under its rule, issued coin, and concluded treaties with Indian rulers. Some of these powers it held by grant from the Mughal emperor, some under its English charter, and some it simply came to exercise.

The working definition of statehood I rely on requires a permanent population, a defined territory, a government exercising effective control, and the capacity to enter into relations with other states. Work through that definition criterion by criterion and say honestly where the Company at its height qualifies and where it does not. Then take the further step: was it a state, a private appendage of a state, a hybrid form that later legal systems deliberately eliminated, or something else? Finally, say whether this borderline case shows that the state/corporation distinction is doing real conceptual work, or whether it is mainly a juridical line drawn by a particular history.
