I'm a literary translator, and I'm starting to worry about a clause in my professional association's code of conduct. The exact wording is:

"A member shall produce a faithful and complete rendering of the source text, preserving its content, meaning, tone and effect, and shall neither add to nor omit from nor otherwise alter any element of the original."

I'm currently tranlsating a Brazilian short story that is built entirely around the word "saudade." The author is dead, so I can't ask. The story doesn't just use the word; the narrator keeps offering paraphrases and then showing how each one adds or loses something, and at one point a character says flat out that the word can't be translated. As the story presents it, the word covers something like a longing for an absent person, place, or time, touched with warmth and with the sense that the absence can't be repaired — but the story treats any such list as itself a kind of mistranslation.

My problem isn't really which English word or phrase to pick; it's that the clause seems to forbid every option. If I render "saudade" as "aching, tender nostalgia for what is gone," haven't I added shades the Portuguese doesn't commit to? If I keep the word untranslated and let context teach the reader, haven't I omitted an element the story is built around? And if I add a translator's note or footnote, is that an "alteration"?

The drafters were plainly thinking of mundane cases — skipped paragraphs, inserted opinions, style touched up. What I want to know is what the clause can reasonably be taken to demand in a case like mine. Could you work through what the plain words say, what the no-add/no-omit rule is for, and what the drafters probably would of said if this exact story had been put to them? And is there a reading of "faithful and complete rendering" that's coherent here, or does the clause quietly assume that an equivalent is always there to be found?
