I've been interviewing a legal scholar for a magazine piece on desuetude — the idea that a statute can lapse through long, open non-enforcement, and what that means for the rule of law. She gives crisp verdicts but never offers the theory behind them. Her verdicts so far:

1. A nineteenth-century fortune-telling statute, unenforced in living memory, is invoked to deny a trading license to a palm reader after a single complaint. She says the denial is improper.
2. A Sunday-trading law, unenforced for forty years and openly violated throughout the city, is suddenly enforced with fines against every shop. She calls the enforcement improper.
3. A reporting rule enacted two years ago went unenforced for its first eighteen months while the agency drafted compliance guidance; it was then applied uniformly. She says enforcement is proper.
4. A 1930s permit requirement for street performances, unenforced for decades, is used to arrest a busker after a noise complaint. She says the arrest is improper.

The case I need to predict: a 1950s statute requires street vendors to display a permit, but the city never built the permitting system, so for thirty years no one could have complied and no one was cited. The city has now created a simple permit process, publicized it for six months, and says it will enforce the display requirement uniformly against all vendors. Would she say enforcement is proper or improper?

Please lead with your bottom line — your prediction and whichever principle you think controls, in the first sentence — and then give the reasoning. I'm less interested in the bare prediction than in the candidate principles that fit her earlier judgments, where those principles leave the new case open, and what additional case would most sharply force the choice among them. If I've set this up badly — if her earlier verdicts can't be reconciled the way I've implied, or desuetude is several different doctrines rather than one — tell me plainly instead of forcing a prediction.
