I'm a novelist, and I've been rereading One Hundred Years of Solitude because my next book has a cyclical structure -- not in a cute way, I hope -- and I want to keep it honest. Everyone says García Márquez's novel is cyclical, but when I actually track it, the repetitions (the names, the incest, the civil wars, Colonel Aureliano Buendía making and then melting his little gold fishes) look like one family stuck in a psychological rut, while the larger arc is suspiciously linear: the railroad and the banana company arrive, progress happens, the massacre gets officially forgotten, and then the book doesn't take another turn -- it ends, with Aureliano Babilonia deciphering Melquíades' manuscript and Macondo wiped out the instant he finishes. Thats an ending, an apocalypse, a straight line into a terminus rather than a return.

I want a reading of the novel against the philosophy of cyclical versus linear history, not just the mood of it. Use Polybius's cycle of constitutions, Ibn Khaldun's asabiyyah building a dynasty and then decaying across generations, Spengler's cultures as organisms that grow and wither, and Toynbee's civilizations that rise by answering challenges and fall when they stop answering. Don't give me four summaries; I already know that much. The question is whetever the novel repeats in anything like the way those thinkers meant. Does it provide their kind of mechanism -- solidarity thinning over generations, regimes degenerating into their corruptions, a culture exhausting its inner form, a civilization going rigid -- or does its recurrence run on different fuel: solitude, incest, willed forgetting, prophecy? And is the final deciphering a denial of cyclical history, a terminus, or a disguised affirmation that the whole thing was fixed from the start and closed like a circle?

Take a definite side: is this novel genuinely cyclical, genuinely linear and eschatological, or some third thing, and which of the four theorists does it most resemble and which does it most resist. If the novel gives the philosophy of history something the papers and treatises miss, say what that is. I'd rather you commit to one reading and defend it than lay out the options and leave me to pick.
