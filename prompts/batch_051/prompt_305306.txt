I have been reading about sortition and lottocracy, and I find the positive case more compelling than I expected. On the theory as I understand it, a legislature selected by random sample—perhaps stratified to match the population on a few dimensions—would be descriptively representative in a way elected bodies almost never are; it would break the link between holding office and the traits that make someone good at winning campaigns rather than good at legislating; and it would remove the standing temptation to serve donors, party machines, and one's own re-election. The principle behind it has a clean statistical rationale: a large random sample inherits the composition of the population, without any further act of selection to justify. On this view, elections are not the natural default; they are a filter with a systematic bias, and we have mistaken familiarity for legitimacy.

And yet I have an intuition I cannot make go away: I do not want a stranger chosen by lot—with no track record, no demonstrated competence, and no personal mandate—writing tax law or deciding defence procurement. Nor do I want a system in which no individual can be removed by the governed for a bad decision. Confronted with a choice between a competent elected official whose judgment I distrust and a random draw whose judgment I have no reason to trust in the first place, I would, without much hesitation, choose the elected official. So my reflective endorsements pull one way and my political instincts pull the other.

I am not asking for a verdict on whether lottocracy is right. I want to know what should yield. Should a lottocrat try to debunk the intuition—for instance, as an artefact of having lived our whole lives under elections, or as status-quo bias, or as a misplaced demand that a citizen-legislature behave like expert administration? Or should the intuition force amendments, such as competence screening or certification, a mixed chamber with an elected and an allotted house, direct recall, or restricting allotted bodies to agenda-setting rather than final votes? Or is there a distinction—for example, between expert administration and lay legislation—that lets both the theory and the intuition survive? Please give the strongest version of each side. For each of the three moves—debunking the intuition, amending or abandoning the theory, or finding a reading on which both survive—tell me what would justify it and what it would cost, and then say which move you would make and why. Please write your answer in American English.
