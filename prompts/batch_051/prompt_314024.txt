I am recently retired from a career in physical cosmology, and I have only lately begun to read moral philosophy seriously. My habit from the sciences is to insist that, before one chooses among modifications of a theory, one should state what the modified theory must be able to do: which phenomena it must recover, which distinctions it may not erase, which results it may not contradict. I would like your help constructing that specification for ethics in infinite worlds.

The text that set me on this is Nick Bostrom, "Infinite Ethics" (2011). The passage I keep returning to is this:

Suppose the world contains an infinite number of people and a corresponding infinity of joys and sorrows, preference satisfactions and frustrations, instances of virtue and depravation, and other such local phenomena at least some of which have positive or negative value. More precisely, suppose that there is some finite value ε such that there exists an infinite number of local phenomena (this could be a subset of e.g. persons, experiences, characters, virtuous acts, lives, relationships, civilizations, or ecosystems) each of which has a value ≥ ε and also an infinite number of local phenomena each of which has a value ≤ (‒ ε). Call such a world canonically infinite. Ethical theories that hold that value is aggregative imply that a canonically infinite world contains an infinite quantity of positive value and an infinite quantity of negative value. This gives rise to a peculiar predicament. We can do only a finite amount of good or bad. Yet in cardinal arithmetic, adding or subtracting a finite quantity does not change an infinite quantity. Every possible act of ours therefore has the same net effect on the total amount of good and bad in a canonically infinite world: none whatsoever.

Aggregative consequentialist theories are threatened by infinitarian paralysis: they seem to imply that if the world is canonically infinite then it is always ethically indifferent what we do. In particular, they would imply that it is ethically indifferent whether we cause another holocaust or prevent one from occurring.

I have noticed that much of the subsequent discussion treats this as a problem for utilitarianism in particular and offers local repairs: discounting over distance, value densities, hyperreal or surreal valuations, restricting the domain to agents, and so on. I do not want to evaluate those repairs one by one until I know what the repair is supposed to achieve. So my request is the specification, not the scorecard.

Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of what we ought to do must meet if it is to cope with a canonically infinite world — or, more conservatively, with the live possibility that the actual world is canonically infinite. For each item, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a mere preference, and say whether you judge it non-negotiable or a commitment of one particular camp. I would particularly like you to:

(1) identify the distinctions such a theory cannot afford to collapse — for example, the distinction between an act's being swamped in the global aggregate and its being morally indifferent, the distinction between local and global betterness, and the distinction between a theory's declaring two options tied and its failing to return a verdict at all;

(2) say which results from mathematics and cosmology it must remain consistent with, and where those results themselves force a choice of formalism — cardinality, limits, non-Archimedean values, partial versus total orderings, and the like;

(3) flag any subset of the desiderata that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, since a list that is itself impossible is the most useful kind of result rather than a defect in the list;

(4) say which single requirement does the most work to exclude the stock fixes people reach for, even if you only gesture at how the list applies to them.

Throughout, hold yourself to the standard I would have imposed on a collaborator: tell me how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point you are least sure of. If any of the stock fixes I mentioned rests on assumptions I may not share, say so rather than quietly importing them.
