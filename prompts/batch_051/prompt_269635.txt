Im in year 12 doing maths and philosophy, and I've been going down a rabbit hole about Thomson's lamp, and I noticed something that might be a daft parallel with ethics. I want to know whether it's actually deep or I'm just pattern-matching.

The lamp: off at t=0, on after 1/2 a minute, off after another 1/4, on after another 1/8, and so on, all before t=1. The sequence of states is off, on, off, on..., which has no limit. So at t=1 the setup just doesn't determine whether the lamp is on or off; you cant fix it by saying "take the limit" because there isnt one. Some people say the lamp is impossible for that reason, others just say the state is undefined.

Now the ethics side. Imagine someone trying to decide whether to do some action by going through reasons one at a time from an potentially endless list, and each reason is binary: it flips their provisional verdict from do-it to dont-do-it and back. If there were infinitely many reasons and they could get through them in finite time (a supertask of deliberation), then at the finish their sequence of verdicts would be off, on, off, on... in exactly the same shape. Nothing settles down, so no final all-things-considered verdict is determined. The mathematical obstacle, a non-convergent alternating sequence, seems to carry over word-for-word.

What I want to know is whether the matching shape is doing any real work, or whether it's just a colourful way of saying "if reasons keep overturning each other forever you never reach a verdict". Does anything about non-convergent sequences actually transfer to weighing reasons, or does it break because real reasons have strengths and can be outweighed rather than just voting binary yes/no? Another thought: in maths some infinite sums change value depending on the order you add the terms in. If the analogy were deep, it might predict that the order in which someone considers reasons matters once there are infinitely many, and I cant tell whether that's a feature or a sign the whole thing is forced. And does the fact that nobody can really perform the supertask make the analogy empty, or is it still useful as a constraint on idealised theories of deliberation (e.g. a theory that permits infinitely many reasons owes you an account of how they are summed, just as the lamp needs an extra stipulation about the state at t=1)?

Be honest about where you're least sure here. In particular tell me what would change your mind: what kind of argument or example would make you flip your verdict on whether this parallel is deep or superficial. And if I'm pattern-matching, I'd expect there to be at least one thing the analogy predicts that comes out false on the ethics side, so say what that is.
