I keep going back and forth on Quinn's self-torturer and I want someone to force the issue rather than just pick a side.

The setup, roughly: a device has a long series of settings from no pain to excruciating, arranged so each step up adds a discomfort too small to reliably notice or care about, and each step pays a real chunk of money. The catch is that each step is reversible, but the person keeps advancing because, compared to the previous setting, the next setting always looks like a good trade. At the end they're in a state they would have refused outright at the start, and they'd pay to undo it. So the pairwise preferences along the series look intransitive: step 1 is preferred to step 0, step 2 to step 1, and so on, but the last step is obviously dispreferred to step 0.

Here's the theory I keep finding myself defending. The local comparisons are the ones doing the real work, and there's nothing wrong with them. Any cutoff between "discomfort worth taking for this money" and "discomfort not worth taking" is going to be arbitrary: a rounding error away from a nearly indentical cutoff that draws the line one step earlier or later. So the rational move is to accept that preference over a continuum with no sharp joints just isn't transitive, and that demanding transitivity is a fake consistency constraint. The view has real virtues: it's honest about vagueness, it refuses to pretend a just-noticeable-difference threshold is a boundary in the world, and it gives a tidy account of the money-pump argument—yes, an agent with intransitive preferences can be pumped, but the pump is just revealing path-dependent values, not a bug.

But the intuition I can't kill is that the self-torturer is being taken, and that the global verdict is the authoritative one. Offered the whole package up front—all the money for all the pain—I'd refuse, and the fact that the stepwise procedure predictably delivers me somewhere my own all-things-considered judgment rejects feels like a failure of the procedure, not a feature of my values. I don't want to live in a world where "rational agents just can be money-pumped" is a conclusion; that feels like the central case of a broken local comparator. The problem is I can't point to the step where he should stop, and I don't want to posit a sharp pain-money threshold I don't believe in.

So: which yields, and what would each concession cost? Give me the strongest case for debunking or demoting the global intuition and keeping the intransitive-preference view. Give me the strongest case for amending the theory so it rules out the sequence without needing a precise cutoff. Then tell me whether there's a reading on which both survive—say, local versus global senses of "rational," or representing the agent's preferences as incomplete or interval-valued—and how I'd know that's solving the puzzle rather than relabeling it.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble, no restating the question.
