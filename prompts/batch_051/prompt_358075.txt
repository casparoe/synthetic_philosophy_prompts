I am a philosophy professor serving on the listing committee of a state conservation commission; I am the only non-biologist on the committee besides one lawyer. Roughly a dozen times a year we must recommend, within sixty days, whether a disputed population should be treated as a distinct species for legal protection, and the full commission—most of whose members are not scientists—then votes on our recommendation. The biologists on the committee routinely disagree about which species concept should govern, especially with isolated populations, cryptic species, and fertile hybrids. I would like a decision procedure a lay commissioner could actually run: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a "no," and where it hands the matter back to the commission as a question of value rather than science. Then work the procedure through three cases: a ring species whose terminal populations do not interbreed; a cryptic species pair distinguishable only by DNA; and a fertile hybrid population of uncertain origin. Please flag where a species concept is doing normative work. Throughout, separate what is settled in the philosophy of biology from what remains genuinely contested among species concepts, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
