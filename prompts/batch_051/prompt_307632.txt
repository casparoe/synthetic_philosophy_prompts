I spent thirty-five years in a spectroscopy lab watching decay curves pile up signal, so the habit that stuck with me is asking whether a variable is doing real work or just standing in for something else. That habit has followed me into my late-life reading in moral philosophy, and there is one question I keep circling back to: does the bare passage of time, on its own, carry moral weight when we are deciding what may be done about a past wrong? I dont mean the practical mess of witnesses dying or evidence going stale; I mean whether the sheer fact that decades elapsed should, by itself, change the verdict. My current hunch is that it never should. Time, I think, only matters because it tracks something else — whether the wrongdoer has reformed, whether the harm has faded or persisted, whether the victim ever had a fair chance to complain, or whether punishing now would tear apart present-day attachments that didn't exist then. But my own verdicts below dont all cooperate with that hunch, and I want to know if there is one principle that gets all six of them, or whether I should overturn one of my own verdicts instead.

Start with an old embezzler. A man stole from his coworkers' pension fund at thirty, was never caught, and has lived a decent and generous life for the thirty-eight years since; the fraud surfaces now in a routine audit. I think prosecuting him now would be wrong, or at least far less defensible than it was three decades ago. Next, a man raped and murdered a young woman at twenty-four and evaded detection; a DNA database hit catches him at sixty-nine. I think he should be prosecuted no matter how old or settled he is; some wrongs should not decay. Third, a chemical company dumped industrial waste in 1978, and neighbors started getting cancers only in the last few years because of very long latency, with the causal evidence only recently becoming solid. I think the company should be fully on the hook; the clock cannot run out before the victim could even have known there was anything to complain about. Fourth, a woman posted a genuinely ugly racist rant on a small internet forum in 1996 at twenty, then spent the next decades becoming an anti-racist organizer who has repudiated all of it. Someone dredges the post up now. I think using it against her is wrong; the person it records no longer exists in any morally relevant sense. Fifth, and this is the one that bothers me: I bullied a classmate fairly badly when I was fifteen and never apologized. I recently learned he barely remembers the incidents. I still think I owe him an apology, and that neither his forgetting nor the decades have dissolved the debt. Neither reformation on my side nor continuing harm on his seems to be doing the work, yet the verdict feels firm. Sixth, a junior officer in a military dictatorship tortured prisoners in his twenties; he is now eighty, living quietly among his grandchildren, and public debate in his country is split between prosecuting the old and letting the old man die in peace. I think he should still face trial even if any sentence would be mostly symbolic, but I cannot tell whether the feature doing the work is the severity of the wrong, its kind, or the fact that the victims and a polity that never settled accounts are still around.

I'd like the answer in four named sections, in this order, with rough lengths. Candidate principles: about 300 words; state the two or three most plausible principles that could organize these six verdicts, one of them being my proxy-or-confound idea about time, and say in a sentence where they diverge. Fit and failures: the long section, maybe 800 words; walk through all six cases and say which verdicts each candidate gets right, which it has to strain for, and which it gets outright wrong, and be candid about the apology case and the old torturer. Extensions: about 350 words; take the candidate you think best and predict what it says about two cases I haven't settled — one where a museum is asked to return an object taken under colonial coercion in 1890, and one where a professional society decides today whether to strip honors from a famous researcher whose fraud was committed in 1975 and discovered only after his death. My bottom line: about 200 words; tell me which of my six verdicts I should give up or refine if no principle captures all of them, and state the surviving version of my hunch.
