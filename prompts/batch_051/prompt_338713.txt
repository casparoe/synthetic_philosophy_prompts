I am preparing a teaching exercise on the underdetermination of theory by evidence, and I need a worked example that goes beyond the usual curve-fitting gestures. Please construct a pair of theories—call them T1 and T2—about a small domain of your choosing; you may invent the domain, provided the theories are specified precisely enough that their observational content is fixed. A toy ecosystem, a miniature particle system, or a simple signal-detection setup would all be acceptable. The point of the exercise is to show students that underdetermination can arise even when both rivals look like serious science rather than skeptical or gerrymandered constructions, so a case in which one rival is made ad hoc or observationally inert fails the assignment.

The case must satisfy all of the following specifications. T1 and T2 must be logically incompatible in what they claim about unobservable entities or processes. Relative to a fixed, commonsense conception of what can be observed in the domain, they must be strongly empirically equivalent: every observational consequence of each must also be a consequence of the other, across all possible measurements an idealized inquirer could make, not merely across the data collected so far. Neither theory may be a notational variant of the other, and neither may be a shared core with one extra unobservable posit bolted on that does no observational work. Neither theory may be a skeptical scenario, such as a brain-in-a-vat or simulation hypothesis, and neither may achieve equivalence through gerrymandered predicates or ad hoc corrections whose only apparent purpose is to preserve agreement with observation. Each theory should also have at least one motivation independent of fitting the target data—for instance, unifying otherwise disjoint phenomena or falling out of a wider explanatory framework.

After constructing the case, show that it meets the specifications. State each theory's central unobservable claim and its observational consequences, and explain why the two sets of consequences coincide; the equivalence should be generated by the theories' internal workings rather than stipulated. Identify the specific feature of the case that makes the underdetermination ineliminable, and say what would happen if that feature were removed. Finally, discuss how a scientific realist—the position the case is designed to challenge—would attack the example, for instance by denying that the two theories are genuinely distinct rivals or by arguing that extra-empirical virtues such as simplicity settle the choice, and assess whether that reply succeeds; then contrast this with how an empiricist or instrumentalist would treat the choice between T1 and T2.
