For a term paper on the semantics of indicative conditionals, I have been defending a broadly suppositional view: the acceptability of a plain indicative conditional goes by the conditional probability of its consequent given its antecedent, so that in some sense the probability of If A, then C is P(C|A). I thought of this as modest and psychologically plausible until I worked through the triviality argument set out below, which seems to show it cannot be right. Now I suspect there is a trick, but every step looks unavoidable when I read it, and the friend I argued with says I am simply refusing to accept a proof.

Here is the argument as I understand it. Assume, as a working hypothesis, that indicative conditionals express ordinary propositions, so that a rational agent's credence P over a Boolean algebra of propositions assigns them probabilities in the usual way; and assume the algebra is rich enough that any proposition with intermediate probability has a nonempty proper sub-proposition with positive probability. Fix arbitrary A and C with P(A)>0, P(A∧C)>0, and P(A∧¬C)>0.

(1) Adams's Thesis (AT): P(A→C) = P(C|A), for every indicative conditional A→C.

(2) AT is proposed as a general constraint on rational credence, not a feature of one's current P alone; so it should be preserved under ordinary Bayesian learning. In particular, it should hold for the conditionalized functions P(·|C) and P(·|¬C).

(3) By the law of total probability, P(A→C) = P(A→C|C)·P(C) + P(A→C|¬C)·P(¬C).

(4) Applying AT to P(·|C): P(A→C|C) = P(C|A∧C) = 1.

(5) Applying AT to P(·|¬C): P(A→C|¬C) = P(C|A∧¬C) = 0.

(6) Therefore P(A→C) = P(C).

(7) Combining (6) with (1): P(C|A) = P(C).

(8) Since A and C were arbitrary, this says every non-vacuous pair of propositions is probabilistically independent. Now pick any A with 0<P(A)<1, and let C be a nonempty proper sub-proposition of A with positive probability. Then P(C|A) = P(C) implies P(A)=1, contradicting 0<P(A)<1. So P assigns probability 0 or 1 to every proposition.

But ordinary rational uncertainty is full of genuinely intermediate degrees of belief, so AT together with the stated assumptions appears to be false.

I would like you to tell me, precisely, whether this argument is valid as stated, and if not, which step fails and why. If it is valid, I want to know which premise a defender of the suppositional view is rationally required to give up. My own suspicion is that step (2) quietly assumes more than Adams's Thesis licenses, but whenever I try to say exactly why, my objection sounds ad hoc; perhaps the real fault lies earlier, in the treatment of conditionals as propositions.

In your answer, please keep two things apart. First, what is mathematically settled: the derivation of the conclusion from the premises, once the premises and the setup are granted. Second, what is genuinely contested among people who work on conditionals: which premise to abandon, how much of the suppositional picture survives, and whether the result refutes Adams's Thesis outright. Do not present one contested diagnosis as if it were the field's consensus.
