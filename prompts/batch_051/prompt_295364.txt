I am a software engineer, and much of my recent work has been on supply-chain security. Two packages can be functionally identical and pass every test, yet we accept one and reject the other purely because its provenance cannot be verified; trusting code is not just about what it does when invoked, but about who built it and who is accountable later when it fails. Reading Dennett, as I understand him, on counterfeit people, I was struck that he is making the same move about persons. His worry is not mainly that a deepfake or AI avatar will fail a behavioral test of personhood, but that it can pass short interactions while lacking the reputation, accountability, and vulnerability to praise and punishment that make a real person someone we can trust; letting counterfeits circulate would degrade the practice of taking someone to be a person the way counterfeit money degrades the practice of accepting money. My question is whether the parallel is deep or I am merely pattern-matching. In software, the shift from testing behavior to verifying provenance was forced by concrete failure modes such as rogue dependencies and supply-chain attacks. Does Dennett's argument have the same structure, and if so, what is the analogue of provenance for a person — biography, embodiment, liability, the capacity to suffer, something else? Or does the analogy mislead because code is an artifact with an author, whereas a person is not a signed artifact and personhood is ultimately about behavior and functional organization?
