I run a small analytics unit in an environmental public-health agency, and every few weeks we are asked to attach causal readings to directed acyclic graphs learned from observational data: a discovery algorithm such as PC, GES, or FCI, or a hand-fitted model, produces a graph, and then a program director has to decide whether an edge like "wetland restoration reduces disease incidence" or "lower stocking density reduces pathogen load" is solid enough to justify spending money or changing a regulation. I would like a reusable decision procedure for reading a candidate DAG before we present it, something an analyst can run in half a day without special training in causal philosophy. It should be built out of the debate over why minimal Bayes nets track causation at all — the Causal Markov Condition (each variable is independent of its non-descendants conditional on its direct causes), faithfulness (the only conditional independencies in the population are the ones the graph implies), and minimality (no edge can be removed while preserving the Causal Markov Condition) — including the worry that a graph can fit the data without representing the causal structure when parameters are fine-tuned, when variables are deterministic functions of one another, or when the system is under feedback control. But it must be written as an operational checklist rather than as theory.

The procedure should be an ordered checklist that handles at least these failure modes: (i) the causal sufficiency assumption fails because a plausible unmeasured common cause could explain the same dependence or independence pattern; (ii) selection bias or conditioning on a collider has manufactured an edge, deleted an edge, or oriented an edge the wrong way; (iii) unfaithfulness, including exact path cancellation, deterministic constraints, and near-faithfulness violations in which a weak effect cannot be distinguished from no effect; and (iv) relaxed minimality, where a sparser graph or a differently oriented graph fits the same independence claims once faithfulness is not assumed. For each item, say what observable signal in the data or the domain should trigger suspicion, what would count as sufficient reassurance, where the procedure should stop and refuse a causal reading, and where it should hand the decision to a person because the remaining question is domain judgment that no data analysis can settle. Then run the procedure on two worked cases. In the first, a learned DAG indicates that a habitat-restoration program lowers human disease incidence through a mediated path involving predator density, but restoration sites were not randomly assigned and an unmeasured socioeconomic variable is plausible. In the second, a dietary variable appears to cause a health outcome, but the estimated edge reverses sign when adjusting for a variable that is plausibly downstream of the outcome, so the orientation may be a collider artifact. Make sure at least one case ends with the procedure refusing a causal reading, even though the analyst who built the graph will dislike that outcome.

Please also say what the procedure cannot do — where it will systematically over-trust or under-trust a DAG — and where you are least confident in your operationalization. In particular, tell me which step of the checklist is most likely to mislead in practice, and what would change your mind about the procedure itself: for example, if you became convinced that faithfulness violations are not rare pathologies but systematic features of the biological systems we study, how would you restructure the checklist, and which check would you move to the top?
