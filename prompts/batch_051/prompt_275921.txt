Take the Cambridge Analytica scandal — the personality-quiz app that hoovered up data on the quiz-takers' friends, passed it to a political consultancy, and fed microtargeted ads during the 2016 campaigns — and analyze it through the lens of the idea that personal data is property: information about you belongs to you, and using it without your say-so is a kind of theft. I know the rough outline; what I want is the careful mapping. Who owns what at each step (the quiz answers, the friend-network data, the inferred profiles, the ad targeting), and what would the property view say should have happened to each. Then give me an honest account of where the property framing misdescribes the thing — nothing was exactly 'taken' because data gets copied, much of the harm came from aggregation and inference rather than any single stolen fact, and a lot of the damage landed on people who never took the quiz. Finish by saying what the episode does to the theory: is Cambridge Analytica a case for treating personal data as property, or a case that property is the wrong frame for it?
