I'm a novelist, and a year into my term on the city's public art committee I thought I understood what we fund: we administer a small grant program that, under the ordinance, supports the creation and public presentation of works of art. The phrase isn't defined anywhere else, and that never mattered because the applications were all murals, string quartets, public sculptures, the sort of thing nobody blinks at. This month we have a proposal that has the committee split right down the middle. A local artist is asking for enough money to rent a vacant storefront for six months and run what she calls a durational social-practice artwork. Her plan is to hire a rotating group of residents from two neighborhoods that have spent decades avoiding each other and pay them to sit in the shop, at a table with a coffee machine, and actually talk; passersby can come in, listen, or join. There's no object, no score, no opening night, no fixed audience, and no obvious point at which its considered finished. Half the committee says this is clearly art—that kind of thing has been standard in contemporary practice for decades—and the other half says it's community mediation with a grant writer attached, and if we fund it then the next pothole repair will show up called a sculpture.

I'm the one who has to circulate a short written opinion before Thursday's vote: a concrete recomendation—fund it, reject it, or fund with conditions—plus the reasoning, and it has to take seriously the strongest argument on the losing side rather than just rolling out a definition of art like a conclusion. As a novelist I know too much to pretend there's an obvious line between a made thing and a real thing, but I also can't write "art is undefinable, so flip a coin" in a memo the city council might read during budget season. Could you actually draft the memo for me? It should be something the committee can act on, not a seminar paper.
