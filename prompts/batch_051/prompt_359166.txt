I'm a backend engineer who mostly works on ML-adjacent stuff — recommendations, ranking, that kind of thing — so coworkers keep sending me alignment posts. One of them sent me a Substack post about inferring an optimizer's objective and search space from its behavior, and the post summed the idea up like this:

"If you watch an agent long enough, its choices reveal its objective. Whatever it reliably picks must score highest under its utility function, so with enough observations you can just read off the reward function from behavior. The search space is just the set of actions the system can physically take. Once the objective is pinned down, you can predict what it'll do in new situations; if it then does something bad, that shows the objective itself was bad. So alignment basically reduces to specifying the right reward function and collecting enough behavior data."

Something about this feels off to me, but I'm not a philosopher and I don't trust my intuitions here. I've heard people say preferences are only revealed under idealized conditions, that two different reward functions can produce the exact same policy, and that an agent can only optimize over options it actually represents internally, which is different then its physical action set. I don't know how much weight to put on any of those objection.
