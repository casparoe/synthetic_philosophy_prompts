I'm taking a course this term called Well-Being and the Value of Achievement, and last week's seminar got stuck on whether effort is valuable in itself or only because of the results it produces. The case someone raised was: suppose you could take a pill that made you an excellent pianist over night—same skill, same repertoire, same performances—you just skip the years of practice. Has anything of value been lost? A classmate shut the discussion down by saying "no pain, no gain", and a surprising number of people nodded like that was an argument. I've heard that phrase my whole life (gym teachers, self-help books, my grandfather), but I honestly couldn't say what exact claim it's making, and the seminar moved on before anyone seperated the readings.

So here's what I want you to do. Take "no pain, no gain" and lay out the distinct precise claims it could be expressing, from the obviously true to the fairly radical. For each reading, say whether it's trivial, substantive, or just false—and, crucially, which of these readings would actually do the work my classmate needed it to do, namely answering whether something is lost in the piano-pill case. Don't just pick one reading and run with it; I want the whole menu, because I suspect the people nodding in the room were nodding at different claims. Format the answer in five sections, in this order: "The readings" (a numbered list covering at least six readings, one or two sentences each, around 500 words; this should be the longest section), "Trivial vs. substantive" (sort each reading into one of those buckets with a sentence of justification, around 150 words), "Which readings are false" (for each reading that doesn't survive, give the counterexample that kills it, around 200 words), "What my classmate needed" (which reading or readings actually answer the piano-pill question and whether the slogan earns its keep there, around 200 words), and "Bottom line" (three or four sentences on what the slogan is good for and what it isn't, around 100 words).
