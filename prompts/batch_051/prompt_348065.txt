Suppose someone lives a life that is comfortable, pleasant, and secure, but radically monotonous: the same routines, places, foods, conversations, and tasks repeat for decades, with no surprises, no unfamiliar people, no new skills, no adventures, and no risks. Many people judge such a life to be deficient in a way that hedonism and preference-satisfaction theories of well-being seem unable to capture. I am collecting distinct explanations of what, exactly, such a life is missing. Set aside the purely instrumental answer that variety counteracts hedonic adaptation and thereby sustains pleasure; that answer concedes the hedonist's picture, and I am interested in explanations on which monotony is bad over and above its effect on felt pleasure or desire satisfaction.

The explanations I already have are these:

(1) Boredom as an irreducible bad. Tedium is a distinctive objectionable condition of consciousness, not merely the absence of pleasure or the presence of pain; a monotonous life is pervaded by it, so any theory that counts only pleasures and pains miscounts the life.

(2) The stimulation requirement. A flourishing life requires a threshold level of novel stimulation or arousal; a life that is comfortable but perpetually familiar falls below that threshold even when the subject has stopped feeling acute boredom.

(3) The growth requirement. A good life is a process of developing and exercising one's capacities, and development necessarily involves tasks that are new to the agent; a life of repetition is stuck, and therefore fails as a flourishing life even if it is pleasant.

(4) The narrative requirement. A human life is lived well only if it has a narrative form—episodes, turning points, quests, and a temporally organized shape—and a perfectly repetitive life lacks that form no matter how high its aggregate pleasure.

(5) The authorship requirement. Part of well-being is being the author of one's own life rather than the occupant of a comfortable routine; a monotonous life may never require any substantial choice or risk, and so it is missing self-direction.

I want one more explanation that is genuinely different from these five: not a rewording of one of them, not a special case of one of them, and not a combination of them. State the explanation clearly in a few sentences, then tell me honestly whether it is any good, and say which of (1) through (5) it most closely competes with or most deepens.
