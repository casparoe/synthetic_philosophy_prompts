I've been trying to get clear on statutes of limitations, and I keep running into the argument that they're epistemically justified because evidence decays over time—witnesses forget, people die, records go missing—so after some number of years the risk of convicting an innocent person gets too high and the trial just isn't reliable enough anymore. That argument assumes a world where memory fades and paper rots. But a lot of evidence now doesn't decay at all, or even improves: emails, cell-site records, DNA profiles, security footage, archived social media. The clearest case is a cold case where a DNA profile sat untestable in 1985 and becomes a definitive match decades later. So here's what I want to know. Does the epistemic argument for limiting prosecution survive cheap, durable evidence? If it doesn't, is the right repair just to say limitations should track actual evidence quality (so some crimes have shorter limits, others effectively none), or does that stop being a statute of limitations in the usual sense? Maybe the argument was never really about evidence and I'm reading it too literally—but then what premise is actually doing the work? I'm interested in what happens to the argument, not in any particular jurisdiction's policy.
