Thomas Hobbes argues in *Leviathan*, Chapter 16, that a commonwealth becomes one person when a multitude authorizes a single representative: the unity of the artificial person lies in the unity of the representer, not in the represented multitude. On this account, the sovereign is not a separate entity standing over against the people, but the people themselves made one by representation, so that the sovereign’s acts can be attributed to each subject because each has authorized them in advance.

I’m puzzled about whether this argument survives the later development of general incorporation and modern corporate personhood. The old charter companies—the East India Company, the Massachusetts Bay Company—were quasi-sovereigns: each was created by a specific grant of public authority and governed a political community or territory. A Delaware LLC or a modern Delaware corporation is not like that. It is formed under general law, treated as a separate legal person, and understood in much of the theory-of-the-firm literature as a nexus of contracts rather than as an artificial person constituted by a sovereign grant. Yet courts and statutes still attribute rights, duties, and intentions to the corporate person itself.

Please give a committed verdict, with reasons, on the following question: does Hobbes’s representer-unity argument still go through when artificial persons are ordinary private legal actors, no longer little sovereign polities? I want you to say which premise is actually under pressure—the need for one undivided representative, the identification of the represented with the representer, or the idea that a multitude can be one person at all. If the argument is dead, say clearly what should replace it. If a repaired version survives, state exactly what it still proves and what it no longer proves, rather than laying out interpretive options.
