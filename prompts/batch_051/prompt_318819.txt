I sit on my town's public safety board, and we routinely vote on proposals that impose small risks on people who never agreed to them — a changed traffic pattern, an event permit, a piece of equipment in a public place. We decide case by case, but I have been trying to reconstruct what rule, if any, we have actually been applying, because I want our next vote to be consistent with our own record.

Here are the recent decisions.

We approved replacing the floodlights around the town hall parking lot with brighter bulbs. The engineer told us the glare would create about a 1-in-120,000 chance per year of a fender-bender for drivers using the access road; those drivers are mostly coming to town hall and are the ones who get the safer, better-lit lot.

We approved a new surface for the public tennis courts. The material grips more strongly, which the parks director said would produce roughly one additional sprained ankle for every 80,000 playing hours, but it drains better and lets the same players use the courts earlier in the spring.

We rejected a bus company's request to make a left turn out of its depot onto a road used by children walking to the elementary school. The engineer estimated a 1-in-30,000 chance per year that a child would be struck and seriously injured, though probably not killed; the company would have saved money, and the children and their families do not use the service.

We rejected an application for a Fourth of July fireworks show launched from the middle school field. The risk to the audience was modest, but the proposed debris zone covered two blocks of houses, with a 1-in-40,000 chance per year of roof damage or a small fire, and many of the people in those houses would not have attended the show.

We approved a restriping plan on Main Street that introduced a new merge lane. The traffic study projected one additional sideswipe for every 150,000 entries into town, but the plan shortened the evening backup, and the same drivers both bear that risk and receive the shorter commute.

The proposal now before us is the library's request to install an electronic message sign on its front lawn, facing a two-lane road. The sign would mainly help library users and people looking for event notices. The traffic study says it would cause roughly one additional fender-bender at the neighboring intersection for every 450,000 trips past the sign, almost all during the evening commute, with no realistic chance of serious injury. Most of those passing drivers are not stopping at the library and get no benefit from the sign.

Before our vote, I would like a careful reading of our own record. What decision would be most consistent with our past rulings? Which of the earlier cases is the closest analog? Please identify the different principles that fit all five past decisions, say what each principle would make us do here, and then give me your best recommendation. If you think our past decisions genuinely leave the answer open, tell me which principle we would be adopting by approving and which we would be adopting by rejecting. And if there is an additional scenario whose outcome would settle which principle we actually hold, describe it.
