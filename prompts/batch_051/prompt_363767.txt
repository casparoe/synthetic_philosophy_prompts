I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm working on a longread about how world history gets organized. I keep tripping over the fact that the standard textbook table of contents is basically a single timeline that runs through Mesopotamia, Greece, Rome, and then western Europe, with other parts of the world showing up when they bump into that line. The period labels do a lot of the work: Ancient, Medieval, Early Modern, Modern. Those are plainly European categories, but the textbook applies them to the whole globe at once. I want to ask what philosophical commitments that practice presupposes. Does dividing all of human time into those shared periods commit you to the idea that time is one universal container, that all places are on the same conveyor belt, or that there's a single center to the story? Is there a way to keep one global chronology without smuggleing in a Eurocentric center? And what would a history that's genuinely told "from everywhere at once" have to change about the practice — the labels, the narrative spine, the very idea of progress? Give me the implicit theory, say where the practice is underdetermined rather than forced, and be blunt about what would have to go.
