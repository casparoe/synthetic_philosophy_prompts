I've been reading the Hart–Devlin debate for a legal philosophy seminar and keep coming back to a short passage in Devlin's 1959 Maccabaean lecture, later published as The Enforcement of Morals, where he writes that the criminal law must be justified on the basis of what he calls "the reasonable man" or "the man in the jury box," and that a society may use the law to preserve its morality just as it may use it to safeguard its existence. What's striking is that this passage sits alongside another where he says the law's claims can always be put to a rational test: whether the conduct in question is one that society, speaking through the ordinary person, regards with intolerance, indignation, and disgust. I want help working out exactly what Devlin's position amounts to, and I don't trust how the secondary literature flattens it. Don't adjudicate between Hart and Devlin or give me a reading list. Instead, take the slogan "a society may use the law to preserve its morality as it uses it to safeguard its existence" and treat it the way you'd treat a proverb that gets repeated too easily. What are the distinct precise claims it could be making? I can think of at least four: a conservative-disintegration claim (there is a shared moral code and deviations weaken it), a functional-necessity claim (morality is part of what makes a legal system possible), a public-morals claim (the law may criminalize only what the ordinary person feels as disgust or intolerance), and a majoritarian-harm claim (the mere fact that the majority feels outraged is a moral reason to prohibit). But there are probably more. For each, say whether it's plausible and whether it actually does the work Devlin needed it to do against Hart's harm principle. And warn me which readings are so weak they'd get me laughed at in seminar if I attributed them to Devlin. Oh, and if any of this is just a verbal dispute, tell me which part.
