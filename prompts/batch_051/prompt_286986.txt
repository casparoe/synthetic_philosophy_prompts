I've been trying to use a line from Goodhart's *Problems of Monetary Management: The UK Experience* (1975) as a working definition. He writes:

"Any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes."

So my criteria for a Goodhart-style collapse are roughly: (a) there's a prior statistical regularity in some system, (b) someone starts using that regularity for control, and (c) the regularity degrades because the system adapts to that use, not just because the data got noisier. But I'm not sure where the edges are.

Here's the case. A hospitalist group I know through my health-policy research noticed a pretty stable negative correlation between a physician's average daily number of patient-portal messages and their 30-day readmission rate: docs with higher message loads had fewer readmissions. The group doesn't formal tie compensation or promotion to portal-message counts, but they start posting a weekly ranking of message counts, and the chair pulls high-message docs aside in mentoring meetings to "reflect" on their inbox practices. Within about 18 months the readmission gap between high- and low-message docs shrinks almost to zero. But the mechanism is partly that doctors now write shorter, colder portal replies, partly that they offload more replies onto nurses, and partly that they simply stop logging some quick phone follow-ups.

Is this still a Goodhart case on my criteria? Go criterion by criterion. If it fails one, say whether that's because my criteria are too strict or because the phenomenon is really something else. I'm especially interested in whether "pressure for control purposes" has to mean a formal, quantified, compensation-linked target, or whether informal monitoring and social comparison can create enough pressure. Does this case show that my criterion (b) needs to be revised?
