I am a retired physicist—most of my career was spent on statistical mechanics and, later, on the interpretation of quantum probabilities—and I have only recently begun reading the philosophy of probability systematically. What I miss from my working life is the discipline of forcing an uncertain judgment into a number with an explicit decomposition, so that the weak points are visible. I would like you to do that for me here.

Let C be the following philosophical claim. The Principal Principle, with its usual admissibility clause, is the correct and complete bridge norm between objective chance and rational credence: a rational agent whose total relevant information includes only that the objective chance of A at t is x, together with an admissible history up to t, should set her credence in A to x. Once admissibility is correctly understood, the only departures from this equality that are ever needed concern plainly inadmissible information. In particular, the kinds of cases sometimes used to motivate replacing the principle with a structurally different norm—histories that also bear on what the chances themselves were or would become, and information that functions as a crystal ball about the outcome—are handled by the admissibility clause and do not force a revised principle.

Please give me two things. First, your credence in C as a number between 0 and 1. Second, the decomposition, with at least the following components separated, each assigned a probability and justified in one or two sentences:

(a) Objective chances exist in a sense robust enough for the principle to engage with, rather than chance-talk being reducible to actual frequencies or to credences themselves.

(b) Conditional on objective chances existing, learning a chance places a direct rational constraint on credence, rather than functioning merely as one further piece of ordinary evidence.

(c) The admissibility clause can be made precise and non-circular, and it does the work of excluding the undermining and crystal-ball cases without smuggling in the conclusion.

(d) The resulting norm is best understood as a truth about ideal rationality, not merely as a useful rule of thumb for bounded agents.

Then show explicitly how you combined these components into your final number, rather than gesturing at the combination. Finally, tell me which component your final credence is most sensitive to, and what argument or empirical finding would move that component most. My own credence has been hovering around 0.4, so if your number differs materially from mine, I would also like to know which component most plausibly explains the gap.
