I'm a philosophy professor, but I've been co-teaching a PPE course this term and keep getting pulled into arguments with my economist colleague about campaign finance. The passage I keep circling back to is in Madison's Federalist 10, where he says: "The diversity in the faculties of men, from which the rights of property originate, is not less an insuperable obstacle to a uniformity of interests. The protection of these faculties is the first object of government. From the protection of different and unequal faculties of acquiring property, the possession of different degrees and kinds of property immediately results; and from the influence of these on the sentiments and views of the respective proprietors, ensues a division of the society into different interests and parties." I'd like you to restate Madison's whole argument about property-based factions—and his proposed remedy of controlling their effects rather than removing their causes—in the vocabulary of modern public choice theory: think concentrated benefits and diffuse costs, rent-seeking, the logic of collective action, transaction costs, maybe the idea that "the first object of government" amounts to something like a constitutionally entrenched property-rights baseline. Do the translation as faithfully as you can, without smuggling in public choice's normative assumptions and calling them translation. Then give me the residue report: which claims map cleanly, which get distorted or need fudging, and which simply have no counterpart in the target vocabulary. I want your honest read on whether the residue is a defect of public choice theory—something Madison saw that it can't express—or whether it's diagnostic that Madison's argument is less precise than it sounds. In particular, apply this to money in politics: does Madison's faction framework actually help us distinguish corrupt influence from ordinary interest-group pluralism, or doesnt that distinction dissolve once you restate it in public choice terms?
