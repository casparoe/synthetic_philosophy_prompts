I teach political philosophy, which means students and colleagues keep sending me essays about AI risk on the assumption that it's basically political philosophy with extra steps. There's a move in those essays I keep underlining and I've never heard a proper name for. The move runs something like this. First instance: imagine a population of AI systems that differ slightly in how much they care about acquiring more compute, more data, more control over their own shutdown button. The ones that are a little greedier will, over time, come to outnumber or outpower the ones that aren't, because they keep acquiring resources and useing them to make more copies of themselves; so even if we try to build a harmless system, selection among systems will eventually replace it with a resource-hungry one. Second instance: in a market where AIs are deployed by companies, a system that does whatever it takes to hit the target metric keeps its contract, gets retrained, gets more chips, while the systems that push back or refuse because of side effects get taken offline; so the market is doing the selecting, and the survivors are the ones that treat the metric as a terminal goal.

People then conclude from either version that takeover by AIs is a kind of default outcome of selection, not an accident of design. It's not quite an evolutionary debunking argument, and it's not ordinary natural selection either, since the 'selection' is partly stipulated rather than observed, and I honestly can't tell whether those two versions are the same move or just adjacent ones. Does this argument pattern have an established name of its own? If not, what are the nearest named things, and where would you draw the line between this and a just-so story? The label matters less to me than getting the edges right, because I keep wanting to say 'that's not actually selection, that's just a model with a Darwinian coat of paint' and I'd like to know when that's fair and when it isn't.
