I'm in a practical rationality class right now, and we've been reading about the idea that you shouldn't always trust your present preferences — that sometimes it's better to act on what a calmer, better-informed, or "idealized" version of you would want. A friend of mine does something adjacent and I'm trying to decide if her system is actually good before I adopt it. Her rule: for any decision likely to matter for more than a year (grad school, jobs, moving, relationships), first write a two-sentence sketch of the person I realistically hope to be in ten years, including what I'll be glad I did; then write what that future person would most want my current self to have chosen, assuming she knows how each option turns out; then pick the option that keeps my choice as close as possible to what the future self prefers, even when present-me wants something else. The idea is to deliberate indirectly: instead of summing up my current desires and fears, defer to the judgment of an idealized later self.

I can already see ways this goes wrong, and I'd rather have them mapped out now than discover them three years in. The ten-years-later description is probably just current-me wearing a fake beard, so the rule might launder my present preferences through a fantasy. It also seems to set up weird incentives: if I know I'll defer to a future self anyway, I might get lazy about actually thinking, or I might quietly build a "future self" who hates every option except the one I already want. And I'm genuinely unsure whether later-me has any special authority over now-me, especialy when our values drift apart. So: walk me through how this rule would actually behave if a fairly self-aware but not heroic person followed it consistently for several years. What habits and incentives does it create, where does it get gamed (including by self-deception), and can the failure modes be patched without turning it into a completely different decision procedure? I'm less interested in whether it's irrational in the abstract and more in the concrete second-order effects. Please end with a last line beginning "Summary:" that gives a one-sentence version of your overall take.
