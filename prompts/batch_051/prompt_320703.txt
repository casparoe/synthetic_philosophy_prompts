I keep running into a move in debates about consent — sometimes about sex, sometimes about medical procedures — where someone says consent is basically a contract, and then slides from that to the conclusion that once you've agreed, that's the end of it: the only question is whether a yes was given, and worries about pressure, being misled, or not really understanding are just people trying to get out of a deal they made. The "if you agreed, you agreed" move. I'm suspicious of this analogy but I can't quite articulate where it collapses.

Could you audit it? Start by laying out what is supposed to map onto what — offer, acceptance, consideration, capacity, intention to be bound, whatever you think is carrying weight. Then go through deception, coercion, and competence in turn and say whether the contract picture handles each surprisingly well (contract law does have fraud, duress, and capacity rules) or whether it quietly imports machinery from contract law that doesnt obviously apply to consent in general. I want to know which disanalogies are doing real work for the conclusion and which are just decoration. One person told me the better analogy is a waiver, not a contract, because consent doesn't create a fresh obligation so much as give someone a permission they didn't have; I'd like to know if that patch actually helps or just moves the gaps around. And then the actual question: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the "if you agreed, you agreed" conclusion survive, even in a weakened form? If not, what's a better way to think about it, if any?
