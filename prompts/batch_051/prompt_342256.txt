Ive been prepping a small unit on classical Islamic ethics for an intro philosophy of religion course, and I mentioned the Mu'tazila–Ash'arite split to a colleague who works in contemporary metaethics but doesnt do much history of Islamic thought. He later sent me his pocket summary, and Ive been stewing on it because it feels almost too neat. He wrote something like this:

"The Mu'tazila were the rationalists of classical Islam: they thought acts are good or evil in themselves, that unaided human reason can detect this, and that God, being perfectly just and wise, commands precisely because he recognizes the good. That's the Islamic version of the God-commands-it-because-it-is-good horn. The Ash'arites, by contrast, were voluntarists: nothing is good or bad until God commands or forbids it, and had God commanded theft, theft would have been not only permissible but obligatory. They thought God's commands are wholly arbitrary, and that the only way to learn right and wrong is to consult revelation. So the whole dispute is basically the Euthyphro dilemma played out inside Islamic theology, with the Mu'tazila taking one horn and the Ash'arites the other."

I suspect he has the broad outlines right, but the "wholly arbitrary" bit and the clean Euthyphro mapping worry me. Could you audit this paragraph for me? Go through its main claims one by one and tell me what a careful Mu'tazili and a careful Ash'ari would accept, what they'd want to qualify, and where his summary quietly imports an outsider's framing rather than something either camp would actually say. Also tell me how far the Euthyphro analogy is genuinely useful for teaching this and where it starts to distort. I dont need citations or a reading list; I just want to know which parts of his summary I can safely repeat to undergrads. Please also say how confident you are in each of your main points, and single out the claim you are least sure about.
