Two friends of mine, Rhea and Omar, got into an argument on a long train ride about whether you can sensibly assign probabilities to your own choices, and they kept circling back to it for the rest of the trip. I've been turning it over since, and I'd like a referee's account of who was right about what.

Rhea started. She said there's something wrong with treating your own still-open decision as a random event. If she's deciding whether to take the job in another city, it's not that she can't estimate the odds she'll go; it's that the question "what are the chances I'll choose to go?" is one she can't answer from the outside, because she is the thing being answered. A probability is a prediction about what will happen, and you can't predict something you're right then settling. Omar came back that this can't be right as a general claim, because we assign probabilities to our own future choices all the time: he knows he's maybe 80% likely to take the elevator rather than the stairs tomorrow, and he can plan around that. Even mid-deliberation, he said, he can think "there's a decent chance I'm about to eat the cake I said I wouldn't" while still treating the decision as open. So if prediction and choice conflict, it must only be in some special case, and he didn't see which one survives. Rhea replied that the elevator case is prediction in the ordinary sense and she has no quarrel with it, but when the moment arrives he's either decided or he's still deciding, and there's no room for a stable credence in his own choice. The cake case, she said, isn't a real probability either: the thought "I'll probably cave" is more like a felt temptation or a warning, and it can't be calibrated the way a normal prediction can, because whether it comes true is partly up to the thought itself. Omar countered with game theory: rational players in a mixed equilibrium assign probabilities to their own moves, so the first-person stance can't be what blocks it. Rhea said a mixed strategy is a plan to randomize, not a credence you hold while deliberating — by the time you flip the coin, deliberation is over. So Omar shifted to a case he thought was stronger: he decides now to run tomorrow morning while knowing his lazy later self will reopen the question. Assigning himself 30% odds of actually running isn't a refusal to deliberate tomorrow; it's an honest forecast of his own weakness, and forming it lets him lay out his shoes and set an alarm. Prediction and deliberation can cooperate. Rhea answered that this is all still before the moment of choice; at the actual moment, the intention has either updated into a decision or it hasn't, and if you're still forecasting at that point you've turned yourself into a spectator of your own life.

I honestly couldn't settle it. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each step they each made from clearly mistaken to clearly right, say who is right about which specific point, and spell out what, if anything, both are missing. I'd especially like to know whether Rhea's before-the-moment/at-the-moment distinction is doing real work or just moving the goalposts, and whether Omar's mixed-strategy and weak-will cases actually undermine her line or only score points against a strawman.
