I heard about the digger wasp that stings a cricket, drags it to its burrow, leaves it at the entrance, goes inside to inspect the burrow, comes back out, and then drags the cricket in. If you move the cricket a bit while the wasp is inside, it comes out, drags the cricket back to the entrance, and inspects the burrow again. Move it again and it does the same thing, apparently without limit. People call this sphexishness and say the wasp has no real flexibility or freedom.

Here's an argument I've been turning over, and I can't tell which step is sneaking something past me.

(1) The wasp's routine is rigid because it's just re-triggering a fixed procedure, and that's why we say the wasp isn't acting freely.
(2) A procedure is rigid when the behavior is fixed by a finite set of if-then rules.
(3) Any organism only ever performs finitely many actions in finitely many situations over a finite life.
(4) So for any organism we can pair each situation with the action it actually took and write the whole thing as a finite lookup table.
(5) A finite lookup table is a finite set of if-then rules.
(6) So every organism's actual behavior is fixed by a finite set of if-then rules.
(7) Therefore every organism is as rigid as the wasp, and no one is free in virtue of behaving flexibly.

The conclusion can't be right, but I'd rather keep (1) than give it up. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and what would a correct version of that step say? If it is valid, tell me which premise is false and what rejecting it costs.
