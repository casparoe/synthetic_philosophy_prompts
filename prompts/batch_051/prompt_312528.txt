In the 1980 paper that introduced the law of eponymy, Stephen Stigler writes that the law "in its simplest form is this: 'No scientific discovery is named after its original discoverer.'" In the same paper he disclaims originality, writing that if any idea in it is "not at least implicit in Merton's The Sociology of Science," it is "either a happy accident or a likely error," and he says he is trying to frame a "self-proving theorem." The paper nonetheless appeared under the title Stigler's law of eponymy, and that is what the principle is now called. Do the law, the disclaimer, and the title conflict? One can read them as mutually confirming, since the eponymy of the law would itself confirm the law's content, but that reading may be too quick. Give the strongest account of how these passages fit together, distinguish it from the glib self-application reading, and say where your reconciliation is most likely to fail and which consideration would lead you to a different verdict.
