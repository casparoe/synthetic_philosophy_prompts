Reading Rousseau's Social Contract (Cole translation) and I keep tripping over two sentences in the chapter "Whether the General Will Is Fallible." First: "the general will is always right and tends to the public advantage; but it does not follow that the deliberations of the people are always equally correct." A couple lines later: "the people is never corrupted, but it is often deceived, and on such occasions only does it seem to will what is bad."

If the general will is always right, but the people can be decieved into willing what is bad, then what exactly is the general will—the people's actual decision, their true interest seen from nowhere, or something else? These two look like they conflict unless "general will" is doing different work in the two sentences. Lay out the readings that would reconcile them (different senses of "general will," ideal versus actual deliberation, corrupted versus deceived, anything else you find), and say which reading is most honest to the text and which is special pleading.

Then apply it: in Book I ch. 7 Rousseau says that whoever refuses to obey the general will shall be compelled to do so by the whole body, and that this "means nothing less than that he will be forced to be free." On your favored reading, if the people are being deceived, is the dissenter being made free, or just being muscled by a particular will dressed up as the general will? What would change your mind about that verdict—what argument or textual evidence would flip you? Answer in American English.
