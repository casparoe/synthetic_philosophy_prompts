I've been reconstructing Newton's bucket argument for absolute space, and I can't tell whether it is sound or where exactly it breaks. (1) Assume relationism: motion just is change in the relative distances between material bodies. (2) In the bucket experiment, once the water has caught up with the rotating bucket and rotates together with it, the water is at rest relative to the bucket — the distance between the water and the bucket's sides isn't changing — yet the water's surface is concave. (3) In that steady state the water also rotates as a rigid whole, so its parts keep the same distances from one another; no relevant relative distance inside the bucket-water system is changing. (4) The only relative motion left is with respect to bodies outside the system: the fixed stars, the lab, the Earth. (5) But the argument's real move is the claim that in an otherwise empty universe this same system, run through the same sequence of stages, would still show the concave surface, even though there would be no outside body to rotate relative to. (6) That would make the concavity a real effect that isn't analyzable as relative motion among material bodies, so there must be absolute rotation, and with it absolute space. Which numbered step fails, if any, and why exactly that one? If all of them hold, is relationism simply refuted, or can it still resist?
