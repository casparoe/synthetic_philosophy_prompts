I'm a high school student, and for several years I've interpreted for my parents at medical appointments and in legal offices. Recently I started comparing that experience to how translated literature is talked about in my classes, and I noticed an asymmetry I can't explain. In literary translation, a translator who meets a word that has no clean equivalent and decides to leave it in the original is often praised for respecting the source and preserving how it resists easy comprehension. In medical and legal translation, the opposite standard seems to hold: if a translator or interpreter leaves a term untranslated, that is treated as a failure, because the whole job is to give the client a usable equivalent rather than the texture of the original. The very same act — declining to translate a term — counts as fidelity in one setting and as evasion in another. Is there a principle that justifies this reversal, or is one of the two practices getting faithfulness wrong? People often say the difference is just that poems aim at aesthetic experience while consent forms aim at clear practical uptake. That explains why the two tasks have different goals, but it does not yet explain why being faithful to the poem means preserving its untranslatability while being faithful to a diagnosis means eliminating it. What principle determines when fidelity requires leaving the untranslatable as it is and when it requires rendering it anyway, and should either practice be revised in light of that principle?
