I'm giving a job talk next month in a linguistics department, and I want a Q&A prep sheet before I walk in. The talk defends what I've been calling an efficient-ambiguity account of homonymy. The short version: languages don't put up with rampant homonymy because they have to; they put up with it because ambiguity is an efficient way for a finite vocabulary to cover a huge semantic space, and languages mostly allocate that ambiguity to short, frequent, highly predictable words where context (and, in speech, prosody) makes the cost of a wrong parse low. I'll present a corpus analysis of English homonyms, mostly from spoken corpora, showing that ambiguity load tracks frequency and contextual redundancy, plus a small information-theoretic model in which a rational lexicon designer trading off effort against clarity produces roughly the pattern we see.

The department shapes the questions, so a generic list won't cut it. Most of the faculty are usage-based and functional linguists who are warm to the idea that communicative pressure shapes the lexicon, but two formal semanticists will be annoyed that I glide between homonymy and polysemy, and the computational psycholinguist in the room works on word-sense disambiguation and will come for my sense tagging and annotation choices. The grad students will probably say I've just re-derived Zipf and put a new coat of paint on it. So: draft the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this audience, ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way the person in that seat would phrase it, with a two-minute reply, a note on where the reply is soft, when I should just concede, and which camp the objection is coming from. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. And one meta-thing: as you answer, seperate what is settled in the field from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and please don't present my account as the consensus where it isn't.
