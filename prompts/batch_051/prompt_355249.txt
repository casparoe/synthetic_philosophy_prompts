I write on the science and philosophy of consciousness for a general-interest magazine, and in three weeks I am giving a public lecture at a university's cognitive-science series. The lecture will defend a version of the claim that qualia are an incoherent posit: the familiar notion of a private, intrinsic, introspectively knowable qualitative character of experience does not carve any real feature of the world and should be abandoned rather than accommodated.

I will build the argument around the Chase and Sanborn coffee-tasting cases, reconstructed as follows. Two professional tasters each lose their enthusiasm for a coffee they formerly approved. Chase says the flavor itself has changed: he now tastes something different. Sanborn says the flavor itself is unchanged: he tastes exactly what he always tasted, and what has changed is only his standards, his response to the flavor, or his capacity to enjoy it. My lecture will press the standard lesson harder than usual. I will argue that the cases do not merely show that introspection is fallible; they expose a structural symmetry. For any candidate quale Q and any report that Q has changed, there is always a Sanborn-style redescription on which Q is unchanged and only the evaluative set point has shifted, and no introspective report, behavioral fact, or neural fact available in the case settles which description is correct, even for the subject. A posit with no individuating conditions of that kind, I will argue, earns elimination: there is no fact for it to track, so the question of whether Q changed or one's evaluation changed has no determinate answer, and Q is an idle wheel.

The audience matters. It will be perhaps sixty people: academic philosophers of mind, cognitive scientists working on perception, memory, and affect, and a few clinicians who treat chronic pain. From the organizers I know that several attendees are representationalists who reject reductive sensorimotor accounts and tolerate qualia talk without being realists about raw feels; a minority think the hard problem is a genuine obstacle and will resist any eliminativist conclusion; the clinicians are suspicious of any argument that appears to make pain less real. They are unlikely to be Dennett partisans. They will concede the coffee cases but attack their scope, their authority, and my inference from undetectability to incoherence.

Please draft my Q&A prep sheet for the thirty-minute question period after the lecture. I want the objections I will actually get from this room, in something like the order they will come, phrased the way these questioners would phrase them, not the objections I would face from committed Dennettians. For each objection, give me a short reply that fits the time, say what to concede immediately, and say what I should hold. Also tell me where the replies are weakest and what I should rehearse.

Format your answer as follows, with these sections in this order and roughly these lengths:

1. Fault lines (about 150 words): the two or three background commitments that will most determine which objections this audience raises, and who will line up where.
2. Ordered objections (about 900 words): eight to ten likely questions or objections, ordered by damage multiplied by likelihood, each phrased in the questioner's own idiom, with a note on who is most likely to ask it.
3. Replies, concessions, and holds (about 500 words): for each objection, a concise reply that could be delivered in two minutes or less, what to grant without a fight, and what to refuse.
4. Soft points and the question I should fear (about 350 words): the places where the replies are least secure, the objection that would hurt most if asked well, and one I should not try to answer off the cuff.
