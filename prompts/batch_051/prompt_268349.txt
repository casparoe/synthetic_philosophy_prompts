I've been struck by an asymmetry in how we respond to fluent, confident falsehoods depending on who produces them. A human research assistant who, asked a factual question, gives a polished but false answer with a fabricated citation gets sorted at once into moralized epistemic kinds: liar, if they knew; bullshitter, if they simply did not care whether the claim was true; merely mistaken, only if they checked and got it wrong. A language model that produces the very same fluent, confident falsehood with the same fabricated citation is described as "hallucinating," and the ensuing discussion is about decoding and retrieval rather than honesty, negligence, or indifference to truth — even though the organization deploying the model knew in advance that it would sometimes emit confident falsehood and released it anyway. Harry Frankfurt sharpens the stakes: by his account the liar must track the truth well enough to evade it, whereas the bullshitter is simply indifferent to how things stand, and Frankfurt regards the bullshitter as in some ways the greater threat to a truth-governed practice. I want to know whether this human/machine asymmetry is justified once Frankfurt's distinction is taken seriously, or whether LLM hallucination — and, more pointedly, the decision to deploy a system known to produce it — should be reclassified as bullshit rather than mere error or malfunction. Take a definite position and defend it: either supply the strongest available justification for keeping the asymmetry, or collapse it in a specified direction, and say whose epistemic vice, if anyone's, a hallucination instantiates.
