I'm a software engineer, not a philosophy person, but I've been reading some psychology-adjacent self-help stuff while building a mood-tracking app, and I keep running into the claim that trying to be happy directly backfires. The rough idea is that if you set out each morning intending to maximize your own happiness, you end up less happy than if you'd aimed at something else entirely — a project, other people, just doing the thing in front of you. Call it the paradox of hedonism. I can't tell if this is a genuin empirical claim about how attention and self-evaluation affect mood, or more like a definitional trick: if 'trying to be happy' is defined so that nobody who actually ends up happy counts as having really tried, of course the claim can't fail.

How would you actually test the non-definitional version? I want a concrete design — who you'd study, what you'd randomize or measure, over what timescale, and what numbers you'd compare. Then tell me what pattern of results you'd count as evidence that deliberately pursuing happiness is self-defeating, what pattern would count against it, and what a positive result would still leave unsettled, even in principle. Could you get the predicted result and still not know whether the problem is self-monitoring, or the participant's theory of what 'happiness' is, or just a selection effect in who signs up for that kind of study? Take a definite stance on the single best test you can design and defend it, rather than laying out three options and leaving me to pick.
