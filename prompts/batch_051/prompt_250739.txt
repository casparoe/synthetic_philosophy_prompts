I've been reading a popular history of mathematics, and its treatment of constructibility has left me uncertain how much to trust. The book's summary, as I understand it, runs like this. The ancient Greeks effectively took the rule that a curve or magnitude counted as a legitimate object of geometry only if it could be produced with straightedge and compass. Curves that required more than these tools, such as the quadratrix they used for squaring the circle, were classified as "mechanical" and denied full admission into geometry. That standard held until the nineteenth century, when Wantzel proved that the cube cannot be doubled with straightedge and compass, and Lindemann proved that π is transcendental and therefore not constructible—and with these impossibility results, mathematicians abandoned the constructive criterion and accepted transcendental curves as legitimate mathematical objects.

The story is satisfyingly clean, which is exactly why I want it stress-tested. Which parts of this summary are accurate, which are acceptable simplifications, and which are wrong or misleading? In particular: did the Greeks actually hold a single criterion of "constructible equals legitimate"? Was the quadratrix really denied a place in geometry, or is that a conflation of "not solvable by elementary means" with "not a legitimate object"? And did the constructive criterion really collapse only after Wantzel and Lindemann, or had it already eroded earlier? Please separate the accurate claims from the overstatements, and say what a careful historian of mathematics would replace the questionable parts with.
