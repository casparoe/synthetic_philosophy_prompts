Here's the argument as I keep reconstructing it.

(1) Alice and Briana both get drunk, both text their ex, both run a red light at the same intersection on different nights. Alice finds a child in the crossing and kills her; Briana finds the crossing empty. Their mental states, choices, and recklessness are stipulated to be identical. The only difference is whether a child happened to be in the crossing.

(2) In ordinary moral judgment, people blame Alice much more than Briana. They don't just feel worse about the outcome; they treat Alice as a worse person and as more deserving of censure. I'm setting legal punishment aside and asking about the blaming itself.

(3) If Alice is more blameworthy than Briana, then Alice deserves more blame than Briana. Blame, unlike a fine or a repair order, is apt only if it tracks desert; otherwise calling someone blameworthy is really just calling them unlucky, and we already have a word for that.

(4) Desert is grounded only in what was up to the agent: her choices, intentions, knowledge, effort, and the care she exercised. It can't be grounded in facts that lie outside her control, even when those facts are part of how the situation turns out.

(5) The only difference between Alice and Briana is a fact that lay outside both of their control: whether a child was in the crossing when they went through.

(6) Therefore Alice and Briana don't differ in what they deserve.

(7) Therefore Alice is not more blameworthy than Briana; the ordinary judgment in (2) is a systematic error, and blaming them differently is scapegoating with a nicer name.

The most tempting place to dig in is (3): someone might say blame isn't only about desert, because it also does deterrent and norm-expressing work, so it can be apt for reasons other than desert. But that seems to change the subject from blameworthiness to blame-usefulness. If that's the move, then we should stop saying Alice is more blameworthy and start saying treating her worse is useful, which is a different claim and a lot weaker than what ordinary moral judgment actually asserts. Am I wrong?
