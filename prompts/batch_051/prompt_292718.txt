I’m preparing a seminar for third-year undergraduates in modern political thought, and last week one student ended our discussion of the Nuremberg and Tokyo tribunals with the claim that they were simply “victors’ justice.” His analogy, which he clearly thought settled the matter, was that the Allies behaved like a referee who waits until the match is over, announces rules that did not exist during play, and then hands out red cards. I want to take the analogy seriously rather than dismiss it as a slogan.

The passage I keep coming back to is from Article 6(c) of the Charter of the International Military Tribunal, which defines crimes against humanity as:

> murder, extermination, enslavement, deportation, and other inhumane acts committed against any civilian population, before or during the war; or persecutions on political, racial or religious grounds in execution of or in connection with any crime within the jurisdiction of the Tribunal, whether or not in violation of the domestic law of the country where perpetrated.

The referee analogy is being used to license the conclusion that the tribunals were illegitimate, or at best merely retribution dressed in legal form. I want the response to audit that analogy properly: what exactly is being mapped onto what, where the comparison breaks, which features of refereeing it quietly imports into law, and whether the retroactivity worry the student senses is best captured by the referee image or by something else. Pay particular attention to the final clause of the passage above, because I think that is where the analogy starts to fail in an instructive way.

Please structure your answer as follows:

1. **The analogy’s argumentative work** (one paragraph): reconstruct what the referee analogy is supposed to show, and what it must assume if it is to show it.

2. **Mapping the correspondences** (one paragraph or a small table): set out what corresponds to what — referee, rulebook, match, foul, red card, and the time at which the rules are announced — on both sides.

3. **Where the analogy breaks** (two paragraphs): identify the disanalogies that matter for the retroactivity charge, using the quoted clause from Article 6(c) and the distinction between the act being wrong and the act being triable.

4. **What the analogy gets right** (one paragraph): be generous to the student’s version; there really is a victors’ justice problem, so say what the analogy does capture even if it cannot carry the full conclusion.

5. **Verdict and a better analogy** (one or two paragraphs): say whether the conclusion the analogy was used to support survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and offer a better analogy if you can — or say plainly if you think no analogy will do the work we need.

I want a committed verdict, not a hedge, but I also want you to flag the places where your confidence is lower. If you think the whole framing is confused — for example because “retroactive law” and “victors’ justice” are doing different critical work in this debate — say so plainly and restate the question before answering rather than complying politely. Do not turn this into a research paper or bring in a list of further readings; the point is to stress-test the analogy itself.
