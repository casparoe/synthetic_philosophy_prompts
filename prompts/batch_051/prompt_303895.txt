Question: Suppose a linguist in the 1960s argued that sign languages like ASL are not real languages because language is a vocal-auditory system, because signs are often iconic rather than arbitrary, and because gestures seem holistic rather than built from meaningless parts. Is this a good objection, or does modern sign-language research answer it?

Student answer: The objection is mostly wrong. ASL and BSL are full natural languages: they have their own grammar, including rules for word order, agreement, negation, and question formation, and they are not just English on the hands. Children exposed to sign from birth acquire it on the same general timetable as hearing children learning a spoken language, and sign languages differ from each other in vocabulary and grammar, so they cant be universal pantomime. On arbitrariness, though, the objector has a point: many signs are iconic, for instance signs for TREE in lots of sign languages trace the shape of a tree, so for those signs the form-meaning link is motivated rather than arbitrary. That means sign languages partly violate the arbitrariness design feature, even if they are still languages overall. On duality of patterning, the objection fails: signs break down into handshape, location, movement, and orientation, which are meaningless on their own and recombine, so sign languages do have a kind of phonology. So the only real concession is that sign languages are not arbitrary in the way spoken words are, but that turns out not to matter much.
