I've been reading bits of Blackstone's Commentaries for a class, and one thing in the chapter on the king's prerogative keeps bugging me. Blackstone claims the law attributes to the king both a kind of corporate immortality and a sort of perfect rectitude: the Crown, considered as a legal person, cannot even intend a wrong, so if something exceptionable is done in public affairs, it has to be attributed to ministers or agents rather than to the king himself. I know later writers treat this mostly as a convenient fiction for getting immunity from suit, but the logic itself still seems weirdly alive.

Here's the parallel I can't shake. When philosophers in the group-agency literature argue that corporations or states can be genuine agents, they also need to attribute intentions to a single corporate entity as distinct from the individuals inside it. That looks like the same move Blackstone makes: there's an artificial person with its own capacities, and wrongdoing is assigned to the entity or to its functionaries depending on how you draw the boundaries of that person. But the political conclusions seem opposite. Blackstone uses the artificial person to block the question "did the Crown do wrong?" while the group-agency people use it to open the question "did the corporation do wrong?" Is this a deep structural parallel, or is it just a superficial similarity in talk about "person" and "intention"? What exactly would have to be true of a group for Blackstone's immunity move to transfer to, say, a modern corporation or state? And where does the analogy break?

Also, whatever verdict you give, can you mark what's actually settled in the literature here versus what's genuinely contested—I don't want a contested reading of Blackstone or a contested position in the group-agency debate presented as if everyone agrees.
