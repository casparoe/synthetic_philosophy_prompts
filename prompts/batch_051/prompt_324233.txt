I'm a novelist, and the book I'm working on now is set in a near-future country that has to rebuild its electoral rules more or less from scratch after a constitutional crisis. While I've been reading around, I keep noticing that real democracies draw their lines about who votes in ways that look flatly inconsistent, and I want my characters to have the strongest version of this fight, not just volley op-ed clichés at each other.

Here's the cluster. We fine people, and sometimes lock them up, for not showing up to jury duty, but the moment someone proposes fining people for not voting we treat them as a tyrant. Both are billed as civic duties, so Whats the difference doing the real work there? Second: a sixteen-year-old can work, pay income tax, and in plenty of jurisdictions be tried as an adult for a crime, but cannot vote on the tax rates or the criminal laws that apply to her. Third: we tax non-citizens who live and work among us and hold them to the same laws, but the old principle that you shouldn't be taxed without a say in government applies, apparently, only to citizens. We teach schoolchildren that principle as if it's sacred, then dont apply it to millions of taxpayers.

Are these three just separate compromises, or is there one defensible principle of the franchise that draws all three lines exactly where we've drawn them? Give me the best candidate justification, then stress-test it against the cases that make me uneasy as a novelist: the adult citizen who owns nothing, earns nothing, and pays no income tax but votes anyway; the non-citizen who has lived somewhere thirty years and raised her kids there but still can't vote; the seventeen-year-old with a full-time job and a baby. Tell me which of the three lines you'd keep, reverse, or drop if you had to make the system consistent, and whether consistency would actually be better than the mess we've got.
