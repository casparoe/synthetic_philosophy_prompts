I've been going back and forth with a friend about whether a language model's chain-of-thought text is an actual report of how it reasoned or just a plausible narration it spins up after it has already landed on an answer, and I'm trying to turn that vague worry into something checkable. The rough criteria I've been using for counting a chain of thought as faithful are: (1) sensitivity — if the model's hidden computation had gone differently, the transcript would have gone differently too; (2) relevance — the transcript mostly includes the considerations that actually moved the answer rather than plausible filler; (3) non-confabulation — it shouldn't describe weighing evidence it never really weighed; and (4) ordering — the steps in the transcript should map, in roughly the right order, onto stages of the actual computation.

Heres the case that keeps bugging me. Suppose someone trains a normal transformer to answer reasoning questions, and then trains a separate decoder on its final internal state — a snapshot taken only after the answer is already determined. The decoder's job is to emit a chain-of-thought transcript for whatever answer the model settled on. The transcripts come out startlingly good: they cite the right premises, and when researchers directly intervene on a specific internal feature (say, a feature tracking whether a product contains an allergen) and flip it, the final answer changes and the transcript changes to match. Still, the transcript is a reconstruction from the endpoint, not a trace of the route — it never had access to intermediate steps, only to where the model ended up. Does this count as faithful self-report under my four criteria? Go criterion by criterion, flag where the criteria underdetermine the verdict, and say whether the case shows I should revise the criteria themselves — for instance whether I should care less about ordering and more about whether the system would notice if it were confabulating.
