I've been in a little reading group on AI safety and someone keeps invoking Goodhart's law—"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure"—as if it settles whether you can ever safely optimize a learned reward model. Last meeting it came up specifically in an argument about using an LLM to score "quality of hire" from application materials. The person arguing against it said the whole project is self-defeating because, once the score is the target, candidates will game it and the score stops meaning what its supposed to mean. Fine, but I can't tell what exact claim is doing the work there. Is it a purely statistical claim about correlations falling apart under optimization pressure, a game-theoretic claim about rational agents gaming whatever gets measured, a causal or behavioral claim that the act of targeting changes the underlying thing being measured, a definitional point about what makes a measure "good" in the first place, or some combination of all of these? And does the slogan itself distinguish between these, or does it just blur them together and let people slide from a modest claim to a much stronger one? I'd like a menu of the distinct precise theses that sentence could be expressing—laid out as separate readings rather than one unified theory—with for each reading some verdict on whether it's trivial, plausible but substantive, or actually false, plus a note on which reading the "any operationalization of a fuzzy goal is doomed" argument needs to be relying on if it's going to actually work.
