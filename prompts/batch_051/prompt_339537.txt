I write about science and ideas for a living, and I've spent the last week trying to figure out why philosophers are still fighting about dispositions when the debate looks, from the outside, like it should have been settled decades ago. One side says a disposition like fragility just is a conditional: x is fragile iff, if you dropped x, it would break. The other side says the 'fink' and 'mask' cases kill that. A fink is something that loses the disposition exactly when the trigger arrives — the standard example is a live wire wired to go dead the instant its touched. A mask is something that keeps the disposition but blocks the manifestation — a fragile glass packed in styrofoam won't break if dropped, but it's still fragile. The conditional-analysis camp keeps patching: add ceteris paribus clauses, say 'in normal conditions', 'absent masks and finks', and so on. The other camp says the patches either go circular (fragile = would break unless it wouldn't) or just relocate the problem. What's striking to me is that both sides seem to agree on every concrete case: the wire is live, the styrofoam-packed glass is fragile, and they agree about what would happen in each scenario. So the fight doesn't look like an empirical disagreement about cases. My outsider's hypothesis is that they're running together two questions — what the ordinary word 'fragile' means, and what makes something fragile in the world — and that once you split those, the dispute is largely verbal. But I can see a different possibility: maybe there's a substantive metaphysical fight about whether dispositions are intrinsic powers or relational/conditional properties, and maybe both camps are right about different things.
