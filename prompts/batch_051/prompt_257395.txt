I am a novelist at work on a book about a retired civil engineer who has come to believe a Fomenko-style thesis that the accepted chronology of ancient history is a modern fabrication, and his daughter, a professional historian, who is trying to reason him out of it. I want the novel to be fair to both of them: the father should not be a cartoon crank, and the daughter should not win by labeling. The daughter's central move — and a move I hear constantly in real debates — is an analogy: pseudohistory stands to history as pseudoscience stands to science. The idea is that just as creation science or astrology can be identified and set aside by the criteria that police good science, such as falsifiability, testability, peer review, and consilience with established results, so Atlantis, ancient-aliens narratives, and Fomenko-style chronology can be identified and set aside by the corresponding criteria for history. In the novel, this analogy is being asked to do real argumentative work: it licenses a dismissive conclusion about the father's views before their details have been examined.

I would like you to act as a professional philosopher of history and of the demarcation problem and audit this analogy for me. Think carefully and do not just gesture at the comparison. Begin by brainstorming the strongest versions of both sides, then lay out the correspondences explicitly: in the pseudoscience case, identify what plays the role of the theory, the data, the test, the anomaly, the community of practitioners, and the characteristic failure; then ask what plays each corresponding role in the pseudohistory case. Mark which mappings are tight and which are loose. Then identify the disanalogies that matter for the daughter's intended use, as opposed to incidental ones. In particular, consider whether criteria built around falsifiability and experimental testing transfer to a past that is not repeatable and whose evidence base is archaeological and textual; whether history even has something analogous to the decisive experiment, and whether the analogy silently assumes that it does; whether pseudohistory and pseudoscience fail in the same way or merely look similar from the outside; and what the analogy quietly imports about the epistemic health of ordinary, credentialed history itself. Then deliver a verdict: does the dismissive conclusion — that Atlantis, ancient aliens, and Fomenko-style chronology can be set aside by the same move that sets aside pseudoscience — survive the audit? If it survives only in a weakened form, say exactly which form. If the analogy is doing more work than it can bear, say so, and propose either a better analogy or a better argument that the daughter could actually make. The result should be usable to me as a novelist: I want to write the daughter as a serious thinker who gives specific reasons, and the father as wrong for identifiable reasons rather than because a label has been stuck to him.
