I've spent a weird amount of time over the last few years thinking about the kind of choice you only really see in politics: someone in charge orders something horrible because not ordering it would be worse. The version I keep coming back to is a leader who authorises a strike knowing it will kill some innocent people, because the only alternative is letting a much larger number of people die. I think theres a name for this — dirty hands? — but I've never read the proper stuff on it, so this is just me trying to get my own views straight.

Roughly where I've landed is this. (1) There are cases like that where the leader should give the order, and a leader who refuses because they want to keep their hands clean is actually doing something bad, not good. (2) Even so, the rightness and the wrongness don't cancel out: the decision is right and the act is genuinely wrong at the same time. Saying "it was the right call" doesn't wipe the wrongness away, it just sits next to it. (3) And yet I also think a moral judgement should land on one verdict — right, wrong, or allowed — not on "both". If someone says an act is both right and wrong I usually think they've slid between two different descriptions of it without noticing. (4) Whether the decision was right is fixed only by what the leader could foresee, what the options were, and what those options would bring about; the leader's feelings, their character, their past, none of that changes it. (5) Guilt fits only when you've done wrong. If someone really did the right thing, then guilt is a bit of a mistake — something to get over rather than something it would be sensible to hold onto. (6) But a leader who gives that order and then sleeps fine, or who says "I'd do it again and I wouldn't apologise", is chilling. We should want them to be at least a little haunted.

What I'd like is a proper audit. I can't tell whether these six hang together or whether I'm just switching senses of "right" and "wrong" from one sentence to the next. Which of these really pull against each other, and which tensions are only apparent? Please rank the conflicts by how serious they are, and then tell me what the cheapest single change would be to make the set consistent — and what that change would cost me. I don't want a fence-sitting answer that says every pair is a little tense but can be read charitably; I'd rather know which commitment is doing the most damage. And please write the answer in British English.
