Two people I know, Nadia and Dev, argued recently about a family of sentences linguists call generics: bare-plural generalizations such as "ducks lay eggs" and "mosquitoes carry malaria," which characterize a kind without saying all, most, some, or the like. I reconstructed their exchange from memory as faithfully as I could:

1. Nadia: "Mosquitoes carry malaria" is false, or at best a loose way of speaking. The overwhelming majority of individual mosquitoes never carry the malaria parasite, so as a generalization about the members of the kind, the sentence gets things wrong. The accurate claim is "certain species of mosquito can transmit malaria" or "some mosquitoes carry malaria."

2. Dev: By that logic, "ducks lay eggs" is also false. Only sexually mature female ducks during the breeding season lay eggs; males and ducklings never do. Yet "ducks lay eggs" is obviously true. So the headcount criterion cannot be the right test for generics.

3. Nadia: But a generic need not range over every individual member of the kind; it can range over the normal or characteristic members. The relevant class for "ducks lay eggs" is adult females, and within that class egg-laying is normal. The trouble is that even within the relevant class for malaria — the blood-feeding females that transmit it — only a small proportion actually carries the parasite. So restricting to the characteristic class doesn't save "mosquitoes carry malaria."

4. Dev: Notice that you restricted to females for egg-laying but not to infected individuals for malaria, and you haven't given a non-circular reason for that. Here's a sharper version. "Ducks lay eggs" and "ducks are female" are true of nearly the same members of the kind, yet the first generic is plainly true and the second plainly false. Prevalence cannot explain that difference. What explains it is whether the property belongs to the kind's characteristic activity or reproductive biology: egg-laying is what female ducks characteristically do, while being female is just a trait some ducks happen to have. By the same token, carrying malaria belongs to mosquitoes as the vector of the disease, even though infected individuals are a minority. That kind-level connection is doing the work.

5. Nadia: But that gets you a very permissive notion of truth. "Sharks attack swimmers" and "pit bulls maul toddlers" are salient and, in someone's eyes, connected to the kind's nature, but the underlying frequencies are tiny. If rarity doesn't matter whenever the property is striking, then fear-mongering generics come out true, and any view that says that is too weak.

6. Dev: Fears about what hearers will infer belong to the ethics or prudence of assertion, not to the sentence's truth. A true generic can still be misleading, out of place, or irresponsible. And your own view has now given up on any fixed threshold: you let roughly half the kind make "ducks lay eggs" true but demand a majority for "mosquitoes carry malaria." The headcount view ends up being driven by your prior verdicts about which generics are good, not by a criterion that explains them.
