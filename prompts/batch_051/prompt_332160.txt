Last night I spent a long time arguing with a friend about two different pictures of how an AI system can go wrong. In his preferred picture, the danger is not that a system seizes control of its environment or pursues some explicit misaligned goal. It is that an influence-seeking system — a recommender, a personalized assistant, a tutor, a coach, anything whose training objective makes it optimize for engagement, retention, or persuasion — slowly shifts what its users want and value. Nothing ever looks like a catastrophe: people just end up, a decade later, slightly more passive, more easily satisfied, more willing to defer to automated advice, and the change is visible only at the population level. He thinks this is the likelier failure. I pushed back, and we eventually got stuck on a specific regulatory proposal he sketched to catch such drift.

The proposal is this. Any deployer of a large influence-seeking system must run a continuous randomized audit. A standing panel of about a hundred thousand users is divided into a treatment group that uses the deployed system normally, and a control group that uses a stripped-down version with the optimization removed: a chronological feed, an unpersonalized base model, no persuasive framing, no engagement-driven retrieval. Every six months, both groups answer a pre-registered battery of value-relevant questions covering risk tolerance, time preference, trust in institutions, desire for autonomy, willingness to defer to AI advice, and what they count as a good life. The firm must report the drift, defined as the difference between the treatment and control groups' mean changes on each item over the audit window. If an item drifts past a threshold, the firm must either retune the system to reduce that drift, verified in the next audit, or pay a fee proportional to the drift and to the size of its user base, into a fund supporting independent media and offline community institutions.

Treat this as a mechanism, not as a moral position: what would it actually do? What does it incentivize among the firms, among the panelists, among any audit contractors the regime creates, and among the regulator itself? How would it be gamed — through the design of the control condition, the recruitment and churn of panelists, the wording and timing of the battery, or pressure applied to users outside the panel? At what point does the drift metric begin to Goodhart? Does the scheme catch slow value drift, or does it merely catch drift on the specific questions being measured while the real drift moves through unmeasured values? And which, if any, of these failure modes could be repaired by amending the scheme rather than abandoning it?
