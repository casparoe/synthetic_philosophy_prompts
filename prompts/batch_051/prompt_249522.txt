I am trying to compile a complete set of objections to actual intentionalism about literary meaning — the view that what a text means is fixed by what its author actually intended it to mean. The standard objections, as I have them, are these five: (1) the inaccessibility objection, that intentions are private and often unrecoverable, so a meaning grounded in them would be unknowable in just the cases where interpretation matters most, such as dead, anonymous, or silent authors; (2) the unreliable-self-report objection, that authors' later statements about their own works are frequently inconsistent, self-serving, or false, and are themselves items in need of interpretation rather than a final court of appeal; (3) the publicity objection, that a text's meaning is fixed by the shared conventions of a public language, and no private mental act can make a sentence mean something other than what it conventionally says; (4) the absent-intention objection, that many texts — committee-authored documents, anthologies, works revised over decades — contain no single, settled authorial intention to which meaning could be anchored; and (5) the surplus-meaning objection, that a work is rightly taken to mean more, or other, than its author had in mind, so actual intention can be neither the source nor the limit of textual meaning. I would like one further objection to actual intentionalism as a theory of meaning — not a rewording, special case, or combination of any of the five, and not the separate evaluative point that consulting intention is irrelevant to judging a work's success, nor a shift to an adjacent theory such as hypothetical intentionalism or reader-response theory. State the objection, then say honestly whether it succeeds and what it adds that the five above do not.
