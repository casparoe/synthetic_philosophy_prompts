I have been trying to work out what the Stoic ideal of apatheia—freedom from disturbance—could defensibly ask of us without collapsing into emotional deadness or indifference. I have come to think that defenders and critics of the ideal largely talk past each other because they are using the word disturbance for two different layers of feeling. The first layer is the direct response to something in the world: grief at a real loss, fear in the presence of a threat, anger at a witnessed injustice. The second layer is the response to that response: anxiety about being anxious, shame at feeling angry, the inner voice that says one should be over a loss by now, the rumination and self-policing that surround a feeling judged excessive or inappropriate. I call this the two-tier account of equanimity, and its claim is that apatheia is best read as targeting only the second tier. First-tier emotions carry information and motivate us to act, so the ideal should want them intact and even strong. A person who has lost a friend should grieve; what tranquility rightly extinguishes is the second grief about the grief, the frightened vigilance over one's own sadness, the exhausting negotiation with an emotion one cannot accept.

I realize the line may be less clean than I am making it sound. It may be that the appraisal of an emotion is internal to the emotion, so removing the second tier means subtly altering the first. It may be that eliminating second-order disturbance tends to blunt first-order vividness over time, or that the account smuggles in a rationalist picture of which emotions are fitting. I am also unsure whether this is original or just a familiar idea in unfamiliar terms. I would like an honest assessment: whether the distinction is coherent, whether it already exists under another name, what it gets right, and where it breaks.
