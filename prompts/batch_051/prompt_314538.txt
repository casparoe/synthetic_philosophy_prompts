Lately I have been going back and forth with a friend about accuracy-first epistemology, and the argument has left me genuinely uncertain whether a theory this elegant can be telling the truth about something as unruly as intellectual humility. The theory, as she defends it, runs like this: the sole fundamental epistemic good for a credence is accuracy—closeness to the truth—measured by a strictly proper scoring rule, with the Brier score as the standard example, and a rational epistemic state is the one that maximizes expected accuracy from the agent’s own point of view. That one optimization principle, she argues, does the work of a whole list of epistemic rules: it explains why degrees of belief should satisfy the probability axioms, why one should update by conditionalization, and why some policies for responding to evidence are better than others. I find the unification hard to resist; it turns epistemology from a heap of intuitive prohibitions into a decision theory for belief.

But the same machinery that gives the view its power seems to commit it to something I keep wanting to reject. A strictly proper scoring rule has the consequence that a rational credence must be immodest: it must expect itself to be uniquely most accurate by its own lights. If I assign 0.8 to a proposition while also thinking there is a real chance that 0.6 would be the better attitude to the same evidence, then—so my friend insists—I face an uncomfortable choice. Either my second-order diffidence is not actually integrated into my credences, in which case it is hollow, a way of performing humility without holding it; or it is integrated, in which case 0.8 no longer maximizes my expected accuracy and the theory says I should move until the diffidence disappears. The view seems to leave no room for the agent I most admire: someone who holds a firm first-order view and at the same time honestly regards it as unlikely to be the most accurate view in the room.

Walk me through the strongest version of each side. Is my humility intuition tracking a genuine epistemic virtue that accuracy-first epistemology gets wrong, or is it something that should be debunked—say, a heuristic for future evidence, or a social virtue dressed up as an epistemic one? Could the theory be amended, perhaps by distinguishing first-order and higher-order norms or by admitting a second fundamental epistemic value alongside accuracy? Or is there a reconciliation in which a properly spelled-out accuracy-first view can host the intuition after all? For each move, tell me what would justify it, and then say which one you would make and what would change your mind.
