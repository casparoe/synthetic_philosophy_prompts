I have been developing a distinction in asylum adjudication that I call **recognition load**. A claim has high recognition load when the evidence that would most strongly support it has been made difficult or impossible to produce by the receiving state’s own border and immigration architecture. The central claim is that procedural barriers should sometimes be treated as first-order evidence about claim quality, not merely as a background fairness issue. I formulate it as a principle: each time a receiving state makes a specific protection need harder to prove, it accrues a **negative recognition debt**, and this debt should lower the standard of proof for claims whose strongest available evidence was foreclosed by the state’s own policies.

The clearest case is the interaction between irregular entry and credibility. A state imposes carrier sanctions and visa restrictions that make legal entry practically unavailable, then treats the claimant’s use of a smuggler or false documents as undermining their credibility. My theory says the credibility inference is not just unjust; it is a confound that the state created, and the adjudicator should discount the negative inference by the degree to which the state’s policies manufactured the route. Another example: a receiving state funds or equips a foreign government whose security forces commit persecution against a minority, then requires claimants from that minority to show individual targeting rather than group-based persecution. The funding state has, in my terms, increased the recognition load on those claims and should bear the corresponding evidentiary cost.

I want an honest assessment. Is this a distinct concept or a metaphor for things already worked out under names like procedural fairness, abuse of rights, or evidentiary presumptions? Where does it break—does it wrongly collapse the separation between border enforcement decisions and asylum adjudication, or assign to receiving states an open-ended liability for the conditions under which people flee? And what would a well-designed version of negative recognition debt have to rule out in order to be administrable?
