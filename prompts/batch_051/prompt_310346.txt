I'm teaching Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" next week, and I want to do it through Rawls instead of the usual utilitarianism angle I see eveywhere—specifically the veil of ignorance and the difference principle. Superficially it's a natural fit: behind the veil you don't know whether you'll be the kid locked in the cellar or one of the people at the festival, so rejecting Omelas looks like the rational choice, and the difference principle says an inequality is only just if it works out to the greatest benefit of the least well off. But I want the reading to earn that instead of just asserting it. So a few things I'd want covered. First, could you steelman a defense of Omelas in Rawls's actual terms—someone claims the child's suffering buys a city so good that this is the best available outcome for the child too, and therefore the arrangement satisfies the difference principle? I think the story forecloses that, but I want the counterargument laid out properly, because if it can't be, then Rawls and Le Guin aren't lined up the way I think. Second, what are the ones who walk away doing in Rawlsian terms? Are they refusing to live under terms they couldn't accept behind the veil, and does Rawls's framework actually have room for that as a response to an unjust society, or is walking away a kind of exit that the theory doesn't really theorize? Third, and maybe most important: where does the story resist the Rawlsian reading? The child is known, the citizens are complicit by staying, some feel guilt and stay anyway—none of that is the original position, and I'd rather the reading say where the lens stops fitting than pretend it covers everything.

If I'm forcing the story and Rawls together in a way that misreads one or both, just say that plainly; I'd rather have that than a clever reading that collapses when a student pokes it. And please don't give me the generic "Omelas is anti-utilitarian" essay—do the Rawls-specific version, contradictions and all.
