A friend and I just spent the whole evening arguing about this and now I want someone to settle the principle, not just the case. We kept coming back to the maxim that ignorance of the law is no excuse, but we agreed that can't be absolute: it seems unfair to bind someone by a rule they genuinely couldn't have known about. By the end we'd hammered out three conditions we both accepted for when someone is fairly bound by a rule they didn't know, and then I realized the conditions might not be doing the work they look like they're doing. The conditions are: (1) the rule was actually in force and publicly available somewhere at the time, (2) a reasonable person in there position could have learned about it without absurd effort, and (3) the act wasn't obviously wrong apart from the rule itself. Now here's the case that broke us. A cargo ship captain docks regularly at a small foreign port and for years has discharged treated bilge water eight miles out, which was legal until the port state changed the rule eleven days before his last arrival to forbid discharge within twelve miles. The new rule was published in the national gazette in the local language and pinned on a board inside the harbormaster's office. The captain reads the language a little but not well enough for legal notices, and his ship's agent radioed him on the morning of arrival that there were no new restrictions. No one told him to check the board. He discharges at eight miles, exactly as before. Is he fairly bound by the rule? Work through the three conditions one at a time, say where they underdetermine the answer, and then say whether the case shows we should change the criteria, and if so which one.
