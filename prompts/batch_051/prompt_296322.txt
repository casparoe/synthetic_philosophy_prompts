I am preparing a unit for my upper-level philosophy of mind seminar on what one might call the exhaustibility of experience: whether a finite mind, with finite representational and discriminatory capacities, could in principle run through the space of possible experiences and be left with nothing but repetition, or whether that space is inexhaustible in a way that matters. The class has divided sharply. Some students defend a finitist view: once the finite environment and the finite sensory-cognitive apparatus are fixed, there are only so many distinguishable experiential states, and a long-lived enough subject would eventually be bored in a terminal sense, novelty over rather than merely scarce. Others hold that this is a confusion, because an experience is not simply an episode selected from a menu of scenes; attention, skill, interpretation, and mood generate indefinitely many distinct experiences even within a fixed setting.

I want to spend one session on this through the film Groundhog Day. Phil Connors is trapped in a repeating setting, the same town, the same day, the same people, and he moves from hedonistic experimentation through mastery of various accomplishments, to despair and attempted self-destruction, and finally to a kind of attentiveness and care that seems to transform the day from within rather than supplying new contents for it. Read the film through the lens of this debate. Where does it genuinely dramatize the exhaustion of experience, and where does it resist that reading by showing the same day becoming new through attention, skill, or concern? Does the film take a position on whether the problem is a finite stock of experiences or a finite quality of mind? And is the loop a fair test, given that it grants Phil indefinitely many iterations, or does that distortion illuminate something the finitist view misses? I would particularly value a reading that says what the film adds to the philosophical argument that abstraction alone has trouble conveying.
