I've been developing a view I call Tranquilism and I want it stress-tested, not just validated. The core claim: the only thing with non-instrumental value is the absence of craving (taṇhā, in the Buddhist sense), and the only thing with non-instrumental disvalue is the suffering that craving produces. Everything else—pleasure, knowledge, friendship, beauty, even virtue—is good only insofar as it stills or removes craving, and bad only insofar as it feeds craving. So a life that is pleasant but full of unfulfilled wanting ranks below a flat, boring life where nothing is wanted. I know that sounds harsh.

I've been using a passage to anchor it. In the Dhammapada, a collection of verses attributed to the Buddha, chapter XXIV ("Thirst"), verse 338, Max Müller's 1881 translation reads: "As a tree, even though it has been cut down, is firm so long as its root is safe, and grows again, thus, unless the feeders of thirst are destroyed, the pain (of life) will return again and again."

I read this as saying thirst is the root, and the phrase "feeders of thirst" is doing a lot of work for me—it suggests the real target isn't just the felt urge but whatever feeds it, which fits my claim that everything else matters only through its relation to craving. But I can see a competing reading where the verse is a diagnosis of why suffering recurs, not a theory of value at all, and my move from "thirst is the root of pain" to "nothing but the absence of thirst has final value" is a leap.

Give me the three strongest objections to Tranquilism and tell me whether any of them is fatal rather than just uncomfortable. Also say wether the verse actually supports me or whether I'm overreading it, and whether this position already exists under a standard name—or is it just Buddhism with the metaphysics left out. Please write in American English, and at the end say how confident you are in what you've claimed and single out the point you're least sure of.
