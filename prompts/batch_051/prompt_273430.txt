I'm a retired physical chemist — spent thirty-odd years doing spectroscopy and never read much philosophy beyond what you pick up at dinner parties. Now that I have time I've been working through metaethics, and I keep tripping over a puzzle about embedded moral sentences, which I've tried to turn into a numbered argument. I'd like to know whether the argument is any good.

The view I have in mind is expressivism: the idea that a moral sentence like 'stealing is wrong' doesn't report a moral fact but instead expresses a non-cognitive attitude, roughly the speaker's disapproval of stealing. Saying 'stealing is wrong' is more like saying 'boo to stealing' than like reporting that the stove is hot.

Here's my argument against it.

(1) If 'stealing is wrong' expresses disapproval of stealing, then the contribution it makes inside any bigger sentence must be that same expression of disapproval.

(2) The meaning of a compound sentence is determined by the meanings of its parts and how they're put together.

(3) So, on the expressivist view, the 'stealing is wrong' inside 'if stealing is wrong, then paying someone to steal is wrong' should contribute the speaker's disapproval of stealing.

(4) But a speaker can sincerely accept that whole conditional without disapproving of stealing. They might be genuinely undecided about whether stealing is wrong, or even inclined to think it's not wrong, while still thinking that if it is wrong, paying someone to steal is wrong too.

(5) So in the embedded position, 'stealing is wrong' does not contribute the attitude that expressivists say is its meaning.

(6) So expressivism can't account for what moral sentences mean when they're embedded, and therefore it can't be a full theory of moral language.

It feels valid to me, but I've read that expressivists think they can wiggle out, so I assume something is off. Please go through step by step: is the argument valid? If not, exactly where does it break? If the arguments fine, are all the premises true — especially (4) — and does the conclusion really follow? And if an expressivist wanted to resist it, what's the strongest single step to dig in on, and what would their corrected version of that step look like?
