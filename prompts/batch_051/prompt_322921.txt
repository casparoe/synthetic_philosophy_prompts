One strand of ordinary evaluation, associated with the American Dream, treats rising from humble origins to great success as a reason for admiration: the longer the climb, the more the achievement is taken to reveal merit. Another strand, visible in Nordic Jantelagen and in the tall poppy syndrome, treats even a modest rise above one's immediate peers as an offense to be corrected through mockery, resentment, or obstruction. Put together, these two attitudes produce a peculiar gradient: the more someone surpasses their starting point and the farther away the observer stands, the more the success is praised; the smaller and more local the advantage over peers, the more it is punished. If both reactions were tracking achievement or desert, we would expect praise to strengthen as achievement grows, not to change sign when the reference group shifts from the world at large to the neighbour or coworker.

Give your verdict first, then argue for it. Say whether anything justifies this asymmetry. Try to give the strongest version of the defense: for example, that the two reactions track different values (exceptional merit in one case, equality-maintenance or modesty in the other), or that the evidence for desert is genuinely better in the far-away case. Then test that defense against nearby cases: an outsider whose rise depends partly on luck, a peer whose small advantage is fully earned, and a public figure who is admired by strangers but resented by old friends. State which of the two reactions you would keep, which you would abandon or restrict, and whether the boundary between a praiseworthy rise and a cut-down tall poppy can survive reflection.
