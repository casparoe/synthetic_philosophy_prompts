I have been working through Abelard's view that what makes a sin a sin is neither the outward deed nor the impulse or desire behind it, but solely the will's consent to the deed. A friend of mine thinks the view collapses under an obvious objection, and he laid it out as follows. (1) If sin consists entirely in inner consent, then the outward act—whether the intention is carried out or thwarted at the last moment—adds nothing to the degree of sin. (2) If the outward act adds nothing to the degree of sin, then two people with the same inner consent, one of whom is prevented from acting and one of whom succeeds, have sinned to exactly the same degree. (3) Therefore, on this view, attempted murder and successful murder are equally sinful. (4) But attempted murder and successful murder are not equally sinful, since in the one case someone is dead and in the other no one is. (5) Therefore Abelard's view is false. I have the feeling the argument is too quick, but I cannot locate the step that does the work. Is the argument valid? If not, which premise or inference fails; if it is valid, which premise should a defender of Abelard reject, or is the right response to accept (3) while denying that it refutes the view? Be explicit about which sense or senses of 'sin' are in play at each step.
