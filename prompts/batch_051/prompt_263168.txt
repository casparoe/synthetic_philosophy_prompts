People seem to treat two kinds of surprising testimony very differently. If a bunch of otherwise reliable people report seeing some extremely unlikely but physically possible thing—say a plane crash landing safely after both engines fail at night, or someone winning a 1-in-a-billion lottery twice—most of us would probably believe them, absent special reason to think they're lying. But if the same number of equally reliable people report a genuinely supernatural event—a dead person coming back to life after days, a severed limb growing back overnight—we smell a hoax or collective delusion and think no amount of ordinary testimony should convince us. That looks like an asymmetry: both cases involve eyewitness testimony that's massively improbable on our prior picture of the world, but in one case testimony can overcome the prior improbability, and in the other it supposedly can't. What exactly is doing the work there? Is it a difference in kind (violating a law of nature vs. merely being very unlikely), or just a difference of degree in improbability? If it's a difference in degree, what's the threshold, and could testimony in principle get across it? If it's a difference in kind, spell out what makes a law of nature special. Also, is there any way to make sense of the asymmetry without just saying 'we know the laws are laws'? I'd like you to separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on Hume's Of Miracles or the epistemology of testimony, and not present a contested reading as the consensus.
