In a seminar on political philosophy, I have been reading Hegel's Philosophy of Right, and the section that keeps troubling me is the treatment of war in the part on the state's external sovereignty, roughly around §324 in the standard numbering. Hegel there denies that war should be regarded as an absolute evil or as a merely contingent occurrence. He argues instead that war has a positive ethical significance: it prevents a people from sinking into the stagnant pursuit of private well-being, and by demanding that citizens risk their lives for the political whole, it makes explicit the subordination of the finite interests of civil society—property, personal safety, individual life—to the universal. On his account, war is one of the ways a state's unity is preserved and its citizens are reclaimed from the atomism of civil society.

I want to know whether that argument can survive the fact of thermonuclear war. When Hegel wrote, it was possible to think of war as a bounded though terrible instrument, one that states could undergo and survive, so that the ethical order on the other side of the conflict could be renewed or strengthened. A general nuclear exchange would seem to destroy that background picture: it would not be a contest between particular states from which the universal emerges, but a process that could annihilate the combatants, the non-combatant society, and much of the human future within hours. There would be no surviving political community left to be recalled to health, and the individual's death for the state would no longer have the state as its enduring end. So my question is: which precise premise of Hegel's argument does the nuclear development bear on, and can the argument be restated in a form that survives it—perhaps as an account of limited conventional war, or of the willingness to accept existential risk without actually bringing about civilizational destruction—or does the nuclear case show that the argument was always conditioned on a contingent historical form of warfare? I am not asking for a policy conclusion about nuclear weapons; I want to know what happens to the argument itself.
