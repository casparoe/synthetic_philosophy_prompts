I have no academic training in biology or philosophy, but I have thought about this for a long time on my own, and the view I have arrived at unsettles me. The view is this: judged purely in terms of the welfare of the individual animals that would otherwise have existed, the destruction or shrinking of natural habitats is very often a good thing, because it reduces the number of wild animals and thereby reduces the total amount of suffering. My reasoning is simple. Most wild animals produce enormous numbers of offspring, and only a small fraction survive to adulthood; most die young, and the causes of death, including starvation, predation, disease, cold, and thirst, look painful or frightening. If the average wild life contains more suffering than enjoyment, and I believe that for many small, short-lived animals it probably does, then a thriving habitat is not a refuge but a place where vast amounts of suffering are continuously produced. Clearing a forest or draining a marsh prevents a great many such lives from coming into existence, rather as contraception prevents births. On that narrow accounting, habitat destruction is more like preventing suffering than causing it.

I am not claiming that habitat destruction is good all things considered. I am setting aside human interests, the value of species continuing to exist, aesthetic losses, and effects on the climate, and I am asking only about the net welfare of the animals themselves. What I want is a map of the view's exposure: what would have to be true for it to be wrong, and which findings would merely force me to qualify it rather than abandon it. Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward. I can see some candidates myself. Is there good reason to think most wild lives are on balance worth living, and if so, which kinds of animals and which kinds of lives does that cover? Is my implicit comparison wrong because clearing a habitat usually crowds or displaces animals into worse conditions rather than reducing how many there are, and how could one tell in a particular case? Does the view commit me to conclusions I would find unacceptable, such as welcoming extinctions or treating the slow collapse of an ecosystem as a moral success, and are those commitments real or only apparent? Are there structural problems with the view, such as its reliance on a balance of pleasure and suffering across billions of lives that no one can actually measure, that should reduce my confidence even though they do not refute it?

I would also like an honest judgement on whether I have stated the view in a way that insulates it from any possible evidence, and whether such insulation is a serious defect in a view of this kind. Which of the points against it would a committed defender of the view concede as genuinely counting against it, and which would they dismiss? The finding I would personally find most persuasive against my view would be evidence that the animals that actually predominate in the habitats I have in mind typically have lives worth living, including the small and short-lived ones rather than only the large and long-lived ones. Please write the answer in British English.
