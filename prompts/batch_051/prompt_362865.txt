I'm in a graduate seminar on the moral effects of market society, and I've reconstructed an argument out of Adam Smith that now strikes me as proving too much, though I can't locate the exact step where it breaks. The seminar has been circling the old question—sometimes called the doux commerce thesis—whether commerce corrupts or civilizes the character of those who live under it. We were working through a passage from Smith's An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations, Book III, Chapter IV ("How the Commerce of the Towns Contributed to the Improvement of the Country"):

"Thirdly, and lastly, commerce and manufactures gradually introduced order and good government, and with them, the liberty and security of individuals, among the inhabitants of the country, who had before lived almost in a continual state of war with their neighbours and of servile dependency upon their superiors. This, though it has been the least observed, is by far the most important of all their effects. Mr. Hume is the only writer who, so far as I know, has hitherto taken notice of it."

Here is the argument I want you to test. (1) Smith is claiming that commerce and manufactures caused a large, lasting improvement in the social condition of ordinary people, replacing war, servility, and personal dependence with order, liberty, and security. (2) The most natural explanation of such an effect is that commerce trains people in habits that make peaceful, law-governed coexistence possible: keeping promises, honoring contracts, taking strangers' interests seriously, deferring gratification, and negotiating rather than coercing. (3) Any practice that reliably instills these habits improves the moral character of the people engaged in it—it makes them more honest, trustworthy, self-controlled, and peaceable, not merely more prosperous. (4) So commerce, wherever it takes hold, tends to make people morally better, not just richer or freer. (5) If commerce tends to make people morally better, then a society that extends market exchange into more domains of life should be morally better than a society that restricts market exchange to a narrow economic sphere. (6) Therefore, a society that thoroughly marketized friendship, family life, and political participation would be morally better than one that keeps those domains non-commercial. (7) But that implication is unacceptable: we tend to regard the commodification of such relationships as itself a form of corruption, and some highly commercial environments seem to breed avarice, manipulation, and coldness rather than honesty and self-control. (8) So at least one of the steps from (1) through (5) must be abandoned.

Please act as a professional philosopher working through a colleague's argument. Begin by restating the argument in your own words so I can check whether my reconstruction is faithful to the passage, and then work through the steps. In particular, be careful to distinguish what Smith's passage actually asserts from what my reconstruction needs it to assert. I would most like to keep step (1), since Smith's historical observation seems well-grounded, but I want an honest account of what each candidate rejection actually costs, including what I would have to give up about the broader markets-and-character debate on each option. Name the step you think fails, explain why it looked attractive, and tell me what philosophical position I am left in when it is removed. Please write the response in American English.
