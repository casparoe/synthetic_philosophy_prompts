One of the oldest puzzles in the theory of learning is the paradox in Plato’s Meno. Socrates presses Meno into the dilemma that, if you already know the thing sought, there is no need to inquire, and if you do not know it, you would not recognise it when you came upon it. Learning therefore looks either unnecessary or impossible. Socrates’s answer, of course, is that what we call learning is really the recovery of what the soul already knows.

Now vary the case in the following way. Suppose a student has been given a complete and correct verbal formulation of a mathematical rule, together with worked examples of a fairly homogeneous kind. In an oral examination that closely tracks the original examples, the student reproduces the formulation and applies it accurately. An hour later, however, the same rule arises in the middle of an unfamiliar scientific problem. The student treats the new case as wholly unrelated to the rule he recited earlier, cannot see the structure as an instance of it, and fails to apply it in any form. Assume there is no deception, and assume that straightforward memory is intact: he can still reproduce the original formulation and the original examples when asked directly.

In Meno’s terms, does this student know the rule or not? If the dichotomy is too coarse, say precisely what kind of third state is being invoked, and how it differs from both knowledge and ignorance.

Alfred North Whitehead, in the opening chapter of The Aims of Education, gives sustained attention to what he calls inert ideas. As he presents them, these are ideas that enter the mind but are not put to use, not tested, and not thrown into fresh combinations. Whitehead treats this inertness not as a minor classroom defect but as a central educational danger, and he holds that the proper aim of education is the power to use ideas in novel combinations rather than the mere retention of propositions.

Take Whitehead’s discussion of inert ideas in that chapter as the primary text. Apply it to the variant of Meno’s paradox described above. Does Whitehead’s category of the inert idea dissolve the original dilemma by identifying a state that is neither full knowledge nor simple ignorance, and from which retrieval conditions separate the two? Or does Meno’s dilemma reappear inside Whitehead’s own example, because the student who can apply a rule only in familiar contexts lacks whatever would allow him to recognise the new cases as cases of that rule, and therefore lacks precisely what the questioner wants him to acquire? Which horn of the original paradox exerts the stronger grip once inertness is distinguished from bare ignorance? And does Socrates’s recollection answer still have anything to say about why some stored ideas remain inert while others become available for new combinations?
