I'm putting together a film series for a philosophy discussion group and we're doing Kurosawa's Rashomon next month. I want to pair it with the common prior assumption — the idea in formal epistemology that if two rational agents start from the same prior and each conditions on their own private information, then once their posterior probabilities are common knowledge they can't rationally agree to disagree (Aumann's agreement theorem is the standard version of this). Rashomon gives us the bandit's, the wife's, the dead samurai's (via the medium), and the woodcutter's retellings of the same incident, and they don't just spin the same story differently; they contradict each other on who killed the samurai and why. The film never settles which, if any, is true.

Read the film through this lens, but keep me honest in both directions. Where does the common-prior framework genuinely illuminate the movie — what exactly has to give for the testimonies to diverge this badly, and which scenes show it? Where does the film resist or complicate the framework? For one thing, the witnesses' accounts are also performances with stakes: the bandit's image of himself as a swordsman, the wife's honor, the samurai's pride, and the woodcutter's theft of the dagger. So is the disagreement better explained by different interests than by different information or priors? Does the ending — the baby, the woodcutter's lie, the priest's restored faith — commit the film to there being or not being a disinterested truth to converge on? And does the movie let the viewer sit in the common-prior position, or does it implicate us in the same motivated seeing? I'd rather get a reading that says plainly where the analogy strains than one that makes the film a tidy illustration of a theorem. Please also separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on Aumann and common priors, so I'm not presenting a contested take as the consensus.
