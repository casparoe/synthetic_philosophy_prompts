I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mathematics, and in three weeks I'm leading a two-hour session for an interdisciplinary seminar on mathematical practice. The students are mostly mathematics graduate students plus a few philosophers; they know proof techniques but have never read Lakatos. The assigned topic is whether mathematics is quasi-empirical, and I want the session to turn on Lakatos's Proofs and Refutations rather than on general philosophy of science. I need a teaching plan, not an explanation itself. The plan should use the case of Euler's polyhedron formula, V − E + F = 2, as the carrying example, and should walk students through the sequence of moves Lakatos has his characters make: the initial proof via triangulation, the appearance of counterexamples, the distinction between local and global counterexamples, and the strategies students might propose for handling them, especially monster-barring and exception-barring. I'm less interested in whether Lakatos's history is accurate and more in how the method of proofs and refutations is supposed to show that definitions, proof-analysis, and the meaning of a theorem are shaped by failed proofs and counterexamples. What has to be in place before students can see this? In what order should I introduce the proof and then the counterexamples so the conceptual shift feels forced rather than optional? What misunderstandings should I expect in that room—for example, that Lakatos is just saying mathematicians make mistakes, or that quasi-empirical means experimental in the natural-science sense, or that a proof that admits counterexamples was never a proof at all—and at which point in the session should each be headed off? Finally, what question or short exercise should I use at the end to tell whether they have understood the difference between a body of propositions and a process of concept-stretching, rather than just learning a new label for bad proofs?
