Say a hospital brings in this rule. Any order from a physician that a nurse or a resident believes, in good faith, will cause serious harm to a patient can't be carried out until the recipient states the objection out loud, on the record, in front of the ordering physician and one other person: a second clinician one level up, who has to countersign for the order to go through. The countersigner can refuse to sign without penalty; the original recipient can be disciplined only if their objection is later shown to have been made in bad faith. Treat this as a mechanism rather than a moral question. What does it do to obedience and deference inside the hierarchy? Specifically, does it break the thing Milgram was after — the slide into an agentic state where you stop feeling like the author of what you do — or does it just move the pressure one rung up the latter? How would people game it: nurses lodging bogus objections to stall work they don't want, attendings quietly pre-clearing orders with the countersigner before writing them, the countersigner rubber-stamping because refusing would feel like accusing a colleague of malpractice? And what about the second-order effects of turning every near-miss into a documented judgment call? If it starts failing, which fixes might work — rotating countersigners, random audits, anonymized records — without the thing becoming pure paperwork?
