I am a teaching assistant marking a second-year undergraduate unit on instrumental rationality, and I would be grateful for a second judgement on one script. The question set on the exam was:

'Does the money pump argument show that intransitive preferences are irrational? Set out the argument carefully, distinguish it from Dutch book arguments, and assess whether its force depends on assumptions about how rational choice works over time. Conclude with a clear verdict.'

One student wrote the following:

'Intransitive preferences form a cycle: a person prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A. The money pump exploits exactly this structure. If the agent owns C, a trader can offer to exchange C for B for a small fee, which the agent accepts because she prefers B to C. The trader then offers to exchange B for A, which is accepted because she prefers A to B, and finally offers to exchange A for C, which is accepted because she prefers C to A. The agent is back holding C, but has paid three fees for the circuit. The force of the example is that each transaction is individually supported by one of her own pairwise preferences, yet the combination leaves her with less than she started with. The argument is often grouped with Dutch book arguments, and the parallel is reasonable: a Dutch book exploits incoherence among degrees of belief, while a money pump exploits incoherence among preferences, so the money pump is best understood as a Dutch book against preference rather than against credence. However, the argument only goes through because each pairwise comparison is treated as an isolated auction. The agent is pumped only if she chooses at each step by comparing the two options currently on offer and ignores the wider sequence. The argument therefore presupposes a myopic, step-by-step picture of agency. Once we think of rationality diachronically, the agent can avoid the pump by forming a resolute plan at the outset to refuse the first offer, having already seen that the whole sequence is merely a costly detour back to C. Resolute choice lets her stick to that plan without having to reopen the question when the trader later waves B in front of her. So the money pump shows only that agents who choose myopically are exploitable; it does not show that intransitive preferences themselves are irrational. The same lesson can be drawn from time-biased preferences. A person who prefers £100 now to £110 tomorrow, and also prefers £110 in a year and a day to £100 in a year, has an intransitive preference cycle over dated rewards, yet we do not ordinarily treat such discounting as irrational in the way the money pump argument would require.'
