I run my school's philosophy club, and in two weeks I am leading a forty-five-minute session for about a dozen members who have finished a short introductory unit on consequentialism and deontology. The topic is the paradox of deontology. Please do not explain the paradox to me; I need to be coached on how to teach it. I understand the paradox roughly as the tension between the thought that a deontological constraint exists because acts of a certain kind are bad and the thought that, if acts of that kind are bad, one ought to minimize them, even at the cost of performing one of them oneself. My audience can already roughly distinguish evaluating actions by their outcomes from evaluating them by their kind, but they have not met the agent-relative/agent-neutral distinction, and several of them currently suppose deontology is obviously right as long as it is not turned into a set of absolute rules.

I have a specific pedagogical view I want pressure-tested. I suspect killing cases are poorly suited to exhibiting the paradox, because intuitions about killing are too strong and too noisy to let the structure show. My plan is to carry the whole session with a single non-lethal case — breaking a promise in order to prevent five other people from breaking similar promises — which seems to me to isolate the oddity more cleanly. Assess that choice: does the promise case preserve the structure of the standard cases, or does it quietly change the subject and give the deontologist an escape that would not generalize? Plan the session around whichever example you judge best.

In the plan, give me the order in which the pieces must go in so that the paradox appears to them as an internal problem for deontology rather than as a cheap attack on it. I think the hinge is that they must first feel the pull of 'five broken promises would be worse than one' while also accepting a constraint against my breaking a promise; tell me whether that is the right hinge, whether something else has to be in place before it, and what has to be said for each of those to land.

Then give me a map of the misconceptions to expect. I anticipate at least these three: that the paradox shows deontology is self-contradictory, that agent-relative reasons are just sophisticated selfishness, and that a threshold view dissolves the problem cleanly. For each, say where in the sequence it should be preempted, used productively, or left standing, and add the misconceptions I have not anticipated. Finally, tell me what should be openly left out at this level, and end with a single question I can put to the room whose answer would show that they have understood the structure of the paradox rather than merely memorized a resolution.
