Last weekend, two friends and I were playing a role-playing game at my house. Jordan went through one of the settlements killing every NPC vendor "to see what would happen" and then took their inventories. Elena said that was wrong. The argument they had was better than I expected, and I still can't referee it.

(1) Jordan said nothing wrong had happened: an NPC has no experiences, so nothing can be bad for it, and where there is no possible victim there is no possible wrongdoing.

(2) Elena replied that victimhood is not the only ground of wrongdoing. Part of what makes an act wrong is what it expresses or cultivates: treating a realistic shopkeeper as disposable, for amusement, is a cruel and disrespectful stance, and the same stance toward a person would obviously be wrong. She said the badness is in the attitude, not the pixels.

(3) Jordan answered that this actually concedes his point. If the wrongness is in what the act does to me — my character, my attitude — then the NPC itself still does not matter morally; it is just a prop for self-improvement, like a punching bag. A punching bag is not wronged when you kick it, however it shapes you.

(4) Elena said he was running together two senses of "mattering." Something can be an object of moral concern without being a subject that can suffer, the way a promise made to someone now dead can still bind, or a beautiful building can be defaced without the building feeling anything. The shopkeeper's apparent interests are built into the game the player chose to enter, she said, and those interests give the player reasons the way game rules give reasons — real inside the game, even though the shopkeeper feels nothing.

(5) Jordan said that is exactly the problem. Games are one of the few places where we deliberately act on reasons we reject outside; if in-game cruelty is off the table on character grounds, the boundary between play and life collapses, and we would have to condemn most of the games we play.

Please go through the exchange move by move and say which specific claims in each numbered move are right and which are mistaken, and then identify what distinction or consideration both of them are missing. I'm less interested in who wins outright than in an adjudication of the actual arguments.
