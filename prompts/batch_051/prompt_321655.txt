I retired from a career in the natural sciences — molecular biology, then genomics — and have only recently begun reading systematically in the philosophy of language. I now sit on the editorial board of a journal that, like many in the biosciences, requires a data availability statement in every accepted manuscript. In practice these statements range from explicit undertakings ("Data will be made available upon request") to flat assertions about deposition in a repository, to sentences that appear to have been inserted merely to satisfy a template. The editorial office currently treats them all in the same way, which cannot be right.

I would like you to turn what speech-act theory knows about performative utterances into a reusable decision procedure my editorial assistants can run on any data availability statement. They are intelligent and careful but have no background in philosophy of language, and they must not be made to read Austin or Searle first. The procedure should classify each statement as one of:

(a) a binding commitment — something the authors have done, not merely said, which the journal and readers may legitimately hold them to;
(b) a factual assertion about the data — something true or false, to be checked rather than promised;
(c) empty boilerplate — an utterance that appears to promise or assert but, under the criteria of the theory, does neither; or
(d) underspecified — the procedure cannot settle the matter without an editorial judgment call.

The stakes: funders and readers now treat some of these statements as actionable, and treating a mere description as a promise, or a promise as mere courtesy, has caused retractions and disputes over reproducibility. The decisions are frequent — every accepted manuscript — and the assistant has about ten minutes per statement. The editor-in-chief will review every (d) classification, so the procedure should flag such cases clearly rather than force a decision.

For the procedure itself: give the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a particular classification, and which distinctions of speech-act theory must survive the simplification and which can be dropped. In particular, work through how the procedure should handle the performative/constative distinction, felicity conditions (including whether the authors are in a position to make the statement good), the role of uptake by the journal, the difference between what the sentence says and what it does, and indirect or hedged forms. Be explicit about where the procedure encodes a judgment call of the journal's rather than a result of the theory.

Then run the procedure on the following statements, giving the classification and the path through the procedure for each:

1. "The datasets generated during this study are available in the GenBank repository under accession number KP123456."
2. "Data will be made available upon reasonable request."
3. "The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request."
4. "Data sharing is not applicable to this article as no datasets were generated or analysed during the current study."
5. "Access to the underlying data may be granted to qualified researchers at the discretion of the sponsoring institution."
6. "The corresponding author will make the data available to researchers who obtain ethics approval and sign a data-use agreement."

Include at least one classification the journal will not like, and say plainly which cases the procedure cannot settle and why.

Begin your response directly with the procedure itself, with no preface, no restatement of my question, and no commentary before it; give the reasoning and the worked applications after the procedure.
