I chair the ethics committee at a mid-size teaching hospital. We meet every week and review contested treatment decisions in roughly an hour per case: refusals of recommended care, disputes about withdrawing or withholding treatment, and discharge plans the care team cannot settle internally. Our recommendation goes to the attending physician, who nearly always follows it, so the procedure has to be defensible even though the physician who acts on our recommendation will not have read the reasoning. The committee is mixed — physicians, nurses, a social worker, a chaplain — and none of us has formal training in philosophy.

We currently work from a loose set of principles — autonomy, beneficence, nonmaleficence, justice — that we mostly invoke after the fact to rationalize a decision we have already reached. I have been reading the particularist critique of that way of working, and I want to know whether particularism can be made usable in our room without quietly turning back into generalism.

Please write a reusable decision procedure for this committee that is honestly grounded in moral particularism. By that I mean the view that the possibility of moral judgment does not depend on a supply of codifiable principles, together with the holism of reasons: a feature that counts in favor of an action in one case may count against it, or not at all, in another, so no finite specification of the feature fixes its valence across contexts. The view can grant that a case's moral shape results from its particular features; what it denies is that this dependence can be captured by a finitely statable rule. It is not the weaker claim that principles merely have exceptions, and it is not the claim that anything goes. Mark explicitly where a feature's salience can be mistaken for its valence, where the procedure itself would be doing the work particularism says no principle can do, and where the procedure must hand the decision to a person because what remains is a judgment call rather than a fact. Be honest about where the procedure will misfire — especially where it will smuggle generalism back in under the language of default reasons, prima facie duties, or a ceteris paribus clause — and say clearly what the procedure cannot do.

The deliverable is the procedure itself, not three separate consultations. It should be an ordered sequence of questions, or a checklist, that we can actually run in the meeting, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, the point at which the procedure stops with a no, and the point at which it hands the decision to a person. Then apply it to these three cases: (1) a competent young woman with strong family support refuses amputation of a gangrenous limb, and her family supports her refusal; (2) a patient with advanced dementia, no advance directive, and no identifiable surrogate needs a feeding tube, and the care team is split over whether that prolongs death or supports life; (3) the family of a man who is awake but unable to speak insists he would refuse dialysis that he needs to survive, and the team doubts the family's account. In at least one of the three applications the procedure should return an answer the committee will be reluctant to adopt, and you should say plainly which step produces that answer. Where a case is under-specified, stop and say what further facts you would seek and which way they would cut, rather than inventing facts.
