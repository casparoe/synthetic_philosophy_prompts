I've ended up, in retirement, running a little reading group at are local library. The group is a mix: a few retired engineers, two high-school science teachers, a nurse, and a couple of graduate students in the humanities — no philosophers. We've been reading about how science actually works, and next session I want to tackle Feyerabend's anything-goes slogan. I spent my career as an organic chemist, and I'll be honest: my first reaction to the slogan was annoyance. It sounds like an excuse for sloppiness, as if a horoscope is as good as a controlled trial. But the more I read, the more I think he's onto something subtler and more interesting, and I'd like them to see past the slogan.

Here's what I need: a plan for the session, not the explanation itself. Give me the order of ideas — what conceptual distinction has to be in place before anything goes can mean anything other than no standards at all; where the slogan should enter the session so it doesn't sound silly; and what has to be kept firmly separated the whole way through. Then the carrying example: I was thinking the messy Galileo-and-the-telescope episode, but tell me if that's right. Whatever it is, say what it must have and must not have (for instance, it probably shouldn't be a case of plain fraud, and it shouldn't be so technical that they're lost in the optics). Then the misconceptions: the ones people walk in with — that Feyerabend was anti-science, that anything goes is a method in its own right, that he thought astrology and astronomy are equally credible — and the ones they might form halfway through — that science is just politics or force. Say at what point in the sequence each should be headed off, and what to say, briefly, to defuse it without getting derailed. Finally, tell me how I'll know at the end whether they've understood him rather than just swapped one caricature for another, and what I should openly refuse to try to cover at this level.

One more request: keep straight what is settled from what is genuinely contested. I've seen people claim Feyerabend meant one thing with total confidence while another equally serious person says he meant the opposite. If there are two or three live readings of the slogan, name them and tell me which one your plan is built on and why, and flag it when you're taking a side rather than reporting a consensus.
