I cover cognitive science and philosophy of mind for a general-audience science outlet, and I'm planning a piece on why some researchers treat the "hard problem" of consciousness as a kind of investigative dead end. A colleague passed me a long-form article on illusionism about phenomenal consciousness that has been circulating in my circle, and I want to lean on its framing—but I'm not sure which parts I'd be defending if a careful philosopher began to push back. The article summarizes the position this way:

"Illusionism is the view that phenomenal consciousness—the inner world of qualia, the 'what it's like' of experience—does not exist. When you see a red apple, there is no red quale anywhere in your brain; the redness you seem to feel is an introspective illusion, a useful fiction produced by brain processes whose real business is guiding attention, memory, and action. Illusionists do not deny that people are awake, that they see red, or that they can report their experiences; what they deny is that these reports pick out special, private, non-physical properties. What makes illusionism distinctive is that it treats the challenge not as explaining how physical processes produce qualia but as explaining why we are so powerfully convinced they exist. Critics reply that an illusion of a red experience requires a subject who has that red experience, so the view refutes itself; and for this reason, the piece concludes, illusionism remains an ingenious curiosity accepted by almost no one."

My instinct is that the paragraph is a competent popularization of a serious position but that its closing sociological claim is probably too strong, and I have a nagging sense that the self-refutation critique must have a standard rejoinder I simply cannot reconstruct. Please give me exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion, auditing this summary. Each point should isolate one specific claim or phrase in the paragraph, say whether it is accurate, a defensible simplification, or a subtle misrepresentation, and state what a careful illusionist—or a sympathetic critic—would say instead.
