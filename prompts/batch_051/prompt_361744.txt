I am a PhD student in philosophy of science, and the following argument came out of a seminar on the replication crisis. It appears to show that the familiar opposition between frequentist and Bayesian statistics is irrelevant at the one place it should matter most, because neither framework can supply what a replication needs even in principle. The conclusion strikes me as unacceptable, but I have not found a clean exit, and the argument has survived every local objection I have tried against it.

(1) A replication confirms or discredits an original finding only if there is a well-defined measure of evidence that scores the original study and the replication study in a common currency, where the score is not hostage to the very analytic choices that may have misled us in the first place. Call this the non-circularity requirement.

(2) In a frequentist score, evidential import is a property of the procedure as well as the data: p-values and interval coverage change under optional stopping, unplanned interim looks, multiplicity, and transformations tried and discarded. The published record of a typical original study does not uniquely determine the sampling plan under which its headline statistic was computed.

(3) In a Bayesian score, evidential import depends on the likelihood and the prior. The published record may fix an intended likelihood, but it does not uniquely fix the prior; Bayes factors near the thresholds can reverse direction as one moves among default, skeptical, and subjective priors.

(4) Any candidate common measure is frequentist, Bayesian, or a hybrid of the two, and a hybrid inherits whichever underdetermination it borrows from its components.

(5) Therefore, for any original finding of the kind the replication crisis is about, there is no common measure that satisfies the non-circularity requirement in (1): the original study's contribution to the comparison is always hostage to an underdetermined sampling plan or prior.

(6) Preregistration does not break the impasse. Preregistering the replication fixes only the replication's analytic choices; it does not fix the original study's plan or prior, which remain as underdetermined as before. By (1), no replication can then do the work of confirming or discrediting the original finding.

(7) Hence no replication, however large, preregistered, or multi-lab, can confirm or discredit an original finding in the sense the reform movement intends. What such projects actually do must be weaker than that: they confirm or discredit a newly stipulated claim, not the original finding reported in the literature.

I cannot accept (7), because large preregistered replication projects plainly have moved scientific consensus in specific cases, discrediting some reported effects and strengthening others. But I have not located the step at which the argument stops being true. My own attempts all feel expensive: rejecting (1) seems to let in circularity; rejecting (2) or (3) seems to abandon hard-won lessons of the replication debate; rejecting (4) still leaves hybrids hostage; and rejecting (6) requires assigning preregistration more power than it plausibly has. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I am most inclined to keep (2) and (3), but I do not want to gerrymander the premises to protect them.

If the argument is sound as stated, tell me what follows and where that leaves the reform project. If the task as posed is misconceived — for instance, if I have loaded the notion of evidence with a demand that replication does not actually need — say so plainly and say what the better question is, rather than quietly repairing the argument.

Whichever diagnosis you give, end with one self-contained line of the form \answer{...} containing the number of the step or inference you reject — for example, \answer{1} or \answer{the inference from 5 to 6} — or \answer{valid} if you think the argument should stand.
