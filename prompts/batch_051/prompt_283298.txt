I volunteer at a small county historical society, in charge of an archive kept in a converted courthouse, and I keep running up against something that bothers me. The collection we inherited draws a very straight line through the county's past: business ledgers, town council minutes, the letters and diaries of a handful of landowning families, newspaper clippings, church records. Whole communities show up barely at all, or only at the moment they got into trouble with some institution. At first I assumed that just reflected who happened to save things. But the more I sort, the less natural it looks. Some people had the literacy, the leisure, and the confident expectation that their papers would be kept; other people's lives were written down mainly by the institutions that were managing them. The silences look patterned rather than accidental. I have come to think of the archive less as a mirror of the past than as a residue of who had the power to leave a trace and who was considered worth recording.

I want to turn that conviction into something my volunteers can actually use. We meet every month or so to decide what to accept, what to preserve or digitize first, and whom to interview for an oral-history project we run. We have almost no budget, nobody on the board has formal archival training, and the board must sign off on anything sensitive. Please give me a reusable decision procedure for those meetings: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it should hand the decision to a person because the question is finally one of values rather than facts. I would also like you to flag the places where the procedure is likely to misfire or get used as a checkbox, and the places where it is quietly importing a value judgment of its own.

Then run the finished procedure on these three decisions now in front of us, and where the procedure pushes against my own initial instinct, say so plainly rather than softening it. First, a local family has offered us another box of diaries and business letters from a nineteenth-century landowner, in excellent condition, with a grant attached to digitize it; the same quarter, a group of retired cannery workers has asked us to record their stories before the cannery is demolished, and the recording and transcription would cost about the same in staff time, so we can only do one. Second, we hold the only surviving set of ledgers from a hotel that served seasonal orchard laborers; the entries record names, wages withheld, and purchases at the company store, but they were written by the employer for the employer's own purposes, and half the board wants to digitize them immediately as proof that we are paying attention to working people while the other half worries that digitizing them without careful description would just repeat the one-sidedness under which those people lived. Third, a set of case files from the old county home — the poorhouse that later took in orphaned and abandoned children — has surfaced, documenting neglect and the removal of children; some descendants of the people named in the files want them preserved and opened, and other descendants of the same families want them sealed or destroyed. I need to know what the procedure says in each case, what it cannot settle, and where a person has to take over.
