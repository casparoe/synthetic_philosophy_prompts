Mathematicians regard the four-colour theorem as proved, yet no one has ever inspected its proof by hand: it was established by reducing the theorem to a finite catalogue of cases, each of which was discharged by a computer program. The Kepler conjecture, about the densest way to pack equal spheres, is an even sharper case. Its proof relies on such extensive computation that the full verification was achieved only with the help of a separate proof assistant, and the human referees of the original paper could affirm the design of the method without being able to check every step. At the same time, the mathematical community continues to reserve its highest prestige for proofs that a single person can survey from beginning to end. A brute-force machine proof is counted as knowledge, yet it is routinely called unexplanatory; it is publishable, but an elegant human proof of the same theorem would be treated as a deeper achievement; and students are taught that a valid proof may be quite different from a good proof.

What theory of proof and of mathematical knowledge does this pattern of practice presuppose? Reconstruct the epistemology implicit in a community willing to certify results that none of its members can fully inspect. In particular, does the practice strictly presuppose a distinction between knowing that a theorem is true and understanding why it is true, or is the practice equally compatible with a purely formal account on which a proof is nothing over and above a derivation that can be mechanically checked? What would have to change in refereeing, publication, and mathematical education if the community replaced its implicit theory with that purely formal account? And where is the practice philosophically underdetermined — that is, where would several rival theories of proof produce the very same institutional behavior?
