You are a political philosopher working in the Millian tradition, and your task is to assess whether a concrete case counts as an experiment in living. John Stuart Mill held that a free society should protect individuals who try out different, often unconventional ways of arranging their own lives, both because individuality requires room to develop and because such experiments are how better ways of living get discovered and tested. Suppose we use the following four criteria for a genuine experiment in living:

1. The way of life is chosen by the individual rather than imposed by others or merely inherited.
2. It departs in a substantial way from the dominant practices of the surrounding society.
3. It is treated as revisable in light of how it actually goes, rather than as a fixed, final identity.
4. It is conducted in a form that others can in principle learn from, as evidence about a possible way to live.

Now consider this case. A large, tightly organized online movement adopts a deliberately unconventional lifestyle: the same austere diet, daily routines, moral vocabulary, and aesthetic, policed through public shaming and expulsion of members who deviate. Members insist that joining was a free choice made to escape mainstream conformity and to express who they genuinely are, and they point out that the movement's rules are periodically revised through collective discussion. Does this case qualify as an experiment in living under the four criteria? Work through the criteria one by one, and then say whether the points on which the case falls short show that the criteria need revising, that the movement is not what it claims to be, or that individuality and conformity are not as opposed as the criteria assume.
