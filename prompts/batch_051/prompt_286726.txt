I'm a high school student, and in my school's philosophy club we have been discussing consequentialism. I found myself stuck on a version of an objection that I've seen called the cluelessness objection. As I understand it, the thought is this. An ordinary choice — whether to buy coffee at one shop rather than another, whether to spend an extra hour studying, whether to take a different route home — sets off a causal chain whose long-run effects fan out across everyone who comes later. A small difference now can change which people meet, which jobs get filled, which children are eventually born, and so on, so the resulting world could be better or worse in ways that no one can realistically foresee. If that is right, then a consequentialist seems to be told to perform whichever action makes the world best while being unable to know which action that is. The objection is usually pressed against consequentialism as a practical decision procedure, and sometimes against it as an account of what makes an action right.

The objection was developed at a time when this kind of ignorance looked like a permanent fact of the human situation. Since then, however, we have acquired massive behavioral datasets, cheap large-scale randomized experiments, and machine-learning systems trained to forecast things like disease spread, consumer behavior, labor-market shifts, and other social outcomes. Suppose, for the sake of argument, that such systems become genuinely good at tracing how present choices propagate into medium- and long-run differences — not perfectly, of course, but vastly better than an unaided human could manage. My question is whether that development would actually defuse the cluelessness objection, or whether the objection would survive in a repaired form. I am less interested in how good today's models happen to be than in the conceptual issue. Which premise of the original argument is the improved forecasting power supposed to undercut? And if the argument survives, does it survive by moving the problem elsewhere — for example, from plain empirical ignorance to a question about risk, about which probabilities are good enough, or about the point at which prediction must stop and moral judgment begin?
