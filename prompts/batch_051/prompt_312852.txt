I'm a software engineer, and I spend a lot of my working life in version control. Recently I reread Borges's story "The Garden of Forking Paths," and I was struck by how closely the picture of time it describes resembles the way git models the history of a codebase.

In the story, time is not a single line. At any moment the path can fork, and the unchosen alternatives are treated not as merely possible but as equally real: each fork produces its own timeline, and the result is a labyrinth of adjacent histories that sometimes run parallel and sometimes meet. Structurally, that is close to what I work with daily. Git's object graph is a directed acyclic graph of immutable commits. Branches are only pointers into that graph. A merge commit is a node with more than one parent, which is precisely a point where two histories that had diverged meet again. HEAD names the commit that counts as "now," and because I can move it backward and inspect the repository exactly as it was, the past keeps existing as long as some reference points to it. Even the familiar choice between merging and rebasing sounds to me like two philosophies of time: one preserves the record of every dead end; the other rewrites the timeline so that the route actually taken looks as though it had always been the only one.

I cannot tell whether this is a genuine correspondence, a weak analogy riding on the shared word "branch," or a confusion between a tool for holding alternative code histories and a claim about what time is like. I want someone to stress-test it. What exactly would the mapping be between Borges's temporal forkings and git's commit graph? For a result or a distinction to transfer from one domain to the other, what would have to be true on both sides? And what does the parallel wrongly predict? For example, git makes time effectively reversible, and it lets two histories be reunited through a three-way merge against a common ancestor; Borges's labyrinth is continuous, its meetings are not conflict resolutions by an agent, and it does not seem to delete unreferenced pasts the way git's garbage collector does. Are those fixable mismatches, or do they show that the parallel is only superficial?
