I teach an intro ethics course, and next week we're doing Nozick's experience machine. A student in office hours yesterday pushed on something I hadn't thought through, and I want to see how the standard analysis handles it. The usual thought experiment says you'd be plugged into a machine that gives you any experiences you want, and life inside is indistinguishable from the real thing. Nozick's point is that many of us still wouldn't plug in, because we want to do things rather than merely have the experience of doing them, we want to be a certain kind of person, and we want contact with a deeper reality rather than something merely human-made.

Here's the variation I want worked through. Suppose the machine is different in two ways. First, it cannot edit your memories, beliefs, or character: you go in as yourself and you stay yourself, with your outside knowledge intact enough that you will always know, at some level, that you are plugged in. Second, the machine's library is finite. Because your nervous system has only finitely many discriminable states, the machine can produce only so many genuinely distinct experiences; if you stay plugged in long enough, the menu starts repeating, and you know this from the start because the operators hand you the complete catolog (it is large but bounded) before you decide. So the choice isn't between real life and an endless buffet of anything; it's between real life and a large but exhaustible set of virtual experiences, with no editing of who you are.

I'm interested in whether the anti-machine verdict gets stronger, weaker, or just different under these changed assumptions. Please go through Nozick's three considerations one by one: do they all still apply, do any of them lose force or switch sides, and does the finite menu create an objection that isn't reducible to one of the three? And here's the tricky part I want your take on: if a finite mind in the real world also faces an exhaustible supply of genuinely novel experiences, then is the exhaustibility worry an argument against plugging in, or does it generalize in a way that undercuts the usual contrast between the machine and ordinary life? Please answer in American English.
