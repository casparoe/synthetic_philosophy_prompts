I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I currently work on a self-paced learning application for children aged seven to twelve. Four features of the product concern me, and I would like you to assess them against the philosophy of education I have been reading in my spare time, namely John Locke's. The features are: (a) a child receives points immediately after completing each exercise; (b) the application maintains a daily-use streak and sends reminders designed to make the child uneasy about letting the streak lapse; (c) a class-wide leaderboard ranks children publicly by accumulated points; and (d) an adaptive difficulty controller paces exercises to keep children in a state of sustained engagement, without any adult tutor or parent in the loop. Our marketing calls the product "learning through play." Please give me a careful, reasoned prediction of what Locke would say about each of these four features if he could examine the product, working from the positions he actually defends: that children learn best when learning is made a kind of recreation rather than a task; that habits are settled early through repeated practice rather than through precept; that the right rewards are esteem, praise, and reputation rather than the gratification of appetite through bodily pleasure or material gain; that a tutor matters more than any method because a tutor studies the particular character of the child; and that the end of education is a mind able to deny its own appetites when reason does not authorize their satisfaction. Where these commitments appear to pull in opposite directions—for instance, where something looks like play but is not freely chosen, or where a reward seems to trade on esteem but is engineered to be habit-forming—say so explicitly and explain which commitment you think would dominate in Locke's appraisal. Keep the focus on the four features rather than on general observations about his educational philosophy, and do not fall back on the familiar claim about the mind as a blank slate.
