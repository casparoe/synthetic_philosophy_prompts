Since retiring from a long career in the natural sciences, I have been working through political philosophy and administrative theory, and I find myself drawn to questions about the machinery of accountability rather than its first principles. Recently a friend who spent years in administrative law described a proposal for reforming government confidentiality, and I would like an analysis of how it would actually operate, not a judgment about whether the underlying values are right. The proposal is as follows. Every confidentiality obligation that a government body imposes on an employee, contractor, or grantee must be registered with an independent Office of Information Trusteeship as a secrecy instrument. Registration must identify the specific material covered, state the concrete harm that disclosure would cause, and set an expiration date no more than two years after filing. An instrument may be renewed only by a fresh written finding from the Office, issued after an adversarial proceeding at which an advocate appointed to argue against renewal may contest the agency's claim of continuing harm, although portions of the proceeding may be closed or redacted. Every instrument carries an irrevocable carve-out: a covered person may always disclose the material to the Office itself, and a good-faith disclosure to the Office is immune from civil, criminal, or employment penalty even if the instrument is later upheld. The Office may, on petition or on its own initiative, void an instrument it finds to protect material withheld for embarrassment, convenience, or illegality rather than genuine harm; an agency whose instrument is voided must pay the petitioner's costs from its own budget. Finally, the Office must publish, after each instrument lapses, a redacted abstract of what was concealed and why, along with semiannual statistics on registrations, renewals, challenges, and voids.

Treat this strictly as a mechanism. What incentives does it create for the agencies that impose secrecy, for the people bound by the instruments, for the trustees, and for outside actors such as journalists, contractors, and litigants? How would the system be gamed — through boilerplate registration, through periodic reclassification or relabeling of restrictions so they fall outside the definition of an instrument, through agencies avoiding the creation of records in the first place, or through subtler routes I have not thought of? What second-order effects would follow for candor inside government, for the volume and character of internal records, and for the public's ability to interpret the Office's redacted abstracts? I am especially interested in which failure modes a careful amendment could repair and which are intrinsic to the design.
