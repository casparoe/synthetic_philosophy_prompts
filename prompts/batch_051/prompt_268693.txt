I'm a high school student, and my serious thinking about fiction happens in fanfiction communities and at the tabletop roleplaying table rather than in a philosophy classroom. The vocabulary I actually use for reasoning about stories is built out of canon, headcanon, alternate universe (AU), suspension of disbelief, worldbuilding, Word of God, and player buy-in. I recently read a plain-language account of a philosophical puzzle called imaginative resistance, and I want you to translate the puzzle and the main ways philosophers diagnose it into my vocabulary, then tell me what the translation cannot carry.

The puzzle, as I understand it, is this. Readers accept wild descriptive departures from reality without much friction: a story can stipulate that dragons exist or that faster-than-light travel is routine, and we simply grant it as true in the fiction. But when a story stipulates a moral departure, readers tend to balk. The example the philosopher used was a story that asks us to accept, as true in the fiction, that a mother did the right and praiseworthy thing when she killed her infant because it was a girl. The author's say-so seems sufficient to make descriptive facts true in the fiction, but not sufficient to make moral claims true in the fiction. So the question is why an author's authority over what is true in a story runs out precisely when it touches morality.

Philosophers apparently split on what kind of failure this is. Some treat it as an unwillingness: we are being invited to occupy a perspective that endorses something morally repugnant, and we refuse, even in a game of make-believe. Others treat it as an inability: the fiction is asking us to do something we cannot coherently do, closer to being asked to imagine a contradiction than to being asked to imagine a dragon. I want both diagnoses translated, not just the phenomenon itself.

Please restate the puzzle, and each of those two diagnoses, systematically in the vocabulary of canon, headcanon, AUs, suspension of disbelief, and player buy-in. I am asking for a translation, not an analogy: for each element, tell me whether it maps cleanly onto something fanfiction or tabletop communities already say, maps only with distortion, or has no counterpart at all. For example, does the descriptive case just amount to the ordinary practice of accepting an AU premise, and does the moral case amount to a failure of player buy-in, a refusal to adopt a headcanon, or something else? Keep the translation honest: if the fit is loose, do not quietly tighten it and call the puzzle dissolved.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the philosophers' debate survive the move into my vocabulary, and which parts cannot be said at all in it? I want a committed answer, not a menu of readings: take a definite position on which diagnosis is better served by the translation, and then tell me whether the residue shows that fanfiction and tabletop vocabulary is missing something real, or that the philosophers have built the puzzle in a misleading vocabulary. In particular, I want to know whether my suspicion that this is just a familiar dispute about tags, content warnings, and consent is right, or whether the philosophical issue is something my vocabulary systematically cannot see.
