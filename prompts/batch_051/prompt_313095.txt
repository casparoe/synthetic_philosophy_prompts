I've been thinking through what it means for people to have beliefs and desires, and I've ended up with a set of commitments that will not settle into a coherent picture. I don't have a stake in any one of them, and I'd like someone to audit the whole set. Here is where I am:

(1) The mind is nothing over and above the brain. There is no extra ingredient beyond what the brain does.

(2) Beliefs and desires, as we ordinarily speak of them, are not literally real. There is nothing in the brain that answers to a belief or a desire in the way our everyday talk suggests.

(3) Even so, predicting people by attributing beliefs and desires to them works strikingly well — better than any alternative I know. I could not give up that practice and still make sense of other people.

(4) Predictive success is a reason to think a way of talking is latching onto something real. If a description keeps producing correct predictions, that is evidence in favor of the things it names.

(5) But science has sometimes discarded concepts that were once predictively useful — phlogiston is my standard example — so usefulness alone cannot settle the question of what exists.

(6) Yet science has also kept the useful core of an old description by reinterpreting it in a better framework, as when heat survived as the motion of molecules. So being part of a superseded way of thinking does not by itself force a concept to be thrown out.

Which of these are genuinely in tension, which only look that way, and how severe are the actual conflicts? I would like the tensions ranked by severity, not listed as a flat set. For each genuine tension, tell me the cheapest adjustment and what it costs a person who keeps the rest. If the only real repairs amount to giving up one of these commitments, tell me which one is least costly to surrender, and why.
