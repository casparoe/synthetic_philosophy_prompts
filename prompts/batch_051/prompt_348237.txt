In drug regulation (and a lot of adjacent epidemiology), a giant, carefully adjusted observational study gets treated as giving little or no evidence that prescribing the drug would lower the risk of a serious clinical outcome, while the same people will treat a tiny randomized trial of the same drug as real evidence of exactly that. Yet the broader institutional setup is perfectly comfortable using observational data for safety signals and surrogate endpoints. I'm trying to figure out what theory of causation and evidence this uneven pattern presupposes. Does it actually require something like a sharp distinction between merely observing an association and performing an intervention, or would a more modest view about confounding and bias explain it just as well? And where is the practice underdetermined between those options? Finally, what would have to change, institutionally and conceptually, if the agency began treating an observational study plus an explicitely argued causal diagram (including unmeasured confounders) as at least some evidence of the drug's effect on a hard outcome?
