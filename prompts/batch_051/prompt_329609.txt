I've been in this EU law course where the instructor has one move for every argument about democratic deficit, sovereignty, or treaty change, and I can't tell if it's brilliant or just a metaphor with a lot of confidence. He calls it the bicycle theory of integration: the Union is like a bicycle—it only stays upright while its moving forward, and the treaty's ever-closer union language isn't just an aspiration but a stability condition. Stop pedalling, he says, and the machine doesn't come to rest—it wobbles and falls, and the only question is which side it lands on. On his telling, repatriating powers to national parliaments to fix the democratic deficit gets it backwards: once you start handing sovereignty back to national capitals, you slow the pedalling, the wobble sets in, and you're more likely to get dissolution than democracy. Opt-outs, two-speed arrangements, and treaty referendums that stall ratification are just ways of falling over in slow motion. The only route to legitimate government, he concludes, is to keep pushing forward—a real European demos, more parliamentary control at the centre, more majority voting—rather than pulling back toward the member states.

I keep feeling the analogy is doing a lot of work, but I want to know exactly how much it can carry. Could you audit it? I'd like a correspondence table first—what in the EU plays the role of the rider, the pedalling, the forward motion, the balance, the wobble, the crash, and the road—then the disanalogies, separating the ones that actually matter for his conclusion from the ones that don't. Also say what the image quietly imports about the direction of history, about what counts as falling, about who gets hurt in a fall, and about whether momentum is self-sustaining. Finally: once the disanalogies are on the table, does his conclusion—that more integration is the only answer to the democratic deficit—survive, or was the bicycle carrying the whole argument?
