Draft the joint statement that a defender of primitive Jeffrey conditionalization and a defender of the certainty-reduction view of uncertain evidence would co-sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on how ideally rational agents should update on evidence that is less than fully certain. Address the document to a formal-epistemology reading group and write it as the common position paper both parties can put their names to.

Both parties accept the following background. An agent has a probability function P over a Boolean algebra of possible states. If she learns with certainty that E obtains, her new probability should be P(· given E), with P(E) > 0. Jeffrey conditionalization, henceforth JC, is the proposed rule for the case in which experience does not make any proposition certain but instead reallocates probability across a finite partition {E1, ..., En}. Relative to that partition, the experience imposes new probabilities q1, ..., qn on the cells, with each q_i at least 0 and the q_i summing to 1. The agent's new probability is P′(·) = Σ_i q_i P(· given E_i), with the rigidity condition P′(· given E_i) = P(· given E_i) for every cell with q_i > 0. Ordinary conditionalization is the special case in which a single q_i equals 1 and the rest equal 0.

The first party holds that JC is an irreducible norm of ideal rationality: uncertain evidence is not always evidence for a proposition, the q_i are the content of the experience itself rather than a posterior belief about what was learned, and rigidity is a constitutive feature of the partition's evidential relevance. The second party holds that every legitimate Jeffrey shift is an ordinary conditionalization in a richer state space — standardly on a proposition about the agent's own experiential or evidential state — and that JC is a derived bookkeeping rule for a coarse-grained algebra, correct only insofar as it summarizes a strict update. Both parties acknowledge that for a wide class of shifts a refined-space representation exists; they disagree about what, if anything, that shows.

Produce the document in three parts.

Part 1 is a numbered list of agreed claims. They should be precise and non-vacuous, each stated so that a reader can see exactly what is being claimed. They must cover at least: the formalism itself and the relation between JC and the special case of ordinary conditioning; what the q_i and the partition are and are not being claimed to represent, in wording acceptable to both authors; the significance they both attach to the rigidity condition; what each author is not thereby committed to; and the points about diachronic coherence, accuracy, and composition that the parties can jointly sign — including the fact, which both verify, that two successive Jeffrey shifts with different partitions need not collapse into a single Jeffrey shift, while two successive ordinary conditionalizations do collapse — stated with the caveats each party requires.

Part 2 is a disagreements table. For each row: the question at issue; the first author's position phrased so that the first author would accept it; the second author's position phrased so that the second author would accept it; and a verdict on whether the row is substantive or partly verbal. Include at least these rows: whether the q_i are themselves the evidential content or a summary of a stricter update; whether rigidity is a rational constraint, a stipulation of what the evidence targets, or an empirical claim; whether the refined-space representation is unique or canonical enough to be explanatory; and whether JC's failure to compose is a defect of the rule or a consequence of evidence being partition-relative.

Part 3 identifies three or four cruxes: sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one author. For each crux, give a concrete case or argument that both authors agree bears on it, and record in advance, in wording each author would accept, what each author predicts the case or argument shows — phrased so that if the prediction is disconfirmed as described, that author is committed to revising their position. At least one crux must concern the handling of a specific case: a radiologist examining a smudged x-ray raises her credence that a fracture is present from 0.1 to 0.6 without making any fracture-relevant proposition certain, and the smudge itself is perceived only as a graded pattern. For that case, both authors must say whether the shift is admissible as an instance of uncertain evidence, and if so, what exactly the agent's new evidence is.

Finally, state which parts of the overall disagreement, if any, are verbal rather than substantive — for example, disagreements about the word evidence or about what must hold with certainty — and restate the residual substantive disagreement in terms both parties accept.
