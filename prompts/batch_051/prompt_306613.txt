I have spent the past few weeks working through the back-and-forth over the inefficacy objection as applied to meat consumption, and I keep tripping over a premise the standard reply quietly depends on. The objection, in its simplest form, says that the chicken is already dead and the supplier's next order depends on aggregate demand rather than on my single purchase, so my individual refusal to buy makes no difference. The familiar expected-value reply concedes that point but insists that production is lumpy: hatcheries, growers, abattoirs and supermarkets order in roughly fixed batches, and although my one purchase will almost never be the one that tips the next order down by a batch, the small probability of being pivotal multiplied by the size of the batch leaves me with an ex ante expected effect close to one chicken. That reply was built for a supply chain made of discrete, chunky ordering decisions separated by long lead times.

Now suppose the much-discussed just-in-time future actually arrives. Producers run continuously updated demand forecasts, restock through smaller and more frequent deliveries, and feed each scanned sale back into the next replenishment cycle within hours, so the quantity ordered adjusts smoothly rather than in units of a thousand chickens. What I want is not a verdict on whether one should stop eating meat. I want to know whether the lumpiness-based expected-value reply survives in that environment. Which premise does the new development actually undercut: only the empirical premise about discrete batches, or also the deeper claim that a person's obligation in a collective harm can be grounded in a small probability of making a pivotal contribution? If the familiar version of the reply fails, is there a repaired version that still does philosophical work, or does the development hand the advantage back to the inefficacy objection, perhaps by turning the problem into one about imperceptible contributions that somehow add up to a perceptible harm?

I would prefer the answer in continuous prose only, as in an email: no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables.
