Here's something that's been bugging me. People seem to find it almost self-evident that when a for-profit company runs a territory—raises taxes, runs courts, keeps an army—that's a scandal or at least a creepy anomaly. The East India Company is basically a byword for exactly that. But when a state does the exact same things we just call it governance, even though states are themselves often corporate creatures: they have charters, a seperate legal personality, they can own property, sue and be sued. The Hudson's Bay Company literally governed Rupert's Land for two centuries under a royal charter, and the Massachusetts Bay Colony was a chartered joint-stock company that became a government. So the asymmetry is this: we treat corporate sovereignty as suspect but state sovereignty as natural, even when the corporate form is doing a lot of the work in both. Is there a real difference that justifies that, or are we just letting the words "public" and "private" do the thinking for us? I want a committed answer. Either build the best case you can that the asymmetry tracks something genuine—profit motive, accountability, permanence, membership, something else—or conclude that it can't be justified and say what we should give up. Should we get more nervous about state sovereignty, or less nervous about company rule, or both? Test your line against a few edge cases: a company town like Pullman, a modern charter city, a fully private city, and maybe the old East India Company territories again. Don't just lay out the menu; tell me which way you'd come down and why.
