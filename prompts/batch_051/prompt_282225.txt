At a barbecue a couple weeks ago, two friends of mine spent an hour arguing about a startup that offers "graded uploading." The deal is: a surgeon replaces one neuron at a time with a silicon chip that's certified to be input-output identical to the cell it replaces. You're awake the whole time, and they pause after every swap to ask if anything feels different. Nobody has ever said it does. My friends were arguing about whether the person sitting up at the end is still the original person or just a very good copy. I stayed out of it because I honestly couldn't tell who's argument was better. Here's the exchange as I remember it.

(1) Dana said she'd do it tomorrow. If each chip does exactly what the neuron it replaced did, then no single swap changes anything that matters. And if each individual swap leaves you the same person, the whole chain leaves you the same person—identity is transitive. She added that the system never goes down during the procedure, so there's no gap where the old person could be said to leave and a new one show up.

(2) Tom said that was a sorites in a lab coat. The fact that no step is the step where you die doesn't mean you make it out the other end; the Ship of Theseus is still the original after one plank is swapped and isn't after ten thousand, even though no particular plank did the job. And the "lights stay on" point can't carry much weight, because the lights go out every night when Dana sleeps and she doesn't think she dies at bedtime.

(3) Dana admitted the "lights stay on" thing wasn't doing the real work. She said what matters for personal identity is psychological continuity—memories, plans, character—and that's realized by the network as a whole, not by particular cells. The chips keep the network's function, so from the inside nothing changes. She also said that if Tom insists on preserving the original material, then no adult is identical with the child they were, since the body has already replaced most of its matter.

(4) Tom said psychological continuity is too cheap, because you can get it without surviving. A machine that scanned your brain and printed the same pattern into a fresh brain in another room would produce a being psychologically continuous with you—same memories, same plans—but it would obviously be a copy, not you. So what gradual uploading buys you is not survival but a very confident later claimant. And the guy on the table saying "still me" is exactly what a copy would say, so that's evidence for neither side. He also said Dana's turnover analogy was weak: neurons are the cells you mostly keep for life, so swapping them out isn't like ordinary metabolism.

(5) Dana said the scanner example is off because it produces another thing somewhere else, whereas gradual uploading transforms the same thing in place, the way a river changes its water or a caterpillar becomes a butterfly. If one chip doesn't kill you—and Tom hasn't given any reason it does—then either identity fails at some definite point during the procedure, or it never fails. Since any choice of point would be arbitrary, Tom's position has no boundary. She'd rather bet on the procedure than on a view that can't say when she'd stop being her.

(6) Tom said the boundary problem doesn't save her, because vague distinctions can have wildly different endpoints without any sharp line between them—a full head of hair and a bald head differ even though no single hair makes the difference. So he doesn't have to name the neuron; he just has to say the thing at the end is too far from the original to be it. He added that the river and the caterpillar just beg the question, since whether those things survive their changes is exactly the kind of thing in dispute.

Tom's last line was something about a fine counterfeit being the one that says "I am the genuine article," and then the burgers where ready, so I never heard Dana's answer.
