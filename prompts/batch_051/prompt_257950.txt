In a couple weeks I'm giving a talk at a joint philosophy–sociology workshop on moral cultures, and I want a prep sheet for the Q&A rather than a polished defense. The talk defends the unfashionable claim that victimhood culture isn't a moral regression and that, stricly speaking, none of the three moral cultures — honor, dignity, victimhood — is morally superior to the others overall. My line is that each is a coherent grammar for handling offenses, matched to a different enforcement environment: honor where retaliation and reputation among rough equals do the work, dignity where you can outsource punishment to a credible impartial authority (courts, HR, the liberal state), and victimhood where enforcement falls to a distributed third-party audience — a platform, a campus, a workplace grapevine — whose attention and sympathy become the scarce resource. I'm not saying victimhood culture is good; I'm saying its signature pathologies (pile-ons, status races over who's most wounded, public confession as leverage) are mostly pathologies of the audience environment, not of the underlying grammar, and that dignity culture looked just as degenerate from inside an honor culture as victimhood culture looks from inside a dignity culture. The quick argument: what counts as a harm worth mobilizing over is partly constituted by the culture itself, so rankings that 'obviously' put dignity above honor are often smuggling in dignity's own standards; and the supposed direction of moral progress might largely be the direction of shifting enforcement technology.

The audience is about forty people. Half are moral philosophers, mostly liberal, who think dignity culture just is the moral achievement of modern liberal societies and will hear my thesis as slipperiness about honor killings or online harassment. There's a sociologist who did fieldwork in rural Sicily and thinks the honor/dignity/victimhood trichotomy is a crude stereotype her informants wouldn't recognize. And there's a cluster of grad students who work on online conflict, already like the victimhood-culture framework, but think my 'blame the platform, not the grammar' move lets the whole thing off too easy. Walk me through the questions I'll actually get, in roughly the order they'll come and phrased the way those people would phrase them: which objections have real force, what should I concede up front, where is my own position weakest and should be defended rather then hoped away, and what's the one reply that has to land for the talk not to fall apart?
