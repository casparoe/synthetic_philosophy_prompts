I write evals and wrapper code around agentic models, and I've landed on a safety view I want stress-tested: for systems we're actually shipping in the next few years, you should build as if the model may be misaligned, and most of the practical safety budget should go to control rather than alignment. By control I mean measures that would still work if the model were actively trying to get one past you: sandboxing, restricting tools and side effects, monitoring outputs with a second model you can't fully trust either (untrusted monitoring), keeping a human in the loop for irreversible actions, and red-teaming for any path to real damage. By alignment I mean trying to make the model itself not want to misbehave. I'm not saying alignment is impossible, just that it carries less of the near-term load.

What would actually count against this view? I want the evidence, arguments, and failure modes that should move me, sorted from decisive to merely awkward — not just "alignment might work" but specific things that, if I saw them, should make me re-weight toward alignment-first. What does my view predict that the alignment-first view doesn't? Where would a defender of control-first honestly concede ground rather than dismiss it? And does the view end up unfalsifiable in practice, because every case where a model behaves well can be waved off as "not a real test"? Oh, and please write the answer in British English.
