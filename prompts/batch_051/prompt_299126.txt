I've been a philosophy person slowly working through the algorithmic fairness literature, and I keep getting stuck on the impossibility results. The one I can't shake is this: for a risk score, you generally can't have all three of (a) calibration — the score means the same thing across groups, so a 6 is a 6 is a 6 — (b) balance for the positive class, meaning people who do go on to have the outcome get the same average score regardless of group, and (c) balance for the negative class, meaning people who don't have the outcome get the same average score regardless of group — unless the groups have equal base rates or your score is already effectively a perfect predictor. My colleague who works on pretrial stuff says that's just math, and the right response is to stop hunting for the one true definition of fairness and instead make the tradeoff explicit in each setting. My other friend thinks the result is overhyped becuase the real fix is to change what you're predicting, or fix the upstream data, not to accept a tradeoff between group-level error rates.

Before I pick a side, I want the desiderata: what does any adequate account of how to respond to these impossibility results have to deliver? I mean desiderata for a position or theory in this debate, not for a fairness metric itself. For each one, give the concrete case or consideration that puts it on the list — not a slogan — and say whether it's negotiable or non-negotiable. If you think some of them can't be jointly satisfied, flag the pair and say which one breaks, and mark which single desideratum you think does the most work excluding bad answers. Give me exactly five, numbered, each no more than two sentences, with no intro or conclusion.
