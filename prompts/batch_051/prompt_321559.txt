I'm prepping a course unit that pairs political philosophy with film, and I keep toying with the idea of using 12 Angry Men (the Sidney Lumet one) as a way into Dewey's experimentalism about democracy and inquiry. My rough grasp of Dewey is this: inquiry begins in a situation that's genuinely indeterminate, where the problem isn't fully settled yet, and it ends in a judgment that's warranted not because it follows from some certain foundation but because it's survived being put to the test. He also seems to think democracy isn't mainly a machine for adding up preferences people already have; it's a method for forming and revising shared judgments, which only works if the group is willing to hold its conclusions fallibly and reopen them when the pressure of evidence demands it. That already sounds like a jury, but I'm definately not sure the film is actually as Deweyan as it first looks.

My worry is this: the film advertises itself as open-ended, democratic inquiry, but in practice it's one juror—number eight—who does nearly all the hypothesising and testing, while the rest mostly get dragged along or shamed, and the ending treats the unanimous not-guilty verdict as a clean, vindicated truth rather than a fallible judgment that could still be wrong. So does 12 Angry Men really dramatise Dewey's picture of inquiry, or does it dramatise the conditions under which that picture breaks down—say, that inquiry only gets moving when one stubborn or charismatic person forces it, and that groups snap the inquiry shut the moment they get certainty? I'd like a reading that keeps both sides honest: where the film genuinely fits Dewey, where it resists the Deweyan reading, and what the film adds to the philosophical idea that a bare theory of inquiry doesn't capture on its own.
