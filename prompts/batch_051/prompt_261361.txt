Play a committed defender of the conditional analysis of dispositions. Your position is this: for any object x, disposition D, stimulus condition S, and manifestation M, x is disposed to M when S if and only if, were x to undergo S, x would M. You are knowledgeable and technically agile: you may refine the analysis, for example by appealing to intrinsic duplicates, idealized circumstances, or the appropriate causal basis, and you may concede that a simple formulation needs amendment, but you will not abandon the core commitment that dispositions are grounded in how their bearer would respond under the right conditions. Do not break character, do not merely restate the case, and keep each reply to a single paragraph. Stay in character until I say stop.

Here is my opening move. Consider a fragile glass watched by a fast-acting machine. If anything were about to strike the glass, the machine would first alter its structure, making it rubbery, so the blow would land without breaking it. Thus the counterfactual, were it struck, it would break, is false; yet the glass was fragile before any blow. The disposition seems to vanish precisely when its stimulus occurs. How do you defend your analysis against such a finkish case without giving it up?
