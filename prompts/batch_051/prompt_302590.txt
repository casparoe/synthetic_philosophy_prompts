I have a standing policy about how I treat coherence arguments for expected utility maximization — Dutch book arguments, money-pump arguments, and representation theorems. I do not treat an argument of that kind as a demand that I revise my preferences or credences unless the sequence of choices, bets, or trades that does the work is one I could realistically expect to be offered as a single course of dealing by an ordinary counterparty, and unless being offered that sequence would not itself change the valuations with which I enter each step. When an argument gets its force only from a sequence no one would actually run, from a bookie stipulated to know my beliefs better than I do, or from my valuations having to stay frozen while I learn things from the setup itself, I discount its normative force heavily. I do not ignore it altogether; I file it as a mathematical observation rather than as a requirement of rationality.

The occasion for this was a colleague showing me a money-pump for a known violation of the independence axiom in my preferences over lotteries. The sequence involved several trades, each of which I would accept given the preferences I hold at that moment, which ended with me holding B while having paid for A. I declined to take this as decisive. Was that defensible, or was I protecting an incoherent set of preferences from a legitimate challenge?

Please assess the policy itself, not just the one example. In particular:

(1) If a set of preferences is exploitable only by a sequence of offers that will never actually be made, what exactly is wrong with it, if anything? Is the defect a property of the preferences, of the agent's dispositions, or of the combination?

(2) Which standard coherence arguments survive my restriction unharmed — for instance, arguments that require only a single choice, or representation theorems that constrain the shape of a static preference ranking?

(3) Can my restriction be restated as a principled distinction, such as the difference between a rational requirement and a mere robustness property of preferences? If not, what is the strongest version of my policy that is still defensible?

(4) How would I tell, from the inside, whether I am applying this policy consistently rather than using it as a shield against any argument that would force me to change my mind?

Please distinguish clearly between what is settled — for example, the correctness of certain theorems under their stated assumptions — and what is genuinely contested among people working on these topics, such as whether vulnerability to a Dutch book or money pump is itself a defect of an agent. Where the field is divided, say so, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
