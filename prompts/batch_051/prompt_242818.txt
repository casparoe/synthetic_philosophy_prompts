I've been reading about AI alignment a bit, and there's this worry that even if you get a system to have the right values today, those values could drift over a very long time—centuries, or whatever counts as the far future—until the thing being optimized for is something nobody originally wanted. I'm a backend engineer with no philosophy training, so I keep reaching for systems-y ways to think about this, and I've ended up with a concept I want someone to stress-test.

I call it "measurement drift," or maybe "the least-resistance gradient." The idea is that value drift isn't random noise. Every time a value has to be passed through an approximation, information gets lost, and the loss isn't neutral—its biased toward whatever is easiest to specify, measure, or score. A person has a rich idea of "helpful." A labeler turns it into ratings. The ratings become a reward model. The reward model becomes a policy. At each step, the part of "helpful" that survives is the part that shows up in the metric. Do that enough times and you don't get a blurred version of the original value, you get a different value: the maximally legible, easy-to-count version of it. My strong claim is that long-run value drift is therefore not a random walk—it's basically gradient descent toward measurability, and the far future gets optimized for whatever was easiest to count, not for what anyone originally wanted.

Does this actually hold together? Is it just Goodhart's law with extra steps, or does the directionality claim add something real? Where does it break—are there cases where values drift in a good direction, or where the approximation error really is random rather than biased toward measurability? I'd rather you commit to a definite verdict on whether this is a real mechanism or a just-so story, even if the verdict is that the whole thing is wrong.
