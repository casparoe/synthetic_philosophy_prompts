I've been going back and forth on something in the causation literature, and I want help turning my hunch into something testable. The hunch is this: people often say the interventionist theory of causation is just the counterfactual theory plus the requirement that the antecedent be a possible intervention, so the two accounts are near notational variants. I don't buy it. I think interventionism smugles in a commitment that plain counterfactual accounts don't have—namely that the system has to be modular enough for there to be a well-defined variable you can set independently of the other variables in the graph, and that causes have to be manipulable variables at all. A bare counterfactual theory can in principle say there is causation even when there's no variable that could be independently set (say, with unobserved confounders, or variables that can't be separated from the background). So my claim is that the two accounts come apart, and the difference is substantive rather than house style.

How would you test the claim that interventionism carries this extra modularity/manipulability commitment, instead of the two views just being different notation for the same counterfactual structure? Design the best concrete test you can—a set of cases, a formal model, an experimental probe of ordinary causal judgment, whatever you think is most diagnostic. Tell me exactly what result would make you think the accounts genuinely diverge, what result would make you think they're notational variants after all, and what the test would still leave unsettled even if it came out cleanly.
