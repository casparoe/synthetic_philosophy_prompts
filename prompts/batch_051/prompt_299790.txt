At one point in the first edition of Thomas Robert Malthus's An Essay on the Principle of Population, he offers a grim thought experiment about entitlement in an already-occupied world. His argument runs roughly as follows: a person born into a full world who cannot get support from his parents and whose labor society does not want has no rightful entitlement to any share of the food supply. His presence is, in Malthus's view, a brute fact rather than a right. Nature's table is already full; nature directs him to leave and will carry out that direction unless someone else forcibly makes room for him. But any act of making room, Malthus warns, invites a further influx of people with the same request.

I have been turning that passage over while thinking about a broader asymmetry in how we respond to failed predictions. When a hurricane forecast misses landfall by a hundred miles, or a fisheries model predicts collapse within a decade and the fish do not collapse, the model is usually treated as faulty or incomplete; the next step is to change the model, not to insist that its mechanism was sound but somehow delayed. Yet when Malthus's central prediction—that population tends to outstrip the means of subsistence, with famine, disease, and other positive checks repeatedly restoring the balance—was followed by two centuries in which death rates fell, wages rose, and the famines he described became more regional than systemic, many sophisticated readers still describe him as right but early, or as having identified a tendency that technological change temporarily postponed.

I want to know whether that asymmetry in our intellectual practice can be justified. Is there a principled difference between a short-run prediction that fails and a long-run structural prediction that fails within a specified window, such that the first is falsified while the second only suffers a delay? Does the passage from Malthus help his defenders here, or does it make their position harder, since it is so specific about the consequences of inviting people to a table with no vacant place? What would a Malthusian prediction have to look like in order to count as genuinely falsified, and does the answer require a method for distinguishing legitimate rescue by auxiliary hypothesis from mere immunization? I would like the strongest possible defense of the right-but-early move, followed by a candid judgment about whether it works.
