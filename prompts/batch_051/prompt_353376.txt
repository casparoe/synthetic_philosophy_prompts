Euhemerism — the view, associated with the Hellenistic writer Euhemerus, that the gods were once people: specifically, that the traditional gods began as mortal rulers or benefactors whom grateful subjects deified and whose mortal origins were later forgotten — is almost always tested against unrecoverable prehistory. I want to test it instead against a case we can observe in the historical record: the Roman imperial cult from the deification of Caesar through the Principate.

Take the standard facts as given; I am not asking you to recover them. Julius Caesar was formally enrolled among the state's gods as Divus Iulius within a few years of his assassination; the comet that appeared during the games of 44 BCE was read as his apotheosis, and his heir soon made divi filius, son of the deified, central to his self-presentation. After Augustus died, the Senate voted him divine honors, a priesthood (the sodales Augustales), and a temple. The pattern then hardened into an institution: the Senate's post-mortem judgment determined whether an emperor became a divus or instead had his memory condemned and was judged unworthy of cult. Living emperors were generally not made full gods at Rome, though honors to their genius, and provincial ruler cult, blurred the distinction. And the new divi remained a class apart from the traditional dei; Jupiter Optimus Maximus and the other old gods continued to receive their own cult alongside the deified emperors.

Analyze this episode through the lens of euhemerism in both directions — theory illuminating case, case testing theory.

First, give the strongest version of the imperial cult as a confirmation of euhemerism. Identify precisely which features of the theory's mechanism the episode instantiates, and say why watching deification happen in full historical light makes this a uniquely promising case for the euhemerist rather than merely an analogy.

Second, identify where the episode resists the euhemerist account. Attend especially to: who initiated deification and for what political purposes; the evaluative selectivity of the institution, in which a deserved emperor became a divus and a failed one was condemned; the speed with which the new divine status was constructed; the continued worship of the old dei alongside the new divi; and what participants are most likely to have believed about the emperor's actual divinity.

Third, render a verdict. Does the Roman case confirm, refute, or merely complicate euhemerism as a general account of how gods are made? If it complicates the theory, say exactly which premise does the questionable work — for instance, a premise about a spontaneous, bottom-up process of collective memory — and what the episode shows about that premise.

Be disciplined about the mapping. Say which features of the episode the theory explains, which it must strain to accommodate, and where it is simply silent. I would rather have an honest statement that the theory does not speak to something than a forced application of it.
