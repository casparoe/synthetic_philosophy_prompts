Two friends of mine got into an argument after a reading group, and I keep flipping on it. The first said Malthus has basically been falsified: he claimed population tends to grow geometrically while food production grows arithmetically, so unless something checks population you get recurring famine and misery, but instead population grew several-fold, per-capita food supply rose, famines got rarer and mostly political, and the ceiling never came. The second said that's too quick — Malthus identified a real mechanism, which is that any exponential growth against a finite resource base has to hit a ceiling eventually, and what actually happened is that technology, fossil energy, nitrogen fertilizer, and the demographic transition postponed the reckoning. So he was "right but early."

The first replied that "right but early" is a textbook rescue device: if any disconfirmation can be absorbed by saying "not yet," the theory is compatible with everything and predicts nothing. The second — who tends to steelman anything with a mechanism in it — said not every "not yet" is ad hoc; an engineer who says a bridge will fail under a growing load isn't wrong just because the collapse hasn't happened on schedule, and long-run predictions about complex systems just come with wide error bars. The first said the timing isn't a detail, it's what the theory was for: Malthus thought the crunch was near enough that poor relief would mostly produce more mouths, and two centuries of postponement should push you toward "some new mechanism will turn up" rather than "the original mechanism is still right, just late." The second said treating every "not yet" as evidence for "never" is its own fallacy, and that a prediction can get the timestamp wrong and still be right about the sign of the trend.

Go through the exchange and adjudicate move by move: which specific claims are right, where does each overreach, and is "right but early" ever a legitimate way to defend a long-run prediction? Does Malthus actually qualify, given what we now know about food production and fertility? And what are both of them missing? I dont care who "wins" as much as I want the cruxes separated out.
