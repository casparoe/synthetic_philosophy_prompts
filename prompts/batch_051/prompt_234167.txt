I've been turning over something about the way history gets written. Every historical explanation is put together after we already know how things turned out. That feels like it has to distort things, but I can't quite see where the step is bad. Here's the argument as I see it:

(1) A historian explaining, say, why a war started is working backwards from an outcome she already knows.
(2) At any point before the war there were thousands of conditions and events she could mention.
(3) She can't mention all of them; she has to pick a handful as "the causes."
(4) The thing that tells her which of those earlier events to pick is, ultimately, that the war actually happened afterward.
(5) So the causes she picks are the ones that point at the actual outcome, and the ones that would have mattered only if things had gone a different way get left out.
(6) If you filter the past through the outcome in that way, whatever story you tell is going to make the actual outcome look like it was building up and likely to happen.
(7) The same is true no matter what the outcome is: had things gone the other way, a different set of picked causes would make that outcome look just as inevitable.
(8) Therefore the hindsight bias isn't a mistake a careful historian can avoid; it's baked into narrative explanation itself, and we can never tell from a historical account weather an event was actually predictable or just looks predictable after the fact.

I keep thinking step 6 is where the trick is, but step 6 also seems plainly true: picking only the strands that lead to the outcome is exactly what makes a story feel inevitable. Is the argument valid, and if not, which step actually fails? If you need to, say what the strongest repaired version of the argument would conclude.
