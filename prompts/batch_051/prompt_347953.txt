I coordinate a small working group in the foundations of physics, and two of our members have spent months arguing about what identical quantum particles are. One of them defends a non-individuality view: when the state of a many-particle system is permutation-invariant, there is no fact of the matter about which particle is which, and the objects of quantum statistics are not individuals in any robust sense; on this view the failure of the classical Principle of the Identity of Indiscernibles is a symptom of the world's structure, not a problem to be repaired. The other defends an individualist view: quantum particles remain individuals, but their individuality is carried by relational rather than monadic properties — for instance, two fermions in a spin-singlet are weakly discerned by the symmetric irreflexive relation of having opposite spin projections along every axis, and this suffices to make them two distinct things. They have agreed to conduct an adversarial collaboration, and I need the document they could both put their names to.

Draft that document. It should open with a numbered list of claims both sides can sign, stated non-vacuously and covering the shared formal terrain: permutation symmetry and the unobservability of particle labels, the symmetry constraints on bosonic and fermionic wavefunctions, the failure of classical-style discernibility for same-kind quantum particles (they share every intrinsic, monadic property, and in maximally symmetric states no standard relational property distinguishes them either), what exchange and entanglement do and do not establish, and what each side must grant about the other's stock examples — the two electrons of a helium atom in the spin-singlet, and a Bose–Einstein condensate whose atoms all occupy the same single-particle mode. Then give the exact residual disagreement, phrased so that each side would accept the wording of its own position. I especially want the cruxes made precise: whether a symmetric irreflexive relation can ground distinctness or instead already presupposes two relata; whether two bosons in the same one-particle state can be weakly discerned at all, and whether their case forces the individualist toward primitive thisness; and whether the absence of any observable that distinguishes two particles is an epistemic limitation or an ontic fact. Format the disagreement as a table, one row per crux, with columns for the crux; what both sides agree would bear on it (a formal result, a model calculation, or a constraint any interpretation must respect); the non-individualist's advance prediction; and the individualist's advance prediction.

Close by identifying which parts of the dispute, if any, are verbal — disagreements about how much should be built into the word individual rather than about the physics or the metaphysics — and which parts would survive once the terminology is fixed.
