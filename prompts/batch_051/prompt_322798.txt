I'm writing a paper on how a theory of value is supposed to combine the value of parts into the value of a whole, and I keep running into people who pick a rule first — additive, multiplicative, lexical — and only then defend it against one or two favorite cases. I want to do this backwards: write out the full specification that any theory of value combination must meet in order to be adequate, and then let the candidate rules fall where they fall. The rules on the table are roughly these. Additive: the value of a whole is the sum of the values of its parts. Multiplicative: value behaves like the product of valence, intensity, duration, and something like level of consciousness, so that any zero factor zeroes the product. Lexical: certain goods or harms rank above any quantity of another kind, so that no amount of one ever outweighs a single instance of the other.

The domain I most care about is the combination of experienced value — pleasure and pain, intensity and duration, conscious level — but I also want the account to speak to aggregation across persons, since one of the live questions is whether within-a-life and across-person aggregation should obey the same principles. Cases and distinctions that have come up in my reading and in discussion: whether a sufficiently large number of mild unpleasant experiences can outweigh a single intense agony, and whether denying that is a lexical claim or just a very steep weight; the chain of barely noticeable worsenings from something trivial to something monstrous, and whether a combination rule must be transitive across such chains; whether positive and negative values can cancel within a life, or across persons; whether a life that is only barely positive at each moment can become very good just by lasting long enough, and whether the analogous claim about populations is acceptable; what happens when intensity or consciousness is zero, and what that implies for dreamless sleep, low-arousal states, or barely conscious pleasures; and whether intensities and durations trade off linearly, or whether one of them should dominate. For each requirement, give the strongest case or argument that forces it, and mark clearly which requirements are non-negotiable and which are parochial to a particular camp — hedonic utilitarians, pluralists, prioritarians, and so on.

I want the deliverable to be a specification, not a survey. A table is fine. After the list, tell me which single requirement is most damaging to the popular rules, and then say whether you think any subset of the non-negotiable requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — if there is a tension between, say, transitivity, respect for persons, and some plausible form of aggregation, that is the part I most want to see. Don't pause to summarize the standard debates; spend the space on the requirements themselves.
