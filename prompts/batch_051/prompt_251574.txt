Adopt these working criteria. Testimonial injustice occurs when a hearer assigns a speaker a credibility deficit not warranted by the evidence, because of prejudice or stereotype about the speaker's social identity. Hermeneutical injustice occurs when a person cannot make an important area of their experience intelligible to themselves or others because shared interpretive resources are skewed toward a more powerful group's experience, and this gap harms them.

Now consider this case. A medical review board interviews two clinicians about an unusual cluster of adverse drug reactions. One is a native speaker of the majority language; the other speaks fluently but with a strong regional working-class accent. The board members are not consciously hostile toward the second clinician and would deny any bias, and they genuinely find her speech harder to follow, which contributes to their skepticism. They interrupt her more often, read slight hesitations as evasiveness, and later describe the testimony as hard to follow and lacking confidence. Her report was in fact more detailed and more accurate than the first clinician's, but the board's final report relies almost entirely on the first account.

Is this testimonial injustice, ordinary miscommunication and comprehension difficulty, or something in between? Work through the criteria one at a time. In particular, address whether a credibility deficit can be caused by prejudice about social identity when the hearers disavow any prejudiced belief and a real processing difficulty is part of the causal story. If the criteria do not settle the case, say exactly where they underdetermine it and whether the criteria themselves need refining.
