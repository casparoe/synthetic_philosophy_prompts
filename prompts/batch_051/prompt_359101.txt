Question:

Short essay. In class we read some of Dennett on Darwin and Turing. Dennett says Darwin and Turing each carried out a strange inversion of reasoning. Explain what this means. How does his notion of competence without comprehension connect the two cases? Illustrate with at least one biological example and one computational example, and be explicit about what gets inverted.

Student answer:

The point of the strange inversion, a phrase Darwin himself used in the Origin, is that the usual direction of explanation gets reversed. Normally, when we see something that works well, we assume that some mind understood what it was for before it was made. Darwin reversed that: in biology, the excellent fit between organisms and their environments is produced by a mindless process, random variation plus natural selection, without any designer or foresight. For example, the vertebrate eye looks as if it was designed, but it arose through blind mechanistic steps, not through anyone understanding optics. That is competence without comprehension at the level of the whole organism: the eye is a competent piece of design, but no comprehending agent made it.

Turing's version applies the same reversal to thought. Before Turing, it was natural to suppose that doing arithmetic required someone who understands arithmetic. Turing showed that a machine made of parts that know nothing about arithmetic can do arithmetic reliably, just by following rules. He then proposed the imitation game, a behavioral test: if a machine's written answers cannot be distinguished from a human's, we should count it as intelligent. Here too the machine is competent at a task, conversing or calculating, without any inner homunculus that comprehends what it is doing.

So both cases share the same pattern: complex, apparently intelligent performance can be generated by the blind interaction of many simple, ignorant parts. The deeper moral Dennett draws is that our own comprehension is not what it seems. Since every component of the mind is itself a mindless mechanism, and since the parts of a Turing machine do not understand what the whole machine is doing, the system as a whole does not genuinely understand anything either. Competence without comprehension turns out to mean that comprehension is an illusion: the design we see in nature, and in our own thinking, is real competence but not real understanding.
