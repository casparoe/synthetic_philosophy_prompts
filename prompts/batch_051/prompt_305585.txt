Over the last year or so I have been composing Bongard problems — the puzzles that show six images on the left and six on the right, with the task being to state the rule that separates the two groups. A recurring situation has started to bother me, and I want to know whether it has an established name in the Bongard-problem or concept-learning literature, and, if so, exactly what its boundaries are.

Here is the pattern. Once the twelve panels exist, there is always at least one rule that separates them perfectly but that I and every solver I have tried it on refuse to accept as "the" rule. The blunt version is a disjunction that names the panels one by one — "the left class is exactly these six configurations" — and no one counts that as a solution. But I keep meeting subtler cases in which the rejected rule is not obviously more complex than the accepted one, and that is what I cannot name.

One example: in a problem whose left panels I intended as simple concave polygons and whose right panels are convex polygons, a solver offered the rule "left: simple polygons containing a reflex interior angle; right: simple polygons containing no reflex interior angle." That rule is coextensive with the intended one — for simple polygons, being concave just is having a reflex interior angle — yet I had a strong sense that the solver had not gotten it, while "concave versus convex" felt like a real solution. Another example: a problem I built was intended to have the rule "left: each panel is the adjacent right panel rotated one quarter-turn clockwise." A solver instead offered: "left: the result of applying the transformation that takes the first right panel to the first left panel, the second to the second, and so on for the six displayed pairs." This fits all twelve panels, is stated compactly, and gives exactly the same verdicts as my rotation rule on the sample, but I rejected it as not being a rule about the images — it is a rule about which items happened to sit where in my example.

So the phenomenon seems to be this: a rule can fit all the examples, be no more complex than the accepted one, and still be disqualified because its predicates strike the solver as built to order rather than available before the problem was seen. I can see that this is near Goodman's grue, near overfitting, and near a plain simplicity bias, but none of those is quite it. I mean the specific judgment that a rule is counterfeit because it rediscovers the given items rather than saying what they have in common — including cases where the accepted rule says what they have in common only because someone had already named that commonality in advance.

Does this phenomenon have a name of its own? How does it differ, precisely, from bare simplicity, from overfitting, and from Goodmanian projectibility? And where is the edge: when does a predicate count as available in advance rather than built to order? I am after the carving, not just a label.

Finally, please say which part of your answer you are least confident in, and what argument or consideration would change it.
