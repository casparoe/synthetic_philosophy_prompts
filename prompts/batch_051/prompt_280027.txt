Over the past few months our group has been training language models on multi-step reasoning tasks under two supervision regimes, and we have reached an impasse about what the results mean. In outcome-based supervision the model receives a single reward when its final answer matches, with no feedback on intermediate steps. In process-based supervision the model receives step-level feedback, typically from a learned process reward model trained to predict human judgments of whether each step is promising, plausible, or on-track. On our benchmark a process-trained model and an outcome-trained model reach roughly equal final accuracy, but the process-trained model's written reasoning is noticeably tidier and includes fewer dead ends, false starts, and irrelevant digressions. One member of the group concludes that the process-trained model has 'actually learned why the answer follows from the premises', while the outcome-trained model is still pattern-matching against final answers. Another member insists that this is either false or untestable: the process-trained model may simply have learned to emit intermediate steps that the process reward model, and the humans behind it, will approve of — and approval is not the same as correctness, even when the two are highly correlated in the training distribution.

I would like you to design the strongest empirical test of the claim that process-based supervision produces something over and above an outcome-matching policy with a polished chain-of-thought: something like correct internal reasoning, or at least reasoning that transfers for the right reasons rather than because the intermediate steps look the way raters expect. Specify the experimental setup concretely, including what is held fixed between the two training conditions and what is measured, and say which result would favour each side. Then be honest about what the test would and would not settle. In particular, treat separately the case where intermediate steps are independently checkable (mathematics, code, formal proofs, database queries) and the case where they are not (open-ended judgment, planning, explanation), and say whether the phrase 'really learned why the answer is right' should be kept after the best test you can design, or replaced by something more precise.
