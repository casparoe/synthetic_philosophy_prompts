I work on AI systems, and I have recently been asked to evaluate a model trained by iterated amplification and distillation: a human overseer repeatedly decomposes a difficult task into smaller subtasks for a model to perform, and the model's behavior on those subtasks is then distilled into a successor intended to perform the whole task without the overseer in the loop. The team behind the system argues that it is aligned because, at each round, the overseer judged the subtask executions to be both competent and directed at the overall objective.

In response, I have adopted the following evidential rule. I treat an alignment claim about such a system as credible only if it can be traced to subtask-level claims whose correctness could in principle be checked without already using the overall alignment judgment. Where the evidence bottoms out in the overseer's holistic sense that the pieces look right or that the final behavior still reflects the original goal, I discount it almost to zero. A colleague objects that this rule is biased against precisely the hard cases: many objectives decompose well enough to train on but not into subtasks with independently checkable success criteria, and holistic overseer judgment can be genuine evidence of alignment even when it cannot be articulated. She adds that I hold evidence of competence to a laxer standard, which suggests I am singling out one kind of judgment without justification.

Is my policy defensible as a guard against the distillation process converting an unarticulated judgment into the appearance of verified alignment? Or is it a bias dressed up as a rule? If it is defensible in part, how should it be restated so that it blocks this laundering without discarding the only evidence available when alignment resists decomposition?
