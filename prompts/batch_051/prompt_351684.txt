I have been trying to settle a running disagreement about whether, in ordinary moral thinking, a larger population is a better one even when average wellbeing falls. The question keeps showing up for us in different guises: whether to have an extra child whose life will be decent but modest, whether a growing city is improving as it becomes more crowded and less pleasant on average, and whether conservationists should prefer a larger but less flourishing population of some species to a smaller but flourishing one. We have provisionally agreed to assess such cases using five criteria. (1) Total Value: an outcome is better if the sum of lifetime wellbeing across all affected lives is greater. (2) Average Value: an outcome is better if the mean wellbeing per life is greater. (3) Person-Affecting Restriction: an outcome is better only if it is better for particular people, and merely adding new people cannot by itself count as an improvement. (4) Lives-Worth-Living Threshold: additional lives count in favor of an outcome only if each is above a minimal quality of life. (5) Inequality Aversion: holding total wellbeing fixed, a more equal distribution is better.

Now consider this borderline case. A city of 100,000 people has an average wellbeing of 7.2 on a 0–10 scale of lifetime quality. A proposed housing-and-growth policy would add 30,000 new residents whose lives would be clearly worth living but would average only 5.1, and it would lower the existing residents' average from 7.2 to 7.0 through congestion and strained public services. Total wellbeing rises markedly, while the average across all 130,000 falls to roughly 6.6. The newcomers are particular people who would not exist under the no-growth alternative, and the distribution becomes more unequal, though no one is below 5.1.

Is the policy an improvement in the sense the five criteria are trying to capture? Please work through each criterion in turn, say where the criteria conflict or underdetermine the verdict, and then say whether this case shows that the criteria themselves need revision—for instance, whether one should be weighted more heavily, split into finer notions, or replaced.
