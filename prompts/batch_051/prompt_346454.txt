After a philosophy of biology seminar on what counts as a biological individual, two friends of mine started arguing at the bar and kept dragging me in to referee.

Sam said an ant colony is basically an organism — a superorganism, if you want the fancy word. It has a life cycle, most of the ants are sterile workers, and it reproduces as a unit when the winged reproductives fly off to start new colonies. If an organism is just the unit that natural selection acts on, he said, the colony is the unit, not any single ant.

Dana said that was way too quick. Sterile workers don't stop being separate animals just because they never reproduce. Every worker grew from its own egg; it isn't a cell in a bigger body. You can injure one ant and the ant limps while the colony trundles on. She said a cow plus its gut microbes is a better candidate for being one organism than an ant colony: the cow can't get much out of grass without the microbes, and the microbes don't have a stable gut to live in without the cow. That's physiological integration. Ants, she said, are just separate bodies living side by side and signaling to each other.

Sam came back that ants pass food mouth-to-mouth and coordinate through pheromones so tightly that the line between one body and many bodies is doing all the work. If Dana is happy to count a bunch of completely different microbial species as part of the cow just because they're metabolically coupled, then physical separateness can't be a dealbreaker for the ants either. And the microbes aren't passed on through the cow's reproductive line the way the cow's own cells are, so the holobiont isn't even a unit of reproduction — which the colony at least is.

Dana said, fine, then draw the non-arbitrary boundary. If gut microbes don't count, what about mitochondria? They're bacteria that moved in ages ago and now we count them as parts of cells. Why is it one organism when the merger is old but a holobiont when it's recent — that's just chronocentrism or whatever you call it. Then she turned it around: by the only non-arbitrary line, the single-cell bottleneck, neither the cow-holobiont nor the ant colony counts as an organism, because the microbes and the worker ants trace back to different eggs. Both can still be biological individuals in some looser, pick-your-criteria sense.

Sam said the bottleneck standard is just mammal chauvinism: plenty of plants and fungi are a single genetic individual spread across acres without ever passing through one bottleneck, so that can't be the line. And if we're being functional about it, he said, the ant colony is the best game in town.

I honestly couldn't score it. Please explain who's right about which specific points, what they're both missing, and who is more right overall.
