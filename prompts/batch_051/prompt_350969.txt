A friend and I recently argued late into the night about whether exit can do for state authority what voice cannot. He held that charter cities, private cities, and seasteading, even taken at their best, do not let anyone genuinely opt out of the state; they only relocate a person from one jurisdiction to another, and every jurisdiction ultimately rests on coercion. I held that this underrates exit: if a person has a genuinely available alternative—a rival jurisdiction with tolerably good governance and low costs of relocation—then remaining in a state looks much more like consent than remaining when one has no alternative, and that difference ought to matter for political obligation.

I would like your own number, not only a discussion. Please give your credence for the following claim:

For a typical citizen of a reasonably just liberal democracy, the existence of a genuinely available exit option—a realistically accessible rival jurisdiction, such as a well-run charter city or private city, where one could expect tolerably good governance and basic rights protection—would, if the citizen nonetheless remained, make that citizen's political obligation to their state better justified than it would be without any such exit option.

Then decompose the estimate. I believe the main moving parts are these, though you may amend the list:

1. Whether political obligation is in any significant part consent-based, rather than grounded entirely in fair play, associative duties, or a natural duty of justice—that is, your probability that consent carries at least some independent justificatory weight for political obligation.
2. Whether remaining while having a feasible exit genuinely constitutes tacit consent, rather than being explainable by inertia, ties of place and family, language, or risk, so that not leaving reliably signals acceptance.
3. Whether the rival jurisdiction must itself be reasonably just, or merely available, for exit to have legitimating force.
4. Whether an exit option actually strengthens the state's claim, or merely removes some grounds for complaint without adding any positive justification.

For each, assign a rough probability and explain how the pieces combine into your overall number. My friend ended near 0.2 and I ended near 0.8, and we could never isolate exactly where we diverged—so please say which sub-question most likely houses the disagreement, and whether the difference is probably philosophical or empirical. Finally, tell me what evidence or argument would move your overall number the most, and in which direction.
