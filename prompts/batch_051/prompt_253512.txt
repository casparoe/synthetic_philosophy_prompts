I teach an upper-level seminar in medieval Jewish philosophy and am writing the final exam. I want one essay question on Maimonides’ treatment of the sacrificial laws in *The Guide of the Perplexed* III:32 that cannot be answered by merely reciting the “concession” interpretation. I’ll give the students a close paraphrase of the passage rather than a single translation, so the question doesn’t turn on an English wording: Maimonides compares the sacrificial cult to God’s not leading Israel to the promised land by the direct route through Philistine territory. Just as the people were not ready for that route, they were not ready to be commanded all at once to abandon every form of offering and slaughter. Sacrifice was therefore not the end the law sought; it was a means suited to people habituated to idolatrous worship, through which they were to be drawn toward the worship of the one God.

Please write the exam question, a model answer of roughly 700 words, and a marking scheme. The question should force students to confront an internal problem: if sacrifices are a concession to historical limitations, then God’s legislation looks like that of a fallible ruler who commands not what is best absolutely but what is feasible for a particular people at a particular time. But Maimonides elsewhere treats the Torah as a work of divine wisdom, unified and suited to human nature. Is this a genuine contradiction in Maimonides, or can the two commitments be held together? A good answer will need to distinguish between the historical origin of a commandment, its pedagogical function, and its ongoing normative status; it should also consider whether Maimonides can say the sacrificial laws remain binding after their original rationale has lapsed.

The marking scheme should make explicit which moves separate an A from a B. An A answer must state the apparent contradiction precisely, give the strongest version of the objection to Maimonides, and then reconstruct at least two possible resolutions without simply declaring the problem solved. A B answer will correctly report the concession theory but fail to press the problem of whether it entails that the law is contingent or merely instrumental. The model answer should model how to argue the dilemma rather than just survey the text.
