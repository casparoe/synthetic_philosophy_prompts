I have been defending a principle I call the non-parochial epoch principle, and I would like it assessed rather than flattered. The principle is this: a calendar's epoch — the origin from which it counts years — is defensible only if it is either (a) openly arbitrary, a co-ordinating convention with no pretence of significance (midnight on 1 January 1970, the Unix epoch, is my model), or (b) a genuine physical singularity, such as the Big Bang. What is indefensible is the middle case: an epoch that presents a parochial event as the neutral origin of time. The Common Era's hinge between 1 BC and AD 1 presents a religious event as the axis of history; the Holocene calendar, which adds 10,000 years to Common Era numbering so that its year 1 falls roughly at the onset of the Holocene, merely relabels an anthropocentric geological boundary as a scientific one. On my principle, a calendar should count from a cosmological singularity or own up to counting from nowhere in particular. Is this coherent? Does it already exist under another name — it feels too obvious to be original? What does it get right, and where does it break? Give the three strongest objections and state whether any is fatal rather than merely awkward.
