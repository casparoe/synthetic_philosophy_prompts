I’ve been reading Mill’s On Liberty alongside some computer science material on operating systems, and I keep noticing a structural parallel that I can’t quite sort out. In On Liberty, Mill holds that society may restrict an opinion only when the circumstances of its expression make it a positive instigation to some mischievous act. As I understand him, merely false, foolish, or even morally revolting opinions are to be tolerated because they remain, so to speak, inside the speaker’s own sphere of belief and persuasion; the law’s enforcement boundary is crossed only when the expression becomes act-like in relation to another person’s rights, as with incitement, fraud, or threats. Mill’s claim is not that speech has no consequences, but that the mere influence of speech on belief is not the kind of consequence the state may police.

The parallel that bothers me is with protected-mode isolation in an operating system. A user process is free to do almost anything within its own address space: it can hold corrupt data, reason from false premises, loop pointlessly, overwrite its own variables, or generate nonsense, and the kernel does not intervene. The kernel’s enforcement boundary is crossed only when the process attempts to act on protected objects outside its own address space, for instance by issuing a system call or writing to memory it does not own. That looks a lot like Mill’s line between self-regarding belief and other-regarding expression: local cognitive or computational error is tolerated; external act-like interference is regulated. It even suggests that Mill’s harm principle might be understood as a kind of capability-based access control for speech, where ordinary assertion is user-space activity and incitement or fraud is a system call.

Is this a deep structural resemblance, or am I being taken in by a loose analogy? I’d like you to state the mapping explicitly, say what would have to be true of both speech and computation for results to transfer from one domain to the other, and identify something the analogy predicts that turns out false—for example, about persuasion that predictably changes harmful behavior without meeting the legal test for incitement, or about whether social enforcement in Mill differs from kernel enforcement because the “protection” we give to others is fixed differently. Please also tell me how confident you are in your assessment and single out the specific point you are least sure of.
