A friend and I spent much of a long evening arguing about lethal autonomous weapons, and we ended stuck on a disagreement I would rather like settled: what the demand for meaningful human control actually presupposes. In the international debate about such weapons, the recurring requirement is that the decision to use lethal force remain under meaningful human control; at the same time, the systems militaries actually field already automate large parts of targeting, and the human operator who retains final approval is often the least reliable part of the loop. I would like you to reconstruct the implicit theory behind this requirement. Does it presuppose a control-based account of responsibility, on which a person can be blamed only for what they could decide or prevent? Or something stronger — for instance, that there is a distinctively human capacity for moral judgment that no machine computation can stand in for? Is that commitment consistent with how targeting decisions are in fact dispersed across staffs, software, and sensors before any single human sees them? Distinguish what the demand strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, identify the points at which several rival theories would all support the same requirement, and say what would have to change in targeting doctrine and accountability practices if the underlying view were replaced by a purely outcome-based or purely systemic account.
