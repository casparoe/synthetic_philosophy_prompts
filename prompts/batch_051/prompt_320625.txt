I've been turning an idea into notes for an essay on a limit of the debate proposal in AI alignment, and I'd like comments on the argument's structure before I commit to a longer version. I'll restate the setup as I understand it and then give the claims.

The proposal, as I understand it: a human judge with bounded time and expertise can reliably supervise a much more capable system by having two copies of that system argue opposite sides of a question, with the judge deciding which side is better supported. The intuitive guarantee is that a dishonest argument must contain some false or unsupported move, and an honest debater can point at that move, ask for it to be expanded, and the judge will then see the error.

The essay I'm sketching argues that this guarantee covers only one class of failure, and that the proposal underrates a second class. I'm calling this an obfuscated argument: an argument whose persuasive force rests on a false or unsupported load-bearing claim that is not locally refutable. Each component, inspected on its own, looks defensible; the problem lies in the joint pattern of several individually innocent claims, or in a suppressed premise that never stays on the table long enough to be pinned down. When the dishonest side controls pacing and emphasis, an error of this kind is not like a bad step on a blackboard that can be circled; it is closer to an adversarial example for the judge's bounded checking procedure, optimized against the shortcuts the judge must use when she cannot verify everything.

From there the notes make three moves. First, they recast the problem in Goodhart terms: under debate, "convinces the judge" becomes the quantity the debaters optimize, so the measure is applied under optimization pressure rather than read off passively. Second, they argue that the honest debater's ability to ask questions does not automatically restore the guarantee, because an obfuscated error is a pattern rather than a point: expanding one claim at a time may never localize it, and the dishonest side can spend the cross-examination budget forcing the honest side to clarify harmless claims. Third, they propose a distinction between locally checkable errors and globally misleading patterns, and claim the motivating examples for debate are all of the first kind while obfuscation exploits the second.

I'd like comments on three points. (1) Am I smuggling in a weak judge, or does the challenge transfer to the stronger version in which debaters cross-examine each other and the judge selects follow-up questions? I don't want to have attacked only a strawman in which the judge passively listens. (2) Is the distinction between locally checkable errors and globally misleading patterns doing real work, or is it just restating bounded rationality in fancier words? (3) Where does the adversarial-example analogy break in ways that matter? If the judge can ask arbitrary questions, in what sense is she still like a fixed classifier, and in what sense not? I'm more interested in whether the argument is sound than in prose suggestions.
