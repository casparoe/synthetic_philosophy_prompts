I am persuaded by a broadly Boethian solution to the problem of divine foreknowledge, and I want a map of what would count against it. The view I hold is this. God does not exist at one time and look ahead to later times. God is eternal in the special sense of possessing a limitless life all at once rather than living through an unending succession of moments. Consequently God's knowledge of what I will do tomorrow is not, for God, foreknowledge; it is knowledge from the eternal present, rather as my seeing you raise your hand now does not force you to raise it. Infallible knowledge alone does not make the thing known necessary, and atemporal knowledge seems to me the most promising way to explain how God can infallibly know everything that happens without causing it or turning it into fate.

I want to know what would have to be true for this position to be false or seriously inadequate, and which of its commitments it stands or falls with. Set aside the stock complaint that 'the eternal present' is only a metaphor and the bare objection that the view is obscure. I am after the deeper exposure. Does the view require a particular metaphysics of time — eternalism, the B-theory, or at least the denial that there is a metaphysically privileged present — and would a successful argument for irreducibly tensed facts or a real moving now undermine it? Can an atemporal knower possess essentially indexical or first-personal knowledge, such as knowledge of which moment is present, and does omniscience demand this? Is there a coherent account of how an atemporal being acts in history — creates, sustains, answers prayer, or becomes incarnate — without standing in before-and-after relations to creatures, and is the absence of such an account a genuine cost or a category mistake? Does the view collapse into a rival, such as a tenseless-knowledge view indistinguishable from hard determinism, and what would show that collapse?

Please rank these and any further defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each say what a committed Boethian would themselves concede counts against the position as opposed to what they would dismiss as an objection to a caricature. I also want a judgment about whether the position has been insulated by endless qualification: if every difficulty is met by an appeal to the mystery of eternity, has the view stopped risking anything, and is that insularity a problem? The defeater I would personally find most persuasive is one that shows the atemporal relocation does no modal work — that what seemed to force the future was the infallible truth of what God knows, not the temporal standpoint from which God knows it, so putting the knower outside time leaves the modal threat exactly where it was.
