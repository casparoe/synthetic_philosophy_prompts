I'm a PhD student in population ethics, and I'm lead TA for a third-year undergrad course on population and procreation. My section has about 25 students, most of them non-majors, and I have one 75-minute session to spend on the repugnant conclusion. I want an in-class game, structured vote, or forced-choice exercise that makes them feel the pull of it, not just hear me describe it: the point where they realize that a much larger population with lives barely worth living keeps winning on their own total-sums reasoning, and their only way out is to reject a premise they'd already accepted. I want it runnable: rules, materials, a scoring or payoff scheme that doesn't use real money, and a debrief script. The activity should force a sequence of small trades (a bit less wellbeing per person, a lot more people) and ideally at some point a student looks at a world they just ranked as better and goes no way. Then the debrief maps the stages onto total vs average utilitarianism, what it means for a life to be barely worth living, and the usual escape routes (rejecting transitivity, denying that the barely-worth-living lives count, or just accepting the conclusion). Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—students who refuse the first trade, students who flip to "actually the huge population is fine", discussion eating the activity time—and how to handle each in the moment. And be honest about what the activity cant do: it cant tell them whether the conclusion is false or whether we should swallow it, and it probably won't capture value views where wellbeing near zero adds less than linearly to the total. I'm happy if half the room leaves unsure rather than converted.
