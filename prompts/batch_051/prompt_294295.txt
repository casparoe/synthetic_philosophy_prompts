I am a high school student working on a long paper about whether moral considerability is best understood as a gradient rather than a binary property. In the paper I try to build a single scale that runs from a thermostat at one end to a honeybee at the other without making any abrupt jumps. I call my construct "evaluative boundary density." A system, as I define it, has interests to the degree that it maintains a network of boundaries between states it acts to continue and states it acts to avoid, between itself and the world, and between its present condition and the future it registers. A bimetallic thermostat maintains essentially one such boundary: the temperature at which it switches. A honeybee maintains many: it distinguishes nestmates from intruders, adjusts its foraging to its own energetic state, learns to avoid a color after a sting, and regulates hive temperature within tolerable thresholds. My central claim is that moral status just is the density of these boundaries, weighted by how much of the redrawing is done by the system itself. Having many flexible boundaries is not a symptom of sentience; it is what existing to that degree consists in.

I have tried to anticipate the obvious counterexamples and would like an honest assessment rather than encouragement. I see that a plant closes its stomata and in some sense tracks sunrise, and that a learning thermostat builds a schedule, so neither internal adjustment nor memory can by itself mark the boundary between an organism and a device. My reply is that in the plant and the thermostat the relevant thresholds are fixed by the hardware or the genome, whereas in a bee the boundaries are reweighted by the bee's own history and current state, so that the system treats one and the same stimulus as more or less urgent depending on itself. I want to know whether this construct is coherent, whether it already exists under some better name, what it gets right, and exactly where it stops being distinctive. In particular: would it assign stronger moral claims to a flexible, self-rewriting robot than to a mouse, and if it does, is that a reductio of the theory or a result I should try to defend? Tell me the strongest version of my idea and then tell me where it breaks.
