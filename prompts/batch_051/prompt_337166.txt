I sit on a tax reform commission that our national legislature has asked to decide whether to recommend replacing the estate tax with an inheritance tax assessed on recipients rather than on the estate. The proposal before us gives each heir a lifetime exemption of one million dollars; gifts above a small annual threshold count against that exemption; amounts received beyond it are taxed as ordinary income of the heir, at a top rate of roughly forty percent, with a fifteen-year payment schedule for business or agricultural assets. The current estate tax taxes the decedent's estate once, with a single exemption per estate regardless of how many heirs it is divided among. The systems diverge in cases like a six-million-dollar estate left to one child rather than six: under the current system the estate's treatment is the same, while under the proposal the sole heir would receive a largely taxable windfall and six heirs receiving a million each would owe little or nothing. The proposal's stated aim is to slow dynastic accumulation without taxing modest bequests, and it raises the underlying question whether equal opportunity should be measured by the stock a decedent held at death or by the flow an heir receives over a lifetime.

Draft the short memo I'll circulate before next week's vote. I'm the swing member, and I've read the usual arguments on both sides without finding either side's standard case fully decisive, so I want the strongest version of each rather than a caricature. The memo should open with its recommendation — adopt, reject, or adopt with specific amendments — in the first sentence, then lay out the reasoning in terms a legislator could act on. It must engage seriously with the strongest consideration against the recommendation, especially the claim that the estate is the proper unit because it taxes a single lifetime of savings at its final transfer and that taxing heirs on receipts from their parents either double-taxes the same money or penalizes family provision. Please also say, explicitly, which empirical claim or normative premise would, if false, reverse or materially alter the recommendation, and where the overall argument is most likely to fail. Begin with the memo itself, with no preface or commentary.
