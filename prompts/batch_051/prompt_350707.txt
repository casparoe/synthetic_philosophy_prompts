In my school's philosophy club, a debate about insect welfare has stalled in a way I find difficult to diagnose myself. We all accept the same empirical picture: each individual insect is probably not sentient, yet the number of insects alive at any moment is enormous. From these shared premises, one side concludes that expected suffering is so large — a small chance of sentience multiplied by a vast population — that insect welfare should dominate our moral priorities. The other side rejects exactly this move, arguing that multiplying a vague, low probability by a huge number is arithmetic rather than moral argument, and that our concern should stay with the beings whose sentience we are more confident about and the harms we can actually see. Both sides accept the math and the biology; neither side moves. My working hypothesis is that this is not a factual disagreement but a disagreement about the legitimate use of expected-value reasoning under deep uncertainty. What kind of disagreement is this, actually — a hidden empirical question about how to estimate the probability or degree of insect sentience, a difference about risk or aggregation, a difference about what counts as a moral reason, or something else? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress, if anything?
