I have been developing an account I call “instinctual denomination,” and I would like it checked. I take as my starting point Darwin’s claim in The Descent of Man that the moral sense arises from social instincts, and that these instincts originally bound members of the same tribe rather than the whole species. My proposal is that every moral norm has an implicit denomination inherited from the social group in which it first became adaptive, analogous to the denomination of a currency. The denomination does not set the content of the norm but sets the boundary of who falls within its domain. A norm denominated for a band of fifty will misfire when applied to a nation of millions: it will feel authoritative while tracking nothing about the actual social ecology. This, I think, explains why people report strong moral conviction about distant policy outcomes but act beneficently only toward nearby others; why religious communities can more easily sustain costly cooperation; and why moral language so often takes an impersonal, universal form even though the underlying emotions remain group-bound. I realize this resembles moral-circle-expansion ideas, evolutionary accounts of parochial altruism, and perhaps something in Durkheim. What I want to know is whether the term “denomination” marks a real distinction or just re-describes familiar ideas. Give me the three strongest objections to this account, and say whether any of them is fatal rather than merely a limitation. Maximum 500 words.
