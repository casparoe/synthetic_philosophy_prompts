A donor-advised foundation has adopted an uncompromising act-consequentialist policy: before every grant cycle it tries to identify the option with the greatest expected good and then chooses it. Stipulate that the usual problems of uncertainty and missing data do not arise; the foundation has excellent estimates of each candidate project's expected value. The difficulty I want you to examine is computational. Choosing a bundle of projects under a fixed budget is an instance of the knapsack problem, and arranging the funded activities into a schedule and assigning staff to them raises scheduling and assignment problems that are NP-hard in their general forms. Assuming the standard conjecture that P is not equal to NP, no tractable procedure reliably guarantees that the foundation has found the genuinely optimal bundle. Please set out the most important implications of this intractability for the following questions. First, practically: should the foundation continue to think of its work as maximizing expected value, or should it deliberately adopt satisficing or heuristic decision rules, and what would be lost in that shift? Second, normatively: does the fact that the best option may be computationally unlocatable show that an act-consequentialist theory that tells agents to choose the best option is incomplete as a guide to action, or is the theory untouched because the difficulty is one of searching for the best option rather than one of there being a fact about which option is best? Third, epistemically: on what basis can the foundation trust that the heuristics it is forced to use track the actual optimum, given that it can often know an optimal solution exists without being able to identify it? Finally, say whether intractability is best understood as a feature of the world, a feature of the agent's bounded rationality, or a limitation of particular decision procedures, and explain which reading matters most for whether the maximizing ideal survives.
