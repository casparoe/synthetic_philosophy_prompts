I run a small reading group on the metaphysics of grammatical categories, and we have been arguing about whether traditional case labels carve grammar at its joints or are only pedagogical conveniences. The argument got concrete when a member brought in an old school grammar. Its entire statement of the genitive rule reads as follows:

"A noun is said to be in the genitive (or possessive) case when it is inflected to show that it possesses, or is closely associated with, the thing named by another noun. In English the genitive is formed by adding 's to the singular noun, as in 'the boy's book.'"

The book says nothing about what happens when the possessor is expressed by a multi-word phrase, and it plainly was not written with such cases in mind. Yet English is full of them: "the man next door's car," "the mayor of Chicago's car," "somebody else's problem" — and, in colloquial speech, "the man I saw's car." In each, the 's attaches to the last word of the phrase, whether a common noun, a proper name, an adjective, or a verb, rather than to the possessor noun itself. I would like you to work through what the quoted rule gives when read literally, what its purpose gives when read charitably, and what a reasonable reader of the book would take it to mean on meeting these examples; then say what the authors would most plausibly have answered if asked, and which of these approaches should be decisive if our real question is whether English 's is genuinely a case ending or instead a phrase-level clitic. Finish by saying what that verdict reveals about whether "the genitive case" names a natural kind in English or only a convenient label for a cluster of related grammatical facts.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
