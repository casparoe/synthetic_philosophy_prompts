I'm a philosophy professor teaching an upper-level seminar called Cooperation and Its Discontents. Fourteen students, 90 minutes, mostly philosophy majors plus a couple of economics students who will treat a mushy game as beneath them. I need a runnable classroom game on free riding and social parasitism: what, if anything, is wrong with taking the benefits of cooperation without contributing. I don't want a lecture with a game attached. I want the rules, payoffs, and roles set up so that students actually do the thing before anyone says the words free rider or parasite. Use real small stakes—extra credit, candy, whatever—so choices aren't cheap talk.

The activity should have at least three phases: (1) a contribution stage with a genuinely tempting reason not to contribute, (2) a stage where contributions become visible and people can spend some of their own payoff to punish or exclude someone, and (3) a closing variation that separates the unwilling free rider from the unable non-contributor—maybe the group has to decide whether someone who can't contribute, or contributes in a different currency, still counts as a parasite. Include exact roles or rules, a timeline for the 90 minutes, and a debrief where you map specific moments in the room onto distinct philosophical diagnoses of the wrong: unfairness, exploitation, undermining a cooperative practice, and so on.

In the debrief notes, seperate what's settled from what's genuinely contested. Don't present the claim that free riding is morally blameworthy as a consensus: there's live disagreement about whether the wrong is located in unfairness, exploitation, or the damage to the practice, and about whether it's always wrong at all. Flag which parts of the activity push one of those views and which parts are neutral.

Also tell me what will predictably go wrong when this is run—people playing nice because they're being watched, a group that all contributes and leaves nothing to debrief, students ganging up on one kid—and how to handle it without killing the point. Include an honest note on which features of the phenomenon the game can't reproduce: real stakes, long-term reputations, exit options, the fact that in life the label 'parasite' is often applied to people who can't rather than won't contribute, and that institutional rules partly decide who looks like a parasite in the first place. What should the students come away seeing? That the resentment is real and they can feel it, but the step from resentment to a moral verdict is less automatic than it feels.
