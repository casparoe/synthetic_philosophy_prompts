I am a novelist, and for years I have been trying to write characters who do terrible things without turning them into stage villains. The difficulty is that the more I study real perpetrators, the less confident I am about what the word 'evil' actually picks out. I would like a working definition of 'evil' as applied to persons, one that separates evil from ordinary wrongdoing, from callousness, and from structural injustice, and that gives a definite verdict on each of the following cases:

1. A man who kidnaps and tortures a stranger for the pleasure of watching him suffer. I take this to be a clear case of evil.
2. A mid-ranking official in a totalitarian state who, without any particular hatred, diligently organizes the transport that carries thousands of people to camps. He would be genuinely distressed if asked to strike anyone himself, and he seems mostly concerned with doing his job competently. This is the kind of person Hannah Arendt described in her account of the Eichmann trial, and her suggestion that what made him horrifying was not demonic depth but its absence — a kind of unthinking, conventional shallowness — is part of why I no longer trust my intuitions here.
3. A pharmaceutical manager who, afraid of losing her job, signs a document she has not fully scrutinized, with the result that a dangerous drug remains on the market and several patients die. She never intended anyone harm.
4. A man who notices a child struggling in a shallow pond and walks on, not from malice but because he is late and assumes someone else will help.
5. A legal and economic order that predictably keeps an entire class of people destitute, with no single decision-maker ever intending that outcome.
6. A devout man who sincerely believes he is saving souls, and on that ground forcibly separates children from their parents and has them re-educated.

I think (1) is evil, and I suspect that (5) is not evil in the same sense, though I am not certain; if the definition has to split into two senses to handle it, I want that said plainly. The middle cases pull in different directions: some suggest evil is in the motive, some in the act, some in what a person becomes, some in what a system does regardless of anyone's state of mind. Propose the definition, run it through all six cases, and tell me where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and where you would revise the definition or defend a surprising verdict.
