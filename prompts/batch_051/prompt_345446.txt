I am taking an upper-level course this term on obligations to future generations, and I have been trying to connect one unit on existential risk with another on the simulation argument. The result is a principle I have been calling the Simulation Cap, and I would like an honest assessment of it.

The Cap begins with the standard simulation argument's trilemma: either (1) almost every civilization at roughly our stage goes extinct before it can run large numbers of ancestor simulations, or (2) almost every civilization that reaches that capacity chooses not to run them, or (3) we are almost certainly living in one. Longtermist arguments typically depend on the thought that our present choices could steer an astronomically large future. The Cap says that this kind of far-future expected value should be multiplied by one's credence that we occupy the only branch in which such a future exists in the required sense: branch (2), where a real, nonsimulated posthuman future is out there for our descendants and our world is not a simulation. In branch (1) the vast future simply does not happen. In branch (3) there may be a long future inside the simulation, but it is not the future longtermism needs, because its continuation, scope, and boundary conditions depend on simulators rather than on anything our actions can predictably influence. So an intervention justified almost entirely by its expected effects on humanity a billion years from now would have that expected value capped twice over: once because the disjuncts concentrate much of the probability in branches where no influenceable far future exists, and again because, conditional on branch (3), the apparent far future is not an appropriate object for longtermist decision theory at all. A friend in the course says this double-counts and merely relabels ordinary uncertainty, but I cannot see exactly where. Where does the Simulation Cap go wrong? Give me the strongest objections you can, and tell me candidly whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable.
