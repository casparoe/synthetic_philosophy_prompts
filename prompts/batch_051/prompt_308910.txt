For much of the twentieth century, the strongest case against interpersonal comparisons of utility rested on an operational constraint rather than on a direct metaphysical claim: a person's utility was held to be evidenced only by observable choice, and choice evidence identifies a utility function only up to positive affine transformations, leaving no interpersonally shared zero point or unit. From this it was concluded that statements comparing levels or differences of utility across persons are either meaningless or covert value judgments. Since then, cross-person comparisons have become routine in applied work—self-reported life-satisfaction scales, quality-adjusted life years, and experience-sampling measures are all used to assert that one person or group is better off than another, and sometimes by how much. Does the classical argument against interpersonal comparability survive this development, and if so, in what form? I would like the analysis to separate three questions. First, which premise is actually under pressure: the behaviorism about admissible evidence; the claim that utility is represented only as an ordinal or, at best, affine family of scales; or the thesis that interpersonal comparability is intrinsically a matter of evaluation rather than fact. Second, distinguish comparisons of levels, differences, and ratios of utility, and say which of these a given instrument—a 0–10 life-satisfaction scale, say, or a quality-adjusted life year—can support without further assumptions, and which assumptions (a shared anchor or zero, a common unit, interpersonal calibration of response categories) would have to be added for each. Third, state the strongest defensible remainder of the anti-comparability position: if it cannot be saved in its original form, say what weaker restriction survives, and whether the argument is best described as refuted, repaired, or redirected.
