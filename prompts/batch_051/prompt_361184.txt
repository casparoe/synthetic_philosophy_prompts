You are a philosopher of action and decision theory. I am collecting arguments for the claim that deliberation crowds out prediction: while an agent is genuinely deliberating between two options, she cannot rationally assign a subjective probability to one of her own still-open choices.

Here are the arguments I already have.

(1) The stance argument: forming a probability about one's own choice requires taking a theoretical, spectator stance toward it, whereas deliberation requires taking a practical, agential stance; one cannot simultaneously occupy both toward the same action.
(2) The feedback argument: any self-assigned probability becomes an input to the expected utilities that drive the choice, thereby changing the very dispositions it purports to measure, so there is no stable probability out there for it to track.
(3) The admissibility argument: a rational decision procedure must not let an agent treat her own choice as evidence about states that are merely correlated with it, but probabilities over one's own choices invite exactly this evidential bootstrapping, as in Newcomb-style cases.
(4) The baselessness argument: before deliberation concludes, the only available grounds for such a probability are provisional intentions, which are disguised decisions rather than evidence, or facts about character that leave the choice underdetermined; so the assignment always lacks a non-arbitrary epistemic basis.

I want one more argument for the same claim that is not on this list and not a rewording, special case, or combination of these four. Take your time and brainstorm several candidates before settling on the strongest one. Then state it, say in a sentence or two why it is genuinely different from each item above, and tell me honestly whether it is any good. In your assessment, say what the strongest objection to it is, what consideration would make you revise that assessment, and where your reasoning about this is most likely to fail.
