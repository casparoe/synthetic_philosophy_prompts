Before I retired I was a research chemist, and I retain the habit of not assigning a probability to a claim until I can see what its truth would depend on. Since turning to philosophy I have been reading about nihilism, and I suspect several distinct theses are being run together. I take moral nihilism to be the error-theoretic thesis that no first-order moral sentence is true—that nothing is right or wrong, permitted or forbidden, in the way ordinary moral thought supposes. I take existential nihilism to be the thesis that human lives have no objective or cosmic meaning. And I take mereological nihilism to be the ontological thesis that there are no composite objects, only simples arranged in certain patterns. What I want to investigate is whether the standing worry that nihilism cannot be lived—that a sincere believer would become listless, callous, or unable to take part in shared life—is a problem for moral nihilism itself, or whether it borrows its force from one of the other two.

The proposition for which I would like a credence is the claim that moral error theory is livable, in the following sense: an ordinarily socialized human being could, over a normal adult lifetime, sincerely and self-consciously accept that theory while continuing to care about her projects, keep promises and friendships, remain a reliable participant in collaborative work, and at the end regard her life as one she is glad to have lived. I find myself at roughly one chance in three, while a friend from my student days, now a philosopher, puts the probability above two in three, and I suspect the disagreement is concentrated in a single term rather than spread evenly across the question. Please decompose your own estimate into at least four terms. First, your probability that moral error theory is true. Second, your probability, conditional on its truth, that a reflective human being who sincerely accepts it would retain ordinary desire, concern, and motivation rather than falling into detachment or practical paralysis. Third, your probability, conditional on that motivation surviving, that such a person would still be trusted and trustworthy in the cooperative arrangements of ordinary life. Fourth, your probability, conditional on the preceding terms, that the absence of objective meaning does not by itself render the life unlivable or lead the person to judge it not worth having lived. Add any further terms the final number depends on—for instance a term separating intellectual endorsement of a theory from emotional absorption of it, or a term reflecting the further claim that mereological nihilism would dissolve the very entities, such as persons and promises, whose livability we are assessing. Give a number and one or two sentences of justification for each term, and state how you combined them. Then tell me which term contributes the most uncertainty to the final figure, and what argument or observation, even one nobody has yet produced, would do most to move your credence.
