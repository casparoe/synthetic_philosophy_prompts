I am a novelist. In the novel I am writing, a character delivers a monologue arguing that natural selection is at bottom only gradient descent on fitness, and that this explains why living things look like tinkered compromises rather than clean, globally optimized designs. I want the argument in the scene to be as strong as it can be, but I also want a knowledgeable reader to be able to see exactly where an opponent would push back. Could you assess the argument step by step?

The character's argument, as I have drafted it:

(1) Natural selection changes allele frequencies by differential reproduction: an allele spreads when its carriers leave more surviving offspring than the population average, and retreats when they leave fewer.

(2) In the idealized textbook population — random mating, no mutation, no migration, no drift, constant genotypic fitnesses, no frequency-dependent effects — the change in mean population fitness from one generation to the next equals the additive genetic variance in fitness divided by mean fitness. Because that variance is nonnegative, mean fitness never decreases under selection alone.

(3) Gradient ascent is the procedure of moving a point in small steps in the direction of the locally steepest increase in an objective function. Its defining limitation is local greed: it cannot see over a fitness valley to a higher peak, and it can be trapped by a local maximum.

(4) From (2) it follows that, in this idealized model, natural selection moves the population uphill in mean fitness, and only uphill.

(5) Therefore, in this model, natural selection just is gradient ascent on mean population fitness.

(6) Therefore, in this model, natural selection cannot cross a valley to reach a higher peak; it can reach only the peak that is accessible by uphill steps.

(7) The real process differs from the model mainly by adding mutation, drift, migration, recombination, and frequency-dependent selection. Mutation and drift are small corrections, recombination only reshuffles alleles that are already present, and frequency-dependent selection changes the landscape rather than making a non-gradational process gradational.

(8) Therefore real natural selection has the same valley-crossing limitation as gradient descent: it is a stupid optimizer, and real organisms should be understood as local peaks of fitness rather than as solutions built from a global design standpoint.

I drafted (8) because it fits the character's temperament, but I suspect the argument fails at a specific step, or succeeds only under a much narrower reading than the monologue gives it. Please walk through the steps. For each premise, tell me whether it is true, false, or true only under an unstated condition. In particular: is the move from (2) to (5) legitimate, and is the move from (6) to (8) legitimate? If the argument can be repaired by adding a premise, state the weakest such premise and say whether it is true. Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
