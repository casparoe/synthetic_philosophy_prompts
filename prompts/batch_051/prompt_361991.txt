I am drafting a set of commitments about moral trade for a political philosophy working group, and I would like a consistency audit before I circulate them. By "moral trade" I mean the practice of exchanging concessions across distinct value-laden issues, each party yielding where it cares less and gaining where it cares more; I contrast this with compromise, where both sides split a difference along a single evaluative axis. My commitments are: (a) some acts are wrong to perform regardless of their beneficial consequences, and being embedded in a larger advantageous deal does not cancel their wrongness; (b) moral trade is substantially underused and is often morally better than either principled intransigence or compromise; (c) moral trade differs from compromise in kind, not merely in degree, because no value is watered down—each party only sets aside a lower-weight consideration while retaining its full scheme of values; and (d) a legislator who votes for a bill she regards as partly unjust in order to secure another legislator's vote for a bill she regards as vastly more important has acted well rather than corruptly, provided she has not misled her constituents. Which of these are in genuine tension, which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection, and what is the least costly revision that restores consistency? Please rank the genuine tensions by severity and say, for each, which commitment would have to give.
