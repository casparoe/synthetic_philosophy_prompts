I have been trying to make sense of Aristotle's account of natural slavery in Book I of the Politics. He writes: "For he is by nature a slave who is capable of belonging to another （and that is why he does so belong）, and who participates in reason so far as to apprehend it but not to possess it; for the animals other than man are subservient not to reason, by apprehending it, but to feelings." The criterion "apprehends reason but does not possess it" sounds to me like a testable psychological claim: a natural slave would be someone who can grasp and obey an instruction whose rationale he cannot formulate for himself. If that is right, would Aristotle be committed to saying that anyone who satisfied this condition—say, a person trained from infancy to defer to others and never deliberate about ends—would be a natural slave and justly enslaved, even if the condition was caused by upbringing rather than by nature? I am less interested in whether the conclusion is repellent than in whether his argument really is meant to work as a piece of empirical classification.
