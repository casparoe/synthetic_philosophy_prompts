I'm a retired geologist — oil and gas, mostly — so I spent forty years deciding whether to drill on maybe a 20% or 30% chance of a big find. I've never been allergic to expected value. Lately I've been reading philosophy and trying to get straight on what I actually think about tiny probabilities and enormous payoffs. I have gut calls on a bunch of cases, and I'd like to know if theres a principle behind them.

Here goes.

1. A friend offers me a fair coin toss: heads I get $120, tails nothing. Or he just gives me $50. I'd take the toss.
2. But change the amounts: I'd rather take a sure $900 than a 50% chance at $2000, even though the expected value of the gamble is $1000.
3. The St Petersburg game — flip a coin until tails, and the payoff doubles with each heads. I wouldn't pay more than about $30 to play, infinite expected value or not.
4. A completely above-board lottery, run by a trustworthy outfit with audited random drawings, sells tickets for $1. The prize is a trillion dollars and the odds are exactly one in a billion, so the expected value is $1000. I'd buy the ticket, and not just for the fun of it. I think buying it is actually the right call.
5. A stranger on the street says that if I give him $10, he'll use a machine he invented to make a billion dollars appear in my bank account tomorrow. He won't explain how it works or show any evidence. I don't pay.
6. Same idea but bigger: another stranger says he has a button that will destroy the world, and he'll press it unless I pay him $10. I put the chance he's telling the truth at something like one in 10^20. I still don't pay, though this one nags at me in a way #5 doesn't.
7. I strongly support spending a few billion dollars a year on asteroid detection and deflection, even though a civilization-ending impact in any given year is on the order of one in a million. That seems obviously sensible to me.
8. I'd also fund, in a heartbeat, a careful peer-reviewed research program that has a one-in-a-hundred-thousand chance of producing a treatment that saves ten million lives over the next century, if it costs a million dollars.
9. Now the one that's been bothering me. The same trustworthy lottery outfit from #4 also sells a $1 ticket with a one-in-10^18 chance of winning 10^30 dollars. Expected value is a trillion dollars. My gut says I wouldn't even bother buying it, and I wouldn't think I'd made a mistake by skiping it. But I can't square that with #4.

Is there a principle that captures all or nearly all of these? Walk me through the candidate principles you'd try, which verdicts each one can't handle, and which of my gut calls I should give up. And whatever the best principle is, what does it say about a further case: an absolutely well-calibrated model says there's a one-in-10^12 chance that a specific asteroid will cause the same kind of civilization-ending impact mentioned in #7. Should the fact that the probability is now one-in-a-trillion instead of one-in-a-million change my support for spending on deflection, and if so why?
