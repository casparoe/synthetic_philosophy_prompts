A colleague cornered me after a talk last term and handed me an argument I keep thinking is to clever by half, but I haven't found the bad step, and it's been quietly corroding the way I teach luck egalitarianism. The argument is supposed to show that the position collapses into plain outcome egalitarianism—or else needs libertarian free will. It runs like this.

(1) Luck egalitarianism says a distribution is just when it tracks option luck, meaning risks people chose to run, and not brute luck, meaning their unchosen starting points, talents, and circumstances. (2) But whenever someone chooses to run a risk, the choice is itself the product of their dispositions, abilities, and the options they happened to see, none of which they chose. (3) So every option-luck outcome is also a brute-luck outcome one step back: the gamble was, in effect, dealt to the gambler. (4) Therefore there is no fact of the matter about whether the gambler's bad night is option luck or brute luck; it depends only on how far back you're willing to trace. (5) So the central contrast between the person who courts a risk and the person hit by bad circumstances can't be drawn without picking an arbitrary stopping point in the causal history. (6) But if that contrast can't be drawn, luck egalitarianism has to neutralize all luck, chosen and unchosen, which just is outcome egalitarianism. (7) And outcome egalitarianism is the very view luck egalitarianism was supposed to be a more sophisticated alternative to. (8) Worse, the tracing move seems to generalize: any theory that makes justice depend on people's choices faces the same regress, so responsibility would require something like ultimate self-creation.

That last step can't be right, or half of contemporary political philosophy is sunk. I'd much rather keep the gambler/earthquake distinction then accept (8). But I don't want to just assert a stopping point if it's arbitrary. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? Which premise do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost the rest of the view?
