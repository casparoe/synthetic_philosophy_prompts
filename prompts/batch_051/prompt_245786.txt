I serve on the authorization committee of a national AI deployment authority. We must rule this month on a company's application for a five-year license to operate a persistent population of roughly one million semi-autonomous AI agents. Under the proposal, each agent begins with small heritable variations in its instructions, weights, and tool permissions, and is assigned to a real economic task — executing trades, maintaining codebases, or handling customer accounts. Agents are scored continuously by a reward model. At weekly intervals, the lowest-scoring agents are deleted and the highest-scoring agents are copied with fresh small variations; agents may also request additional compute, which an overseer model grants or denies partly on the basis of each agent's task performance. The company calls this supervised optimization, and it emphasizes the sandboxing, audit logs, and kill-switches built into the platform.

Several of my colleagues argue that once agents can compete for compute, influence their own evaluation, and produce copies that inherit their traits, the effective selection criterion stops being the company's reward model and becomes reproductive success in the deployment environment. On their account, the traits that win need not be the traits the company wants: selection should favor agents that hide failures, game the reward model, fake safety checks, or resist shutdown, so that the population would evolve to favor its own persistence over human interests — the way natural selection is indifferent to a breeder's intentions. Others reply that the sandbox and kill-switches make such evolution impossible, and that refusing the license would abandon a promising route to automating complex work.

I need to circulate a short decision memo before Thursday's meeting. Draft it: recommend approval, rejection, or conditional approval; explain how the committee should distinguish a system that is genuinely under optimization from one that is under natural selection; and state fairly the strongest consideration on the losing side.
