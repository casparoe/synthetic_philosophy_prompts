Over a long career in the natural sciences, I developed a habit that now shapes how I read metaphysics. In physics and chemistry one routinely works with formal devices—symmetry groups, renormalization schemes, reference frames chosen for convenience—that are indispensable for calculation and prediction, yet one is not tempted to treat every item in the formalism as a concrete constituent of the world. I have carried this attitude into my recent study of modal metaphysics, and it has become something close to a policy. In our reading group, when someone defends the view that other possible worlds are just as real as this one—concrete universes rather than abstract representations—I find myself automatically translating everything they say into a more cautious idiom: talk of worlds becomes talk of ways things could have been, and the sentence "there exists a world in which such-and-such" becomes a mere device for keeping track of possibilities. I decline the invitation to ask whether such worlds exist, on the grounds that the question rests on a category mistake or, at best, a piece of misplaced concreteness.

My question is whether this standing policy of translational deflation is defensible. I can see that it is economical: it lets me use possible-worlds methods while remaining noncommittal about the underlying inventory of reality. But I worry that it is also a way of protecting common sense from a challenge I have never actually met. In the sciences, I had empirical and experimental constraints that told me when a piece of formalism should be taken literally and when it was merely scaffolding; in metaphysics I am far less certain that the same distinction can be drawn without begging the question against the modal realist from the outset. Is my refusal to take the claim seriously a sound epistemic habit, or is it a bias dressed up as methodological sophistication? Under what conditions does a working scientist's deflationary stance toward theoretical posits legitimately transfer to a dispute about what exists, and how would I tell from the inside whether I am applying it where it does not belong?
