Friend and I were up half the night arguing about whether it even makes sense to blame a country, rather than the actual ministers and generals, for starting the First World War. I kept coming back to the Fischer controversy: the way one German historian's book in the early 1960s argued that imperial Germany had deliberately risked and partly willed a European war to revise the status quo, and the way that blew a hole in West German public memory. So here's the counterfactual I want you to think through. Suppose that in 1919 the Allies, under American pressure, leave any war-guilt clause out of the Treaty of Versailles entirely—no Article 231, no legal hook for collecting reparations on that basis. Everything else about the treaty stays the same. Predict the shape of German and European debate about responsibility for the war from 1920 to 1980. In particular, does something like a Fischer thesis still emerge in the 1960s, and does it still produce a political firestorm, or does the missing clause change which arguments are even available? I want less of a yes or no than the causal chain: which factors—reparations, Weimar politics, Nazi propaganda, interwar document collections, the Cold War, East German archives—were doing the real work, and which of your predictions would fall apart if one of those factors changed. Where are you confident, and where are you guessing?
