I teach a philosophy of science course for juniors and seniors — 16 students, one 75-minute session — and I want a simulation/game that makes them feel why "right but early" is such a slipperly defense of a failed prediction. I'm thinking specifically about Malthus-style population-resource predictions, where the predicted collapse keeps not happening but people keep the basic story alive by saying the timing was off. I want the students to actually be the ones doing the rescuing, not just hearing about it from me afterwards. By the end I want them to be able to say, in their own words, what it actually takes to falsify a long-run prediction and why "right but early" is a claim about timing that's almost impossible to cash out ahead of time.

The game: four teams of four, each team managing a little world with a population number and a food supply. Keep the model dead simple — population doubles every three rounds, food goes up by a fixed amount each round, and there's a standing "Malthusian" forecast that the population will outrun food and collapse around round 6. But at the start of each round, draw an event card: a new crop, a plague, a land expansion, a war, whatever. The event changes the numbers just enough that the collapse moves to a later round. After each non-collapse, every team has to publicly choose one of two moves: (a) declare the forecast falsified and stop using it, or (b) keep it and add a rescue clause ("the mechanism was right, the date was wrong," "we just didn't model technology," "the collapse is now at round 9"). I want the scoring to make both moves genuinely attractive — points for calling a collapse that actually happens, a penalty for crying wolf too many times, but also points for holding onto a theory that eventually gets vindicated, so the "right but early" team can win if they time it well. Make sure the rules force each team to commit in writing before the event card is revealed, so they can't just hindsight their way through.

What I want back: the full rules, the card list, a round-by-round timeline for 75 minutes, a scoring scheme that doesn't use real money, the debrief, and two extra things. The debrief should map what happened in specific rounds to the actual conceptual machinery: falsification vs. adding auxiliary hypotheses, the difference between "the theory is false" and "the theory hasn't failed yet," and why a prediction with no fixed deadline is so hard to kill. The two extras: tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (e.g., teams refusing to ever commit, or treating every rescue as cheating) and how to fix it on the fly, and be honest about what the game can't reproduce — my guess is it makes the Malthusian side look more reasonable than it actually was, becuase in the game the mechanism really is simple and the shocks really do keep saving it, whereas in the real case the original arithmetic-vs-geometric story was already off, not just early. But I want your take on that.
