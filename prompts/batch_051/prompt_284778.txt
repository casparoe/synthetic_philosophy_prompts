I'm a postdoc in a lab that does gain-of-function work on flu, and my labmates and I have been arguing for three weeks about whether to post a preprint that would basically tell people how to make a nastier strain. There are nine of us with pieces of the data, and the submission system lets any one of us post the draft unilaterally. We keep going in circles, because individually each of us seems to want to post, but whenever we talk about pooling everything we all get cold feet and say we'd vote to bury the data. I've heard people call this kind of thing the unilateralist's curse, and the argument as I understand it runs:

(1) If acting has positive expected value given *all* your evidence, you should act.

(2) Each of the nine, given only her own private dataset, would conclude posting is positive expected value; if she were the sole decider she'd hit submit.

(3) Since any one of us can post unilaterally, and each of us is rational, at least one of us should already have posted. So the group posts even if most of us would vote against it.

(4) If all nine datasets were pooled, every one of us would conclude posting is negative expected value and would vote to destroy the data.

(5) So a bunch of fully rational people, each of whom would oppose posting on the pooled evidence, will nonetheless post — and nobody involved is making a mistake.

Step (5) is where i get stuck, but I can't tell whether the argument is sneakily right or whether one of the steps is doing something illegal. I half-suspect step (3) conflates "would decide X in isolation" with "will decide X as one of nine," but when I try to say exactly what extra evidence the non-isolated person has, I can't make it precise.
