I rewatched Match Point last night for maybe the fourth time, and I've also been reading on my own about this idea some philosophers call moral luck: the thought that how much we blame someone, or how guilty we judge them to be, depends partly on things they never controlled. The stock example is two drunk drivers, one swerves into a kid and one doesn't, and we treat the first like a monster and the second like someone who made a dumb mistake, even though the only difference between them is where the kid happened to be standing. The film seems literaly built around that idea, from the opening bit about the tennis ball hanging on the net to the whole murder plot: Chris kills Nola, the ring he throws away bounces off the railing and lands somewhere he never intended, and that one bit of pure luck is a big part of why he doesn't get caught and just goes back to his comfortable life.

I want to write something for a small film discussion group reading the movie through that lens. But I'm not sure the movie is actually an argument for moral luck as opposed to just a story about a cold guy who gets lucky and never feels much about it. So what would a real reading look like if you used the idea of moral luck as more than a slogan? Where does the film dramatize the different kinds of luck people talk about—the luck of what kind of person you are, the luck of what situation you get dropped into, and the luck of how things happen to turn out—and where does it maybe confuse moral luck with something else, like fate, or the idea that the universe is just indifferent so nothing matters anyway? Is there a scene where the film undercuts the very view it seems to be pushing, or where an honest reading has to admit the film doesn't really take a position and just uses luck as window dressing?

Also, since I don't have the background to know this: could you flag what's basically settled among people who work on moral luck versus what's genuinely still contested, because I don't want to write a whole piece that hinges on some disputed take and presents it like it's the standard view.
