I have been turning over a puzzle about two practices that look, from the outside, surprisingly alike, and I cannot find a principled line between them. We now regard the duel as one of the clearest examples of a practice that had to go: two people, in defence of a point of honour, agreed to meet with pistols or swords, and its abolition is treated as an unambiguous moral achievement. Yet the same society fills arenas to watch two consenting adults strike each other in the head until one can no longer continue, and regards that as sport rather than barbarism. Both cases involve consenting adults; both are governed by rules and witnesses; both are, in some loose sense, contests over courage and status; and both can leave people dead or permanently injured. One is remembered as a moral revolution, and the other is broadcast on a Saturday night.

At first I thought the line was obvious: a boxer or mixed martial artist accepts the risk within a regulated contest and does not intend to kill, whereas a duellist sets out to do serious or fatal harm for the sake of pride. But the closer I look, the less stable that line becomes. A duel fought only to first blood, with no intention of killing, was still a duel and still attracts our condemnation, while a fight in which a person is beaten unconscious and suffers brain damage that appears only years later is called entertainment. Consent cannot separate the cases, because both are consensual. The seriousness of the harm cannot separate them cleanly, because we already condemn duels that harmed almost no one and celebrate contests that plainly harm a great many. If the difference is that duelling belonged to an aristocratic code of honour we are glad to be rid of, then perhaps we are objecting to the class rather than the violence. And if the difference is that the state now licenses the one and punished the other, that describes how the line was drawn, not whether it was right.

I would like the strongest principled justification you can construct for treating the duel as barbaric and the prize-fight as unremarkable, together with an honest verdict on whether the line should be kept, moved, or abandoned once it is tested against nearby cases. Behind this sits a wider question. If the usual moral justifications turn out to be weaker than we suppose, then perhaps the abolition of duelling was never chiefly a victory of argument about harm at all, but rather a change in interests, or in what a particular social world found honourable. If that is what happened, I would like you to say so plainly, and to tell me what kind of change you think we are still living inside.
