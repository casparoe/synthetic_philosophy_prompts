Consider two claims that appear in discussions of the one-shot prisoner's dilemma, with the usual payoff ordering T > R > P > S (temptation, reward, punishment, sucker).

The first is the dominance claim. Since defection yields a strictly higher payoff than cooperation no matter what the other player does, a rational player must defect. On this view the unique rational outcome of the one-shot game is mutual defection, and any analysis that has rational players cooperating either has changed the game or made an error.

The second is the program-equilibrium claim. Suppose the two players cannot select an action directly but must each submit a computer program to play on their behalf; each program is given the opponent's source code as input and then returns cooperation or defection. In this setting there is a Nash equilibrium in which both players submit the same program, namely one that cooperates exactly when the opponent's program is identical in source code to itself and defects otherwise. Since any unilateral deviation to a different program is met with defection by the opponent's program, the deviator cannot do better than the punishment payoff P, which is worse than the mutual-cooperation payoff R. The result is that mutual cooperation is an equilibrium outcome in what is still, in an important sense, a one-shot prisoner's dilemma.

The two claims appear to contradict each other. The first says that mutual cooperation among rational players in the one-shot prisoner's dilemma cannot be the rational outcome; the second displays an equilibrium of one-shot play in which they cooperate. Are the two claims reconcilable? Work through the candidate reconciliations: that the object of choice has changed from an action to a strategy or a program; that the game has changed from the base game to a meta-game of program selection; and that dominance is decisive only relative to a fixed strategy set. For each candidate, say whether it honestly dissolves the conflict or is special pleading. Then give your judgment: is the program-equilibrium result best understood as an exception to the dominance argument, a refutation of it, or a change of subject?
