You're a professional philosopher of mind, and I'd like you to think carefully and brainstorm a few candidates before you commit. I've been going in circles on Kim's causal exclusion argument — the one that says every physical effect has a sufficient physical cause, so a mental state can cause a physical effect only if it either just is that physical cause or else it overdetermines the effect, and overdetermination is supposed to be the bad kind. I'm trying to build a taxonomy of the genuinly distinct ways people have resisted this, and so far I have five:

1. Bite the identity bullet: the mental cause just is the physical cause, and all the "qua mental" talk is hand-wringing about nothing.
2. The counterfactual reply: "M caused P*" is true as long as P* wouldn't have happened without M, even when there's also a sufficient physical cause; causation isn't a zero-sum race between rival producers.
3. Deny physical causal closure outright and be an interactionist dualist.
4. Accept the overdetermination but say it's benign, because the mental cause and its physical realizer aren't independent — the mental supervenes on, or is realized by, the physical.
5. The proportionality move: the physical realizer is too specific to count as the cause, and the mental state is the one at the right grain.

I'm pretty sure there's at least one more strategy that isn't a special case, combination, or relabeling of any of these. Give me one, say as precisely as you can how it differs from each item on the list, and then tell me honestly whether it actually answers Kim or just changes the subject. If your best candidate is the interventionist line, fine, but then you have to explain why it isn't just item 2 with extra steps.
