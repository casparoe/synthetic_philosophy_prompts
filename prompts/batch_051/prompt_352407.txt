Contemporary psychiatric diagnosis is organized around checklists. For many disorders, no single symptom is necessary or sufficient; instead, a patient qualifies by meeting a threshold number of criteria drawn from a longer list, with at most one or two privileged symptoms singled out. Major depression is a representative case: the current diagnostic scheme requires either depressed mood or loss of interest or pleasure, together with enough further symptoms from a larger list—fatigue, changes in sleep or appetite, feelings of worthlessness, difficulty concentrating, and the like. Two patients can therefore receive exactly the same diagnosis while sharing only a small minority of their symptoms. At the same time, the scheme draws a sharp numerical cut-off between having the disorder and not having it, and clinicians mostly speak and act as if the resulting label names one stable underlying condition.

Reconstruct the theory of concepts that this diagnostic practice presupposes or fits best with. Consider at least these positions: the classical view, on which concepts are defined by necessary and sufficient conditions and membership is all-or-nothing; the prototype or family-resemblance view, on which membership is graded and categories have central and peripheral features; the exemplar view, on which categories are anchored in stored particular cases; and the theory theory, on which categorization depends on causal-explanatory knowledge of the underlying kind. Distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes, what it merely fits well with, and where it is underdetermined—that is, where several different theories of concepts would license exactly the same diagnostic decisions. In particular, say what philosophical work the numerical threshold and the privileged symptoms are doing: does the threshold quietly reintroduce a classical crisp boundary into a prototype-style category, and do the privileged symptoms function as prototypical features, as explicitly codified necessary conditions, or as something else? Finally, say what would have to change—in the manual, in clinical practice, or only in how practitioners describe what they are doing—if the presupposed view were replaced by a consistently classical, consistently prototype-based, consistently exemplar-based, or consistently theory-theoretic account, and whether any of those replacements would actually require different diagnostic decisions.
