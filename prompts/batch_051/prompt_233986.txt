Suppose that in the mid-2030s a mid-sized parliamentary democracy, after a closely fought referendum, legalizes a tightly regulated market in votes: any registered voter may offer their vote for the next election on a public exchange, with a state price floor, anonymity for both parties, a cap of 1.5 percent of all offered votes per buyer, and an unconditional right for the seller to withdraw the offer and vote in person at any time before the polls close. Predict, with your reasoning visible at each step, how this polity looks after three election cycles. Focus on whether the standard wrongs attributed to vote-selling — treating the franchise as an alienable commodity, corrupting its expressive meaning as an act of equal co-authorship, and converting wealth into direct political power — actually materialize under this design, or are instead redirected into circumvention, brokerage, and new forms of canvassing; and say whether the practice loses its repugnance or remains stigmatized, and why. Then distinguish the predictions you regard as robust from those that hinge on particular assumptions about voter motivation or enforcement capacity, and state what would have to be false for the overall prediction to fail.
