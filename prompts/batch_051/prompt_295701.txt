I have been gathering my colleague's settled judgments about moral worth for a problem set in my graduate seminar, and I want to know whether the pattern of her verdicts commits her to a definite view before I put the exercise in front of students. She works in moral psychology, and she gives crisp verdicts on the following vignettes; the names and details are lightly altered, but the structure of the cases is unchanged.

1. A shopkeeper gives a child the correct change, but only because shortchanging the child would cost him repeat custom. She says: no moral worth.
2. A bystander jumps into a canal to pull out a drowning stranger, moved entirely by immediate sympathy, with no thought of duty, praise, or reward. She says: full moral worth.
3. A donor gives to a disaster-relief fund because her name will be printed in the program. She says: no moral worth.
4. A teenager helps his friend run away from an abusive foster placement, even though the teenager believes that running away is wrong and expects to be punished; he acts because his friend is suffering and he cannot bear to leave him there. She says: full moral worth.
5. A nurse covers an extra shift during a staffing crisis because her patients would otherwise go unattended; the thought that agreeing is the right thing to do does not cross her mind. She says: full moral worth.
6. A man signs up for a neighborhood cleanup only because someone he wants to impress signed up. She says: no moral worth.

The case I want to use to test what she is running is this. Rebecca finds a memory stick on a corridor floor. The stick itself is a cheap promotional item; its only significance is that it contains the only copy of a colleague's immigration paperwork, and if the files are lost the colleague will miss a filing deadline and face deportation. Returning the stick is right because it averts that loss; treat the fact that the stick was not hers as having no independent justificatory weight in the case. Rebecca returns it immediately, but solely because she adheres to a strict personal policy of never keeping lost items, which she regards as a matter of honesty. She has not thought about what the colleague stands to lose, and the thought that returning the stick is morally right does not figure in her motivation.

Predict how my colleague would classify Rebecca: full moral worth, partial moral worth, or none. Then give me: (a) the rule or rules consistent with all six earlier verdicts — in the vicinity there are at least a strict from-duty view, on which worth requires acting from duty, a right-reasons view, on which worth requires being moved by the features that make the act right whether or not one thinks of the act as right, and an approved-motive view, on which worth requires only that the act issue from a motive on an independently approved list such as compassion, honesty, and conscientiousness; (b) what each live rule predicts for Rebecca; (c) a committed best guess about her verdict, with your reasons for favoring that interpretation of her record; and (d) one further case, clearly specified, that would discriminate between the rule you favor and its nearest rival. I do not want a survey that leaves every option open.
