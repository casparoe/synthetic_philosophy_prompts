I'm a novelist and I write historical fiction, so I spend a lot of time in excavation reports, museum catalogues and object databases. A while ago I noticed how often an object gets labelled as ritual or ceremonial precisely when nobody can say what it was actually for. I don't mean the obvious cases, like a carved figure found at an altar, but the borderline ones. A pierced bone disc that could have been a spindle whorl. A row of postholes that could have been a granary but gets written up as a shrine. A burnt patch called a sacrificial fire when it could just be a hearth. After seeing enough of these, I adopted a rule for my own reading: when an interpretation claims something about past beliefs or institutions on the basis of an object alone, unless it can point to something independent to back the claim - wear patterns, residues, distribution across sites, repeated associations, texts, ethnography - I mentally translate 'ritual' as 'we don't know' and give the belief-claim zero evidential weight. I've starting to apply the same rule to pop archaeology and museum labels.

My question is whether this rule is actually defensible or just a bias with good posture. I know I'm not an archaeologist, so maybe I'm being unfair to the one kind of inference that's hardest to make from material remains. But it feels as if 'ritual' is a wildcard, and an argument that uses a wildcard isn't much of an argument. I also wonder whether the rule is just my own preference for mundane explanations in disguise. Is my rule too strong? Is there a defensible version of it that a layperson like me can actually run? And am I even entitled to run a rule like this, given that I can't check each site report myself?

Could you answer as a philosopher of archaeology or an epistemologist who works on material-culture inference? Think carefully, and maybe start by steelmanning my rule before you try to break it.
