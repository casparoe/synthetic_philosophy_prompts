I serve on a small committee drafting a university policy on who owns the rights to software, teaching materials, and lab inventions developed with campus resources. At our last meeting two committee members got into an argument about a passage from Thomas Jefferson's letter to Isaac McPherson of August 13, 1813, and the argument ended up consuming the meeting. One of them read this passage aloud:

"It would be curious then, if an idea, the fugitive fermentation of an individual brain, could, of natural right, be claimed in exclusive and stable property. If nature has made any one thing less susceptible than all others of exclusive property, it is the action of the thinking power called an idea, which an individual may exclusively possess as long as he keeps it to himself; but the moment it is divulged, it forces itself into the possession of every one, and the receiver cannot dispossess himself of it. Its peculiar character, too, is that no one possesses the less, because every other possesses the whole of it."

Then, a little later in the same letter:

"Society may give an exclusive right to the profits arising from them, as an encouragement to men to pursue ideas which may produce utility, but this may or may not be done, according to the will and convenience of the society, without claim or complaint from anybody."

The exchange went roughly as follows. First, one member said that Jefferson had already settled the question we were stuck on: intellectual property is not a natural right but a discretionary grant made "as an encouragement," and the phrase "without claim or complaint from anybody" means inventors have no standing to object if the community grants them less or nothing. So the policy should abandon rights-talk and ask only what arrangement produces the most benefit. Second, the other member replied that this conclusion depends on premises Jefferson has not established. Jefferson himself calls the origin of any kind of property a moot question, yet he then treats stable ownership as a gift of social law; someone who holds a labor-based natural-rights view will not grant him that premise. Non-rivalry shows only that a scarcity-based justification for property does not apply to ideas, not that there can be no other basis. She added that Jefferson was writing about inventions and patents, while much of our policy concerns written code and documents, which are expressions rather than ideas. Third, the first member answered that the idea-expression distinction does not do the work she needs: a copied codebase or text is just as non-rival once disclosed as any idea is, and Jefferson's test of "no one possesses the less, because every other possesses the whole of it" applies directly to expressions too. Unless she supplies a further principle distinguishing them, the label does not argue for itself. Fourth, she said the further principle is that the author's labor has brought into existence something of value that did not exist before, and that a claim on it need not depend on scarcity. She also noted that Jefferson himself says a person may exclusively possess an idea by keeping it to himself, so disclosure is the pivot: society must justify overriding the creator's choice to disclose and profit, rather than treating the choice as morally weightless.

I came away genuinely unable to say who was right, and I have to write the policy preamble. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: say which specific points each got right, which moves are weak or question-begging, and what both of them are missing. I want to know what from this argument should actually shape the policy.
