Consider Newcomb's problem, modified as follows. A Predictor with an extremely reliable track record informs you that it has already acted on a distant, causally isolated civilization: if it predicted that you would take only the opaque box, it sent the civilization a cure for an epidemic; if it predicted that you would take both boxes, it sent a blight. You cannot communicate with that civilization, and no choice you make now could causally alter what the Predictor has already done; the Predictor will never observe your actual choice, and its action cannot be enforced, withdrawn, or modified after the fact. Its prediction was formed in advance from a model of your decision procedure. Locally, you face Newcomb's usual boxes: a transparent box containing $1,000, and a sealed opaque box that, in this variation, contains nothing you value. Taking only the opaque box is the act the Predictor demanded and predicted; taking both boxes is the defection.

The familiar conflict between decision theories reappears: a causal/dominance view says to take both boxes, since the civilization's fate is already fixed and the $1,000 is free; an evidential or decision-procedure view says to take only the opaque box, because choosing one box is evidence about the kind of decision procedure you are, and that is the only thing correlated with the cure. Work through each view in detail for this variant. Then answer the following: Is this a genuine acausal threat, a pure prediction, or something neither of those categories captures? Does the one-boxing verdict survive when the reward is not in the box and the sanction cannot be causally enforced? And is taking only the opaque box best understood as a surrogate goal — a goal adopted not because the act itself is valued, but because the act is correlated with the outcome that is actually valued? If so, does the variant show that at least some surrogate goals are rational, or does it show that the one-boxing verdict has been stretched past its breaking point?
