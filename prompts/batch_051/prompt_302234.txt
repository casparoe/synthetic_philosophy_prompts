I teach a small "philosophy for everyday life" discussion group at a public library, and this month we were reading Confucius. One passage in Book 5 of the Analects, in James Legge's translation, has been bugging me for weeks. Tsze-kung says, "What I do not wish men to do to me, I also wish not to do to men," and the Master answers, "Ts'ze, you have not attained to that." I always thought that negative golden rule was the one moral principal I actually tried to live by, so I can't figure out whether Confucius is telling Tsze-kung he's too inconsistent to manage it, or whether the rule itself isn't enough.

So I started collecting my own gut verdicts to see what pattern, if any, is underneath. (1) I stop myself from posting an anonymous comment mocking a co-worker's stutter, because I wouldn't want to be mocked for something I can't control — fine. (2) A friend asks me whether his self-published novel is any good, and I tell him a gentler version of the truth, the kind I'd want to here about my own writing — fine. (3) My sister loves surprise parties, but her best friend has said she'd be mortified to be the center of one; I don't throw the surprise party, even though the golden rule as I'd always heard it says "give her what you'd want" — fine, but it sure isn't what the rule says. (4) A man pounds on my door late at night asking where my neighbor is so he can "talk to her"; I lie and say I don't know — fine, even though a person in his shoes, with his intentions, would want to be told the truth. (5) I find out a colleague padded her résumé to get a promotion we're both up for; if I were the one who'd padded it, I'd want someone to let it slide, but I reported her, and I think I was right. The two that sit uneasily together are (3) and the one I can't settle: my mother has told me for years that if her cancer came back she wouldn't want to be told, and now it has, and I would absolutely want to know if it were me. In (3) the other person's preference wins over what I'd want, but in the second I keep feeling that what I'd want should win over hers.

Is there one cleaned-up principle — a fixed golden rule, or something else entirely — that captures (1) through (5) and tells me what to do about my mother? What does that principle make of Confucius's reply to Tsze-kung, and which of my five settled verdicts, if any, should I give up?
