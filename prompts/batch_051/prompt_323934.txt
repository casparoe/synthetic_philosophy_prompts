I taught physical chemistry for over thirty years before retiring, and one habit I can't shake is that a name ought to pick something out. Aspirin is a molecule; if the molecule turned out not to exist, we'd retract the name or mark it as a historical error. So I'm a bit thrown by my granddaughter's Sherlock Holmes wiki. It has an elaborate constitution for what counts as canon, and their are grown editors with PhDs arguing for hours about whether a bit from a 1940s film counts. The wiki states flatly that Holmes lives at 221B Baker Street and is a violinist, but it treats the famous deerstalker cap as not really true in the stories, mostly because it apparently comes from illustrations and movies rather than Doyle's text. They also have a running dispute about Watson's war wound, which the stories put in his shoulder in one place and his leg in another; the editors can't agree whether the wiki should record both, pick one, or flag the contradiction as an authorial slip. It's a whole database of facts about a man who never existed, policed as if something important goes wrong when a false fact slips in.

I want to understand what philosophical commitments that practice carries. Does running a wiki that records of Holmes that he played the violin commit the editors to saying Holmes exists in some sense, the way a lab notebook entry commits you to the existence of whatever crystallized? Or can an editor cheerfully insist that the name is empty, referring to nothing at all, and still treat the wiki as more than make-believe? And what does the canon line presuppose about where in-fiction truth comes from: the author's intentions, the written texts themselves, early readers, present-day fan consensus? I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory of truth in fiction and empty names that this practice presupposes, say which parts of the practice could be run just as well under several rival theories and are therefore underdetermined, and note what would have to change if someone held, say, a broadly Russellian view of names or a Walton-style pretense view. Please write the answer in American English.
