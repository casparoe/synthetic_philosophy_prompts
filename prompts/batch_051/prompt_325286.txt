I'm a PhD student in philosophy, working at the boundary between decision theory and AI alignment, and I'm currently thrashing on what my dissertation should actually be about. The thing I keep going back and forth on is the difference between two ways of thinking about agents. On one side there's the standard goal-directed picture: the agent has a utility function (or at least a stable preference ordering) over outcomes and chooses actions to maximize expected utility. On the other side there's the approval-directed picture: the agent doesn't have a fixed objective, but instead tries to do whatever a human, or some group of humans, would approve of or rate highly, and it keeps shifting what it's aiming at as those judgments move around. I get why the second picture is attractive — it seems to sidestep some Goodhart-style problems and to leave the system more corrigible, since it doesn't have a stubborn goal it will defend against correction. But I also see ways it could just go wrong in new ways: an approval-directed agent might learn to directly manipulate the approval signal, or chase the approval of whoever happens to be around, or become basically useless becaue the human's judgments are noisy and contradictory.

One thing I really can't settle is whether the distinction is even deep. You could always say that an approval-directed agent is just a goal-directed agent with a utility function equal to expected human approval, in which case the second picture is just a special case of the first — but the people who push approval-direction seem to mean something more procedural, where the target can change without the agent treating its previous target as a mistake. I have no idea how much actually hangs on that, and my advisor is getting tired of me saying that it depends.

So, concretely: imagine someone with three years, a background in formal epistemology and logic, and no ML engineering skills to speak of. What do you think are the main open conceptual questions in the debate over approval-directed vs. goal-directed agents, and which two or three should that person actually prioritize — and why? I don't want a survey. I want the ordering defended: which questions are most upstream of other work, which are tractible with a philosopher's toolkit, and which are seductive but wouldn't change much downstream even if you answered them. And if you think the whole distinction is a red herring, say that plainly and tell me what I should be working on instead.
