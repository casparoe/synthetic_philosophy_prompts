I'm teaching an upper-level epistemology module on pragmatic encroachment and epistemic contextualism, and I want to devote the final two sessions to a single work of fiction rather than the usual vignettes. Assume I know the film and the two positions; give me a reading, not a summary. The work is Twelve Angry Men — the 1957 film and its stage adaptations — in which a jury moves from an initial eleven-to-one vote for conviction to a unanimous acquittal, not because any new evidence is produced but because one holdout makes the rest re-examine the evidence they already had, while the standard of proof beyond reasonable doubt becomes a matter of dispute. Read the film through the contextualism/encroachment debate. Make the mapping explicit before drawing conclusions: when the question is whether the defendant's guilt has been established, what should count as the knowing subject — the individual juror, the jury as a group, the instructions — and does the reasonable-doubt standard behave more like a context-sensitive expression whose content shifts with the conversation, or like a fixed threshold that is itself sensitive to the practical stakes of the verdict; where in the deliberation can one find an analogue of the bank-case structure of the same evidence under different stakes. Then say where the film genuinely dramatizes each view, where it drives a wedge between them that the standard cases do not, and where it resists both: the defendant's stakes are grave and constant from the first ballot, so any stakes-threshold story must explain why the jurors' standard appears to move only as the holdout raises the salience of error and exposes their non-epistemic motives. End by telling me what the film adds to the philosophical debate that the bank cases lack, and what a careful critic should refuse to claim on its behalf. Do not reduce the film to an illustration, and do not treat contextualism and pragmatic encroachment as interchangeable.
