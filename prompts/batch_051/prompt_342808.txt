Suppose the standard Haidt-style empirical picture is roughly right: people who identify with the political left rely most heavily on care/harm and fairness/cheating, while people who identify with the political right rely on those two but also grant substantial and comparatively even weight to loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, and sanctity/degradation. A colleague and I have been arguing about whether this descriptive asymmetry tells us anything about which side is closer to being right. She treats it as a purely debunking fact about moral psychology: it shows that different groups have different evolved intuitions, but no group earns rational credentials from its intuition profile. I suspect the asymmetry is not automatically neutral, because some of these foundations may be more epistemically trustworthy than others.

I would like you to settle this dispute by way of an explicit rating. For each of the five foundations — care/harm, fairness/cheating, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, and sanctity/degradation — assign a reliability score from 0 to 1, where 0 means the judgments generated primarily by that foundation are no more likely than chance to track moral-political truth, and 1 means they are fully reliable. Present the five scores as a sorted list or table and give a sentence or two of justification for each. Then use those scores together with the left-right weighting asymmetry described above to state whether a rational observer should update toward the political left, toward the political right, or not at all, and explain the reasoning behind that overall conclusion.
