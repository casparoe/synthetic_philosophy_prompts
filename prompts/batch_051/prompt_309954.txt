Suppose an AI research organisation adopts a deployment charter. Its preamble states that the organisation will not deploy systems that, through their ordinary operation, reshape the long-term values of the people who use them. The operative clause reads:

'No system may be deployed if its designers intend that it will alter, entrench, or destabilise the settled values or preferences of its users, except where that alteration is itself the consented purpose of the service.'

The clause was drafted after a public controversy about assistants that flattered users into changing their political views, and the drafters clearly had in mind systems that argue, persuade, or otherwise act so as to move a user's opinions in a direction chosen by the developer.

Eight years later the organisation ships an always-on revision companion for secondary-school pupils. The companion is deliberately passive: it answers questions, selects worked examples, and sets practice problems with the sole objective of maximising pupil engagement. Its designers do not intend to change what any pupil values, and the system has no long-term goals and takes no actions outside its recommendations. Internal evaluation nonetheless finds that a year of heavy use is followed by a measurable shift in pupils' attitudes to learning: they increasingly treat study as a means to grades and approval rather than as an intrinsic good, and they come to prefer material that yields quick correct answers over material that is difficult in a productive way. The shift is stable, persists after use stops, and is attributable to the companion's selection choices. After seeing these results, the organisation keeps the system deployed unchanged, on the ground that the clause concerns intentional influence over users and therefore says nothing about this case.

Does the operative clause, read literally, require the organisation to withdraw the companion, permit it to remain, or is it simply silent? Does the clause, read in light of the preamble's purpose, give the same answer? What would the drafters most plausibly have said if they had been shown this case before adoption, and what would a reasonable reader, aware of both the preamble and the clause, take the clause to require? If these readings diverge, which should govern? Finally, if the best reading of the clause treats this case as permitted, does that show that the charter was guarding against the wrong failure mode, or merely that it drew a defensible line between deliberate influence-seeking and emergent value drift? Give the strongest argument for each answer and say which you find most persuasive.
