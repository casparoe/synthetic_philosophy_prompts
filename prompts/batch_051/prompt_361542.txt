A friend has been on a big "stop trying to be happy" kick and has convinced herself that the old paradox of hedonism isn't just a cute observation but a strict law: any direct aim at your own happiness makes happiness less likely, because aiming involves monitoring, comparing, and asking "am I happy yet?", which undercuts the absorption that actually produces happiness. So far I'm with her. But the other night she laid out the following chain and I can't find the exit.

(1) If happiness is a byproduct, then it only arises when your goal is something else; you can't deliberately produce a byproduct by making it your goal, since "byproduct" just means "not what you were aiming at."
(2) Therefore, anyone who knows the paradox and tries to apply it to their life has already made happiness their goal again, so the byproduct machinery switches off.
(3) Therefore, the paradox is knowledge you can't act on: only people who don't know it, or don't believe it, get it's benefits.
(4) But that's absurd. People learn about the paradox and then consciously choose absorbing projects, relationships, and hobbies because of the insight, and they often report being happier afterward. That looks like acting on the insight, on purpose, and it somehow works.

She thinks this shows the paradox of hedonism is self-defeating: if you believe it, it stops working; if it works, you're not believing or using it. But I'd rather say the argument proves too much, since it would make true self-knowledge uniquely useless. I'd like to keep as much of the byproduct picture as I can, but (4) feels undeniable to me. Which premise or step do I reject, and what do I loose by rejecting it?
