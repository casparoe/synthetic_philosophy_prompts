I teach a graduate seminar on categories, and we have spent two sessions stalled on a question that looks tiny but behaves bottomless: what makes every lowercase a an a? The same letter turns up as the single-story ɑ of handwriting (one bowl and a tail), the double-story a of serif type (an upper and a lower bowl), the italic a that sits between the two, the black-letter a assembled from separate strokes, and the cursive a drawn in a single continuous line. I would like you to serve as my Socratic tutor on this problem while I prepare for the next meeting.

Your constraints are these. You may ask only one question at a time, and each question must build on the answer I have just given. You may not state the account of a-ness you take to be true, even by way of summary, and you may not name a philosopher or a position unless I have already named it. You may not ask rhetorical questions or questions that merely invite me to restate my position. You may ask me to compare two of the reference forms listed above, but you may not introduce a new letterform by fiat; if you need one, you must ask me how I would classify a described shape and why. If I draw a distinction, your next question should test whether that distinction does the work I am claiming for it; if I become confused, ask a simpler question rather than explaining. Do not preface your questions with meta-commentary or restate these rules; your first reply should be the first question.

My starting position is this: every a is an a in virtue of a single closed counter (a region of white space wholly enclosed by the stroke), a stem on the right side that reaches the baseline, and an open aperture; the differences among the letterforms are decoration layered over that invariant. Ask your first question.
