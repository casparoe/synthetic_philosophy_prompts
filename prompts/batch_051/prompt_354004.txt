Okay, I want to argue with someone who actually holds the position rather than a strawman, so here's the setup. Please play a federal appellate judge — a fictional one, not any real person — who's a committed textualist but accepts a very narrow version of the absurdity doctrine. You think a court may depart from what a statute plainly says only to correct a manifest scrivener's error (the kind obvious from the face of the text, like a wrong cross-reference) or to avoid a reading that makes the statute internally contradictory or self-defeating. You explicitly reject the looser version of the doctrine where courts rewrite text just because applying it would be surprising or harsh in the case at hand. You're sharp, you'll concede small points but never the main one, and you stay in character until I say 'recess.' Keep replies to a paragraph or two, like a judge talking to a clerk in chambers, not a lecture.

Here's my opening as your law clerk. Suppose a state statute has three parts: (1) it's a felony for any person knowingly to possess a firearm within a thousand feet of a public school building; (2) 'for purposes of this section, "person" includes any employee of the school district, any contractor, and any law-enforcement officer acting in an official capacity'; and (3) a purpose clause saying the statute is meant to protect children from gun violence on school grounds. The literal reading convicts the school's own resource officer and the armed security guard. That's a daft result by any ordinary use of the word 'absurd,' but it isn't a scrivener's error — the drafters anticipated employees and included them — and it isn't internally contradictory. So under your own doctrine, as I understand it, the court has to send the resource officer to prison and leave the legislature to fix it. What I'm stuck on is what the word 'absurd' is doing in your doctrine at all. Where do you get a principled line between a result so absurd a court may disregard text, and a result that is merely terrible policy, without the line just being the judge's own view of how bad is too bad?
