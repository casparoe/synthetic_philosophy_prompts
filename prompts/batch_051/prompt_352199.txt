I've spent the past few years reading across behavior genetics and its philosophical and social-scientific critics, and the argument about what a high heritability estimate for a complex trait actually shows seems to me completely stalled. One side — call it the variance-decomposition side — insists that heritability is a local technical statistic: the proportion of phenotypic variance in a particular population, within a particular range of environments, attributable to genetic (usually additive genetic) variance. On this view, a high estimate licenses no inference about how readily the trait could be changed by a novel environmental intervention, no inference about the causes of differences between populations, and no claim about which particular genes do what in any individual. They reach for examples like phenylketonuria, a condition that is about as genetic as a condition gets under the standard definition and yet is routinely prevented by something as low-tech as diet. The other side — call it the causal side — treats this as evasive. They take the same numbers as evidence that, in the ordinary causal sense, these traits are substantially driven by genetic differences among people, and they argue that the statistician's careful gloss cannot survive contact with the way the numbers are actually deployed in psychology, education, and public argument. Both sides have been making versions of these moves for decades, no one seems to move, and the disagreement is not really about a number so much as about what kind of fact the number reports.

My working hypothesis is that this is not one disagreement but at least three glued together: a verbal disagreement about what genetic and inherited mean, a divergence between a descriptive variance question and a causal-manipulation question, and a further disagreement about what a responsible inference from a population statistic looks like in public argument. Diagnose this. Is it substantive or verbal; do the sides differ about facts, about standards of evidence, about which cases count as data, or about starting intuitions that neither side can argue for; is there a hidden empirical or formal question doing the real work; and is the question perhaps ill-posed? And given the diagnosis, say what would actually count as progress — a distinction, a change of vocabulary, an intervention study, a formal result — or argue that progress should not be expected because the sides were never answering the same question.
