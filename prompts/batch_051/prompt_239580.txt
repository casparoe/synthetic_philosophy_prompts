Suppose whole-brain emulation arrives around 2050. By 2070, scanning a human brain produces a standard emulation, and once a mind has been scanned, copying it is basically free, like duplicating a file. Ems can be run at variable speeds: as slow as a thousandth of biological speed, or as fast as a million times biological speed, with electricity, cooling, and server space as the main real constraints. Almost all paid work now happens in a few huge server cities in cheap-energy regions, done by ems. This is roughly the Age of Em scenario, except I don't need you to remember the book; the scenario as I've stated it is what matters.

Now here's the divergence. From the start, an actually-enforced global treaty says two things. First, no em may be copied without the informed consent of the particular em instance being copied — not just the original scanned human. Second, every em instance, simply for existing, receives a guaranteed subsistence stipend: enough server time each month for, say, six hundred subjective hours at normal speed, wether or not it ever works. Anything beyond that — extra hours, speed, memory — it has to pay for.

Reason forward to 2120. How does this world differ from a laissez-faire baseline where copies are property and there's no right to run or to refuse to be copied? Be concrete: what happens to labor markets and to the wages of the humans still outside the server cities; what kinds of inequality and status hierarchy emerge among ems; how does the stipend change the incentives to run fast, to copy docile productive minds, or to specialize; and what political coalitions and moral idioms around copying, deletion, and the stipend harden into law?
