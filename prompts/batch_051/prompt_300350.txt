I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I ended up reading Mill's *The Subjection of Women* after a conversation about default configurations in a permissions system I was designing. The idea I kept returning to was that a well-designed system should not make every user explicitly opt out of every restriction; restrictions should be the thing that has to be positively enabled and justified. Mill seems to me to be making the same point about political argument in the first chapter. He writes:

"In all other cases, the burthen of proof is supposed to lie with the affirmative."

"Again, in practical matters, the burthen of proof is supposed to be with those who are against liberty; who contend for any restriction or prohibition; either any limitation of the general freedom of human action, or any disqualification or disparity of privilege affecting one person or kind of persons, as compared with others. The à priori presumption is in favour of freedom and impartiality."

Here is the asymmetry that bothers me. In most contexts we do seem to run the default the way Mill describes: if someone proposes a restriction on speech, a new criminal punishment, or a legal distinction between groups, we expect them to make the case, and liberty plus equal treatment are treated as the baseline that needs no defense. But with sex-based legal subordination—and with many present-day disputes over sex-specific exclusions, dress rules, labor protections, or parenting presumptions—the default often seems to be the restriction itself. The person asking for equal treatment is the one who has to prove the restriction is unjustified, while the restriction's advocates treat their position as innocent until proven guilty. Mill's book is, in part, an attempt to flip that default for women, but I want to know whether the asymmetry itself is defensible.

Is there a principled reason why sex-based restrictions should carry a different default burden than other liberty restrictions? Or is the reversal just a fossil of the very custom Mill was arguing against? If you defend the asymmetry, defend it against a version of the case where the only relevant difference is that the protected trait is sex rather than, say, religion or political opinion—same asserted public interest, same evidentiary uncertainty, same kind of disparate impact.
