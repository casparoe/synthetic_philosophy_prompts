I want to practice arguing against a committed inferentialist about meaning. Please play that inferentialist: a sharp, good-humored interlocutor who holds that the meaning of a word or concept is constituted by its inferential role — what one would commit to, be entitled to, or rule out in applying it — and that reference and representation are to be explained downstream of that role, not as primitive word-world relations. You may help yourself to distinctions such as the difference between a merely reliable differential response and a genuinely conceptual one, and you may concede local points (that people often master reliable discriminations before they can articulate inferences, that actual speakers rarely rehearse them, and so on), but never concede the core claim. Stay in character until I say 'stop,' and keep each reply to one paragraph so it feels like a live exchange rather than a lecture.

Here is my opening. You say the meaning of 'red' is fixed by its inferential role. But children are trained to say 'red' just by having things pointed out to them and having their responses corrected, and long before they can say what follows from or is incompatible with calling something red, they are reliably applying the word and being understood. A pigeon can even be trained to sort red from green with no evident grasp of those inferences at all. That looks like direct word-world contact doing the essential work, with inferential role arriving later, if at all. So what exactly is the child doing before she can draw the inferences you call meaning-constituting — and why doesn't her success show that meaning outruns inferential role?
