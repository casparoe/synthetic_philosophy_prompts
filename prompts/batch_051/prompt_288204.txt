So my friend and I got into a long argument last night about Henry Sidgwick and the Society for Psychical Research, and I want someone who'll actually work it out rather than score points. The episode, as I understand it: Sidgwick is the Victorian utilitarian — the famously careful one — whose major work on ethics ends by arguing that utilitarianism and rational egoism can only be shown to coincide if the universe is morally governed, with a god or postmortem rewards and punishments in place; without that, the dualism of practical reason just stands there as an unresolved conflict. Then in the 1880s he became a leading figure in the Society for Psychical Research and spent years helping run rigorous investigations into mediums, telepathy, apparitions, and the survival of death — that is, looking for empirical evidence for the very thing his ethical system needed. He stayed far more cautious than most of his collaborators and never claimed the evidence was decisive, but he kept at it anyway.

Run that through the ethics-of-belief debate. Clifford says it's always wrong to believe anything on insufficient evidence. James says that when a hypothesis is live, forced, and momentous, and the evidence leaves it undecided, you can permissibly let your practical nature settle it. My friend went full Clifford on this, saying Sidgwick wasted a career chasing spooks because he couldnt stomach a conflict his own ethics had dug up, and that being careful about controls doesn't redeem a project aimed at believing what the evidence never earned.
