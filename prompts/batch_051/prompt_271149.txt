I'm working on a magazine article about how a nineteenth-century thinker might respond to today's culture of health optimization, and the writer I keep circling back to is Nietzsche. He seems like the most interesting test case: a lifelong invalid who wrestled with migraines, digestive collapse, and periods of near-blindness, and who treated those bodily failures not as a biographical footnote but as a philosophical problem in their own right. In his late autobiographical writing he goes out of his way to discuss meal timing, what he ate and drank, the climates he could and could not think in, and the way a person's metabolism shapes the style of their thought. I want to avoid the cheap version of the argument, which would have him simply sneering at modern wellness from a mountaintop. The more plausible and more interesting reading is that he would have been split against himself: drawn to any technique that turns the body into an instrument of self-overcoming, yet repelled by the moralism, medicalization, and herd conformity that surround so much of contemporary health culture.

With that in mind, I'd like you to write out his imagined positions on three developments that postdate him, treating each as a genuine problem rather than a target for satire. First, continuous self-tracking by wearable devices and the quantified-self movement. Second, GLP-1 receptor agonists such as Ozempic, and the broader project of pharmaceutically suppressing appetite. Third, the rise of influencer-driven diet orthodoxies and the status of something like the gut microbiome as a new locus of moral anxiety. For each one, give the strongest internally Nietzschean case you can construct, and then identify where his own commitments would pull him in the opposite direction. Close with a short assessment of what he would have diagnosed as the real illness beneath the wellness industry's surface, and whether he would have seen that illness as something to flee or something to use.
