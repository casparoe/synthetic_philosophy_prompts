I'm an engineer, not a philosopher, so maybe I'm missing something, but this asymmetry looks to me like a bug in the legal system. If my neighbor's plumbing negligence floods my apartment, I can sue him. If a private contractor does the same thing, I can sue the company. But if a city water main floods my apartment through documented negligence, in alot of places sovereign immunity means I can't recover unless the government has chosen to let itself be sued. Same harm, same negligence, same kind of worker and inspector; only the identity of the defendent changed.

The obvious justifications don't feel like they do the work. Sure, a judgment against the city comes out of tax money, but a judgment against my co-op also comes out of member dues and I can still sue the co-op. Sure, the state makes the law, but that sounds like a reason to hold it to the law, not to exempt it. Sure, voters can punish a bad mayor, but voters can't compensate me for a ruined apartment. I can see why the remedy might need to be different — a compensation fund, insurance, a specialized tribunal — but I can't see why the default should be no liability at all.

Give me the strongest principle you can find for treating the state as special in exactly this way, then test it against police misconduct, a state-run hospital giving the wrong medication, and a school bus crash. I want an honest verdict: keep sovereign immunity, abolish it, or replace it with something specific.
