Imagine that you are helping me prepare for a podcast interview with George Berkeley. The episode is devoted entirely to the master argument for immaterialism: the argument in which Berkeley asks his interlocutor to try to conceive of a sensible object, such as a tree, existing wholly unperceived by any mind, and then notes that the very attempt seems to put the object before a mind after all. I want the interview to stay in the weeds of the argument's logic rather than trade in the familiar slogan about whether a falling tree makes a sound.

Draft nine or ten questions, each with a specific follow-up. The follow-up should begin by briefly paraphrasing the reply Berkeley would most naturally give, and then press that reply rather than merely restating the original question more sceptically. The questions should collectively probe the following fault lines:

1. The inference from inconceivability to impossibility: does the master argument require the slide from an inability to conceive of an unperceived object to the impossibility of such an object, or can it instead be run as a purely dialectical challenge to the materialist's own professed conception of matter?
2. The distinction between the content of a thought and the act of thinking it: Berkeley seems to hold that because the tree is thought of, it is already in the mind; the critic replies that a thought of a tree existing unperceived has a content that outruns what is presently before the thinker. Where does Berkeley think that reply goes wrong?
3. The scope problem: ask whether the same pattern of reasoning would prove that other spirits, God, or times before any finite perceiver existed cannot be conceived, and whether Berkeley can confine the argument to sensible things without that restriction's looking arbitrary.
4. The role of God as the permanent perceiver: does the master argument rule out existence unperceived by any mind whatsoever, or only existence unperceived by finite minds, and how much does that difference weaken the advertised conclusion?
5. The criterion of reality: if a real thing differs from a dream only in its orderliness, coherence, and independence of the finite will, ask what is left of the ordinary distinction between the real and the imaginary once matter is removed.
6. The problem of notions: Berkeley allows that we have a notion of spirits even though we have no idea of them. Ask whether the master argument can be restated for spirits, and if it cannot, whether the materialist could claim an analogous exemption for the concept of matter.

Vary the register so that some questions sound like a sympathetic reconstructionist and others like a precise hostile examiner, but keep every question substantive and focused on the master argument itself. Write the questions in direct speech as I would ask them, and write the whole reply in British English.
