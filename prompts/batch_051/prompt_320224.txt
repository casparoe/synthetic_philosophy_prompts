I'm in a logic course that lately has been all constructivism, and it's messed with my head enough that I keep second-guessing even the proofs I used to think were airtight. We saw this classic argument that there exist irrational numbers a and b such that a^b is rational. It goes:

1. Let x = sqrt(2)^sqrt(2). By the law of excluded middle, x is either rational or irrational.
2. If x is rational, take a = sqrt(2), b = sqrt(2), and we're done.
3. If x is irrational, take a = x, b = sqrt(2). Then a^b = (sqrt(2)^sqrt(2))^sqrt(2) = sqrt(2)^(sqrt(2) * sqrt(2)) = sqrt(2)^2 = 2, which is rational. Done.
4. So either way we've exhibited irrational a and b with a^b rational.

Our prof said that's fine classically but not constructive, and then just moved on, and I've been chewing on it alot. I get that step 1 is the obvious suspect — it straight-up asserts excluded middle for a claim we can't decide. But I want to be sure that's the only place it goes wrong. Is the rest of the argument okay by constructive lights? And does rejecting this proof mean a constructivist has to be agnostic about the conclusion, or does the conclusion itself still count as established some other way?
