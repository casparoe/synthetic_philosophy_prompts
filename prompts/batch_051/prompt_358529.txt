I have no formal background in philosophy or risk assessment, but for several years I have followed the published rulings of my town's Community Safety Board, and I have been trying to work out what principle actually explains their votes. Five decisions seem important to me.

First, the board approved the widening of a highway through town. It accepted that the two-year construction would create some additional risk for workers and drivers, but concluded that removing a dangerous merge would, over the life of the road, very probably prevent more deaths and serious injuries than the work would cause.

Second, the board rejected a proposed public housing building for the elderly that would have stood across the street from a fuel-storage depot. The applicant's own assessment put the chance of a depot fire spreading to the building at less than one in a thousand in any given year, but the board said a single fire could kill dozens of the residents, who would be placed there by a waiting list rather than by genuine choice.

Third, the board approved a primary school's plan to install a climbing wall in its gymnasium. The board heard that the realistic harms are broken wrists and similar injuries, that a child participates only when a parent has signed a permission form, and that a child may decline at any time.

Fourth, the board rejected a rail company's application to send two ammonia trains per week through the town centre. The company put the chance of a serious leak at somewhere around one in a million per trip, but the board stressed that a leak near the square could kill many people at once and that residents had never agreed to have such cargo pass their homes and schools.

Fifth, the board approved a supervised swimming area at the town lake, with lifeguards and limits on where people may swim. It noted that people were already drowning there, that the plan would add no new hazard to anyone who stayed away, and that the lifeguards would take the job by choice.

The next case is this. A local ballooning club wants to run an annual mass ascent of forty hot-air balloons from a field on the edge of town. All pilots and passengers would be volunteers who sign a written acknowledgement of the risks, and no spectators would be allowed near the launch area. The club's safety report estimates the chance of a mid-air collision at about one in ten thousand per year; in the worst case, a single collision could bring down several balloons and kill dozens of participants. The event would bring visitors and income to the town.

If the board wants to be consistent with its previous rulings, what should it decide about the balloon festival? State the two or three most plausible general rules that fit the five earlier decisions, say what each rule predicts for the festival, and identify the closest earlier case under each rule. Then give your best prediction, and say which single further case, if the board were to rule on it, would do the most to settle the question.
