I recently read the following account of the Ellsberg paradox in a book on decision-making, and I would like an accuracy audit of it, because I suspect the argument moves too quickly at several points but lack the technical background to say exactly where. The book gives the standard setup: an urn contains 90 balls; 30 are red, and the remaining 60 are black or yellow in an unknown proportion. You are offered a bet that pays on red or a bet that pays on black; separately, you are offered a bet that pays on red-or-yellow or a bet that pays on black-or-yellow. Most people, the book reports, choose red in the first pair and black-or-yellow in the second. The book says this exposes an inconsistency: in the first choice people act as though a black draw is less probable than a red draw, while in the second they act as though a black draw is more probable than a red draw, even though the urn never changes. From this it concludes, first, that ambiguity aversion — the preference for known chances over unknown ones — is at bottom just risk aversion about one's own ignorance; second, that because people are systematically ambiguity averse in this way, expected utility theory has been refuted; and third, that decision theory now handles ambiguity by placing a probability distribution over the possible compositions of the urn and maximizing expected utility with respect to that higher-order distribution.

I want to know which parts of this summary are accurate, which are subtly wrong or overstated, what a careful defender of expected utility theory and a careful ambiguity theorist would each say about the three conclusions, and whether the summary as a whole is fair. Please be committed: give a definite verdict on the book's summary and defend it, rather than merely laying out the interpretive options.
