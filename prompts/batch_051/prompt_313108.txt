I'm a biology grad student and I've drifted into a pretty strict rule about how I read papers and talks. If a paper says a structure is "for" something—say, that a bird's hollow bones are for flight—I refuse to take the "for" as an explanation. I translate it in my head to something like: the trait spread because variants that had that effect on flight left more descendants. I don't do this when a friend says a traffic light is red "to" stop cars—that's design intention, fine. It's specifically biological function talk I don't trust.

My advisor says I'm being a pain, that function language is how the field communicates and everyone knows it isn't spooky. But I've noticed people slide from "wings are for flying" into assuming every trait has a purpose, or into talking as if the organism had a goal. One lab mate literally said a gene was "trying" to maximize fitness.

Is my rule defensible, or am i throwing away a real kind of explanation just because it can be abused? When does the rule actually block good science, and is there a version of it that keeps the guardrail without me having to be the lab's pedant? Bottom line up front, then your reasoning.
