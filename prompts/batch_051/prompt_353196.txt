I have spent the last few years defending a clean, output-based account of creativity: something is creative when it is both novel and valuable within its domain, regardless of how it was produced. I like this account because it gives one criterion for proofs, paintings, slogans, and engineering fixes alike, and because it does not require me to posit any special mental ingredient behind creative work. Recently, however, a friend showed me a project that has made me doubt it. He wrote a program that generated thousands of variations of an image by randomly mutating parameters and then scored each variation against a fitness function he had coded; among the survivors were a few images that were genuinely unlike anything in the input set and, to my eye, genuinely striking. My theory says those images are creative. But I find myself wanting to resist that conclusion. The program never treated the alternatives as options, never cared which one was better, and never grasped what made the good ones good. It feels more like filtering a heap of rubble and discovering a jewel than like making an image.

I cannot tell whether that resistance is tracking a real distinction—something to do with authorship, selection, or understanding what one is doing—or whether I am romanticizing human activity. If I revise the account to require something about the internal process, I worry that I will smuggle in the very mysterious creative spark I wanted to avoid. If I keep the account as it is, I seem forced to call blind algorithmic search creative, which I do not want to do. Please give me a negotiated verdict: should I debunk the intuition as a bias, abandon the output-based account, or is there a third way that preserves the clean criterion for ordinary cases while excluding blind search? Explain what would justify each of these moves rather than simply choosing one. Keep your answer under 600 words.
