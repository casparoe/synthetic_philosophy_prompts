I'm teaching Bernhard Schlink's The Reader in a course on responsibility after atrocity, and I want to bring Karl Jaspers's fourfold distinction among kinds of guilt to bear on it. Jaspers, writing about postwar Germany in The Question of German Guilt, separates criminal guilt (the violation of positive law, judged by courts and attributable only to individuals), political guilt (the liability all citizens bear for the deeds of their state, collective in a qualified sense and judged by the victors), moral guilt (responsibility before one's own conscience for one's own acts and omissions), and metaphysical guilt (the guilt of the survivor who did not risk everything to save others, a breach of human solidarity whose judge is God alone). Read the novel through this taxonomy. Where does Hanna Schmitz belong: is her guilt criminal, moral, or something the categories cannot place, and does her illiteracy and the shame around it change the analysis? What about Michael and his generation — is their guilt political, metaphysical, or a distinctly second-generation phenomenon the framework does not anticipate? Where does the novel vindicate Jaspers, where does it complicate or resist his insistence that criminal guilt cannot be collective while political guilt can be, and what does the narrative add to the philosophical question that the taxonomy alone cannot capture? I want a reading that is faithful in both directions — that does not reduce the novel to an illustration and does not distort Jaspers to fit the novel — and that is willing to say where each fails the other.
