Two participants in a philosophy of science working group have been arguing for months about the right analysis of causation. One is a token-event counterfactual theorist in the lineage of David Lewis: she holds that the fundamental causal relata are events, and that c caused e just in case e counterfactually depends on c, or there is a chain of counterfactual dependence running from c to e; type-level causal claims are, for her, derivative summaries over token cases. The other is an interventionist in the lineage of James Woodward: he holds that the fundamental relata are variables in a causal model, and that X causes Y just in case some possible surgical intervention on X, holding the other variables fixed, would change Y or its probability; token causal claims are, for him, context-relative claims evaluated against a variable set and a contrast class. They agree that pure regularity theories are inadequate, that counterfactual or interventionist dependence is doing the real work, and that causation must be distinguished from mere correlation. But each keeps accusing the other of changing the subject: the counterfactual theorist says interventionism answers an epistemological question about how we detect and represent causes rather than saying what causation is, while the interventionist says the counterfactual theorist's similarity ordering over possible worlds quietly does causal work while giving up context-sensitivity. They have spent their last three meetings on one recurring case: late preemption, in which an assassin's bullet reaches the victim first, while a second assassin, acting independently, would have shot and killed the victim a moment later had the first not fired. The death would have occurred either way, and the two disagree about whether the claim 'the first shot caused the death' is a context-free truth or only an artifact of how the variables and contrasts are chosen.

Draft the joint statement these two could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Structure it as follows: (1) a numbered section of agreed claims that are non-trivial enough to matter and worded so neither side would object that it is slanted; (2) a disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is stated twice, once in the words each side would accept for its own view, together with a neutral statement of the question at issue; (3) a short list of cruxes, meaning the sub-questions such that settling any one of them would move at least one side; and (4) for each crux, at least one concrete case, argument, or piece of evidence both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. If you judge that part of their dispute is verbal, say which part and why. Please end by stating how confident you are in the formulations and which point in the document you are least sure of.
