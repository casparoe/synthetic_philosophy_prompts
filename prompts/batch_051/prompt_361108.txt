A month ago I was appointed to a citizens' advisory committee that reviews proposed energy projects, and I keep running into the same pattern. A developer who wanted to install a pilot battery-storage system had to produce scenario analyses for rare fires, worst-case chemical releases, and end-of-life disposal before the committee would even discuss the application; meanwhile the diesel peaker plant the project was meant to help retire has operated beside a residential neighborhood for thirty years, with documented particulate emissions, and no one has ever demanded that its operator prove the risk is acceptable. The new technology is treated as guilty until proven safe; the old one is treated as safe enough until proven harmful.

It seems like a general feature of how the precautionary principle is actually deployed: it is aimed at novel risks while incumbent risks are left alone until someone demonstrates harm. I cannot tell whether that is a principled application or just status quo bias dressed up in the language of caution. I have to write a short report for the committee on how it should approach such cases, and I want the reasoning to be better than that suspicion. Find the strongest justification you can for holding new risks to a higher burden of proof than old ones, whether epistemic, moral, or institutional, and test it honestly against nearby cases, such as an old technology later shown to be harmful, or a new technology that would reduce overall risk. Then say whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up, and under what conditions.
