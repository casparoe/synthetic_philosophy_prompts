I keep running into a family of statements that look like identity statements but seem to break the usual rules, and I would like to know whether they are all one phenomenon and whether it has an established name. In ordinary identity, if x is identical to y and y is identical to z, then x is identical to z; and if x is identical to y, then whatever is true of x is true of y. But consider three cases.

A bronze statue is the same bronze as the lump of metal that composes it; the lump is the same bronze as the statue; yet the statue and the lump are not the same thing in an ordinary sense, because the lump could survive being melted down and recast while the statue could not. A person at seventy is the same human being as a certain infant, but on some views not the same person as that infant, because psychological continuity has not been preserved; sameness of human being and sameness of person can come apart. And traditional Christian doctrine says of each of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit that each is God, while also saying that the Father is not the Son, the Son is not the Spirit, and that there are not three Gods but one God.

I am not asking whether any of these claims is true. I am asking about their logical shape. Is there an established notion of sameness that is relative to a kind-term or count noun — same bronze, same person, same God — so that two things can be the same F without being the same G, and identity-like transitivity holds only within a kind? Or is the received view that all three cases are ordinary identity plus different predicates, with the apparent relativity of sameness being loose speech? I half-remember the phrase 'relative identity,' but I want more than a label. I want to know exactly what must change in the logic of identity if the phenomenon is taken seriously, whether the Trinitarian case really has the same form as the statue case or the person case, and how a defender of classical, unrelativized identity would dissolve each example. If no single established concept covers all three cases, say which cases belong together and which do not.

Please give a committed answer: take a definite position and defend it, rather than laying out the options and leaving me to choose. At the very end, on its own line, write \answer{...} containing the term you would use for the phenomenon, or, if no established term fits, \answer{no established name; nearest is X} with X replaced by the nearest concept.
