In my morphology course, the professor has spent the term assigning English past tenses to one of three categories: default rule, minor rule, or stored. By a default rule she means the productive -(e)d pattern. By a minor rule she means a non-default pattern shared by a class of verbs, one that she treats as a genuine rule of the grammar rather than as a list of memorized items. By stored she means a pairing that must be listed in the lexicon, either because it is suppletive or because it is an isolated exception to everything else.

Her rulings so far are these: talk/talked and want/wanted, default rule; sing/sang, minor rule (the i–a class, alongside ring/rang, drink/drank, swim/swam); dig/dug, minor rule (the i–u class, alongside stick/stuck, spin/spun, win/won); keep/kept, minor rule (vowel shortening plus -t, alongside sleep/slept, creep/crept); bring/brought, minor rule (the -ught group, alongside think/thought, buy/bought, seek/sought); hit/hit, minor rule (the zero-change class, alongside cut/cut, put/put); go/went, stored (suppletion); and be/was–were, stored (suppletion).

Next week she will rule on sneak/snuck. This is the first case she has considered in which a historically regular past (sneaked) has been joined by an irregular past created by analogy with an existing class (snuck, patterned on stuck and dug). Predict her verdict. Say which principle she is implicitly running — a shared-pattern criterion, an established-membership criterion, or something else — state what each candidate principle predicts about sneak/snuck, give your best guess with your reasons for preferring it, and name one further form, real or invented, whose ruling would settle which principle she is actually applying.
