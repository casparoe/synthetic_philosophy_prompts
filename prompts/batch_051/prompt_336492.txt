I'm not an academic, just someone who got sucked into Hume's essay "Of Miracles" and can't stop poking at this passage:

"All probability, then, supposes an opposition of experiments and observations, where the one side is found to overbalance the other, and to produce a degree of evidence, proportioned to the superiority. A hundred instances or experiments on one side, and fifty on another, afford a doubtful expectation of any event; though a hundred uniform experiments, with only one that is contradictory, reasonably beget a pretty strong degree of assurance."

The part I keep coming back to is the subtraction picture: weigh opposite experiments and deduct the smaller number from the greater. That made sense when a miracle report was a few people saying something wild happened. But now we can imagine a report that has different kinds of evidence pointing the same way—say, several videos shot by strangers who never met, medical scans, lab results, records from two institutions that historically hate each other. These aren't just fifty more "experiments" on the testimony side; they're different lines of evidence, and its not clear they can be tallied and subtracted the way Hume says. Does Hume's argument against miracles survive this, or does that subtraction premise get undercut? I'm not asking whether any actual miracle ever happened. I want to know which premise in his argument is actually doing the work, whether the new scenario breaks it, and if there's a repaired version that still says something firm about miracle claims without that arithmetic.
