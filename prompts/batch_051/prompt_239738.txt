I am a university student taking a course on liberal political theory and colonial rule, and I want to use a methodological passage from John Stuart Mill to interrogate his later defence of British rule in India. In his *Considerations on Representative Government*, Mill writes:

“To these stand opposed another kind of political reasoners, who are so far from assimilating a form of government to a machine, that they regard it as a sort of spontaneous product, and the science of government as a branch (so to speak) of natural history.”

Now consider a borderline colonial case. Imagine it is 1885, and there is a large dependency under British administration. It has an established court system, a railway network, Anglo-vernacular schools, and a substantial urban Indian commercial and professional class. At the same time, the rural majority remains largely illiterate, caste and religious divisions are politically salient, and there is little experience of representative government at the provincial level. 

Mill repeatedly treats representative institutions as unsuitable for peoples he regards as not yet prepared for them, while also insisting that despotic rule over such peoples can be legitimate if it aims at their improvement. I want you to apply the contrast in the quoted passage to this case. Before answering, briefly brainstorm two or three plausible readings of what Mill means by calling government a “spontaneous product” as against a “machine,” and state which reading you are using. Then work through the following questions: on Mill’s own criterion, would he classify this dependency as still belonging to the “natural history” stage, in which a civilising despotism is justified, or as having crossed into the stage where representative institutions should be introduced? Which features of the case would his criterion treat as relevant, and where does the criterion leave the answer underdetermined? Finally, say whether the tension between the two views of government in the passage helps explain why Mill could combine a strong commitment to representative government for England with a defence of imperial despotism for India.
