I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about logical omniscience and I still feel like we went in circles. His line was that the standard possible-worlds argument for epistemic closure is really just an artifact of one representational choice: knowing P means P holds at every world the agent considers possible, so if Q holds at every P-world, Q automatically holds at every world in her knowledge-set too, and the model predicts she knows Q no matter how hard the entailment is. That is the step that forces knowledge of all tautologies and closure under implication, and he thinks it was never a substantive claim about minds. His kicker was that we now have systems—LLMs—that store something belief-like as a probability distribution over token continuations instead of as a partition of possible worlds. In that setup there is no mechanism that forces "knows P" together with "P entails Q" to land you at "knows Q"; the system just fails to know consequences sometimes. He says this newer representation dissolves the old problem, because the bug only existed relative to a semantics nobody is forced to adopt.

I pushed back that this is a category mistake, or at least too fast. The possible-worlds argument was never about what a late-arriving engineering artifact can compute; it was about what follows once you accept a certain semantics for knowledge as a propositional attitude. If the token-distribution representation doesn't license the inference, then maybe the original argument neither survives nor dies—it just stops applying. But then I didn't know what, if anything, remains of closure as a normative principle about rational agents, and we stalled there.

I'd like you to act like a careful formal epistemologist and walk me through this slowly. Before choosing sides, set out as precisely as you can which premise of the possible-worlds argument for logical omniscience is doing the real work, and keep the descriptive reading (real agents in fact know the consequences of what they know) separate from the normative reading (ideal agents ought to). Then tell me whether the LLM-as-probability-distribution development actually kills the argument, forces a repair, or leaves it intact but simply irrelevant to the new systems. If something survives, give me the strongest weakened version you would defend, with its precise scope. If nothing survives, say openly what should replace "knowledge" as the target of epistemic logic. I am less interested in whether chatbots score well on entailment benchmarks than in what the development does to the argument itself.
