I sit on the board of a regional infrastructure body, and we must decide this month whether to approve a coastal flood-defence scheme made necessary by rising seas and more severe storms. Construction would be paid for by present taxpayers, while most of the deaths prevented and damage avoided would lie forty, seventy, even a hundred years in the future. Our economist has appraised the scheme using the agency's standard rules: any benefit is counted as less valuable the further off it is, so a death prevented in eighty years ends up worth only a small fraction of a death prevented next year. He offers two arguments for this. First, people are generally impatient; they prefer a benefit sooner rather than later, and public policy may legitimately mirror that. Second, future people are very likely to be richer than we are, so a given amount of money, and perhaps a given improvement in safety, will mean less to them. A residents' group condemns this way of weighing future lives. They say a life is a life, and that a future person's death is not less important merely because it occurs in 2100 rather than 2030; they call our appraisal rules ethically indefensible. I am not an economist or a philosopher, and I must decide which side has the better argument before I vote. Please help me think through the underlying issue rather than offering a politician's compromise. What exactly is the philosophical problem here, and which side, if either, is right? Finally, I want your answer to be self-critical: tell me which consideration or empirical finding would most change your verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
