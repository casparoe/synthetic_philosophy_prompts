Last week a friend and I argued well into the night about whether the image of the moral circle expanding—from kin and tribe to nation, humanity, and perhaps beyond—really tracks moral progress or is mostly a self-congratulatory story. I wanted to say that something real has changed; my friend kept replying that every alleged expansion hides a new exclusion, and that the word progress does more moralizing than describing. The argument kept resetting because we never stabilized what we meant by expansion or progress, nor which mechanism we thought did the widening. I want a handout I can use to sort this out before the next round.

Please construct a decision tree—or, if the material forces it, a compact taxonomy with explicitly labeled branches—that takes someone from their pre-theoretical views about the expanding circle to a named position. Build it from the logical space rather than from a list of labels: choose the discriminating questions first, place the named positions at the leaves, and mark any combinations of answers that are empty, saying in a sentence for each whether the empty combination is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply underexplored.

The instrument is for educated non-philosophers. Phrase every node as a question about a concrete case or a plainly statable claim that someone can answer without already knowing which position it routes them toward; avoid items that ask readers to self-identify with a label. Include at least three concrete cases as diagnostic items—for example, a distant stranger at risk in a disaster that has just reached the news, a farm animal whose welfare one has just learned about, or a policy that will mainly harm people not yet born—and use them only where they genuinely separate leaves.

The leaves must include at least these five positions: a rationalist position on which the circle expands because impartial reasoning extends moral consideration, and this is genuine progress; a sentimentalist position on which expansion is driven by empathy and narrative identification, with reasoning mostly rationalizing afterward; an institutionalist position on which legal, technological, and material changes do the real widening rather than individual moral psychology; a skeptical position on which the apparent expansion is largely redescription or cherry-picked examples rather than a real trend; and an anti-progressivist position on which the circle changes but calling the change progress is a contested normative judgment, perhaps ideological, rather than an established fact. If the logical space forces a sixth or seventh leaf, add and name it, or mark it as unnamed if it has no standard label.

Finally, flag any two positions the tree cannot properly separate, and say what further question would separate them.
