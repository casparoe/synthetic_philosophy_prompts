A piece I read on why public debate is so hard offers the following account of Aumann's agreement theorem. If two people are ideally rational and begin with the same prior probabilities, then as long as each honestly shares everything they know, they will end up assigning exactly the same probability to any given claim. The piece says Aumann's actual result is stronger still: the two need not disclose their private evidence at all, because merely announcing their final probability estimates to one another is enough to bring their estimates into line. That, it explains, is why the theorem is usually summarized as the claim that rational people cannot agree to disagree. The piece then draws the moral that whenever two well-informed people persist in disagreeing about a factual matter, at least one of them must be irrational or not genuinely trying to update.

This feels like it is overstating something, but I cannot locate the exact problem. Please give an accuracy audit of the summary: which parts are correct, which are simplified or subtly wrong, and what conditions the actual theorem requires that the closing moral ignores? Does the claim about real-world disagreement follow from the accurate parts?
