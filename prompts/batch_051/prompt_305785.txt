I'm giving a seminar paper next month at a political theory workshop, and I want to walk in with the Q&A prep already done. The paper defends a version of longtermism against the standing charge that it rationalises totalitarian control. My thesis, in one sentence: longtermism is a claim about what makes outcomes good — that the far future contains most of what matters morally — and once you combine it with ordinary moral constraints plus deep, in-principle uncertainty about which present actions actually make the far future go better, it pushes toward caution about coercive state action, not toward overriding rights and democratic processes for the sake of the future. The slide from the future matters most to we may seize control now trades on a surpressed premise, namely that present agents can reliably pick out which far-future states their coercive actions will bring about. Absent that premise, longtermism can at most support a patchy, experimental, distinctly liberal kind of intervention.

The room will be about fifteen people: mostly liberal political philosophers and historians of political thought, several of whom work on twentieth-century totalitarianism, plus two people sympathetic to effective altruism who will defend the longtermist project but press me on whether I've gutted it. There's also an older historian who brings up the Khmer Rouge in every talk, and a democratic theorist who I suspect will object to the very idea of unaccountable actors running experiments on society. Nobody in the room is a utilitarian, and several are openly sceptical of consequentialist reasoning. I want a prep sheet rather than a full defence: the objections ordered roughly by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way this audience would actually phrase them, each with a short reply and an honest note on where that reply is softest.
