I am fluent in denotational and operational semantics for programming languages, compositionality, syntactic recursion, free and bound variables, type systems, referential transparency, and monads as a device for keeping side effects separate from pure values.

Here is the position as I understand it. Expressivism says that a moral sentence such as that torturing animals is wrong does not report a moral fact; instead, it expresses a non-cognitive attitude, such as disapproval of torturing animals or acceptance of a norm against it. The Frege–Geach problem is that the same moral sentence also occurs in contexts where the speaker is not expressing that attitude in the ordinary way, such as in the antecedent of a conditional, under negation, inside a disjunction, or as a premise in an argument. Yet the phrase used to attribute wrongness must mean the same thing in each of these contexts if arguments like modus ponens are to be valid; if its meaning shifts from context to context, the argument equivocates, while if it keeps its non-cognitive meaning, it becomes unclear what attitude the compound sentence expresses and unclear how the validity of moral arguments is to be defined.

Restate this position and the objection entirely in the vocabulary of programming-language semantics, as faithfully as you can. Treat moral sentences as terms in a small language, assign them semantic values or effects, and show exactly where the naïve expressivist view breaks when the moral predicate is embedded under conditionals, negation, and disjunction. Then show what a compositional attitude semantics would need to look like, including the types, effects, or monadic structure it would have to introduce, to recover the standard inferences.
