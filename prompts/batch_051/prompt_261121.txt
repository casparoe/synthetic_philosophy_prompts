I'm in a social philosophy course and we've been going over the gender-equality paradox, and I wrote out an argument that's bugging me. It seems like it must be wrong but I can't locate the exact failure. Here it is:

(1) In more gender-equal countries, people are under less legal and social pressure to follow traditional gender roles, so they're freer to follow their actual preferences.
(2) But, as we learned it, the gender gap in career choices is larger in more gender-equal countries, not smaller.
(3) If gender-typical career choices get stronger exactly where people are freer to choose, that's evidence the differences reflect real underlying preferences rather than discrimination or outside constraint.
(4) If the differences reflect real underlying preferences, then the gender-skewed outcomes aren't a problem of justice—they're just people doing what they want.
(5) So in more gender-equal societies, the gendered career divide is less of a justice problem, and egalitarians should maybe stop treating it as one.

Is this argument valid? If not, which step fails and what would a corrected version of that step have to say? I half-suspect the slide in step 3 from "freer to choose" to "real underlying preferences" is doing the sneaky work, but maybe step 4 is worse.
