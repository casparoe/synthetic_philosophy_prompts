I've been reporting a piece on the empathy industry — the apps, workplace trainings, and AI companions that promise to make people more compassionate — and I keep getting stuck on a much older fight. Schopenhauer made compassion (Mitleid, literally suffering-with) the sole basis of morality: for him it's the one impulse that gets past self-interest because you actually feel another creature's suffering as something worth stopping. Nietzsche replied that pity is the sick one, not the solution — it doubles the suffering in the world, shields what should be allowed to fail, and often dresses up a will to control or look down on the person pitied.

Now we have a few decades of moral psychology that make this much less abstract: compassion turns out to be parochial, it drops of hard as the number of victims grows, it burns out with repeated exposure, and strong empathy for your own group can make you nastier toward outsiders. What I want to work out is whether Schopenhauer's argument — that compassion can ground morality — actually survives those findings, or whether the lab results basically run Nietzsche's critique for him. Does the argument only need compassion to be a possible motive, not an unbiased or renewable one? Does it collapse once the pure compassion it needs turns out to be rare, biased, and exhaustible? Or can it be repaired by replacing raw felt empathy with something like trained, impartial concern — and is that repair even still Schopenhauer's argument?
