I'm a political theory PhD student, and my supervisor keeps leaning on the nation-as-family analogy: he says a nation is just a family writ large, so co-nationals have special claims on each other that outsiders don't — if you'd feed your brother before a stranger, a state should feed it's own citizens before it sends food aid abroad, and voters should prefer their own in immigration policy. I want to audit how much work the analogy is really doing before I use it in a chapter on nationalist partiality. Can you give me the correspondence table first — what maps to the parents, the siblings, the family home, the black sheep, the inheritance — and then say where the mapping breaks? The obvious disanalogies are that you don't choose your family but you sort of choose (or are stuck with) a nationality, families are small and face-to-face, families don't conscript you or tax you at the border, and the intimacy of family life doesn't obviously generalise to millions of people you'll never meet. But I don't just want a list of differences. I want to know which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion that compatriots have special claims, which are superficial, which features of the family the analogy quietly imports that a political community might not have, and whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table — or whether the family analogy was carrying the argument the whole time. And if it's doing illicit work, is there a better analogy for associative obligations among compatriots, or does the position need to be argued for without the analogy?
