I am a policy analyst for a state Law Revision Commission, and our current mandate is to rewrite the schedule of limitation periods that determines how long after a wrong the state may prosecute a crime or a private party may bring a civil suit. The commissioners argue the same question at every meeting: is a limitation period merely an epistemic concession—an admission that memories fade, witnesses die, and documents disappear—or does the passage of time carry moral weight of its own, so that even with perfectly preserved evidence it can become wrong, or simply pointless, to punish or to extract compensation? We need a reusable decision procedure, not a one-off memo. It must be something a group of careful non-philosophers—mostly lawyers, retired judges, and victim advocates—can run offense by offense in under an hour once the facts have been assembled, and it must be defensible in a public hearing in plain language. We revisit the schedule every few years and the same fights recur, so the procedure should survive reuse across quite different wrongs. Each recommendation will be voted on by the full commission and then by the legislature, which means the procedure has to show us which steps are factual, which are matters of value, and where the two cannot be separated.

I want the procedure to force us to answer three questions separately, because our debates keep collapsing them together: (a) the epistemic question of when evidence has become too unreliable for a fair proceeding; (b) the defendant- or offender-centered question of when a person's changed life, the community's interest in finality, or the sheer lapse of time makes it wrong or unnecessary to reopen the matter; and (c) the victim- or survivor-centered question of whether telling a person that their claim has expired is itself a fresh injury that must be weighed in the balance. If you think one of these questions is a red herring—for example, that time never morally extinguishes what is owed and limitation periods are only practical rules about evidence—say so in the framing and build the procedure around that conviction. Two commitments we want tested, because they come up constantly, are that the moral significance of delay differs as between a discrete completed act, a continuing harm, and a concealed wrong, and that later blameless conduct may count even if earlier guilt does not. We are not asking for a survey of what other jurisdictions do, or for citations to cases; we need the underlying reasoning.

Please organize your answer in these sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths:

1. "Framing" (200–300 words). State which of the three questions above do the real normative work in your view, and give the principle that should govern decisions about the others.

2. "The procedure" (500–700 words). Numbered steps, written so a commissioner can follow them in order. For each step, state the question it asks, what counts as a sufficient answer to move on, when the step stops the analysis and with what result (including "no limitation period" or "a range of years"), and when the step returns a value judgment that a person rather than a rule must make.

3. "Worked applications" (300–400 words). Apply the procedure to three cases: (a) a serious assault committed at twenty and prosecuted at sixty, after a decades-long blameless life, with a credible eyewitness still alive; (b) a fraud concealed by the perpetrator whose last act was thirty years ago but was only discovered last year; (c) toxic waste buried in 1980 by a now-dissolved company and discovered last year, with successor companies still operating. Present each as "facts that matter," then "how the procedure handles it," then "residual judgment." Make sure at least one case returns an answer you expect the commission will not like, and explain why the procedure returns it anyway.

4. "Limits of the procedure" (150–250 words). Identify the kinds of cases, questions, or values the procedure cannot settle, and say what the commission should do when it reaches one of those points.
