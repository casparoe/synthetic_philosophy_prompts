I have been collecting my own verdicts about moral responsibility in a series of concrete cases, and I would like you to work out what principle, if any, lies behind them. I am especially concerned with whether that principle can survive Galen Strawson's Basic Argument, so I will state my verdicts, a tentative principle I am drawn to, and the difficulty I see. I am not asking for a survey of positions or a reading list; I want the best version of the principle and an honest account of what it costs.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. A man in his forties starts shoplifting repeatedly, entirely out of character. A scan reveals a tumor pressing on his orbitofrontal cortex. After surgery the behavior stops; when the tumor regrows, the behavior returns. I judge him not responsible for the shoplifting in the tumor phases.

2. A child is taken from infancy and raised inside an isolated group that systematically rewards cruelty and punishes kindness. As an adult he tortures someone. I judge his responsibility to be severely diminished, perhaps to nothing; he was built to be cruel.

3. A teenager with an unremarkable upbringing defaces a parked car on a dare. He knows it is wrong and is under no unusual pressure. I judge him fully responsible.

4. A man notices that he is cowardly and selfish, and over many years deliberately forces himself into small acts of courage and generosity until they become habitual. In a crisis he then acts heroically. I judge him responsible, indeed more responsible or more creditable than the teenager in case 3, because the character behind the act was self-made.

5. A woman with severe depression promises to file a friend's paperwork but does not, because the illness leaves her unable to act; she describes the failure not as a choice but as something that happens to her. I judge her responsibility diminished, though not entirely absent.

6. A man has struggled for years to stop gambling, knows the odds and the harm, and one evening, after half an hour of deliberation, walks into the casino and loses the rent money. He says afterward that the urge was stronger than he was. I judge him responsible; the felt compulsion is not an excuse.

7. Two siblings grow up in the same home with similar temperaments and comparable pressures. One becomes dependably kind, the other dependably cruel. I want to hold both responsible for what they are and for what they do, but I can identify no point at which the cruel one chose to become cruel, and no point at which the kind one chose to become kind. My verdicts in cases 2 and 3 already say that unchosen formation of character can remove responsibility, and that unchosen formation can be fully compatible with responsibility; case 7 makes me suspect that some unstated distinction is doing all the work.

The principle I am drawn to is roughly this: an agent is responsible for an act to the extent that the act expresses a settled feature of the agent's self, provided that the feature was not installed by a process that bypasses or overrides the agent's own agency, such as a tumor, thoroughgoing indoctrination, or severe mental illness, and provided the agent had, or could reasonably have had, some awareness of how the feature was forming. Deliberate self-shaping, as in case 4, increases responsibility; ordinary unreflective formation, as in case 3, leaves responsibility intact; formation that overwhelms agency, as in cases 1, 2, and to a lesser degree 5, diminishes or removes it.

Strawson's Basic Argument seems to show that this principle collapses under pressure. To be truly morally responsible for what one does, one must be responsible for the mental nature that produces the action. To be responsible for that nature, one must have chosen to be that way, and any such choice would itself have to issue from a prior nature for which one would likewise need to be responsible, and so on indefinitely. Since the regress can never begin, nobody is ever ultimately responsible for anything. If that is right, the teenager's unreflective character in case 3 is no more self-chosen than the torturer's indoctrinated character in case 2; both are ultimately products of genes, upbringing, and unchosen circumstances. What looked like a difference in kind, that agency was bypassed or it was not, may be only a difference in degree within an unchosen causal history.

Please give me the best principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that does all of the following: each clause is motivated by one of my cases; it says something definite about the two further cases below; it states plainly which of my verdicts in cases 1 through 7 it cannot accommodate, if any; and it either answers Strawson's argument on its own terms or proceeds honestly after revising the verdicts that cannot be saved.

Here are the two further cases.

8. A person is given a drug that reliably causes irritability. In the first version he is not told about this effect. In the second version he is told about it but still agrees to attend a high-stakes meeting. In both versions he insults a colleague. My gut says he is more responsible in the warned version, because he had notice and could have arranged his situation around the drug; but a Strawsonian might say that whatever lets a person heed or ignore warnings is itself another unchosen feature. Does the principle you arrived at preserve the warned/unwarned distinction, and if so, on what basis?

9. A person's character changes suddenly after a near-death experience he did not choose, and in the following years he behaves generously where he had previously been selfish. I find myself thinking that the later acts are genuinely his and can be credited to him, but less so than the self-reformer's acts in case 4, because the new character happened to him rather than being built by him. Is that a stable verdict under the principle, or must it be revised?

Please state the principle precisely and work through the cases rather than offering a historical overview.
