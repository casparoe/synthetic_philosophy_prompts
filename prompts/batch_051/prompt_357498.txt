Here is an argument in favor of letting prices for essential goods spike after a natural disaster, which I have reconstructed as a step-by-step argument:

(1) After a hurricane disrupts supply, the quantity of bottled water available is less than the quantity people would buy at ordinary prices, so the water must be allocated by some method or other.
(2) Among the feasible methods of allocating a scarce necessity, the method that does the most good is the one that channels the good to the people who place the highest value on it.
(3) In a competitive market, a scarce good is bought by whoever is willing to pay the most for it.
(4) How much a person is willing to pay for a good is, in general, a measure of how much that person values it.
(5) Therefore, a competitive market for scarce bottled water channels the water to the people who place the highest value on it.
(6) Therefore, by (2), the competitive market does the most good among the feasible methods of allocating scarce bottled water.
(7) Therefore, anti-price-gouging laws, which suppress that market, leave disaster victims worse off than they would be if prices were allowed to rise.

I cannot tell whether this argument is valid. If it is not, exactly which step fails? I am torn between two diagnoses: first, that (4) is false because willingness to pay is constrained by ability to pay; second, that (4) is true as a rough generalization but the inference from (3) and (4) to (5) moves illicitly from a claim about what an individual's willingness to pay measures to a claim about what a population-wide market outcome achieves. Are those two diagnoses really different, and is either the right one? If the argument is valid, is it sound?
