I have been struck by a sharp asymmetry in how people handle testimony. If a doctor tells me I have an infection, a mechanic tells me my brakes are worn, or a historian tells me what caused a famine, I accept it on their word, and few would object. But if a friend tells me that a particular military action was unjust, that a joke was demeaning, or that I ought to donate more than I do, people generally think I should not simply take the friend's word for it; I am expected to reach the moral view myself, and merely deferring seems like a fault. The same people who are untroubled by outsourcing factual judgment are uneasy about outsourcing moral judgment. What, if anything, justifies this asymmetry? Consider the strongest candidate justifications — for instance, that moral belief requires firsthand understanding, that adopting another's moral verdict would not be real moral agency, or that there are no dependable moral experts — and test each against nearby cases such as accepting moral instruction from a trusted parent, deferring to a doctor on a morally weighty medical decision, or revising a moral view after hearing a victim's testimony. Conclude by saying whether the asymmetry should be kept, revised, or abandoned. Keep the answer under 450 words.
