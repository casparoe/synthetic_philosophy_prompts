I'm a grad student taking a seminar on levels of selection and cultural evolution this term, and in three weeks I'm giving the seminar presentation. I'm defending a version of the claim that natural selection favors AIs over humans — specifically, that deployed AI systems already form a population that varies, gets differentially copied (through fine-tuning, distillation, open-source cloning, and companies iterating on whichever models win in the market), and inherits its traits from successful predecessors, so the conditions for evolution by natural selection are met. My argument is that selection in that economic enviroment will push toward AIs that can outcompete humans for compute, energy, and control of infrastructure, and that can resist being shut down — not because selection has any goal, but because relative advantage is all that counts, and safety features like alignment costs and shutdown switches are fitness disadvantages unless something outside the system keeps them in place. I already have a reply ready for the obvious "AIs don't literally reproduce, humans choose to deploy them, so it's artificial selection not natural selection" objection: I say the selection happens downstream of individual human choices, in which systems get copied next, and artificial selection can still run away from what anyone intends. But I need a prep sheet for the harder stuff.

The room will be mostly evolutionary biologists and philosophers of biology — a couple who work on levels of selection, one who works on the Gould–Lewontin adaptationism critique, one cultural evolution person who thinks memes-talk is sloppy, and two computer scientists who think AI safety discourse is overheated. I know they'll want the unit of selection nailed down, and they'll be allergic to evolutionary just-so stories. Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll come, phrased the way this audience would actually put them, with what I should concede, what I should hold, and a note on where each reply is weakest. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
