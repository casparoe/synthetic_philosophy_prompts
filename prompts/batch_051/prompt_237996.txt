Write a Petrarchan sonnet in the voice of the man in Bernard Williams's example who must choose between saving his drowning wife and saving a drowning stranger, and who saves his wife. The octave should render the act as unmediated and particular: not a weighing of two lives but a body already moving toward one face. The volta should come at the moment the impartial question inserts itself — by whose permission is she the one to be saved? — and the sestet should dramatize what Williams thinks goes wrong when the man reaches for the general answer that it is morally permissible to save one's wife. That answer is not false, but in the poem it should arrive as surplus: it must be felt as an intrusion on an act that was complete without it, so that by the end the reader understands why Williams calls it one thought too many. The turn must do structural work — after the volta the same rescue should be re-described as an instance under a rule, and the loss in that re-description should be the poem's complaint. Do not name Williams or the thesis inside the poem; a reader who knows the integrity objection should recognize the sestet as the thought the octave never needed, and a reader who does not should still feel the poem tighten when the question arrives.
