Read Ursula K. Le Guin's short story "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" through the lens of the problem of interpersonal utility comparisons—the question of whether and how one person's good and another's can be measured on a single scale. The story's bargain appears to presuppose an answer: it treats the child's abject misery as a price worth paying for the city's flourishing. Write a critical reading that asks how the story actually treats that presupposition—as a factual discovery, a shared convention, or something the narrator and reader are being made to decide. Attend to the narrator's way of presenting the bargain and to what the ones who walk away represent for the story's view of commensurability. Say both what the story illuminates about interpersonal comparisons and where it may resist or overstate this reading, and close by saying what it adds to the philosophical debate that abstract argument alone tends to miss.
