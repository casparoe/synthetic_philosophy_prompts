I recently sat in on a long argument between two members of a logic seminar, and I would like a careful adjudication of it. The disagreement concerned whether a sentence could literally be both true and false, and by the end neither of them had budged.

Mara began with the Liar. She wrote on the board the sentence S: "S is false." She said that if S is true, then things are as S says, and S says that S is false, so S is false; and if S is false, then what it says—namely, that S is false—is correct, so S is true. Either way, she concluded, S is both true and false, so at least one contradiction is true.

Jonas replied that this is a reductio of one of the assumptions behind the argument. If a contradiction is true, he said, then explosion follows: from P and not-P, any sentence whatsoever follows. Since not every sentence is true, S cannot be both true and false; something must have gone wrong instead.

Mara answered that she accepts the contradiction and rejects explosion. She pointed out that the standard derivation of explosion runs through disjunctive syllogism, the rule that from "P or Q" and "not-P" one may infer Q. In a paraconsistent logic, she said, that rule is blocked when P is both true and false, so a contradiction can be local and need not spill over into triviality.

Jonas answered that disjunctive syllogism is a piece of ordinary reasoning. He gave the case of the keys: if the keys are in the office or in the car, and they are not in the office, then they are in the car. Revoking the rule, he said, means giving up reasoning by elimination, which is a worse price than the puzzle demands. It is more credible that S fails to express a proposition, or that truth is only partially defined, than that a rule this basic is invalid.

Mara replied that every solution to the Liar has a cost. If S fails to express a proposition, one owes a principled account of which self-referential constructions are meaningless, and strengthened versions of the Liar are designed to recreate the problem for whatever diagnosis one offers. Giving up disjunctive syllogism in full generality sounds worse than it is, she added, because paraconsistent logics recover classical reasoning in consistent domains; the difference is quarantined.

Jonas replied that "consistent domains" was doing all the work. Consistency is not something one can check before reasoning, he said, because one establishes it by reasoning. If disjunctive syllogism is not a generally valid rule, then there are elimination arguments one cannot make, since one never knows whether a contradiction is hiding in the background. The promised quarantine, he said, is retrospective rather than usable.

Mara answered that the recapture results are stronger than a retrospective apology: when consistency is explicitly on the table, classical consequences return. Ordinary reasoning already leans on many background assumptions, she said, and this merely adds one more where the possibility of contradiction is live. Every solution revises something, she ended; the question is which revision is principled and preserves ordinary reasoning. She thought retaining classical logic while qualifying semantics leaves the strengthened Liar unsolved; he thought qualifying logic corrupts reasoning by elimination.

I listened to all of this and could not referee it. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: for each move, say whether it is right, wrong, or partly right, and briefly justify the assessment; then say who is more right overall, and what, if anything, both parties are missing. Please write your adjudication as continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables—the way you would write an email or a letter.
