A university's standing committee on names and memorials is governed by a charter containing the following clause:

"Section 7(b). In considering whether a memorial should be removed or renamed, the Committee shall not judge the commemorated person by the moral standards of the present day. It shall instead ask whether the person's conduct fell below the moral standards that prevailed among thoughtful people of the person's own time and place, and shall confine its grounds for removal or renaming to a finding of such a shortfall."

The committee must now rule on a building named for Thomas Jefferson. Jefferson held enslaved people for most of his adult life, and in those same decades he was a principal author of public documents that rest their authority on a doctrine of universal natural equality. The case presents a difficulty the clause's drafters do not appear to have anticipated. Jefferson's time and place were not morally unified on the question of slaveholding: an articulate abolitionist movement existed on both sides of the Atlantic during his lifetime; a few American states had already begun to curtail or abolish slavery while he was a young man; contemporaries, including Black writers, publicly condemned the institution in the language of natural rights; and slaveholders themselves offered elaborate defences of the practice, treating it as something that needed to be justified rather than simply assumed. In short, the clause appears to presuppose that a time and place has a single prevailing moral standard, but on the very issue before the committee, Jefferson's own society was internally divided.

Apply the clause to this case under each of the following approaches, and then say which should decide the matter and why: an approach that follows the plain wording of the clause; an approach that follows the purpose the clause was evidently meant to serve; an approach that asks how a reasonable reader of the charter would understand the phrase "the moral standards that prevailed"; and an approach that asks what the drafters would most plausibly have said if they had been asked about a figure whose own time and place were contested. Does it matter that the demanding standard one might apply to Jefferson was not a later invention but was available to him in his own time, and was indeed partly articulated by him? What does the case reveal about the assumption that usually does the work in the man-of-his-time defence?
