I am preparing an article on whether split-brain findings should lead us to count more than one conscious subject in a single human body, and I need a working definition of 'a single unified consciousness at a time' that will not beg any of the hard questions. Propose such a definition — or, if you think the phrase really covers a unity-of-contents notion and a unity-of-subject notion that can come apart, give distinct definitions for each — and then run it through the following cases. For each case, say what the definition entails, whether that matches my provisional view where I record one, and where the definition surprises you or forces a revision. Do not give a literature survey; the definition must stand or fall on the cases.

Cases:

(1) A neurologically ordinary, fully attentive adult reading while holding a cup of tea; visual text, inner speech, and bodily sensations are all available together. (Provisional view: clearly a single unified consciousness.)

(2) A split-brain patient in the standard divided-visual-field setup: a stimulus shown only to the right hemisphere leads the left hand to select a matching object, while the speaking left hemisphere, which did not see the stimulus, offers a confabulated explanation for the choice. (Provisional view: this is the best evidence I know for a second stream of consciousness, but I am unsure whether it shows a second subject.)

(3) The same patient hours later, walking through a crowded street, cooking, and conversing without any obvious behavioral split. (Provisional view: one subject in these contexts, but I cannot say what has happened to the second stream.)

(4) A healthy driver who is absorbed in planning a lecture for twenty minutes and later has no memory of the road, though their driving was safe and responsive. (Provisional view: one subject with a lapse of episodic memory, but I want to know why the definition does not force a second stream here if it forces one in case 2.)

(5) A person diagnosed with dissociative identity disorder, when one alter performs a shopping trip and another later reports losing time for it. (No settled view.)

(6) A split-brain patient in a modified task: the two hemispheres are given separate yes-no questions and answer simultaneously by pointing, each hemisphere unable to report what the other was asked or answered; the answers conflict. (Provisional view: if this is not two subjects, I am not sure any behavioral evidence could establish two.)

(7) A patient under adequate general anesthesia whose cortex still responds discriminatively to speech but who shows no sign of awareness and reports nothing afterward. (Provisional view: no conscious subject at all, so the definition should distinguish one from zero, not only one from two.)

Finally, tell me which of your verdicts is least secure and whether the cases reveal that one part of the definition is doing all the work.
