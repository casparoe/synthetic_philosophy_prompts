I've spent the last couple years covering the AI-and-mind beat, and the argument about whether large language models actually understand language has started to feel stuck in a way I can't quite diagnose on my own. Everyone I interview agrees on the surface facts: the models are trained to predict next tokens, they produce remarkably fluent text, they pass an impressive range of tests, and then they break in brittle ways on slightly reworded versions of the same problem. The disagreement only starts when someone says what all that means. One camp treats understanding as a behavioral or competence notion, a direct descendant of the Turing test's dodge: if a system reliably does the linguistic jobs we ask of it — following instructions, answering questions, revising itself when challenged — then it understands, and the skeptic owes us an account of what extra ingredient is missing. The second camp says no: underneath the fluency theres just next-token prediction over internet-scale text, and that internal story lacks the connection to the world, and the intention to mean something, that real understanding requires. The behavior resembles understanding but isnt the thing itself. A third, smaller group thinks the question is ill-posed because 'understanding' was never the kind of term that picks out a hidden essence; it's a word we use for social, explanatory, maybe moral purposes.

My working hypothesis is that the stalemate isn't really an empirical disagreement anymore. Nobody is waiting on the next model or the next benchmark; the camps have settled into different views about what kind of concept 'understanding' is — a competence term, an inner-causal-state term, or a pragmatic label. And I keep feeling the Darwin/Turing inversion underneath the whole thing, the move people now call competence without comprehension: Darwin showed you can get design-like outcomes without a designer, Turing showed you can get mind-like behavior without an inner understander, and one side thinks LLMs are just the biggest case of that yet, while the other thinks the inversion gets stretched too far and at some point the missing comprehension becomes the whole ballgame.

But I wouldn't bet the rent on my diagnosis. Maybe there's a hidden factual question in there — about whether the right kind of world-grounding, self-model, or meta-cognition is present or absent in these systems. Maybe the sides differ about what counts as evidence for attributing a mind at all. Or maybe they have bedrock intuitions about whether a piece of software can mean anything, and no amount of behavior would move them.

So what I actually want is a diagnosis of the disagreement, not a verdict on who's right. Is this a verbal dispute, a substantive one that's frozen because the evidence underdetermines it, two different questions sharing one word, or something else? And given that diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction that would stop the talking past each other, a particular kind of evidence or experiment that would move at least one camp, a reformulated question, or an argument that no progress should be expected? Be blunt about whether anything could actually end it. Oh, and write in American English.
