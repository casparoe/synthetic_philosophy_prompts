I have been teaching a seminar on intertemporal ethics, and I find that my own views on pure time preference are less settled than I would like. I would like you to extract the principle behind my verdicts, test it, and tell me which of my verdicts, if any, must be revised. Here are the cases as they strike me, not as conclusions I have already worked out.

1. Ameena is choosing between a painful medical procedure today and the same procedure in six months. She chooses today, because six months of anticipatory dread is itself a present cost. I think this is fine; she is not discounting the later pain itself but weighing a real current bad against it.

2. Basil discounts all future benefits to himself at 15 percent per year. He has no reason to expect death, no expected change in his preferences or capacities, and no uncertainty about whether the benefit will arrive; he simply discounts because the payoff is farther away in time. I think this is not fine.

3. Clara discounts her own future utility at 1 percent per year. She says her present self is more vivid to her than her future self, though she knows the future self is the same person. There is no uncertainty, no opportunity cost, and no expected change in values. I find this basically acceptable, or at most a minor rational failing of the sort we do not usually criticize.

4. A health-policy model discounts lives saved five decades from now at 3.5 percent per year, with no adjustment for uncertainty, purely because those lives are in the future. I think this is not fine.

5. A government evaluates a climate project using a 5 percent discount rate, not because it regards the future as less important, but because 5 percent is the observed market return on capital and the analysis is meant to answer an opportunity-cost question. I think this is fine for that limited purpose, though I would want a separate welfare ledger for distributional and intergenerational concerns.

6. Dmitri is indifferent between a headache next Tuesday and a slightly worse headache next Wednesday, but whenever he must choose, he will take a Wednesday headache that is up to 30 percent worse rather than a smaller Tuesday headache. This day-of-week pattern is stable, and he can offer no reason for it. I think this is irrational, even though it is not exactly a case of discounting over time.

7. Eloise is evaluating a scholarship fund that could either support one student this decade or, if endowed, support several students in eighty years. She discounts the future students' benefit at 2 percent per year solely because they will live later. I think this is not fine; later existence alone does not seem to me a reason to weight them less.

8. Felix says that his past pleasures matter much less to him now than last week's pleasures, solely because they are further in the past. I think this is also a mistake, but I find it harder to explain why it is not symmetric with discounting the future.

My tentative principle is that temporal distance by itself never justifies discounting; a discount is justified only as a proxy for uncertainty, opportunity cost, or a real present cost such as anticipatory dread. But Clara's case worries me, because if I allow small first-person pure time preference as a harmless quirk, I do not see what stops the exception from leaking into the social cases.

What principle, if any, captures all or most of my verdicts? Please state it precisely, explain which clauses are motivated by which cases, and say which verdicts it forces me to drop or amend. Then apply it to a case I have not listed: a philanthropist can fund an intervention that saves 100 lives now, or invest the same money to save 1,000 lives in 70 years, with no uncertainty and no assumption that lives matter less just because they are richer or poorer. Finally, say how your principle handles the difference, if there is one, between an individual's self-regarding pure time preference and a social discount applied across distinct persons. End with a single line beginning exactly "Summary:" followed by a one-sentence statement of the principle you endorse.
