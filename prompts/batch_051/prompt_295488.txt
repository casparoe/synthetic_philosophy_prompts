In Section VII, Part I of An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, Hume writes:

"When we look about us towards external objects, and consider the operation of causes, we are never able, in a single instance, to discover any power or necessary connexion; any quality, which binds the effect to the cause, and renders the one an infallible consequence of the other. We only find, that the one does actually, in fact, follow the other."

Consider the following change in how this passage is read. Some philosophers argue that this argument is only about what experience can give us. They read Hume as saying that a single observed sequence, or even a single pair of events, cannot reveal a real necessary connexion, because all that appears is succession. But they say the passage does not rule out the existence of real causal powers or necessary connexions in the objects themselves; it only rules out our ability to discover them through the senses. On this "New Hume" reading, the argument is epistemological rather than metaphysical: it removes necessary connexion from the content of experience, not from the world. Does the argument in this passage survive that reading? If so, what exactly does it still establish, and can a regularity theory of causation be built on it? If not, which step in the argument is undercut, and what would a repaired argument need to add? Treat the question as one about the argument, not about which interpretation is textually correct.
