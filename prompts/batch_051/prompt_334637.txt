I have been trying to make precise a line of thought that worries me, and I am unsure whether it is sound. It runs as follows.

(1) Every historical account selects a small number of items—events, causes, conditions, omissions—from an indefinitely large set of available candidates.
(2) Such selection can proceed only by applying criteria of significance.
(3) These criteria are not present in the sources themselves; they are brought to the sources by the historian.
(4) The criteria a historian brings are formed by their intellectual formation, institutional context, and present concerns.
(5) Hence every historical account rests, at least partly, on standards whose source lies in the historian's own time.
(6) Resting on standards whose source lies in one's own time is sufficient for presentism.

Conclusion: presentism is inescapable in historical practice.

I am not asking whether the conclusion is unwelcome, but whether the argument actually establishes it. Is the argument valid, and are all the premises true? If it fails, state exactly which step fails and under what reading of 'standards' or 'presentism' the argument would work.
