I keep flip-flopping on the knowability principle. The verificationist in me finds it hard to give up: if a claim is true, there should in principle be a way for someone to come to know it—reality can't contain facts forever beyond any possible grasp, because then truth would float free of everything anyone could ever be warranted in believing. But I also have a rock-solid intuition that some truths will never in fact be known: the exact number of leaves on a particular tree at a particular instant, or the statement "no one will ever know this statement." And Fitch's argument seems to sharpen the clash: take any truth p nobody ever knows. Then p-and-nobody-ever-knows-p is also true, so the principle says that conjunction could be known. But knowing it would mean someone knows p while also knowing that nobody ever knows p; since knowledge is factive, that second conjunct would then be true, contradicting the first. So the principle seems to force the absurd conclusion that every truth eventually gets known. Which should give—the principle or the intuition? Or is there a restriction (only "basic" truths, or a logic where the Fitch move is blocked) that keeps the verificationist spirit without the omniscience conclusion? I want a negotiated verdict, not a side; say what would justify each move.
