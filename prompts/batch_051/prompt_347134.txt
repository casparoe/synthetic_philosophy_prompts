Since I joined the board of my family's foundation last spring and began helping to allocate a seven-figure annual giving budget, almost everyone I read on cause prioritization, and a consultant we hired, has reached for the same analogy: causes should be selected the way a value investor selects stocks. In this picture, the cause area is the company; importance is the size of the opportunity, the total good at stake; tractability is the quality of the business plan, how much of that good is achievable with the tools now available; and neglectedness is the mispricing, the gap between what a marginal dollar currently buys in that cause and what it would buy if funding were allocated the way an efficient market for social impact would allocate it. The conclusion the analogy is used to support is always the same: a rational donor should systematically overweight neglected causes, the way a value investor overweights undervalued stocks, because that is where an additional dollar does good that would not otherwise get done.

I am now using this to rank a shortlist that includes a proven global-health program, a speculative research area, and a cause none of our other advisers has heard of, so the analogy is doing real work for me. Audit how far it actually holds. Give me the correspondence table: what is the company, the share price, the dividend, the management, the competitive moat, the exit? Then identify where the mapping breaks. I can already sense several places where it might break — no market sets a price for a cause, returns are not paid in a common currency, a crowded cause can still do enormous good, and an empty cause can be empty because it is intractable — but I cannot tell which of these are fatal and which are merely cosmetic. Tell me which disanalogies matter for the intended use, what the stock-market picture quietly imports that the cause-selection picture lacks, and whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table.
