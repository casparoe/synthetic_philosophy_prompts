I am a novelist preparing a volume of short fiction, and I would like you to draft one of the stories, roughly two thousand words, from the brief below.

The premise is this. Since childhood, the protagonist — call her Linnea — has kept a private ledger not of events but of first experiences: each entry records, not what happened, but the exact grain of a moment's felt quality the first time she entered it. The weary sweetness of being kept awake by rain; the prickle of a suspicion before it finds its words; the particular hollowness of a platform just after the train has gone. She is a collector of phenomenal textures, and she has never repeated an entry because she believes, without having named it, that the supply of such textures is inexhaustible.

In middle age she falls in with a small group of researchers who claim to have proven the opposite. They have built a formal model of the experiential state space of a human being — the set of all distinct, qualitatively different ways a person can feel, perceive, or be — and their model says that this space, though astronomically large, is finite, closed, and in principle enumerable. They can locate each of her recorded entries as a point in that space, and from their catalogue they can estimate how many distinct experiences remain to her. They give her a number. It is small enough to live with, and too small to ignore.

Write the story that follows from her being given the number. Do not let the story decide whether the researchers are right; keep their science plausible but slightly out of focus, something the reader feels rather than something explained. The drama should turn on what the number does to her — whether she rations the remaining entries, tries to spend them all at once, loses her appetite for ordinary surprise, or comes to distrust her own system of classification. Work in the recursive shadow of the idea: the experience of learning that experience is exhaustible is itself an experience, and so is the waiting for the last entry, and the story should make the reader feel the question of whether that discovery reopens the very space it claims to close — without ever arguing it. Let the prose be slow and interior, avoid moralising, and end on an image rather than a verdict.
