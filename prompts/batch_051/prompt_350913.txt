I've been thinking about the designated survivor practice — how during the State of the Union one cabinet secretary is sent off to a secure location so that if the Capitol is hit, someone is left to take over — and I find myself pulled in two directions. On the one hand, the continuity-of-government case for it seems basically right. In a mass-casualty attack you can't have a gap in the chain of command; the alternative isn't a more democratic president, it's a constitutional crisis, a scramble among agencies, or worse. The Presidential Succession Act gives a clear, pre-announced line of succession, and everyone in it holds an office that was filled through normal constitutional means: the president was elected, the cabinet secretaries were confirmed by the Senate, and the statutory line was chosen by elected legislators. So, the theory says, emergency legitimacy comes from an arrangement agreed to in advance under ordinary democratic conditions; the fact that the survivor wasn't personally voted for is beside the point, because what's being chosen is continuity rather than a mandate.

But I can't shake the intuition that there's something deeply anti-democratic about a person nobody ever imagined as president — often someone like the Secretary of Agriculture or Commerce, picked partly because they're low-profile and uncontroversial — being handed the full powers of the presidency because everyone above them died in one evening. They were confirmed to run a department, not to command the military or launch nuclear weapons. The public never consented to them as a head of state, not even indirectly. It feels like the continuity argument treats democratic legitimacy as a checklist (is this person in the line? yes? fine) rather than as a relationship to the electorate, and I don't know whether that's a real defect or just a feeling I should train myself out of.

I don't want someone to just declare one side the winner. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing I drop — what exactly it's confused about — what it would take for the continuity theory to be amended or abandoned (should succession be restricted to people with an electoral connection, or should a successor's powers be temporarily limited until some kind of ratification?), and whether there's a reading of 'legitimate' on which both the continuity point and my unease survive. Be explicit about how you'd tell which move is right, not just which move you like.
