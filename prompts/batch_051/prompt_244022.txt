ok so I keep noticing a move in math that I dont have a word for, and I want to know whether it has one. I'm a programmer so I know about normal forms and equivalence classes, but this feels nearby yet not the same thing.

The classic case: you adjoin a root. You write "let α be a root of x² − 2." But there are two roots, and the textbook immediately says it doesn't matter which one you call α, because the map that swaps them fixes every rational number and respects addition and multiplication — the two roots are algebraically indistinguishable. Fine. But then a chapter later the same book is writing √2 and −√2, and it says √2 is the positive one. Except "positive" only means something after you've already decided to look at the roots as real numbers and used the ordering. So somewhere a choice quietly got made, while the book kept saying no choice was needed.

Same thing with the complex numbers: i gets introduced as a square root of −1, and everyone says −i would do just as well, nothing depends on which one you pick. Except sometimes it does depend: the orientation of the plane, the sign of the cross product, which direction is "clockwise." You have to go back and check that whatever you built is unchanged when you swap i and −i, and sometimes it isnt.

So the pattern I'm after is this: you have two or more objects that are symmetric in the sense that some map exchanging them preserves all the structure you have so far, you pick one purely as a label, and then you want a guarantee that everything you build out of the label doesn't actually depend on which one you picked. Sometimes that guarantee is real and provable, and sometimes people just say "it doesn't matter" and find out later that it did.

Is there a name for this move, or for the guarantee? I know "without loss of generality" exists, but that seems to be about picking one case of an argument because the other cases are analogous, whereas here the point is that you often can't even express the difference between the two choices without adding extra structure. And I know "well-defined" from programming, but that's usually about equivalence classes and representatives, and here the two roots aren't the same object — they're distinct objects that a symmetry exchanges. So is this a third thing, or a special case of one of those? And is there a name for the failure mode, where someone assumes the choice is harmless but what they define is only invariant after they add something like an ordering, and different choices give genuinely different objects?

If there's no established name, I'd rather have a precise definition and boundary cases than a label.
