I run an undergrad seminar on cooperation and competition—about 18 students, one 75-minute session—and I want to do a game that makes them feel the force of a multipolar trap / Moloch before I ever introduce the name. The thing I want them to run into themselves is: a situation where every individual move is locally sensible, but the sum of those moves leaves everyone worse off than if they'd all held back, and anyone who tries to hold back alone gets punished.

So I'm imagining an activity where each student runs a little food cart in a tourist town. Every round they secretly choose whether to buy louder advertising (a jingle, a speaker, whatever) or not. The payoff schedule should make advertising stictly better for the individual no matter what the others do, but each additional advertiser makes the whole town a bit more obnoxious or costly for everyone, so the all-hold-off round has the highest average payoff and the all-advertise round has the lowest, even though every individual would rather be advertising given everyone else's choices. They can talk between rounds so they can make little agreements, which makes it hurt more when someone defects for a better score.

Can you turn this into a runnable activity? I need exact rules and materials, including a numerical payoff table that doesn't use real money (points or tokens); number of rounds and a timeline for the 75 minutes, including a debrief; a public scoreboard or some other device so the group can see the trap closing; and maybe a second phase after the debrief where they can try to escape by changing the rules (a town council with an ad tax, a permit system, whatever works). The debrief should map what happened onto the idea of a multipolar trap: the dominant strategy, the collectively ruinous equilibrium, why the agreements kept breaking, and what the rule-change phase shows about exits. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—secret ballots turning into voting blocs, people not caring about points, someone gaming the scoreboard—and how to handle it. Be honest about which feature of a real Moloch trap this can't reproduce: I suspect the fixed, legible payoff matrix is a big one, and the fact that in real cases nobody had to intend the bad outcome. The game should not use the word "Moloch" anywhere in student-facing materials.
