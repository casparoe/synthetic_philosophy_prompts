I'm putting together a small interactive piece for a typography blog, and I'd like the centrepiece to be a "which theory of the letter are you?" quiz, in the spirit of Hofstadter's old Letter Spirit project. The question behind it: what makes every 'a' an 'a'? Typefaces and handwriting render the lowercase 'a' in wildly different ways — the elementary-school single-storey shape (a circle with a tail), the double-storey 'a' in Times and most serif romans (a bowl with an upper loop), the italic 'a' that often looks like a single-storey 'a' tilted right, blackletter 'a's that look like neither — so it's genuinely unclear whether they all share a defining feature.

The quiz should sort readers into one of five positions, but by what they say about concrete borderline cases, not by which description sounds flattering. The five positions to map onto (leave them unnamed in the questions) are:

1. Feature essentialism: an 'a' has a checkable list of defining features (say, a closed loop plus a stem or tail on the right), and a mark is an 'a' iff it has them.
2. Prototype/family-resemblance: no shared checklist; 'a's hang together by graded similarity to the best examples, so a case can be more or less 'a'-ish.
3. Historical lineage: a newly drawn shape is an 'a' because it descends by a chain of copyings, stylisations and design decisions from earlier 'a's. Novel shapes become 'a's by being tweaked descendants, not by fiat.
4. Slot/function view: a shape is an 'a' because it fills the 'a' role in a writing system — first letter, the vowel in "cat", contrast with e and o.
5. Reader-coordination/deflationary: there's no deeper essence; it's an 'a' if the relevant readers accept it and keep using it as one.

Give me 8–10 questions, each a concrete case with two or three options. Good raw material (use any of these and add your own):

- a font where the 'a' is just a triangle with a dot inside, no loop or stem, sitting where 'a' sits and read as /a/ in context;
- the same circle-with-a-tail shape used as an 'a' in one font and as a decorative mark or numeral stand-in in another;
- an optically sized family where the 'a' is single-storey at text sizes and double-storey at display sizes — one letter or two allographs?
- a child's wobbly 'a' with the stem not touching the bowl, which the teacher corrects as a spelling slip rather than as an unrecognised letter;
- an AI trained on tens of thousands of 'a's outputs a shape unlike anything in its training set, and readers accept it as 'a' when it appears in words;
- a calligraphic 'a' distorted beyond resemblance in a logo, still read because the brand name primes it.

The scoring key should map patterns of answers onto the five positions, and should say plainly which patterns leave someone tied between two positions. Also flag any pair the quiz can't cleanly seperate given these cases, and say whether that's because the pair is blurry in reality or because the cases needed are too weird to include. Since the goal is sorting by case judgements rather than chosen label, no question may just ask "do you think the letter has an essence?" or name the positions.

Keep the whole thing under 1,400 words and write it in British English. And make it actually fun to take — this is for a typography blog, not a seminar.
