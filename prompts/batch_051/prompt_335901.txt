Suppose we reconstruct the ancient atomist account of what it is to be a body (roughly, what we would call matter) as the conjunction of four conditions. A body is (i) extended in three dimensions; (ii) solid, meaning internally continuous, with no pockets of void inside it; (iii) impenetrable, meaning that it excludes every other body from the place it occupies and offers resistance to anything that tries to enter that place; and (iv) capable of acting and being acted upon. The void, by contrast, is extended but yields: it has no solidity, offers no resistance, and can neither act nor be acted upon. Its existence is posited, on this view, precisely so that bodies have somewhere to move into.

Now consider, as a borderline case, the electron as understood in contemporary physics, together with the electromagnetic field through which it acts on other charged particles. The electron has mass and charge and is acted upon; it also exhibits an exclusion principle, but it is standardly treated as a point particle with no known internal structure, so it is not obviously extended or solid, and it has no surface in the ordinary sense. The electromagnetic field, meanwhile, fills regions of space and exerts forces, but it can be superposed with other fields and offers no resistance to a body occupying the same region.

Does the electron qualify as a body on the four criteria above? Work through the criteria one by one; where a criterion underdetermines the answer, say what further assumption would settle it. Does the electromagnetic field fare any better? And does either aspect of the case show that one of the four criteria is not necessary for matter, or that the atomist contrast between body and void no longer lines up with the distinction contemporary physics draws between what is real and what is empty?
