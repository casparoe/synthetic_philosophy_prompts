I am a novelist, and I am writing a novel about a society that adopts something like John Stuart Mill’s plural voting scheme. While reading Mill’s Considerations on Representative Government, I kept returning to this passage:

“All speculations concerning forms of government bear the impress, more or less exclusive, of two conflicting theories respecting political institutions; or, to speak more properly, conflicting conceptions of what political institutions are. By some minds, government is conceived as strictly a practical art, giving rise to no questions but those of means and an end.”

A friend who has read the book summarized it for me like this: “Mill is saying that there are two schools of thought about institutions: one treats them as machines to be invented, the other as plants that grow. Mill rejects the organic school and adopts the machine view, which is why he thinks we can design the franchise to produce good government; if the wise are more likely to choose well, they should have more votes than the ignorant. So his plural voting is just applied engineering.” Something about this summary feels too crisp to me, especially because the passage seems to be describing the two theories rather than declaring which one Mill accepts. Is my friend’s summary fair? I would like to know which parts are accurate, which are subtly wrong, and whether the conclusion about plural voting follows from what Mill actually says in the passage.
