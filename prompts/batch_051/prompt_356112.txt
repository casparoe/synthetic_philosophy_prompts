I chair the Board of Zoning Appeals of a small coastal city, and we must decide an appeal that has left the board sharply divided. In 1932 the city adopted an ordinance requiring owners of buildings whose windows, cornices, or signs project over the public sidewalk to hold an annual sidewalk-projection permit, with a per-foot fee and interest on arrears. The last permit issued, fee collected, or citation written under the ordinance appears in city records in 1958. In the decades since, the zoning office has issued certificates of occupancy to hundreds of storefronts that plainly project over the sidewalk, and the city has twice administered façade-improvement grant programs for such buildings without once mentioning the dormant permit.

The revenue department has now revived the ordinance. It cited the owner of a corner café, who bought the building in 2019, for having no permit and assessed five years of unpaid fees with interest, rather than the full arrears back to the 1950s. The owner says she had no notice of the ordinance and reasonably relied on the city's decades-long practice of treating it as defunct. Some board members insist the citation must be upheld: the ordinance was never repealed, and an unelected administrative board should not be able to nullify a duly enacted law by ignoring it. Others reply that enforcing a long-unenforced law against a good-faith purchaser is arbitrary, and one member has called this a textbook case of desuetude.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate as chair before Thursday's meeting. It should recommend a concrete decision on whether to uphold, dismiss, or modify the citation, including any timeframe or transition policy the city should adopt afterward, and it should engage honestly with the strongest consideration on the losing side—including whether this board is even the right institution to conclude that a law has lapsed.
