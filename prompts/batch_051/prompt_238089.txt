I chair a city's public memory commission. We must decide whether to approve a proposal to install a permanent contextualizing plaque at the base of a much-loved statue of the city's founder, who unquestionably established the city but also organized a massacre of the region's earlier inhabitants. A preservation society wants the monument left exactly as it is; an advocacy group argues that without the plaque the statue presents a false past; and several councillors insist that the unamended statue is a needed source of civic inspiration and identity. Our live options are to leave the monument untouched, to approve the plaque, or to move the statue to the city museum for interpretive display. A colleague has proposed that we analyze these options through the three kinds of history Nietzsche distinguishes in his early essay on the uses and disadvantages of history for life: monumental, antiquarian, and critical. Draft the commission's short decision memo. It should recommend one option, state who would carry it out and by when, use the three kinds of history as analytical tools rather than labels, and give an honest treatment of the strongest consideration favoring each option you reject.
