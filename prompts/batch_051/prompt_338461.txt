Randomized controlled trials are typically approved only when the relevant clinical community is in equipoise: there is genuine uncertainty among experts about which arm of the trial is superior. Yet the very same system permits an enrolling physician who personally believes one arm is better for a particular patient to randomize that patient anyway, and it permits a trial to continue after an unblinded data monitoring committee has observed an interim trend favoring one arm, provided the difference has not crossed a pre-specified stopping boundary. What view of clinical uncertainty, and of the moral difference between treating a patient and contributing to generalizable knowledge, does this practice presuppose? In particular, does it presuppose that a patient is not wronged by receiving a treatment the enrolling physician regards as inferior, so long as the expert community has not yet settled which treatment is better? Reconstruct the implicit theory of trial participation embodied in this practice, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits well with, identify the points at which the practice is philosophically underdetermined, and explain what would have to change in the oversight of randomized trials if one instead held that a physician must always act on her own best judgment about the individual patient's interest.
