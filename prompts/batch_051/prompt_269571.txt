I lead the team that writes and maintains the automated decision rules for a state benefits program. The system processes tens of thousands of eligibility determinations a month, the rules run without a human in the loop except when they flag a case for manual review, and any change that will shift more than a few hundred determinations a month has to be signed off by my managers. I have no philosophy or logic background; I have learned by maintaining a growing tangle of if-then rules.

The problem I keep hitting, which I have come to think of as the problem of the ignored background, is this. Every rule we write has to say both what matters and, by omission, what does not matter. A rule like — if a claimant reports income above X, reduce the benefit — only works if the system is allowed to ignore everything the rule does not mention: that the claimant's cousin is sick, that the income was earned out of state, that the form arrived on blue paper. We cannot list all the things that should not matter; the list has no end. But when we leave them unlisted, we get cases where some unmentioned fact actually did matter and the rule returned a wrong decision. Then we patch the rule, and each patch creates new edges and new near-miss cases downstream. My team now spends most of its time arguing about whether a proposed exception deserves to be a condition, should be left to a human reviewer, or should be ignored entirely.

I would like you to write a reusable decision procedure for deciding, when someone proposes a new condition or exception to a rule, whether it should be added to the automated rule, handled by a human review path, or ignored. Base the procedure on whatever is actually known about how a rule-following system, human or machine, can decide relevance without explicitly representing everything it is ignoring. The procedure should be something my team can run in a two-hour meeting. It should have questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a decision to automate, where it stops with a decision to send the case to human review, and where the question is a values or policy decision rather than a logical one. It should give us a test for when a rule has accumulated so many exceptions that it should be replaced rather than patched, and it should say how to audit for sleeping facts: real facts that would change a decision but are not currently reachable by any rule. Be explicit about what the procedure cannot decide and where it is likely to misfire.

Then run the procedure on three proposals currently on my desk. One: adding an exception for claimants whose reported income drops by more than half because of a job loss during the benefit period. Two: adding a condition that checks whether the claimant has ever been flagged for fraud in an external database. Three: removing a condition about household size that fires dozens of times a week but almost never changes the outcome. I want at least one case where the procedure returns an answer my managers will resist, and I want the procedure to flag which step forces that answer rather than hiding it.
