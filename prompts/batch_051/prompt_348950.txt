Since I stopped doing bench work and started reading philosophy I keep coming back to the statute of limitations debates, and what nags me is that the law treats the moral significance of time as a step function: one day a wrong is prosecutable and the next day it isnt. So here's a mechanism I've been tinkering with. Scrap fixed limitation periods. Instead, let the certainty required to convict rise continuously with the time since the offence — each year the trier of fact needs to be a little more sure, on some explicit schedule — and at the same time let the maximum available sentence decay by a fixed percentage a year, say eight percent. So an old embezzlement is still chargeable in principle, but only for a fraction of the sentence and only on unusually clean evidence. Walk me through how this would actually behave. What do prosecutors do with their dockets, how do defendants and witnesses game the schedule, what happens to plea bargaining and to victims deciding whether to come forward late, and where does it get gamed or break first? And can one decay rate do the work if time matters morally for some kinds of wrong but only matters as evidence-degradation for others, or is that a fixable flaw in the design?
