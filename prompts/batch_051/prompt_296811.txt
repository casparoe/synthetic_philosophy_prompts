Consider a design choice that is increasingly common in hospitals and clinics deploying machine-learning systems for image-based diagnosis. A system trained to detect findings — say, fractures on X-rays — flags suspected cases. The radiologist makes the final call. If she overrides the system's flag, she must document a clinical reason for doing so; if she accepts it, she is not required to cite the system's performance record as the basis for her decision. In particular, she is never required to defer to the system merely because its validated error rate on this class of image is lower than that of the average radiologist — even when she knows this, and even when the gap is large. Some institutions actively discourage deference to the system: the clinician is expected to see the finding for herself rather than adopt the machine's verdict on the strength of the performance statistics alone.

The radiologist's own reading of the image is first-order evidence about this patient. The error-rate comparisons are higher-order evidence: evidence about how much confidence to place in first-order readings of this kind. The practice therefore looks like a deliberate limit on the force of higher-order evidence. It may trigger re-examination, documentation, or a second read, but it is not treated as sufficient by itself to settle the verdict.

Reconstruct the implicit epistemology of this practice. Does it presuppose that a clinician may rationally let her own reading of the image settle the matter even when her total evidence — including what she knows about the system's superior accuracy — favors the system's verdict? If so, is that a defensible principle about the relation between first-order and higher-order evidence, or is it an institutionalized license for epistemic akrasia (acting on a verdict that conflicts with one's own best assessment of the total evidence)? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely reflects about liability, accountability, and the social role of clinicians. Finally, say what would have to change in the design of such systems if one instead adopted the view that evidence about one's own unreliability can license deference all by itself. Take a definite position on what the practice is committed to and whether that commitment is the one a well-designed system should have.
