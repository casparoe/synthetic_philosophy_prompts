I've been teaching confirmation theory for years, and lately I've been rereading Section 10 of Hume's Enquiry concerning Human Understanding — the section on miracles — which keeps bleeding into a problem I have about my own methodological intuitions. Hume says, and I'm paraphrasing rather than quoting, that when someone reports an event that violates a law of nature, we have to weigh the evidence for the testimony against the evidence from uniform experience, and belief should be proportioned to the evidence. So a report of a miracle can't be rationally credited unless it would be more contrary to experience for the report to be false than for the miracle to have happened. That principle is seductive, but when I try to apply it to ordinary confirmation, not miracles, my gut verdicts stop forming a coherent set.

For instance: I think a surprising new prediction that comes true confirms a theory a lot, while a prediction that was almost certain anyway confirms hardly at all. I think the perihelion anomaly strongly confirmed general relativity even though the anomaly was known before the theory, but a theory tuned after the fact to fit an anomaly is barely confirmed by fitting it — maybe not at all. Yet I also think if two theories make the same prediction and it comes true, the simpler theory is better confirmed, though I can't shake the feeling that simplicity is just an aesthetic preference. And I want "confirms more than" to be transitive, but I can devise cases where surprise and independence pull in different directions and my comparative judgments don't line up. The case that ties me in knots is this: a theory built purely to fit a dataset, which then makes a new, unexpected prediction that comes true. Does the prediction confirm the theory, or does the post-hoc construction poison the whole thing? I can't decide, and I suspect the answer depends on wether the new prediction was genuinely new or just another slice of the same data.

So my question is whether there is a coherent principle — or a small set of candidate principles — about evidence, surprise, simplicity, and transitivity that captures most of these verdicts, and which verdicts does it force me to drop? What does it say about the post-hoc case? And does Hume's proportioning belief to the evidence, once extended to this context, actually help, or does it just restate the problem?
