Act as an expert on the philosophy of causation who has also spent real time in hospital patient-safety work. I want you to think carefully, and maybe brainstorm the hard cases first before committing to the final procedure.

i chair a patient-safety review committee at a mid-size community hospital, and i need a reusable decision procedure for causation-by-omission cases. we meet roughly monthly to do failure-to-rescue reviews, six to ten charts at a time. a patient deteriorated or died, and the question is whether someones not doing something — not escalating a lactate, not starting antibiotics, not checking a result that was flagged — counts as a contributing cause of the bad outcome. the findings feed root cause analyses and sometimes end up pinned to a clinicians file, so people treat these reviews as blame-lite. right now our criteria are basically how bad was the outcome and how angry is the family, and i hate it.

Please write the procedure as a step-by-step checklist a non-philosopher reviewer can actually run in under twenty minutes per case. Ground it in what philosophy of causation actually knows about omissions: that an omission isnt an event like an action is, that not doing X caused Y only makes sense relative to a contrast or a norm about what should have been done, that counterfactual dependence on the omitted act does a lot of the work, and that almost all omissions are inert because there are infinitely many things nobody did. Keep the distinctions that matter; do not simplify it to failure plus bad outcome equals cause.

The committee includes a nurse manager, a quality officer, and sometimes the hospital's defense attorney, so do not assume medical jargon or philosophical literacy. For each step say what question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because its a values call rather than a factual one. Then run the procedure on three made-up but realistic cases: (1) a nurse documented a lactate of 4.2 but did not call it to anyone, and the patient died of sepsis; (2) a first-year resident knew about an experimental therapy from a journal but did not try it, and the patient died; (3) a senior surgeon skipped a required equipment-count step on a checklist, and the skip demonstrably made no difference to the outcome. Make sure at least one worked example gives us an answer we will not like. And be explicit about what the procedure cannot do — where we will still be making a judgment call even while holding a checklist.
