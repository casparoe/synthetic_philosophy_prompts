I'm a postdoc in political science giving a job talk at a sociology department next month, and I'm genuinly unsure how the Q&A will go, because the talk is designed to make two camps that both disagree with me a little unhappy. The claim I'm defending is: suppose the gender-equality paradox is real—that in countries that score better on standard measures of gender equality, women's and men's occupational and educational choices are not more similar, and in some respects are further apart (women even less likely to go into engineering, relative to men, than in less egalitarian countries). If that pattern holds up, the right lesson is neither that these differences are biologically fixed nor that culture is irrelevant. What the paradox actually does, I argue, is shift the burden: the usual move from "outcomes differ" to "constraints must be doing the differing" becomes much harder to run once the places with the weakest-looking constraints are the places where choices diverge the most. So the real argument moves to what counts as a constraint and what counts as a genuinely open option. And for justice, I claim egalitarians should get less attached to equal outcomes as the success metric and more attached to whether each option was genuinely available on non-gendered terms.

My audience is mostly sociologists of gender, two comparative welfare-state people, and one social psychologist. Their default is broadly environmentalist about occupational gender gaps: they think the gaps are artifacts of socialization, discrimination, and structural constraints, and they're allergic to anything that smells like "it's just nature." They also care a lot about policy, and they worry my conclusion will be used to justify doing nothing about segregation at work. The core move in the talk is that burden-shifting argument, plus a section where I unpack why both the "nature explains it" and the "culture explains it" readings are too quick. I've got 40 minutes and then a 15-minute Q&A.
