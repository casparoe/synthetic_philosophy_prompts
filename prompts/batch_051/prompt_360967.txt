Alright, here's one: you're a poet who also knows Aristotle's De Anima pretty well. Write a pantoum about phantasia — the capacity Aristotle says is responsible for an image occurring to us. The form has to do actual work, not just be decorative. In a pantoum, the second and fourth lines of each quatrain get reused as the first and third lines of the next, and the last stanza folds the opening lines back in, so the poem loops. Use those returns to enact how a phantasma is a motion left over from actually perceiving something: the line is still there after the thing that caused it is gone. Let each repeated line shift its meaning a little on return, the way imagination can cut up and recombine perceptions into something false even though percieving a color or a sound is always right about its own object. Start in plain perception and drift, by the end, to the claim that thinking always requires an image. It should be accurate enough that someone who knows the De Anima III.3 account would nod — phantasia as neither perception nor thought, produced by actual perceiving, fallible, and necessary for thinking — but it still needs to hold up as a poem for someone who's never read a word of Aristotle. Before drafting, brainstorm a handful of candidate lines and what they'd mean on first occurrence vs. return, then think through the stanza order.
