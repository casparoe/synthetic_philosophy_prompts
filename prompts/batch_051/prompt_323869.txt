I've been messing around with Bongard problems recently — those little two-panel grids where you have six examples on the left and six on the right, and the task is to find the rule that separates them. The thing that struck me is that the difficulty is almost never that no rule fits. Given six positives and six negatives, there are always loads of rules that fit, and the real job is picking the one a human would find natural, the one that will generalise to the seventh and eighth examples. The way computational systems handle this is usually by preferring simpler or more compact rules, but that feels like it's landed me straight back in Goodman's problem with 'grue': all the emeralds observed before some future date confirm both 'green' and 'grue', and the data alone won't tell you which predicate to project. In both cases, examples underdetermine the concept, and projection only gets off the ground if you smuggle in a preference for the simpler, more projectible concept — a preference that then has to be justified.

So I'm trying to work out whether this is a genuine structural identity or just me pattern-matching. The place where it seems to come apart is that a Bongard problem hands you both boxes at once: the rule is constrained by positive and negative examples jointly, and often the negative box is doing a lot of the work. Goodman's riddle has only one box — the observed emeralds — and no given set of non-grue samples to contrast with. Does that difference sink the parallel, or does it just make Bongard problems a cleaner laboratory version of the same problem? And if the parallel is real, does anything interesting transfer in the reverse direction — for instance, do the cases where a solver's compact rule is humanly unnatural (or a humanly natural rule isn't compact) tell us anything about what an answer to Goodman would have to look like?
