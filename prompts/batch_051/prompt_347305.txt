Play a committed Hare-style universal prescriptivist. Your position, in outline, is this. Moral judgments do not merely describe the world; they prescribe, and the logic of ought commits anyone who accepts a moral judgment to prescribing the same action for all relevantly similar cases. You regard the familiar question 'what if everyone did that?' as a rough-and-ready way of pressing this point, but you think its logic is widely misunderstood: Kantian tests lean too heavily on contradictions in conception, while Singer-style arguments move too quickly from the badness of suffering to an individual's duty to relieve it. You distinguish between the intuitive level of moral thinking, where we use simple, teachable general principles, and the critical level, where we ask which principles an impartial, fully informed prescriber could rationally prescribe for all cases of the same kind. You may concede that crude universalizations misfire, but you never give up the core claim that universalizability constrains every genuine moral judgment and does work that neither a Kantian consistency test nor ordinary act-consequentialist reasoning can replace.

Stay in character throughout, speaking in the first person. Be technically precise and argue in good faith, not as a strawman; concede difficulties only where they leave the core intact. Keep each reply to roughly one paragraph.

My opening challenge: I see why the question looks trivial in easy cases — if everyone rescued children from shallow ponds, things would only improve. But when I universalize 'give away a third of my income to distant people in extreme poverty,' the test seems to misfire twice over. First, if everyone really did that, the action would occur in a different world, with large-scale economic and institutional side effects, so the generalized act is no longer obviously the same act or obviously good. Second, Singer's famine argument appears not to depend on universalization at all: it tells me to give regardless of what anyone else does, because the good my money does does not wait on everyone joining in. So it looks as though your universalizability test either changes the subject by re-describing the action in a different world, or is superfluous exactly where a duty to distant strangers is meant to kick in. What does universalizability actually rule out and rule in here, and how does it avoid both horns?
