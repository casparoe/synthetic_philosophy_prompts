I'm a novelist, about two-thirds through a draft where the central character — an old-school union organizer who's spent thirty years teaching other people to hold the line — keeps failing to hold his own, in small daily ways and then in one catastrophic way. Trying to make that psychology sharper than "addiction" pushed me into two literatures that, as far as I can tell, have been circling the same phenomenon for decades without ever properly colliding: the philosophy of weakness of will, and the behavioral-economics of hyperbolic discounting.

On the philosophy side, akrasia is treated as a synchronic drama inside one agent: the person all-things-considered judges A to be best, then does B anyway, and the puzzle is how that is coherent — whether the judgment was wholehearted, whether you need two systems of desire, whether the failure is in practical reasoning or in some principle of continence. On the econ side, it is treated as a shape of intertemporal valuation: people have present-biased, roughly hyperbolic discount functions (beta/delta, quasi-hyperbolic, however you formalize it), so preferences reverse as the smaller-sooner reward approaches in time, and that explains why the person who plans to save, or quit, or speak up, later does the opposite. The two camps cite each other occassionally but, as far as I can see, the exchange has never actually gone anywhere; each side still quietly assumes its own description is the deeper one.

My working theory is that this is mostly a translation failure: one side is modeling valuation across time, the other is modeling self-governance at a moment, and neither fact entails the other. Someone could be a hyperbolic discounter without ever judging that the later reward is better — they just change their mind, so there is no akrasia. And someone could be akratic about a choice with no intertemporal shape at all — keeping quiet right now when they judge, right now, that they should speak. So perhaps the real disagreement is about what the explanandum is. But maybe I am overstating the gap, and a properly specified version of one theory really does subsume the other, or the two turn out to be jointly neccessary for one underlying phenomenon. I would like a diagnosis of the structure of the impasse rather than a survey or a verdict: is the disagreement verbal, are the sides answering different questions, is there a hidden formal or empirical issue (say, whether acting against one's better judgment can even be pinned down synchronically, or whether the moment of choice is the wrong unit), or is it substantive in a way that would move if one side built the right model? And given the diagnosis, what would count as real progress — a distinction, a formal result, a kind of experiment, a change of question — or is the impasse a sign that the two projects should just stay separate? Finally, tell me how confident you are in the diagnosis and single out the one point you are least sure about.
