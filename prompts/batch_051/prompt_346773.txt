Imagine that in 1900 a wealthy patron endows an institute devoted exclusively to forecasting the survival of empires. Its one methodological rule is that analysts must take the outside view: for each empire, they assemble a reference class of roughly comparable past polities (matched on size, ethnic and religious heterogeneity, fiscal and administrative capacity, succession arrangements, mode of expansion, and frontier pressure), derive base rates of survival and typical lifespan from that class, together with the most common mechanisms of collapse, and issue forecasts only from those aggregated regularities. They are forbidden to use the inside view — no assessments of the current ruler's competence, the details of domestic politics, recent diplomacy, or the latest reform program. The institute publishes fifty-year survival forecasts, each with a leading predicted cause of failure, for the British, Ottoman, Habsburg, Russian, and Qing empires in 1900, and repeats the exercise every twenty-five years, adding the Soviet Union once it exists.

Give your reasoned prediction of what this institute would have produced at each round and how its forecasts would have turned out. The verdict on individual empires matters less than the mechanism: which determinants of imperial survival were visible to a base-rate method, and which determinants of actual outcomes were systematically invisible to it? At which round — 1900, 1925, 1950, or 1975 — would the outside view have been most informative, and where would it have been most misleading? How much hinges on construction choices: which past polities count as empires for the reference class, whether analysts condition on time already survived, and whether they pool all modes of failure or separate conquest, fiscal crisis, and internal rebellion? For each link in your argument, mark it robust or a guess, and say what would have to be true for the outside view to be systematically worse than a competent inside view at every round.
