I'm a PhD student in epistemology, and I've been running a policy I've never really defended. Outside my advisor and maybe two close collaborators, I treat everything I hear as a lead rather than a license: I won't cite a reported result, plan an experiment around it, or let it change my mind in print until I've checked the primary source myself. If I can't check it, I'll hold the claim in a kind of doxastic escrow and refuse to act on it. Last month I made a coauthor wait three weeks before I'd put his own experimental result into our draft, because I wanted to re-run his analysis first, even though I trust him and he generated the data.

Lately I've been reading Nyāya epistemology, and they'd find this wierd. On their picture, śabda (testimony, roughly) is its own pramāṇa, a distinct source of knowledge on a par with perception and inference, not a second-rate substitute for seeing it yourself. Their idea, as I understand it, is that when a competent and sincere speaker tells you something, the speech itself can give you knowledge; you don't have to bootstrap your way there from general evidence about human reliability. Their default is acceptance, with defeaters doing the work afterward, whereas my default is suspension.

Is my policy defensible? Or is it an attachment to epistemic self-sufficiency that Nyāya would diagnose as a mistake? I can see that my rule protects me from misinformation, but at the cost of treating honest, competent testimony as second-class. Is there a version of the rule that keeps the protection without demoting testimony? Or should I just get comfortable relying on people?
