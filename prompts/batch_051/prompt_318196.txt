I'm in a seminar on the Anthropocene, and a classmate sketched an argument that now has the room split. It starts from something we read, but he pushed it further than the text does, and I'm not sure where the damage is done. He reasons like this. (1) A geological epoch is legitimate only if its lower boundary shows up as a globally synchronous, stratigraphically recognizable change. (2) Human impact on the Earth system comes in two forms: a slow, diachronous set of changes stretching back to Neolithic land clearing, extinctions, and a weak methane anomaly, or a sharp mid-twentieth-century signal of plutonium, concrete, plastic, and industrial soot. (3) If we take the slow early changes, there is no single boundary, and the Anthropocene simply collapses into the Holocene. (4) If we take the mid-twentieth-century spike, we have marked an event inside the Holocene, not a new epoch, because the interval is too short and the pattern is still being made. (5) Therefore the Anthropocene is either stratigraphically indistinguishable from the Holocene or stratigraphically premature. (6) So the geologists who recently declined to ratify it as a formal unit were conceptually right, and the term should be treated as political rhetoric, not geohistory. He calls this a proof that the Anthropocene cannot be a geological period at all. I want to resist, because it seems to show too much: if this argument worked, it would also make it impossible to define any ongoing transition as a new interval. But I can't locate the weak step. Which premise should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost?
