Hey — I’m putting together a sorting quiz for an undergraduate philosophy club meeting on the ethics of belief, and I want it to actually sort people by what they believe rather than by what labels they already know. You’re a professional philosopher who works on doxastic responsibility and the Clifford–James debate. Start by brainstorming the logical space a bit before you write the final instrument, and don’t just organize the quiz by the usual textbook positions.

The deliverable should be a self-contained sorting quiz with a scoring key. It should take someone who has never read Clifford or James and sort them into one of maybe six or seven positions. Each question should present a concrete case or a yes/no claim, not ask them to choose among named positions. The scoring key should map answer patterns to leaves. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from the usual names: first the questions and the combinations of answers they allow, then locate the actual positions in that space. Flag any answer combinations that are empty, and say briefly whether each is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Also flag leaves that your questions cannot separate rather than pretending the tree is finer than it is.

The first diagnostic question should turn on this passage from William Kingdon Clifford, “The Ethics of Belief” (1877), at the end of section I:

“Every one of them, if he chose to examine himself in foro conscientiæ, would know that he had acquired and nourished a belief, when he had no right to believe on such evidence as was before him; and therein he would know that he had done a wrong thing.”

I want you to use it as a test for whether someone locates the wrong in the belief itself, as opposed to only in the actions that follow from it. But don’t use the shipowner case as one of the quiz items — it’s too well-known and people just pattern-match to it.

The positions should include at least these leaves in some form: strict Clifford-style evidentialism; a Jamesian permissivism that says momentous, forced, live options can justify belief where the evidence can’t decide; a view that says belief isn’t voluntary enough for moral norms to apply to it directly, so only our actions and habits of inquiry are assessable; a social/role-based view where standards vary depending on whether you’re a private person or someone making public accusations; and a view that treats the ethics of belief as purely about reliability or truth-conduciveness rather than moral wrongness. You can add one or two more positions if the logical space calls for them, or name a leaf “unnamed” if nobody cleanly defends it.

Aim for no more than ten questions. The scoring key should be usable: for each question, say how each answer pushes toward which branches, and include the final rule for assigning a leaf. At least two of the questions should be cases not directly about religious belief — e.g., a friend’s suspicion about a coworker, a low-stakes sports prediction, or someone who talks themselves into believing a financially comforting thing. And please make sure the questions are answerable by someone who hasn’t read niether Clifford nor James.
