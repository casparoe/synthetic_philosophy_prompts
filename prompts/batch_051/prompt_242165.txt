I'm trying to sort out what I actually think about karma—not the metaphysics of rebirth, just the everyday idea that there's something like a natural or cosmic law connecting the moral quality of what you do to the quality of what you later get. Lately I've been toying with a principle and I want it checked against my own reactions to cases, so I'd like you to either repair it or tell me which of my verdicts have to go.

The principle I keep coming back to: karma isn't a scoreboard that pays out rewards and punishments to match your deeds. It's more like each kind of action feeds or corrodes the specific capacities that made the action possible in the first place, and the "return" on an action is mostly the decay or strengthening of those capacities—so the liar's punishment is losing access to the world truth-tellers live in, and the generous person's reward is staying able to see people as worth trusting. No one has to do the accounting; the feedback is built into the act.

Here are my verdicts, and I'll flag where things get shaky.

1. A man lies constantly in business. Twenty years on he's rich, but he can't take anyone's sincerity at face value; he reads his wife's affection as a performance and every compliment as a sales pitch. He's miserable—not from guilt but from being unable to be surprised by anyone's goodness. Verdict: this is about as close to karma as real life gets. It's less that he was punished and more that he got slowly walled in. My principle does well here.

2. A scammer defrauds elderly people out of their savings, is caught, and goes to prison, where he spends his life resenting the people who put him there. Verdict: justice was done, but it feels less karmic than case 1. The punishment is external—the legal system—not the internal logic of the scam. If his own suspiciousness had unraveled the scam, it would feel more karmic. Is that a real distinction or am I just biased against institutions?

3. A woman routinely covers for a colleague's mistakes at work, and as a result she's trusted by nearly everyone and her own errors get forgiven quickly. Verdict: the rewards feel earned, but I'm uneasy that generosity should pay in this coin. Being trusted is fine; if the reward had been pure career success I'd think the principle had turned into self-help. So maybe the return has to stay tied to the capacity, not just any good outcome that happens to follow.

4. A teen humiliates a shy kid at school. Decades later, the teen's own son is humiliated at school in an eerily similar way. Verdict: I emphatically don't want to call this karma. It's a matching shape with no mechanism—the son, not the father, suffered, and the symmetry is just narrative. But it bothers me because it has the same structure I liked in case 1: bad act, later bad state, no human administrator. So the principle needs to say why this is off. My suspicion is that case 1 runs through the agent's own capacities while this one runs through narrative coincidence, but I'm not sure that suspicion is principled and not just my aesthetics.

5. A whistleblower reports corruption at her firm, does the right thing, and gets blackballed by the whole industry. Verdict: not karma; if anything the reverse. I don't want the principle to rescue this by saying she gained inner clarity, because that feels cheap. Does the principle say the feedback only runs through the act-type under decent background institutions, and when institutions are corrupt the feedback breaks? Or is karma only ever about internal consequences—in which case maybe I should stop mourning the blackballing, and I can't, so maybe the principle is too internalist?

6. A man stole from his employer in his twenties, genuinely changed, and now lives a modest, honest, unbothered life. He's never caught and never confesses. Verdict: fine. I don't need him to suffer. If karma required the old debt to be settled before things were right, I'd reject karma. But this may be in tension with case 1—why is the liar's misery the system working if I don't need suffering at all?

7. A politician lies constantly and seems to thrive inside the flattering world the lies create; aides tell him what he wants to hear and he appears to enjoy it. Verdict: I want to say he refutes the principle. Where's the corrosion? If you tell me the corrosion is that he can't access the truth, part of me says he doesn't care and is having a better time than the whistleblower in case 5. But another part says that's just envy talking, and the principle can't promise the wages of sin will be felt as pain on schedule. This is the one I most need a theory of.

8. A man kills someone in a drunken bar fight, is never caught, and the guilt destroys him—he can't work, drinks more, dies early. Verdict: this feels deserved even though it's also just tragedy. The punishment is internal, but it tracks the weight of the act more than case 1 does. Is that because the feedback in case 1 is epistemic—the liar loses a kind of knowledge—while in case 8 it's moral—the killer loses the ability to stand himself? My principle as stated doesn't have room for that distinction.

So here's what I want: the best principle you can state that captures as many of these verdicts as possible, and which of my verdicts it forces me to scrap or revise. If some version of the capacities-and-feedback principle survives, how does it deal with the politician, the whistleblower, and the son-humiliation case? And what does the repaired principle say about a case I keep going back and forth on: an activist spends years campaigning against corruption, becomes consuming by the fight, turns brittle and contemptuous toward everyone not in the fight, and ends up estranged from her family. Is her unhappiness karmic in any sense, and if not, what's the category mistake I'm making when I call it that?
