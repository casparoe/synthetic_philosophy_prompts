I keep hearing a version of the cognitive liberty argument that used to sound airtight to me: freedom of thought has never needed a legal fight because the inside of your head is a black box. You can police speech and conduct, but a thought nobody can observe can't be punished, so thought was free by default and the law could mostly ignore it. Now suppose the black box has a cracked lid. Over the next decade or so we've got cheap EEG headsets, brain data collected by gaming and meditation apps, and decoding research that can, in controlled conditions, tell what word someone is silently reading or whether they're actually attending. If a meaningful chunk of inner experience becomes inferable from hardware people buy voluntarily, does the old "thought is free by default" argument survive? I'm trying to figure out wether the premise that actually breaks is an empirical one about observability, or a conceptual one about where the mind ends and the body begins. And if it needs repair, what exactly is the weaker right we should be defending: a right to the raw neural signal, a right not to be judged on inferred mental states, or something else entirely? I don't want the policy argument—I want to know what happens to the argument itself.
