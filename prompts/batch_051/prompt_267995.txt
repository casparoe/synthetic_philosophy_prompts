I keep getting pulled in two directions about how defamation law should treat ratings, predictions, and forecasts, and I want help arbitrating between the pull of a theory and the pull of an intuition.

The theory is this. Defamation liability ought to track a single, principled line: a statement is actionable only if it is capable of being proven false, and it is protected if it is an evaluation, a matter of taste, or a conjecture about the future. The test has real systematic virtues. It is predictable for speakers before they publish; it keeps courts from acting as referees of quality; it explains a great deal of settled law; and it follows from the thought that the law should police false factual claims, not disagreements about how to assess them. Calling a psychiatrist's methods "medieval" would be protected, while claiming she falsified patient records would be actionable; a restaurant review's "disappointing" is protected, while alleging "I saw roaches in the kitchen" is actionable, because the latter can be shown false. I find this picture compelling as a matter of principle, not just as doctrine.

But I also have an intuition I cannot shake. Consider an equity analyst at a bank who publishes a "buy" rating and a twelve-month price target, or a credit-rating agency that assigns a municipality a near-default rating. Suppose the factual underpinnings are false — the model's inputs were wrong, the diligence was never done, the analyst knew the assumptions were junk — and the publication destroys the subject's reputation or standing. Or suppose a "sell" report whose quantitative support was fabricated sends a company's shares and reputation collapsing. I want to say that something false and damaging has been asserted, not merely opined. The label "forecast" or "rating" seems to function as an immunizing wrapper around statements that nevertheless rest on claims about the world. Yet the theory tells me precisely the opposite: a forecast is not true or false at the moment it is uttered, and a rating is a judgment, so neither is a provably false assertion of fact.

Where should I end up? Please give me the strongest version of both sides, and then work through the possible resolutions. What would it take for the intuition to be the thing that goes — for me to accept that even knowingly bad ratings and forecasts are not defamatory? What would it take for the theory to be the thing that goes, or at least to be amended so that some ratings and forecasts count as implying present factual assertions about data, methodology, or disclosure? And is there a reading of "opinion" or "provably false" on which both survive — and if so, does the amended theory retain the predictability and the clean decision procedure that attracted me in the first place? I do not want only a verdict; I want to know what each concession actually costs.
