I follow a standing rule for my moral and political convictions. Before I let one of them guide any decision that affects other people, I require myself to be able to state, in my own words, the strongest case against it — one that an intelligent opponent would accept as a fair statement of their view. If I cannot produce that case, I downgrade the belief to provisionally held and treat it as unavailable for action. Last month that meant refusing to support a measure I had favored for years, because every version of the opposition's best case I produced was a straw man. I apply the rule only to high-stakes decisions, since I cannot run the test on everything I believe. The rule has costs: it makes me uncertain where I used to act confidently, and it gives articulate contrarians a veto over convictions I acquired through long experience but cannot reconstruct on demand. I keep it because I think a confidence that cannot face a live alternative is dogmatism wearing a confident expression. Is the rule defensible, or have I turned doubt from an instrument into a duty? Give me a committed verdict: if the rule is wrong, say exactly where it goes wrong and give me the corrected rule; if it is right, say under what conditions.
