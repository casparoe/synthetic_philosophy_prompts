I manage the proposal-screening stage for a private research foundation that funds work across the natural sciences, social sciences, engineering, and the humanities. Each month I screen 20–30 applications and have roughly 20 minutes per application before I write a one-page recommendation to our awards committee, which makes the final call. The thing I most want to assess, and find hardest to assess well, is the project's feedback environment: if the project's central claims are wrong, will the people involved find out quickly, clearly, and without being able to explain the failure away? My rough belief is that fields with fast, clear, reliable feedback advance faster than fields without it, but I keep seeing that a field can have fast feedback and still not advance, because the feedback tracks the wrong thing. I also know some fields without empirical feedback at all — pure mathematics, parts of theory — advance perfectly well, because proof or exact simulation does the correcting.

Please turn the principle that verifiability drives progress into a decision procedure I can actually run at my desk. It should be an ordered checklist that lets me score an application's feedback environment in about twenty minutes, using only the proposal itself, a quick scan of how the relevant field has changed its mind in the past, and a handful of questions I can put to an applicant by email. For each step, tell me the question to ask, what counts as a sufficient yes, what counts as a sufficient no, and whether a no is a hard stop or just a red flag. The procedure must keep these distinctions alive: fast feedback is not automatically informative feedback (leaderboards, metrics, and pilot studies can move quickly while the field goes nowhere); a slow feedback loop is bad only when the decision cycle is faster than the feedback; and some fields have non-empirical checks that should count as verifiability, not as the absence of it. It should also mark plainly which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure and which steps a generalist can run mechanically.

Then run the procedure on three applications:
1. A machine-learning consortium that will maintain a public, weekly-updated leaderboard for diagnostic image triage, evaluated on a fixed retrospective dataset donated by two hospitals.
2. A twenty-year, multi-site program to reduce recidivism through community-based restorative justice circles, with outcomes measured by re-arrest rates.
3. A pure mathematics project in derived algebraic geometry with no planned applications, whose correctness is checked by peer proof-checking.

At least one of these worked applications should be a case where the procedure returns an answer I will not like, because my own enthusiasm runs the other way; say clearly which case that is and why the procedure overrides enthusiasm. Then tell me plainly what the procedure cannot do — the cases where it will systematically mislead me — and where it should hand the decision to someone with domain expertise.

Write the whole thing in American English. After the procedure, the worked applications, and the limitations, give me a structured summary as a JSON object with these named fields:
- "steps": an array of objects, each with "order" (integer), "question" (string), "passes_if" (string), "fails_if" (string), "stop_on_fail" (boolean), and "judgment_call" (boolean).
- "stop_verdicts": an array of objects, each with "verdict" (string) and "meaning" (string describing what I should do next for that verdict).
- "worked_cases": an array of objects, each with "case" (string), "path" (string describing which steps it passed or failed and why), "verdict" (string), and "overrides_likely_enthusiasm" (boolean).
- "limitations": an array of strings, each naming one case the procedure handles badly and what to do instead.
