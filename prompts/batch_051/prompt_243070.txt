I keep hearing the same move from the whole-brain emulation crowd, and I want you to pressure-test it. The move is: a mind is just software, the brain is the hardware it happens to run on, and therefore emulating a brain is just porting a program to new hardware — no more philosophically scary then moving your files to a new laptop or running an old operating system in a virtual machine. In practice this gets used to license a pretty strong conclusion: that if the scan is faithful enough, the emulation is you, and the whole "is it really you or just a copy" worry is a confusion.

Audit this analogy for me. Give me the explicit correspondence table — what is supposed to map to the source code, the hardware, the inputs/outputs, the operating system, the porting process itself. Then give me the places where it breaks. I want you to be specific about which disanalogies matter for the personal-identity conclusion and which are just engineering details that don't touch the philosophy. For instance: software is routinely copied, so two instances can run at once; a brain scan might be destructive or nondestructive; programs can be paused, stepped through, and run at different speeds; mental states might depend on timing, on the body, on hormonal stuff, on the specific physical implementation in ways a program doesn't. Which of those are real problems for the "it's still you" conclusion, and which are just noise?

Then give me the verdict: does the conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the software/hardware picture doing all the work? And if the analogy is doing illicit work, tell me wether there's a better analogy — or whether this is a case where we should stop leaning on analogies altogether and just say what we mean.
