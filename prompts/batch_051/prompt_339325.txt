After a long argument last night with a friend about sovereign immunity, I am left trying to pin down the rule behind her verdicts. She kept insisting that I was confusing injury with injustice, and she paraphrased Hobbes rather than quoting him, so I do not have an exact formulation. Her firm views on five cases are: (1) the state takes private land for a public road under a lawful condemnation statute and pays fair compensation — not wrong; (2) a police officer uses a chokehold that the criminal code expressly bans — wrong; (3) a legislature passes a housing statute that excludes a minority group from a district, and the constitution's text does not clearly forbid it — wrong; (4) the state defaults on a commercial contract with a supplier after enacting a statute immunizing itself from suit — not wrong; (5) the state indefinitely detains asylum seekers in a way that violates a human-rights treaty it ratified, even though the treaty has not been incorporated into domestic law — wrong. Now I want to know her likely verdict on a new case: the state authorizes its security agency to collect and store all private communications of its citizens, with no exception, under a statute that is valid under domestic law and that violates no treaty expressly. I am less interested in a bare yes or no than in the rule she seems to be running, the other rules that would fit her five verdicts equally well, and what further case would best tell them apart. Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question, in American English.
