I'm working on a novel where a character's gear fails her at the worst possible moment, and I've been rewatching 127 Hours to study that kind of scene. The moment I keep turning over is when Ralston's multitool proves useless — blade too dull and flimsy to get through bone — and he has to break his own arm and then cut through it with the same failing tool. I want to read it through Heidegger's readiness-to-hand and the breakdown of equipment: the idea that a working tool melts into what you're doing and only shows up as a separate object once it stops working. Does the film actually track that distinction, or does it show something messier, where the failure spreads from the tool into his whole way of making sense of the situation? Where does the Heidegger lens light the scene up, and where does it overreach and turn the film into something it isnt? Mostly I'm asking as a novelist: I want to write equipment failure so it lands as philosophy without the prose announcing it, and I dont mind being told the film is doing something the distinction can't capture.
