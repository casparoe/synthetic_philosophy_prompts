I don't have any training in philosophy or neuroscience — just a long-standing interest that has recently been reignited by news about brain-computer interfaces. I keep coming back to a cluster of things I believe about experience and about whether there are experiences we can't currently imagine, and I'd like someone to tell me whether the cluster actually hangs together rather than just telling me it's all fine or all hopeless.

Here are the commitments I seem to hold:

1. I'm a physicalist about experience: every experience I have is either identical to or fully determined by a physical state of my brain.
2. I think there are visually possible colors that normal human vision almost never produces and that I can't picture in advance — so-called forbidden colors, like a color that is red and green at the same time rather than a brownish blend of the two.
3. I think an ideal, completed neuroscience could in principle predict exactly what such a forbidden color experience would be like from the physical description of the relevant brain state alone.
4. I think a precise enough brain interface, stimulating the right combination of cells, could give me a genuinely new color — an experience I couldn't have imagined beforehand, and not just a familiar color or a mix of familiar ones.
5. The Mary's room thought experiment still feels right to me: someone who knew every physical fact about color vision but had never seen color would still learn something when she first saw red.
6. I think the difference between a genuinely new experience and a merely unfamiliar combination of old experiences is a factual difference in the world, not just a difference in how we talk.

What I'd like is an audit, not a lecture. Go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are in tension and how badly. For each pair in tension, say how severe the problem is, which commitment would have to give or be weakened, and what that revision would cost. Also flag any pairs that look tense at first but aren't once you scrutinize them, and explain why they only seem to conflict. Finally, tell me whether there is a single change that would remove the most tension at the least cost. If you think I've set the whole thing up on a confused premise — for example, if "predicting what an experience would be like from a physical description" or "a genuinely new color" can't be made precise enough for the audit to mean anything — say that plainly instead of politely pretending the commitments can be audited exactly as I've written them.
