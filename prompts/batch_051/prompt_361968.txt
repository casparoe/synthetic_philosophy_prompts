I don't have any legal training, but after a long argument about whether a deeply unjust statute is still a law, I started reading around the positivism–natural-law debate on my own. The result is an argument of my own making that seems to prove too much, and I would like someone to tell me where it goes wrong.

The argument runs like this. (1) A legal positivist holds that whether a rule is law depends only on social facts—its sources in statutes, precedents, and official practices—and not on whether it is morally good. (2) The ultimate test by which those sources are recognized is a community's rule of recognition: the practiced, convergent standard officials use to identify what counts as law and how conflicts among sources are to be settled. (3) For that standard to be a social fact rather than a theory, officials must agree enough on what it says that one can identify the law without already doing open-ended moral argument. (4) But in real legal systems officials disagree about exactly this: some say a constitution is to be read by original intent; others say it embodies abstract moral principles; still others treat unwritten custom or natural justice as a standing constraint on every duly enacted rule. (5) These are not disagreements about which rules satisfy an agreed ultimate test; they are disagreements about what the test itself is. (6) So wherever such disagreement runs deep, there is no single social rule of recognition, only competing ones. (7) The positivist must therefore conclude that the law in such a system is often indeterminate until the disagreement is temporarily settled—that in hard cases the law simply runs out. (8) Yet ordinary legal practice treats the law as determinate exactly where the foundational disagreement is sharpest: each side of a constitutional dispute insists the law is already on its side, and the losing side is treated as having misdescribed the law, not as having proposed a worse rule for some future decision. (9) So either positivism convicts ordinary legal practice of widespread error and illusion, or it must produce some further account of how the law is determinate here—and the obvious way to produce one is to let moral argument supply what social facts cannot, which would surrender the separation of law and morals that defined the position in the first place.

I would most like to keep the point in (8), since giving it up seems to make legal controversy less than it plainly is. Which step should I reject instead, and what does that response commit me to? In particular, I want to know whether a positivist can concede (4) and (5) while denying (7), or whether that move quietly turns the rule of recognition into a piece of moral theory.
