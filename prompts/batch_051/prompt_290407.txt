I'm taking a course on political philosophy, and a friend in another tutorial group sent me his revision notes for the week on Marx. One paragraph reads as follows: "Marx's core complaint about capitalism is that it alienates the worker. Because the work is repetitive and the pay is poor, the worker feels estranged from the product he makes, from the activity of working, and from his fellow workers. The root of this estrangement is exploitation. On Marx's labour theory of value, the value of a commodity is the amount of labour embodied in it, so the capitalist exploits the worker by paying him less than the true value of his labour and pocketing the difference as profit. Alienation is therefore the subjective, psychological side of exploitation: once the surplus is taken, the worker experiences the loss as misery and estrangement. That is why Marx's remedy was fair wages — if the worker received the full value of what he produced, the exploitation would stop and the alienation would disappear."

Something about it strikes me as off. I had thought Marx insists the wage bargain is an equivalent exchange at the level of labour-power, and I have been told alienation is supposed to be an objective condition of the worker's relation to the world rather than a bad feeling.
