You are a philosopher of science who works on evolutionary game theory and the evolution of cooperation. Think the question through carefully, and begin by laying out the candidate premises before you decide which one is under pressure.

The classic Axelrod-style account of the iterated prisoner's dilemma made a simple reciprocity strategy—tit-for-tat: begin by cooperating, then copy the other player's previous move—look like a robust path to cooperation among self-interested agents without central enforcement. That conclusion came out of an idealized, noise-free setting in which every action is transmitted and observed exactly as intended.

Later work introduced realistic noise, and the result changes. Acts of cooperation are sometimes misread as defections, and acts of defection as cooperation, and players sometimes make unintended choices. Under plausible noise, strict tit-for-tat is brittle: a single misperceived move can lock two players who are cooperating in good faith into a cycle of retaliation. More forgiving or error-correcting strategies—generous tit-for-tat, which forgives a defection with some probability, or win-stay/lose-shift, which repeats a move after a good payoff and switches after a bad one—then outperform it.

Does the classic evolution-of-cooperation argument survive this development, or did it depend essentially on the noise-free assumption? Identify precisely which premise the development bears on. Then either repair the argument and state the repaired claim carefully, or explain why the original argument is dead. In either case, say what the original argument was really doing: was it identifying something deep about reciprocity, or was it an artifact of a mistake-free tournament?
