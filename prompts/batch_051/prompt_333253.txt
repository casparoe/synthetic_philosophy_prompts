the church-turing thesis always had this weird epistemic status: its not a theorem, but the standard argument for it is that every serious way of making 'effective procedure' precise (turing machines, lambda calculus, general recursive functions) picks out the same class of functions. then quantum computing came along, and now people say a quantum computer can factor large integers and simulate quantum systems efficiently, whereas a classical turing machine (probably) cannot do those efficiently. does quantum computing kill the church-turing thesis? i can already hear the standard reply: the thesis is only about what is computable in principle given unlimited time, not about how fast, and a quantum machine doesn't compute any non-turing-computable function. my worry is that the reply is a retreat: maybe there was a reasonable reading of the thesis as an empirical claim about all physical computation on which those quantum speedups do count against it. so which is it? does the thesis survive, does it need repair, or were we all along mixing up the original thesis with a stronger complexity-theoretic one? and whatever your verdict, tell me which specific result or argument would most likely flip you the other way, or at least which move in the standard defense you're least confident about.
