Ive been rereading Hanslick’s On the Musically Beautiful, and the passage I keep circling back to is his claim, roughly, that music cannot represent or present determinate feelings, only the dynamic side of an emotional process: the way it rises, presses, falters, or eases, but never the feeling itself, because a determinate feeling requires an object or conceptual content that purely musical form cannot supply. Since I don’t have the translation in front of me, that’s a paraphrase, not his wording. But I want to take that seriously without letting it settle the question in advance.

So here is what I’m after. Before I commit to any account of how a piece of music can literally be sad, or sound sad, or make us sad, I want the full spec first. What would a theory of basic emotional expressiveness in music actually have to get right? Give me the requirements list as ordinary prose, the way you’d write it in an email: for each requirement, say what case, phenomenon, or distinction forces it. The cases should include at least the usual sad-music puzzle: we call an adagio sad even when we are not sad while listening; we can recognize sadness in music from a culture or style we do not fully understand; and we can enjoy music that we fully acknowledge is expressive of grief. But do not stop at those. Then tell me which requirements are non-negotiable and which are probably just the prejudices of one particular camp. And finally tell me which single requirement you think does the most work eliminating popular theories of musical expressiveness, and why.

Do not format the response with headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. Just continuous prose, like a letter. And write in American English.
