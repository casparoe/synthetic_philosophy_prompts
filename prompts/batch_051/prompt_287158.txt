I was reading Simone Weil’s On the Abolition of All Political Parties with some friends, and we kept getting stuck on one passage. She writes: “In the Anglo-Saxon world, political parties have an element of game, of sport, which is only conceivable in an institution of aristocratic origin, whereas in institutions that were plebeian from the start, everything must always be serious.” Her broader argument, as I understand it, is that parties are essentially machines for manufacturing collective passions, and that they have an inherent tendency to suppress the kind of individual conscience that truth and justice require. What I can’t figure out is whether that argument still works once the exception she carves out stops looking realistic. In the century since she wrote, Anglo-American parties became mass-membership organizations, and more recently they’ve turned into professionalized electoral machines that mostly manage candidates, donors, data, and messaging. They don’t look much like a game or a sport anymore, and their aristocratic origins are basically gone. Does her argument survive that shift? Or does it instead become more general, since the exemption no longer applies? Or was the exemption doing more work than it should have been doing in the first place?

I’m more interested in the structure of her argument than in a history of party systems. So, separate what is directly supported by Weil’s text from what is your own reconstruction or a contested reading, especially if there is a live interpretive debate about whether her critique was meant to apply only to continental-style parties or to parties as such.
