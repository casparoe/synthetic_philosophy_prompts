Been working my way through the old Marriott translation of The Prince, and the chapter near the end on fortune has me going in circles. I've got the passage in front of me:

"I hold it to be true that Fortune is the arbiter of one-half of our actions, but that she still leaves us to direct the other half, or perhaps a little less. I compare her to one of those raging rivers, which when in flood overflows the plains, sweeping away trees and buildings, bearing away the soil from place to place; everything flies before it, all yield to its violence, without being able in any way to withstand it; and yet, though its nature be such, it does not follow therefore that men, when the weather becomes fair, shall not make provision, both with defences and barriers, in such a manner that, rising again, the waters may pass away by canal, and their force be neither so unrestrained nor so dangerous. So it happens with fortune, who shows her power where valour has not prepared to resist her, and thither she turns her forces where she knows that barriers and defences have not been raised to constrain her."

What I can't get straight is what "valour" is doing in that last sentence. I gather it's translating virtù, but it clearly isn't the ordinary courage/virtue sense, and the river metaphor makes the whole thing feel fatalistic — if fortune is a flood that sweeps away trees and buildings, how much is left of the "other half" of our actions he says we direct? Does the flood-defence image just mean fortune only ruins people who failed to prepare while the weather was calm, or is he claiming something stronger, that preparation actually constrains fortune itself? And is there a real tension here — he starts by saying Fortune is arbiter of half our actions, then writes as if everything comes down to whether you've built barriers and whether your style happens to match the times. I'm trying to understand how the virtù-fortuna distinction is supposed to work in this chapter specifically, not just the dictionary gloss on each word.

Also, when you answer, could you seperate what the standard reading of this passage is from what is genuinely disputed among people who work on Machiavelli? I've got the impression that the "one-half" line in particular gets read in pretty incompatible ways, and I don't want one contested interpretation presented as if it were the settled consensus.
