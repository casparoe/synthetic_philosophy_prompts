You are a journalist who covers science and ideas, and you have been asked to moderate an adversarial collaboration between two research programs on why students make systematic errors in learning science. Camp A, the framework-theory view, holds that persistent errors—for example, treating heat and cold as material fluids, attributing a ball's continuing motion to something the thrower imparted to it, or explaining the seasons by Earth's distance from the sun—reflect relatively coherent, theory-like naive models that students apply across contexts. Camp B, the knowledge-in-pieces view, holds that the same errors arise from a large, loosely connected stock of small, context-sensitive intuitive elements—for example, the sense that closeness makes an influence stronger, or that motion and effort die away unless something keeps pushing—each triggered by particular features of a problem rather than by any consolidated model.

Draft the joint statement the two camps could publish after a year of collaboration. Include: (1) a numbered list of substantive claims both can sign; (2) the residual disagreement stated exactly, in wording each side would accept for its own position; and (3) a crux table. For each crux, give a concrete test or classroom case that both sides agree bears on the question, and record in advance what each side predicts. The central crux to specify carefully is cross-context consistency: whether the same student, on problems that differ on the surface but share an underlying concept, applies the same naive idea, and what pattern of within-student variability would count against each view. Where part of the dispute looks merely verbal, say so and state which part.

Keep the whole document under 900 words.
