I'm a reporter who covers science and ideas, and I'm poking at a piece about whether the standard awful-but-it-saved-lives line on Hiroshima and Nagasaki has gotten too comfortable. I keep coming back to Anscombe's take — that Truman's decision was the deliberate killing of noncombatants, full stop, not a hard call that happened to work out — but I want to go at her position harder than the usual op-ed version does, so I'm hoping you can give me a disciplined two-way analysis. Treat Truman's decision to drop the bombs (August 1945, the two cities, somewhere around one to two hundred thousand dead and overwhelmingly civilians) as a case study for the Anscombean lens, which as I understand it has a few moving parts: an absolute prohibition on intentionally killing the innocent, where innocent means not engaged in the doing of harm rather than morally faultless; the combatant/noncombatant distinction doing real moral work, so civilian deaths can't be entered into the ledger as a cost that purchases lives later; and the claim that the deaths at Hiroshima and Nagasaki weren't a foreseen side effect of hitting a military target but a deliberate use of civilian death to shock the enemy into surrender. Go in both directions. First, apply the lens to the decision: what were the live alternatives (an invasion, a naval blockade, waiting on the Soviet entry into the war, modifying the surrender terms, a demonstration on an uninhabited target), what would the theory have required of Truman and his advisors, and where does it's verdict turn on historical questions about whether the planners intended the civilian deaths as a means or merely accepted them as a side effect? Second, flip it and use the case to test the theory: does total industrial war blur the combatant/noncombatant line in a way the theory can't get a grip on, or does the theory grip it just fine and deliver a conclusion we'd rather not face? End with your honest take on what the episode does to the theory — does Hiroshima make Anscombe's absolutism look noble-but-unworkable, workable-but-condemning, or is that a false choice — and write it in American English.
