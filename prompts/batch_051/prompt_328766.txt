Suppose you are advising the program officers of a private foundation that is about to launch a six-year, roughly $12 million initiative called Philosophy for Working Science. The initiative will fund a small number of research teams in which philosophers work inside active scientific projects—on measurement, experimental design, model selection, replication, or the interpretation of data—rather than doing standalone history or metatheory of science. The foundation's governing board includes several senior scientists who hold the dismissive view associated with Weinberg, Hawking, and Feynman: that philosophy has contributed little or nothing to scientific discovery, that philosophy of science is beside the point for the people actually doing science, and that modern physics has outrun philosophical commentary. The program officers are sympathetic to the initiative but must defend it to this board, in the board's own terms, by pointing to specific lines of philosophical work that could change what working scientists do, publish, or conclude. They have asked you to help set priorities.

Which three lines of work or open questions at the boundary of contemporary philosophy and active science should the initiative prioritize, and in what order? For each item, explain why it is more likely than the alternatives to alter scientific practice rather than only philosophical opinion; how tractable it is for philosophers working in joint teams rather than for scientists alone; and what concrete development over the next two or three years would make you raise or lower it in the ranking. State the criteria you are using to compare items—for example, how many other scientific problems depend on the item, how neglected it is by scientists themselves, and how vulnerable it is to being dismissed as fashionable methodological commentary—and say which widely promoted style of philosophy-for-science you would not lead with before this particular board. Write your answer in American English.
