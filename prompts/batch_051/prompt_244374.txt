I'm on the board of my housing co-op and there's a thing our voting procedure does that's been chewing at me for months. Our rules for damages say a resident has to pay only if two things are both true: that they were negligent, and that their negligence actually caused the damage. The way we vote, we always split the question: first negligence, then causation, each by majority, and then we just combine those two yes's into a yes on the overall "do they owe" question.

Last month it bit us. Three board members. I thought negligence yes but causation no, so the person shouldn't pay. Another board member thought negligence no but causation yes, so they also shouldn't pay. The third thought both yes, so they should pay. That means two out of the three of us, on different grounds, thought the person shouldn't pay. But voted seperately, negligence passed 2 to 1 and causation passed 2 to 1, so the combo said they owe. If we'd just voted on the final question first, it would have come out 2 to 1 the other way. Same three people, same facts, same opinions, different answer purely because of the order and splitting.

So I started wondering what our whole practice is presupposing about what a group decision even is. It seems to assume the committee can have its own position on each little question and those positions should add up to a position on the big question, but they don't. Is that assumption coherent? Like, is there a defensible view of collective judgment where voting question-by-question is the right thing, or is the practice just quietly smuggling in the idea that the group is a kind of single mind? And what would we have to change about our rules if we instead thought the only thing that matters is the final up-or-down vote — or if we thought groups don't really have beliefs at all? Also, is it even a problem that the answer flips when you slice the question differently, or is that just something you have to live with once you let majority rule anywhere?
