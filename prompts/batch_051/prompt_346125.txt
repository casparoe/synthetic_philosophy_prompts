Last night a friend and I spent two hours arguing about whether "Gen Z" and "Millennial" name real kinds of people or are just a convenient way of sorting by age, and we ended exactly where we started. We had the same survey results in front of us, and neither of us moved. She took the cohort labels seriously: a generation, on her view, is a group whose members are shaped during their formative years by the same large events — smartphones arriving in adolescence, pandemic school closures, entering the labour market during a recession — and who therefore develop distinctive and fairly stable patterns of values, expectations, and behaviour. I kept objecting that almost everything called a generational trait is either an age effect (people in their twenties have always been less settled) or a period effect (the 2008 crisis hit everyone alive, not one cohort), and that the labels are arbitrary bins drawn over continuous birth years.

That argument felt like a miniature of a debate that has been running for years, in demography and in public life, without moving. I want a diagnosis of why it is stuck, not a survey of the positions.

As I reconstruct it, the realist side treats generations as real social kinds: shared formative experiences produce a causal mechanism that sustains a reasonably stable cluster of psychological and behavioural traits, and that is enough to license prediction, explanation, and policy targeting. The sceptical side treats generation as a convenient fiction or a marketing category: the boundaries are arbitrary (where exactly does "Millennial" end?), within-cohort variation swamps between-cohort variation, and apparent cohort differences tend to dissolve once age and period are separated — though the snag is that age, period, and cohort effects cannot all be independently identified from the same data, so neither side can simply read the answer off a dataset. The sceptic adds that generational talk does rhetorical work: it converts ordinary lifecycle changes into moralized stories about lazy, entitled, or selfish cohorts.

The two sides also seem to disagree about what would make a kind real at all. An essentialist demands a shared nature; a homeostatic-property-cluster view demands a stable clustering of properties held together by an underlying mechanism; a social-ontological view allows that kinds can become real because institutions and self-understandings make them real; a nominalist says only individuals are real and the rest is classification.

So my question is: what kind of disagreement is this, exactly? I can see several candidate diagnoses, and I want you to argue for one rather than tabulate them. Is it verbal — a clash between different senses of "real kind" that the parties have not separated? Is it substantive but empirical — a hidden question about how much cohort-specific variance remains after controlling for age and period, or about whether some identification strategy would settle it? Is it normative — a disagreement about whether generational framing is harmful because it blames cohorts for structural conditions? Or is "Is Gen Z a real kind?" simply ill-posed, a question that sounds precise but has no univocal sense? Given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress: a distinction, a shared definition, a different question, an empirical result, or an honest recognition that no convergence should be expected.
