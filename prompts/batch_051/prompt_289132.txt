Consider the following position about markets and character. Markets are not just efficient; they are character-forming. Repeated exchange disciplines people toward honesty, promise-keeping, and reliability, because a reputation for the opposite is commercially costly. Voluntary dealing also trains people to take counterparties' interests seriously, and this stance is supposed to generalize beyond the transaction. Finally, by redirecting ambition into peaceful acquisition, commerce weakens the taste for violence and status conflict. I want not an essay for or against this view but a map of what would count against it. For each main claim you can isolate: what would have to be true for it to be false? What kind of observation, argument, institutional condition, or experimental result would count against it, and which would be merely awkward rather than close to decisive? Are there standard qualifications (enforceable contracts, the long run, on average) that let the view avoid definite predictions, and would that insulation be a defect? Be willing to conclude either that it is well insulated or more exposed than its defenders admit.

Please structure your answer in the following sections, in this order and at roughly these lengths: (1) Reconstruction — restate the position as a set of separable claims and the conditions under which each is supposed to hold (150 words). (2) Concessions — what a careful defender would already accept as counting against the position (150 words). (3) Defeaters ranked — claim by claim, distinguish evidence that would be close to decisive from evidence that would be merely awkward (400 words). (4) Insulation — how heavily qualified the view must become to survive, and whether that insulation is a problem (150 words). (5) Verdict — your overall assessment of how exposed the position is (100 words).
