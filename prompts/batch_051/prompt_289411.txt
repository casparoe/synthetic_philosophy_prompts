I'm putting together a handout for an interdisciplinary seminar, half evo-bio people and half machine learning people, and the week's question is whether natural selection is a stupid optimizer and what gradient descent can and cannot learn from natural selection. I want you to produce a decision tree, not a prose essay, that sorts the live positions on this into leaves. Ten-ish leaves; eight is fine.

The main thing: build the tree from the diagnostic questions, not from a list of named camps. Every branch node has to be a question a student can answer without already knowing which position they're walking toward. The distinctions I want doing the work are roughly: (1) whether there is a fixed objective function that selection maximizes, or fitness is just a post hoc redescription of who happened to leave descendants; (2) whether being an optimizer requires foresight or some representation of the problem; (3) whether fitness landscapes are fixed or get moved around by the organisms themselves through niche construction and frequency dependence; (4) whether the relevant unit is the gene, the organism, or the lineage; (5) whether we should judge by process or by product; and (6) whether drift and constraint are noise around an optimizing core or reasons to drop the word optimizer altogether. Use whatever subset or ordering of these makes the cleanest tree, and add a question if the tree needs one.

The leaves should be positions people actually defend, named in a way a student could say that's me, including at least: the strong adaptationist leaf (most everything is adaptation), the constrained/satisficing leaf (it optimizes but against a wall of constraints), the blind hill-climber leaf (local uphill only, no foresight, valleys crossed only by accident), the leaf that says evolution isn't an optimizer at all because drift and neutral change do too much of the work, the leaf that seperates selection-as-short-run-maximizer from evolution-as-historical-process so the answer is yes-and-no, the evolvability leaf (selection tunes its own variation machinery, so it's not stupid, just indirect), and at least one leaf about what transfers to gradient descent and one about what does not. If some of these can't be pulled apart by yes/no questions, say so explicitly instead of forcing them apart; I'd rather have an honest tree that flags a pair it can't separate than one that pretends.

Also: mark the parts of the logical space that end up empty — combinations a person could in principle hold but apparently nobody does — and say for each whether it's empty because the position is incoherent, because it's just unappealing, or because it's underexplored.

For the output: start with the tree itself, root question first, no preface and no restating the prompt. After the tree you can add the notes on empty regions and unseparable leaves.
