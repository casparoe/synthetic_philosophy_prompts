I'm on the board of a small housing co-op and we're rewriting our voting rules. Our decisions usually come in bundles: whether we buy a building depends on whether its structurally sound and whether we can keep the reserve fund above some floor afterward, so the vote on "buy" is a logical consequence of the votes on the two sub-questions. We've been voting each sub-question separately and then reading off the consequence, but twice now a majority said the building was sound, a majority said we could afford it, and a majority also said dont buy—different people in each majority. Half the board thinks this is an obvious bug; the other half thinks any replacement rule will just be weird in a different place.

Before we adopt anything, I want the full requirements list. What should any method for turning individual yes/no judgments on a logically connected set of questions into a single group position actually have to do? Which cases must it get right, which distinctions can't it muddle (a member with incoherent views versus a group with incoherent views; questions whose answers constrain each other versus independent ones), and which demanding-sounding properties are maybe just one camp's prejudice rather than a real requirement? For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it. Then flag any subset of the requirements that can't be jointly satisfied—and tell me which requirement is the one you'd most plausibly sacrifice if the impossibility bites. A short note on how the usual fixes (vote the premises only, vote the conclusion only, majority on everything) fare against the list is fine, but the criteria are the main thing I want.

Also, tell me where your answer is least secure: which requirement would you move from non-negotiable to negotiable if the right counterexample came along, and what would that counterexample have to look like.
