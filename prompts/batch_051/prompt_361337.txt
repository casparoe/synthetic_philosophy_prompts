Its bizarre how stable the prescriptivist/descriptivist fight is. The version I keep getting stuck on is this: my aunt, who's a copyeditor, says 'less books' and 'they was here' are just bad grammar—wrong, full stop. My colleague, who studies dialect syntax, says that's close to incoherent: those forms are fully rule-governed inside the varieties that have them, which is the only sense of 'grammatical' linguistics can work with, so calling them wrong is just enforcing one variety's prestige. The strange part is there's no empirical point I can find where they part ways—they agree on who says these things, in which registers, how the forms pattern, that English has standard and non-standard varieties, and that the standard gets taught and enforced. And yet the argument just loops. My current theory is the real disagreement isn't about grammar at all, but about what the word 'grammar' is doing: describing how language works, or sorting forms by institutional acceptability. That might be too quick, though. So what I want is a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement: is it verbal, a difference about standards of evidence, a hidden factual question, two different questions sharing a heading, or bedrock intuitions about language and class that no one can argue for? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress? I don't need a survey of the sides or a verdict on who's right—I want to know why this thing has been frozen for decades and what, if anything, would move it. Please write the answer in British English.
