I'm a program officer at a private foundation that funds work at the boundary of political philosophy and normative economics. I have roughly $2.5 million to allocate over three years, enough for four or five full-time researchers or an equivalent mix of part-time and workshop support, and I want the portfolio to attack conceptual questions about inheritance, dynastic wealth, and equality of opportunity. The eventual audience is not other philosophers: it is policy staff, legislative aides, and journalists who need to know what equal opportunity actually commits a society to and what, if anything, inheritance taxation should be doing. Empirical estimation is out of scope; other funders do that. I want the conceptual scaffolding that empirical and policy work would later hang on.

I am a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, so do not give me a survey: give me a defended agenda. In particular, I want your ranking of the open questions most worth putting these researchers on, together with the reasoning. For each question, I want to know (a) why it is upstream of other work, meaning which other questions or policy judgments depend on settling it; (b) how tractable it is for philosophers and normative theorists rather than empirical economists; (c) how neglected it is relative to its importance; and (d) what evidence or argumentative progress over the next three years would move it up or down your list.

To anchor the ranking, here are some candidate questions that have come up in my own reading. Treat this as a starting list, not a menu to restrict yourself to:

- Should gifts and inheritances be taxed on a lifetime cumulative receipts basis, as under the accessions principle, rather than by taxing each estate or each bequest? Does the case for the accessions principle survive once one notices that it would also push toward taxing non-financial parental investments such as private schooling, test preparation, unpaid internships, and family social capital? If it does not survive, is that a reductio of the principle, or a reason to accept a much broader tax base?
- Is the primary wrong of dynastic wealth that it violates equality of opportunity, that it converts economic power into political power, or that it creates a hereditary leisure class whose members are in a real sense unfree? These diagnoses pull in different policy directions. Which diagnosis is best supported, and which policy differences actually turn on it?
- Does equal opportunity require equalizing inherited advantage itself, or only ensuring that inherited advantage does not translate into unequal prospects for positions, offices, and standing? Does the answer change if families rather than individuals are treated as the relevant unit of distributive concern across generations?
- How should we think about the donor's side? Do the interests, intentions, or posthumous claims of the dead generate any reason to tax bequests more lightly than windfalls to the living, and does the answer change once we model bequests as partly a payment for attention and care from potential heirs?
- Is the right response to dynastic wealth a tax on transfers at all, as opposed to a tax on wealth holdings, an inheritance-as-income rule, or measures aimed at the intergenerational transmission of human and social capital?

Where two questions are close, say what the deciding consideration is. Also say which of the questions above you would deliberately not fund for three years, and why.
