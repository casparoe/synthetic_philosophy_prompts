hey, I'm a linguistics PhD student (discourse/pragmatics, basically), and I keep getting elected moderator of our grad assembly, which means I run monthly meetings where people fight about motions on funding, grading, and union stuff. last month somebody introduced an amendment about how we should "continue to resist the administration's retaliation against student organizers," and the whole meeting melted down over whether the administration actually retaliated, except nobody could say why the wording had already settled the point before the vote. I tried to explain presupposition and accommodation — that the amendment presupposed the retaliation, and that a chair can either accommodate it (let the score update) or challenge it — but everyone just wanted a rule for what to do in the moment.

so write me a decision procedure for a chair/moderator to use when a motion, amendment, question, or a speaker's framing seems to presuppose something that isnt already common ground. it has to be something a non-theorist chair can run in about thirty seconds mid-meeting, without stopping everything, and it should be reusable. ground it in the scorekeeping/accommodation picture, not generic facilitation advice: keep the difference between presupposition, assertion, and implicature, and the difference between a presupposition being absent from the common ground versus disputed versus just not yet raised. don't give me a literature review or citations, i want the usable thing.

include the exact tests a chair should run, in order, to spot a presupposition — projection under negation and questioning, the hey-wait-a-minute diagnostic, a short list of trigger types (factive verbs, definite descriptions, change-of-state verbs like "stop," iteratives like "again," clefts) — and a decision rule for each of the three options: accommodate it, challenge it ("wait, i don't think we can all accept that X"), or ask the speaker to rephrase. mark the steps that are really judgment calls rather than mechanics, and say what the procedure can't do — especially where accommodating amounts to letting the speaker move the score while nobody objects.

then run it on three cases: (1) a motion with a definite description presupposing a contested fact ("the department's repeated mishandling of the travel fund"); (2) a question with "stop" that presupposes misconduct nobody established ("has the treasurer stopped padding the budget?"); and (3) a case where the procedure tells me, the chair, to accommodate something I personally think is false, so I'll feel the thing the procedure is asking me to do. aim for maybe 1000 words, and one or two honest spots where the formal tests misfire in a real meeting.
