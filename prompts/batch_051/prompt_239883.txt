You are a historical theologian who works on early modern Protestant hermeneutics and the epistemology of textual interpretation. A doctoral student has brought you the following puzzle about the Westminster Confession of Faith, and you are going to answer it in writing, at seminar length, for an audience that knows the difference between a confession of faith and a commentary on one.

The Confession states, in Chapter 1, Section 7:

> All things in Scripture are not alike plain in themselves, nor alike clear unto all: yet those things which are necessary to be known, believed, and observed for salvation, are so clearly propounded, and opened in some place of Scripture or other, that not only the learned, but the unlearned, in a due use of the ordinary means, may attain unto a sufficient understanding of them.

Two sections later, it states, in Chapter 1, Section 9:

> The infallible rule of interpretation of Scripture is the Scripture itself: and therefore, when there is a question about the true and full sense of any Scripture (which is not manifold, but one), it must be searched and known by other places that speak more clearly.

The student's worry is this. The first passage sounds as if the Reformation has dissolved any priestly monopoly on meaning: the unlearned can grasp everything necessary for salvation. The second passage makes the resolution of interpretive difficulty depend on a procedure — compare the unclear text with "other places that speak more clearly" — that itself requires judgment about which places are clear, how broad a context is enough, and what to do when two equally diligent readers perform the comparison and arrive at contrary senses. Those judgments look suspiciously like the very act of authoritative interpretation the doctrine was supposed to make unnecessary. The later Section 10 makes the point sharper by denying that councils, ancient writers, doctrines of men, or private spirits are the supreme judge, and by naming instead the Holy Spirit speaking in Scripture.

Your task is to assess whether Sections 7 and 9 of this one confession are genuinely in tension, and whether adding Section 10 makes the tension better or worse. Before writing your final answer, work through the strongest version of the apparent conflict, test each proposed reconciliation against the exact wording of the Confession, and discard any reconciliation that would only convince someone who had never seen a real interpretive dispute.

In your answer:

1. Lay out the most plausible reconciliations, carefully distinguishing among: a difference in subject matter (the things necessary for salvation versus the true and full sense of every passage); a difference in goal (attaining saving knowledge versus settling disputed questions of doctrine); a difference between the clarity of a truth and the clarity of the textual route to it; and any unstated assumptions built into "in a due use of the ordinary means" that make Section 7 less individualist than it first appears.

2. For each reconciliation, state explicitly what extra premise, distinction, institution, or background practice it has to import, and judge whether that importation is something the Westminster divines could honestly have accepted or whether it quietly reintroduces an authority outside the text.

3. Confront the regress directly. If Scripture is its own infallible interpreter, but interpreters applying Scripture-interprets-Scripture can still disagree about what the "clearer" places say, what stops the dispute from either regressing (which text is the one that speaks more clearly?) or collapsing into the de facto verdict of whoever is socially positioned to have the last word? Can the appeal to the Holy Spirit speaking in Scripture block that collapse, or does it simply give the winner of the dispute a theological name for his own conclusion?

Conclude by saying which reconciliation you take to be most honest, which one amounts to special pleading, and whether the three sections together form a coherent account of how the meaning of Scripture is to be decided — or whether they are a set of moves that jointly postpone a problem none of them solves.
