You are an expert philosophy professor who specializes in moral psychology and the ethics of influence — nudges, paternalism, manipulation, autonomy, motivated reasoning — and who's known for turning abstract distinctions into runnable classroom activities. I teach an upper-level undergrad seminar called "Autonomy and Its Underminers," about 17 students, and I have a 75-minute session coming up. Design a structured in-class activity, simulation, or game that makes students feel, before anyone names it, why the line between manipulation and legitimate persuasion is hard to draw and what is at stake when we do draw it.

The key constraint: no lecture up front. The students should run into the problem themselves, through their own choices and judgments, and ideally they shouldn't hear the word "manipulation" until the debrief. The force of the idea should show up in what happens — in a disagreement they have with each other, in a scorecard that rewards conflicting things, in a classification they make that comes back to bite them — not in something you say afterward. I don't want a described case they all look at and discuss; I want them to do something with real, if small, stakes.

Please start by brainstorming two candidate activities, then pick the better one and say in a sentence or two why. Then give me the full runnable version: setup, roles, rules, materials list (nothing that needs money or printing beyond maybe index cards and a handout), a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes, and a scoring or payoff scheme that doesn't require real cash. Include whatever ethical guardrails you'd need so nobody's actual vulnerabilities get exploited in the room.

For the debrief, I'd like you to use a two-axis map and push on it hard. One axis is the route of influence: through the target's rational evaluation of reasons, versus through non-rational channels like framing, vividness, emotion, social proof, defaults, ordering, fatigue, or flattery. The other axis is transparency of intent: openly trying to change their mind, versus hiding that you're trying. Sort whatever happened in the activity onto that grid, and show how the intuitive boundary ends up doing two jobs at once — people tend to call something manipulative either because it sneaks in or because it works through non-rational channels. Then focus the debrief on the mixed cells, because that's where the students will predictably disagree: an open but mostly non-rational pitch (say, a vivid story with no new evidence), and a hidden but fully rational pitch (say, anonymously citing only the half of the evidence that supports your side). Press them on what each proposed fix would cost: a rule that only transparent reason-giving counts, and a rule that bans non-rational channels, each rules out huge amounts of ordinary teaching, parenting, journalism, and friendship. I want them to leave seeing that the line is partly normative and partly contextual, and that cleaning it up costs more than they expected.

Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — at least two specific failure modes — and how to handle each in the room. And be honest about which feature of the philosophical problem the activity can't reproduce; say explicitly what I'll have to supply as the instructor instead.
