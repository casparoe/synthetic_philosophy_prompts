I have been turning over an argument after reading Thomas Nagel on moral luck, and it seems to prove too much, so I want a diagnosis.

(1) An agent is morally responsible for an action or its outcome only if that action and outcome are under the agent’s control.
(2) No action or outcome is under the agent’s ultimate control: every choice issues from desires, habits, capacities, and available options that were themselves fixed by genetic endowment, upbringing, social circumstances, or chance.
(3) Therefore no agent is ever morally responsible for anything.
(4) If no agent is ever morally responsible, then the reactive attitudes—resentment, gratitude, guilt, and the impulse to punish—are never justified.
(5) But some reactive attitudes are justified: consider the difference between two equally reckless drivers, one of whom hits a child and one of whom does not, or the gratitude felt toward someone who overcame a brutal upbringing.
(6) Therefore either (1) or (2) is false.

I want to keep the intuition behind (5), and I am reluctant to give up (1) entirely, because without control moral judgment begins to look arbitrary. Which premise should be rejected, or is there a step where the argument slides? What would a Kantian, a Strawsonian, and a defender of Nagel’s problem each say this argument costs them? Please write the response in American English.
