Write an essay on which critique of effective altruism cuts deepest: the institutional critique, the earning-to-give critique, or the measurability-bias critique. Just so we're on the same page about what each says: the institutional critique claims EA basically treats the world's institutions as fixed and funnels its energy into individual-level fixes (bednets, deworming, direct cash transfers) while ignoring slower, messier systemic change like regulation or norm-shifting; the earning-to-give critique says advising people to take high-paying jobs, possibly in ethically dicey industries, in order to donate the salary rests on shaky assumptions about replaceability, complicity, and whether the job's marginal harm ever actually gets counted; and the measurability-bias critique says the whole methodology is rigged toward things you can count (DALYs, lives saved, RCT results) and systematically downranks illegible work like advocacy or movement-building. Give each critique it's strongest form, then defend a verdict about which one does the most damage. You can argue one is close to fatal while another is overblown, or that two of them are really the same point in different dress, but don't end on a fence-sitting note: actually land somewhere.
