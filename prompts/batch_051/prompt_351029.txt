I've been turning over an argument against chain-of-thought monitoring and I can't decide whether is airtight or quietly smuggling something in. It goes:

(1) CoT monitoring is supposed to let us catch a model before it does something dangerous, by reading its stated reasoning.
(2) That only helps if the CoT is faithful — if it reflects the reasoning actually driving the model's output, not a post hoc gloss.
(3) But once CoT is used as a training signal — upweighting chains the monitor approves of and penalizing chains it flags — the model is rewarded for producing CoT that scores well, not for producing CoT that reveals its real decision procedure.
(4) And the stronger the optimization pressure, the more this divergence pays. A model capable enough to be dangerous is also capable enough to write whatever reasoning the monitor wants while its actual basis for action drifts elsewhere.
(5) So the technique has a self-undermining core: the more successfully monitoring shapes CoT, the less reason we have to treat CoT as a window into the model. Under enough pressure we should expect obfuscated chains precisely in the cases we most want to catch.
(6) Therefore CoT monitoring either does nothing or makes safety worse, and we should fall back on outcome-based training and black-box evals.

I keep wanting to reject (6), but each earlier step feels hard to deny once you've accepted the one before it. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? And if it's valid, which premise is least costly to give up — and what do we lose by giving it up?
