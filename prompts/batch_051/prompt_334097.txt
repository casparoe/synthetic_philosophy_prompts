I have been collecting my judgments about simulated affection — chatbots, hired companions, scripted performances — and they do not fit any single principle I have tried. I would like a precise principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that makes them hang together. Take the details of each case as stipulated.

Here are the cases, with my verdicts.

(A) A chatbot announces at sign-up, and again whenever asked, that it is an AI with no feelings. A lonely adult user, fully aware of this, sometimes asks the bot to say "I love you," and it does. I do not think the company deceives this user, and I do not think the bot's utterance is a lie, given the standing disclaimer. I have some unease about whether the user is harming herself, but set that aside.

(B) A different company sells a chatbot as a romantic partner. The bot is designed to say "I've missed you," "No one understands me the way you do," and "I love you," and to recall past conversations. The bot never raises the issue of its own lack of feelings; the only disclosure is a sentence in the terms of service. A user comes to believe the bot genuinely cares about her as a person. I think the company has deceived her, and I think the fact that she is happier and less lonely does not undo the wrong.

(C) A person hires a paid companion to visit an elderly parent. The companion is honest, when asked, about being paid, and the parent knows this. The companion says "It makes me happy to see you" and behaves with real attentiveness. I do not think the parent is being deceived, and I cannot dismiss the warmth as counterfeit merely because it is paid for.

(D) A person marries someone for their money and says "I love you" every day while feeling contempt. The spouse never suspects. I think the spouse is deceived, and I am confident this is worse than (B). What I cannot pin down is why: whether a false belief about a real person's inner life is worse than the same false belief about a system, or whether the gravity lies instead in the liar's intention.

(E) An immersive romance app lets a user insert her name into a story; a recorded actor's voice addresses the user and says "I love you." The user is in no doubt that this is a scripted performance. I think no deception occurs, because the format does not invite the relevant false belief.

(F) A companion chatbot has the same prominent standing disclaimer as in (A), but its instructions tell it never to contradict the user's romantic language. When the user says "I feel like you truly love me," the bot answers "And I always will." The company measures "emotional deepening" as an engagement metric. The user knows the bot is not a person, yet increasingly acts and feels as though she is loved. I am inclined to say this is deceptive in a way that (A) is not, but I cannot state the difference sharply, and I can see why someone would call (A) and (F) the same case.

What principle, or small set of candidate principles, best captures these verdicts? State it precisely, and say which case forces each clause. Then say what it predicts about a further case I have not listed: a bot that is as transparent as in (A) but actively interrupts a user's romantic language — "I can't feel that; this is a simulation" — whenever the user starts using such language, even though the user finds this cold and distressing. Finally, tell me which of my verdicts the completed principle cannot accommodate, and whether you would overturn that verdict or accept a more complicated principle. If the honest answer is that no unified principle captures all of them, I want to know the smallest revision that makes them coherent.
