Question (20 points, 350–450 words): In her work on hard choices, Ruth Chang argues that hard choices can't be fully explained either as choices between equally good options or as cases where we're just unsure which option is better. Explain how her notion of parity is supposed to do that work. Make sure you (a) state the trichotomy thesis Chang rejects and locate parity in relation to it, (b) distinguish being on a par from being exactly equal and from being incomparable, and (c) say what, if anything, parity implies about whether there is a fact of the matter about which option one ought to choose.

Student answer: "Chang's target is the idea that any two options must stand in exactly one of three relations: one is better than the other, the reverse, or they are exactly equal. She calls this the trichotomy thesis and says it's false. Her fourth relation, parity, holds when two options are close in value but neither is better, and they're not equal either. Her example is choosing between a career as a lawyer and a career as a philosopher, where each is good in a way the other isn't. The choice is hard, on her view, not because we're ignorant of how good each option is, but because the options genuinely fail to be ordered. Since the options are incommensurable—there's no common measure of value—they're incomparable in the strict sense, and parity is basically Chang's name for this incomparability. But if the options are incomparable and neither is better or equal, then practical reason has nothing left to say: there is no fact of the matter about what I ought to choose. Any reason I give for one over the other would have to be manufactured after the fact, so the choice is ultimately arbitrary. The fact that I can rationally pick either option just shows that the two options are on a par rather than equal."
