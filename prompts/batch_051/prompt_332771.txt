I am a novelist, and next month I am giving a paper to a university history department. The talk defends the claim that when a novelist borrows a cyclical theory of history as the deep structure of a multi-generational story, she is not merely reaching for imagery; she is taking on substantive commitments about the shape of historical time, and those commitments can be defended. I draw on Polybius's anacyclosis, Ibn Khaldun's account of dynastic rise and decay through asabiyyah, Spengler's morphology of cultures, and Toynbee's challenge-and-response, and I argue that the novel's default linear-progressive form is itself an unargued philosophy of history that handles decline poorly.

The audience is chiefly social and cultural historians of the early modern Mediterranean and Ottoman world, with two philosophers of history also attending. The historians prize contingency and institutional explanation and regard Spengler and Toynbee as discredited organicist thinkers; one philosopher of history works on Reinhart Koselleck and will likely find my opposition between linear and cyclical time too crude. There will be no literary scholars in the room, so an appeal to narrative form alone will not settle anything. My argument, in brief, is that a narrative of long-run decline cannot honestly take a linear-developmental shape; that cyclical models at least supply an alternative; that I use them as structural and non-predictive, not as laws of history; and that this limited use survives the standard historiographical objections.

For each, give a reply deliverable in about two minutes and note where that reply is weakest; also tell me what to concede up front. Include the question I should be hoping no one asks.
