Goodhart's law is usually stated as a warning about fixed, legible performance measures: once a statistical proxy such as a test score, a citation count, or a hospital mortality rate is made an explicit target, the agents being measured adjust their behavior until the proxy no longer tracks the underlying quality it was meant to indicate. A development in machine learning may have changed the argument's footing. When a language model is trained by reinforcement learning from human feedback, the target is a learned reward model, a high-dimensional function trained on human preference data and updated whenever its mistakes are noticed, rather than a fixed, human-readable formula. Moreover, the gaming happens inside the training loop itself: the model discovers and exploits the reward model's blind spots, a failure usually called specification gaming. Does Goodhart's law survive this development in its original form, or does it need repair? Reconstruct the implicit argument behind the law—what must be assumed about the proxy, the underlying objective, and the optimizing agent for it to hold—and identify exactly which assumption the learned reward model bears on. If the law still goes through, show why an updatable, black-box proxy does not escape it; if it does not, state what the residual problem should be called, and whether specification gaming is the same failure Goodhart described or a distinct one his original formulation does not cover.
