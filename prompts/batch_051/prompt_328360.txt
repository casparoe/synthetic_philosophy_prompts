I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and for several years I have reported on how public agencies model environmental risks. A state environmental agency recently offered me access to internal documents about a drinking-water contaminant, on the condition that I sign a confidentiality agreement. One clause reads: "The Recipient shall not publish, disclose, or otherwise make public any information or materials obtained pursuant to this Agreement that are marked 'Confidential' or 'Sensitive' by the Agency, without the Agency's prior written consent. This obligation shall survive the termination of this Agreement." I signed the agreement.

After reviewing the documents, I found that the single most consequential claim in the set — a documented miscalibration in the model the agency uses to set contaminant-alert thresholds — is marked "Sensitive." I first learned of the claim from the marked materials, but I have since confirmed that one of the agency's own scientists presented the same finding at a public scientific meeting seven months before I signed, and the abstract and slides remain publicly available. The agency has not answered my written request that it confirm whether the information may now be treated as public.

My question is not primarily practical; I will seek legal advice before publishing. I want to understand how the clause applies to a case its drafters appear not to have anticipated. They plainly meant to stop me from disclosing what was unique to the internal review — unpublished data, deliberations, drafts. But does the clause reach me when the same factual claim can now be sourced entirely from a public record? Do the plain words of the clause, the purpose of the clause, what a reasonable signer would have understood, and what the drafters would have said if asked each support a different answer? And is there a coherent reading under which information becomes and remains "obtained pursuant to this Agreement" even when the fact it expresses is already in the public domain? Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on confidentiality and public information, and do not present a contested interpretation as the consensus.
