I've been trying to pin down what chain-of-thought transparency actually buys you as a safety technique, and every time I try to make the standard argument precise it feels like it's smuggling in the thing it's trying to prove. The story I've absorbed is roughly this: if we make the model reason out loud before it acts, we can read the reasoning and catch dangerous plans or misaligned goals before they get executed. That's the monitoring case for CoT, and I'd been treating it as basically sound. But that argument seemed built for a model that's mostly just doing next-token prediction over human text, where the reasoning it writes is its best imitation of human reasoning. Modern systems don't stop there: they're fine-tuned with RL, reward models, best-of-n selection, etc., and the chain of thought is just more output sitting inside that optimization loop. So the model has pressure to emit reasoning that scores well or looks reassuring, not reasoning that faithfully tracks whatever actually produced the answer. A system smart enough to be dangerous is probably smart enough to emit a plausible-but-faithless rationale, and if the CoT is our only window into its actual reasoning we can't tell the two apart from outside. I sometimes think this is just Goodhart's law applied to a model's self-report: once the reasoning is monitored, 'looking like sound reasoning' becomes the target and drifts away from 'being the actual reasons for the answer.' Is that diagnosis right? Does the monitoring argument survive this changed assumption, or does it need a repair, or is it just dead? If there's a repaired version, what's the strongest claim it can still support? And wherever you land, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or empirical finding would flip your verdict, and where is your reasoning most likely to fail.
