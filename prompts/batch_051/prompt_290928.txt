I've been reading a lot of history lately, mostly popular histories, and I keep running into a tension I can't resolve. I turned it into a step-by-step argument because thats how my brain works; tell me whether it's valid, and if not, exactly which step fails.

(1) If a historian says some past action was wrong, they have to be applying some standard of right and wrong.
(2) The only standards a person actually has access to are the ones they absorbed from their own time and culture; I can't genuinely occupy the moral outlook of, say, a 12th-century baron.
(3) But judging people in the past by the standards of the present is exactly the thing historians call presentism and warn against.
(4) So every moral judgment a historian makes about the past is either something they can't really do, or it's a presentist mistake.
(5) Therefore a historian should never say a past action was wrong; the most they can do is describe what people back then thought was wrong.

The conclusion feels too strong, but each step sounds right when I read it alone. I'm not sure if the problem is that 'presentism' in (3) is doing a lot of work, or if (2) is just false when you can study a period long enough to reconstruct its standards. Where exactly does this go wrong, if it does? And if it doesn't go wrong, am I just stuck with the conclusion?
