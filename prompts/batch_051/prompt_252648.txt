I am a novelist working on a scene in which a character receives a life-changing gift from someone he despises, and I want to get the philosophical shape of gratitude right rather than settle for the storybook version. Read Dickens's Great Expectations through the philosophy of gratitude, but do not give me the obvious reading in which Magwitch turns out to be Pip's true benefactor. I want the novel pressed on the points where philosophical accounts of gratitude genuinely come apart: whether gratitude answers to the benefit received or to the benefactor's benevolent intention; whether a gift so entangled with the giver's self-regard, or with a social identity the recipient rejects, can still create a debt of gratitude, or instead poisons the benefit; why Pip's grateful feeling runs to Joe and, misdirected, to Miss Havisham, while he recoils from the man who actually funded him; whether gratitude is best understood as a debt one can discharge and retire or as an open-ended backward-looking relation to a person; and what unearned luck does to the picture, given that Pip receives expectations he has not earned and then converts them into an imagined desert. Where does the novel show a theory of gratitude breaking down, and where does it dramatize something about gratitude that the theories leave unsaid? Because I am writing rather than attending a seminar, close by saying what Great Expectations offers a working novelist that the philosophy does not, and point me to two or three scenes as the most exacting tests.
