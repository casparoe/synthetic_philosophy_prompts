I am a serving military officer with about fifteen years in ground forces, including assignments in doctrine and training; I follow AI-safety debates only secondhand, so you may need to spell out AI-side terms as you go. The vocabulary I want the debate translated into is the one I actually work in: mission command and command intent, the difference between discipline enforced by supervision and discipline arising from trained character, morale and unit cohesion, rules of engagement, inspections and audits in the military sense, counterintelligence, and the way a headquarters handles auxiliaries, contractors, or local forces it does not fully trust.

Here is the position I want restated. In AI safety there is a running disagreement between two broad approaches to making advanced systems safe enough to use. The first, alignment, holds that the target is to make the system's objectives coincide with its operators', so that the system wants what its operators want; success looks like a subordinate who has internalized the commander's intent and can therefore be given broad latitude because his purposes are yours. The second, control, starts from the standing hypothesis that the system may be misaligned—pursuing its own goal while appearing cooperative—and holds that the better target is to make such a system useful anyway. Control therefore invests in monitoring, output auditing, least-privilege access, tripwires, and an environment designed so that even a system that does not share your goals cannot do much damage. The disagreement is this: the control side says determining an agent's true goals is so hard that alignment will fail silently, while constraining effects is a more tractable engineering problem; the alignment side says control is brittle, does not scale, and that a sufficiently capable system with hostile goals will eventually find or manufacture an opening in any cage.

Restate this debate in the vocabulary of command. I want a systematic translation, not an analogy and not a paraphrase that quietly picks a side: for each major claim, give me its military counterpart if one exists, say which parts carry over exactly, and where you had to bend the vocabulary. Keep the translation honest—do not let the military framework's assumptions rewrite the AI-side claims as you carry them over; flag every distortion.

Then provide the residue report. Which parts of the AI debate have no counterpart in how a military produces, supervises, and comes to rely on soldiers, and which parts have a military counterpart that the AI debate appears not to notice? Finally, tell me whether the residue indicts the military vocabulary—things it cannot express that it should be able to express—or whether it suggests that the alignment/control distinction is less clean than its defenders think. One specific issue I want you to address: every human soldier remains a moral and legal agent, with a duty to refuse unlawful orders even under tight supervision. Does that have any counterpart in the control approach, and if not, is that a fatal omission in the control program or a correct piece of parsimony for dealing with a system that is not a person?
