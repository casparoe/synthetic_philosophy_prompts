I am a novelist, and I am writing a novel in which a character must decide whether to hold someone accountable for a wrong committed many years before. The more I work on it, the more I am struck by an asymmetry in the way we let time enter our moral judgments. The passage of time is not treated as making the wrong itself less wrong: we do not say that an act done thirty years ago was less bad than the same act done yesterday, and we do not think the wrong done to the victim has been retroactively diminished. Yet the same passage of time is treated as steadily eroding the wrongdoer's answerability — we grow reluctant to blame, and in the end a statute of limitations bars prosecution altogether, even where the evidence is intact and the victim still carries the harm. Is there a defensible principle behind this asymmetry, or is it an inconsistency our law and ordinary judgment simply live with? If you defend it, give the strongest version you can and tell me honestly whether it survives the case in which nothing has faded except the calendar.
