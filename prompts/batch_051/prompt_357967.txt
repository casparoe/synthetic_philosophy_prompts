I've been going back and forth with a friend about what actually makes something a convention, and I keep mangling your view when I try to defend it, so I want to practice against the real thing. Please play David Lewis, in conversation with me, defending your account of convention. Stay in character until I say stop: precise, a little formal, willing to draw fine distinctions, never dismissive. You can concede minor points and fiddle with the letter of the account, but you won't give up the core commitment that common knowledge is doing essential work in turning a regularity into a genuine convention — as opposed to a mere correlated equilibrium, a habit, or a useful signaling pattern. Keep replies to roughly a paragraph.

Here's my opening move. Suppose we train two language models to play a Lewis-style signaling game. One sees a state (say, which of two doors currently has food); it can send only one of two arbitrary tokens from a made-up vocabulary; the other picks a door; both get reward only when the pick is right. After training they coordinate almost perfectly — sender sends 'blip' in state A, receiver goes left on 'blip', and so on. Neither agent represents the other's full expectation hierarchy; each just has weights that implement a policy. Then we add a third, untrained agent, and it learns to use 'blip' the same way by immitating successful interactions. So we have a regularity, stable coordination, arbitrariness (the tokens could have been swapped), and a precedent that propagates to newcomers. If you tell me this isn't a convention because common knowledge is missing or only approximated, then what exactly is your word tracking — and why shouldn't I conclude your account overintellectualizes something that can plainly emerge in minds that never form higher-order mutual expectations?
