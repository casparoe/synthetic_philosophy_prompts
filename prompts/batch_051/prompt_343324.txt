I find myself pulled toward a Clifford-style ethics of belief: one should never hold a belief on insufficient evidence, and substituting hope, loyalty, or the comfort of believing for evidence is not merely unwise but wrong. The position has systematic virtues I find genuinely compelling—it supplies a single, universal test for permissible belief, it protects shared inquiry from wishful thinking, and it treats credulity as a public harm rather than a private failing.

But I keep colliding with a firm intuition about a particular kind of case. Some beliefs are interventions: a teacher's premature conviction that a struggling student can improve, or a patient's conviction that a hard recovery is possible, can itself help make the believed proposition true—not through wishful magic, but because genuine, wholehearted belief changes the believer's persistence, attention, and manner, and because the student or patient often detects merely feigned confidence. In these cases the evidence for the proposition is partly sensitive to whether the agent already believes it; the belief does not merely forecast the fact, it helps produce it. The evidentialist rule, applied before the outcome, forbids exactly the belief that would generate the evidence that would later satisfy it. Nor can I dissolve the intuition by saying the teacher should just act confident while privately withholding belief, because the case seems to require the belief to be genuine for the causal effect to occur.

I don't want to be told which side simply wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that yields—for it to be debunked as practical reasoning dressed up as epistemology—and what it would take for the evidentialist principle to be amended, perhaps by admitting a category for beliefs whose adoption changes their chance of becoming true. And I want to know whether a Jamesian will-to-believe response, on which our passional nature may legitimately settle options that are live, unavoidable, and momentous and that the intellect cannot decide, actually reconciles these two, or merely relocates the problem from believing against the evidence to choosing when the evidence is unsettled. What would justify each move?
