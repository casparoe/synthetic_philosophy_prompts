I serve on the board of a small housing co-op, and for the past year we have made decisions by voting separately on the distinct considerations behind a proposal rather than on the proposal itself. I have been trying to write down the principles I actually endorse for how a group should turn its members' views into a view of the group, and before I rely on them in next year's decisions I would like a consistency check. Please go through the commitments below and tell me which genuinely pull against one another, which merely appear to, how severe each real tension is, what the cheapest revision would be in each case, and whether any single revision would restore coherence at the lowest overall cost.

(1) When the group forms a position, each member's judgment deserves equal weight. I reject the idea that seniority, articulateness, or force of personality should give some member's view more weight merely in virtue of who they are.

(2) The group's official answer to any single question should simply be the answer most members give. I do not see what else it could mean for a position to be the group's rather than just some subgroup's.

(3) The group's overall position must be logically coherent. If the positions it officially maintains cannot all be true at once, or if it officially accepts certain considerations but refuses to accept what plainly follows from them, then its position is defective no matter how cleanly the votes were counted.

(4) When a proposal rests on several separable considerations, the group should vote on each one separately — for example, whether the proposal would achieve what it claims, whether the cost estimate is credible, and whether it is consistent with commitments already made — and then let the decision follow from the answers the group has adopted. Voting only on the final proposal would let mood and rhetoric override what the group had deliberately settled point by point.

(5) If every member's own view is internally consistent, then no fair method of pooling those views can produce an inconsistent group view. If incoherence shows up at the group level, it must come from confused members or from the procedure being manipulated; it cannot be a normal output of a fair procedure run over consistent inputs.

(6) On narrowly technical factual questions, a majority vote is a poor instrument. If the members with the relevant training and track record all agree about what a cost model assumes or whether an inspection report supports a repair schedule, I am content for the group to adopt their finding even when most members would initially vote the other way. This looks on its face like it conflicts with (1) and (2), and I have never been able to settle whether it really does.
