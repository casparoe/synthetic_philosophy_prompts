I have been working through Darwin’s treatment of morality and religion in The Descent of Man, and I have built an argument that seems to prove too much—more than I think Darwin could accept, and more than I want to accept. I would like help identifying where the argument goes wrong, or, if it does not go wrong, what I must give up.

The argument runs like this. (1) Darwin explains the human moral sense as an outgrowth of social instincts that natural selection favored because they made groups more cohesive and cooperative. (2) These same evolved social-cognitive tendencies, on his account, also shape human religious feeling and belief. (3) If a judgment or belief is produced by a disposition that natural selection favored for its fitness effects, then the content of that judgment or belief is explained by those fitness effects, not by its tracking of moral or theological truth. (4) Therefore, on Darwin’s account, our moral judgments give us no reason to think that the actions and traits we morally approve of really are good, and our religious beliefs give us no reason to think that the objects of those beliefs really exist or have the features attributed to them. (5) The same explanation covers the normative judgments Darwin himself makes—for example, when he expresses abhorrence of slavery, or when he treats certain forms of moral and religious sensibility as evidence of progress. (6) Thus Darwin’s theory undercuts his own moral and religious evaluations. (7) But that is self-defeating: the theory is presented as a contribution to understanding human life, and it relies throughout on the very capacity to distinguish better from worse moral outlooks that the theory seems to undermine.

I find myself least willing to reject (1) and (2), because they seem to capture Darwin’s actual project. My suspicion is that (3) or (5) contains a subtle slide, but I cannot name the slide without either invoking a form of moral realism Darwin did not clearly hold or weakening the evolutionary explanation so much that it loses its force. Which step should I reject, and what would that rejection cost? If the argument is valid, is the honest conclusion that Darwin’s descriptive-explanatory project and his evaluative commitments cannot both be kept?

Please answer in American English.
