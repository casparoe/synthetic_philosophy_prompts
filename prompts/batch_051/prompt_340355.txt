A widely circulated popular article summarizes Gödel's incompleteness theorems this way:

"Gödel proved that no consistent mathematical system can prove all of the true statements about arithmetic, and that no such system can prove its own consistency. This ended the hope of reducing mathematics to a single fixed set of rules. It also means that any computer, since it can only follow the rules of some formal system, will always be limited as a mathematician: there will be true arithmetical statements it cannot prove, even in principle. Gödel himself took this to settle the question of minds and machines, arguing that because we humans can see that the Gödel sentence is true even though the system cannot prove it, the human mind cannot be a machine."

I suspect this is the sort of confident popularization that is mostly right while quietly overstating several of the things it reports, but I cannot identify exactly where it departs from what the theorems actually show. Please audit it sentence by sentence. For each sentence, distinguish what a careful logician would accept, what needs qualification, and what is subtly wrong. Then assess the two conclusions drawn from the summary: first, that any computer will always be limited as a mathematician in the way described, and second, that the theorems show the human mind cannot be a machine. Indicate which of these conclusions, if any, survive once the qualifications are put back in.
