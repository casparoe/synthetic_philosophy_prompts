I'm a novelist, and I've accidentally made the Meno problem the spine of the book I'm writing — the old puzzle about why knowing the way to Larissa is worth more than merely having a true belief about it. My narrator is a burnt-out "fixer" for a newsroom whose operating code is that the only truth that counts is the one that turns out true by deadline, and how you got there is someone else's problem. I meant for her to be wrong in an interesting way, but now I can't quite say what she's missing, and the character has started to wobble on the page. So I made myself a list of my own verdicts, to pin down what I actually think before I revise her.

- A train dispatcher gets an anonymous phone call saying a rail bridge is washed out; it's true. Another dispatcher has read the engineering report and knows it. The same trains get rerouted either way, but I value the second man's state much more, and I'd put my kids on the train he's running. I can't tell whether the difference is in his mind right now or just in his future reliability.
- A former math student once worked through the proof that the square root of two is irrational. She now remembers only the conclusion and can't reconstruct the argument. I want to say she still knows it, but I value her state less than when the proof was fresh — and more than if she'd picked it up as gossip at a party.
- A gambler always bets on seven, and he's right tonight. True belief, fine, but I don't value his state at all; I'd trust a coin before him.
- A chicken-sexer can sort chicks with near-perfect accuracy and can't name a single feature he's using. I value that as much as knowledge, maybe exactly as much, even though he has no story to offer about why hes right.
- Two hikers are about to descend the same ridge. One read the topo map an hour ago; the other dreamed the route, and the dream happens to match the map. I'd walk behind the map-reader, but I honestly can't tell whether the difference is in their heads at the moment or just a bet about who will still be right next time.

Is there a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures these verdicts? I want it stated precisely enough to rule on further cases I haven't sorted out: a true belief planted by a manipulator that happens to track the truth, knowledge that never once guides action, a belief that's true and held with total certainty for thoroughly bad reasons. And be frank about which of my verdicts the principle forces me to drop, because I suspect they can't all survive.
