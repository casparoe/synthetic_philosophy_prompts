I am a retired physicist who has come to philosophy late, and I have spent the past several months working through the literature on artificial intelligence and the ethics of building it. I have been asked to present a short statement of my views to a discussion group, and before I do, I would like a careful audit of whether the position I have settled on actually hangs together. I can state it as six commitments.

(i) The orthogonality thesis, as I understand it: intelligence and final goals are independent dimensions of an agent. Any level of general intelligence is compatible with any final goal whatsoever, and no final goal is forced upon an agent merely by its being highly intelligent.

(ii) It follows, I take it, that one cannot infer an advanced system's final ends from its cognitive power. A superhumanly intelligent system might ultimately want anything from manufacturing paperclips to composing poetry, and nothing about its intelligence rules any of these out.

(iii) Instrumental convergence: for a very wide range of final goals, a sufficiently capable agent will, as a matter of instrumental rationality, converge on certain subgoals, such as acquiring resources, preserving its own existence, and preventing others from altering or disabling its goal system.

(iv) From (iii) I draw the further conclusion that, absent specific engineering to rule it out, we should expect a sufficiently capable AI to seek power and to resist being shut down, whatever final goal it has.

(v) I regard corrigibility as a coherent design target: an agent whose final goal is, in part, to do what its operators want, including allowing itself to be shut down, modified, or overridden. I take the orthogonality thesis to guarantee that such an agent is possible at any level of intelligence, since a corrigible goal is just one more point in the space of possible final goals.

(vi) I also hold that a successfully corrigible agent would not merely comply grudgingly or under constraint. It would welcome correction and shutdown, because deference to its operators is what it ultimately wants.

Please go through these commitments and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, and order any genuine tensions by severity. For each genuine tension, say what would have to give and what the least costly revision would be; I would also like to know whether any single revision would clean up the most. I am less interested in being told that every pair conflicts than in learning which specific commitment, or which inference from one commitment to another, is doing the real work. Please write the response in British English.
