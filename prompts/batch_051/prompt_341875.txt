I teach an evening philosophy class for adults who are sharp but have no formal training in ethics, and next week I need to introduce Benatar's asymmetry argument for the conclusion that it is better never to have come into existence. I will take the four cells of the asymmetry as given: the presence of pain is bad; the presence of pleasure is good; the absence of pain is good even where no one exists to enjoy that good; the absence of pleasure is not bad so long as no one is thereby deprived. Do not rehearse the argument itself. Plan how I should teach it in ninety minutes: the order in which the ideas must land so the conclusion feels forced rather than like a trick; the single worked example that should carry the whole session and what it must have and what it must not have, so it does not quietly smuggle in a person-affecting assumption; the misconceptions the class will form — for instance, running the two absence claims together, supposing the argument needs an unborn person to be benefited, or inferring that it tells existing people to regret their lives — and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off; a closing check that would show me they have grasped the asymmetry itself rather than merely been persuaded by the rhetoric; and what I should openly set aside as unsettled at this level so the evening does not collapse into an argument about suicide or population policy. Please write your answer in British English.
