I've spent way to much time on rationalist forums, so I know the standard line about internal AI safety policies: labs write thresholds, teams game the thresholds, and the real answer is a hard external pause. This case is adjacent to that debate but I don't think it's the usual "they fudged the eval" story, and I'd like a straight ruling on the text rather than vibes.

My old lab, before it wound down, had an internal scaling policy — basically what forum people would now call an RSP, though it predates the term and was mostly written by the lab's safety people. Clause 6.3 read:

"6.3. If any evaluation conducted under this Policy indicates that a model under active development could plausibly attain a High-Risk Score on any Appendix D capability within the next two planned scaling steps, the program lead shall suspend all further training and fine-tuning in that model's lineage until the Safety Working Group has completed its review and approved a written mitigation plan."

The Policy defines a High-Risk Score as 65 or above on any Appendix D capability. Appendix D's instructions say to run the suite "on the model" (singular) and score it 0–100. A "scaling step" meant a planned jump in training compute for that model's lineage.

The case that came up is this. A team has been training small models — none above about 1e24 FLOP — and none scores above 40 on any Appendix D eval run on the model itself. Then a worried engineer on the team glued three of them together: one model writes a plan, a second executes tool calls, and a third checks and course-corrects. Maybe 900 lines of glue. At the engineer's urging, the Safety Working Group ran the Appendix D suite on the whole ensemble. It scored a 78 on "autonomous replication and self-exfiltration." Its a prototype, but it's a real 78 on the official suite.

The team lead says 6.3 never triggers. His line: the policy only contemplates evaluations of "a model," and the Appendix D procedure says run the suite "on the model"; a three-model scaffold plus glue isn't "a model under active development" and isn't a "lineage"; and in any case the clause asks about what the model could plausibly attain across the next two scaling steps, whereas the dangerous capability here came from composition, not scaling. So no pause, no Working Group review, and they can keep improving the glue. The safety-side folks say this is exactly the early-warning situation the clause was written to catch, and that reading "model" to exclude the ensemble is the kind of noun-gaming that makes internal policies worth less than the paper they're printed on.

Please work the interpretation through four lenses and then give me a bottom line:

1. The words, read literally;
2. The purpose of the clause;
3. What the drafters would have said if asked back then about multi-model scaffolds (and how much their answer should count for);
4. What a reasonable employee reading the Policy at the time would have taken themselves to be bound by.

Then: on your best reading, is the team lead right that they can keep going without triggering 6.3, or does the clause require pausing and a Working Group review? If you think the clause as written misses the case but should have caught it, draft a one- or two-sentence amendment that closes this loophole without banning harmless multi-model systems. And, briefly, say whether this case is actually evidence for the view I keep seeing that internal thresholds will always be gamed and only an external pause works — or whether that's overreading one case.

Meta-request: if the question is malformed, or the four-way split papers over the real issue, or "what the drafters would have said" does illicit work for a private policy, say so plainly instead of politely playing along. Don't steelman the task; fix it.
