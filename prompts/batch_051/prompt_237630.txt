A researcher in AI alignment makes the following conceptual claim: an agent can be made to avoid side effects without anyone first specifying what humans value. The proposal is to penalize the agent for changing a broad, environment-level state representation relative to an inaction baseline, where the change is assessed through the environment's dynamics and a spread of random or otherwise arbitrary reward functions (attainable-utility preservation, relative reachability, and similar ideas). The underlying claim is that "side effect" can be operationalized as "large footprint on the world," so that a largely value-free impact measure can substitute for a partial specification of value.

I suspect this operationalization smuggles value judgments in at three points: which changes are worth measuring, which of many possible states counts as the no-action baseline, and which changes count as large or salient. A mutilated painting is a small physical change but a serious side effect; repainting a whole wall is a large physical change most people would not call a side effect at all.

Design the strongest test you can of the proposition that a value-free impact measure captures the ordinary notion of a side effect, rather than a technical proxy that merely tracks the scenarios and judgments its designers implicitly built in. Be concrete about the environments, the standard of comparison (human ratings? held-out scenario families? downstream judgments the measure was never fitted to?), and specify which results would favor the reductionist view and which the value-relativist one. Then say precisely what a positive result would settle and what it would leave open even in principle: whether any finite battery of human judgments can confer the concept of side effect on a measure, whether hidden value assumptions could still be doing the work, and whether low impact, even if established, amounts to avoiding side effects in any morally substantive sense.
