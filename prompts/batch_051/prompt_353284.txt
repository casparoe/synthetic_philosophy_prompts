I'm doing a PhD that sits somewhere between political epistemology and philosophy of language, and a few weeks back I got into an argument with a friend about whether the way people talk past each other online is best described as a failure of interpretation or a failure of good will. Since then I've had a parralel stuck in my head, and I can't decide wether it's deep or just pattern-matching. Davidson's principle of charity in radical interpretation says, roughly, that you can't get interpretation off the ground unless you assume the person you're interpreting is mostly rational and mostly right by your own lights; massive error is unstateable, because if you attribute too much irrationality you are no longer interpreting, just projecting noise. In political life, serious disagreement looks as if it depends on a similar default: if I write off the other side as arguing in bad faith — as rationalising, performing for their own group, and immune to evidence — then I am not disagreeing with them any more, I am treating them as a mechanism to be explained. Yet that is exactly what partisans do to each other, increasingly, and both sides do it symmetrically. So here is what I want to know: is the norm against attributing bad faith just the principle of charity applied to political argument, or does that parallel break? I'd like someone to work out where the structures genuinely match and where they come apart. One thing that worries me: the principle of charity operates on truth and rationality of belief, whereas bad-faith attribution is about motives and sincerity, and I can't tell whether that is a real difference or just a move from beliefs to reasons for belief. There is also an asymmetry that bothers me: the radical interpreter has almost nothing to go on apart from the speaker's behaviour, whereas partisans have a huge evidence base about the other side's think tanks, media, and polling strategy, so the claim that you cannot even formulate their error without charity might not carry over. What would have to be true of both sides for results from radical interpretation to transfer to political good faith? And is there something the parallel predicts — for instance, that systematic bad-faith attribution should be self-undermining or unintelligible in a functioning political culture — that turns out false in practice?
