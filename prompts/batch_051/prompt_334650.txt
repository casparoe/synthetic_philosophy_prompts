I have been asked to give the ten-minute opening address at a two-day workshop hosted by our defense ministry's capability-development office, on "meaningful human control in autonomous targeting." The room will be genuinely mixed: uniformed officers, several of them former weapons directors who distrust headquarters phrase-making; engineers from the firms that build the sensor-fusion and target-nomination systems; international humanitarian law attorneys who will be listening for the law of distinction, proportionality, and precautions in attack; and a handful of philosophers and policy scholars. The workshop is organized around a deliberately rough working idea of meaningful human control: that it is not enough for a human to be nominally "in" or "on" the loop, because real control requires understanding what the system is doing and why, a genuine possibility of deliberation and intervention, and clear answerability for what follows. I am a former air-defense officer who has moved into a policy role, so I have credibility with the operators and can also speak the engineers' language. I want the address to do real philosophical work rather than serve as a welcome. Specifically, it should argue that meaningfulness cannot be verified the way a performance parameter can — it is a property of the whole decision architecture (how targets get nominated, how recommendations are timed and presented, how crews are trained, and where responsibility falls) rather than a property of merely having a human near the release decision. It should be built around one anchoring vignette from my time on a review board: a system we assessed would flag a recommended engagement in so compressed a window that the operator's role had collapsed into ratifying a decision that was, in every relevant sense, already made, and afterward none of us could reconstruct from the system's presentation why that particular track had been nominated. Please write the address so that this vignette does the illustrative work, so that the operators and engineers hear it as practical experience rather than as a lecture from an ethicist, and so that the lawyers and philosophers are left with a substantive thesis to push against over the rest of the workshop.
