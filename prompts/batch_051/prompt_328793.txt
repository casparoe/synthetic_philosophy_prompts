After too many arguments about the sunk-cost fallacy, I've ended up convinced that the standard lesson drawn from the dollar auction—that it shows rational agents being locked into mutually ruinous escalation—is overstated, and that the game is better read as an under-specified commitment problem. By the dollar auction I mean the usual setup: an auctioneer sells a dollar, bids come in small increments, and both the highest and the second-highest bidder must pay their final bid even though only the highest bidder receives the dollar. My position is this: once a bidder is already the losing second-highest bidder, continuing is often locally rational conditional on a belief about when the other player will stop; the decisive error, when there is one, happens earlier, at the point of entering or staying in without a pre-committed exit threshold, rather than in the marginal decision to keep bidding. Tell me what would have to be true for this to be wrong. I want the defeaters ranked from decisive to merely awkward—formal analyses of the auction's equilibria, experimental findings about where real subjects stop, close variants of the game, and arguments from neighboring escalation settings—plus an account of where a defender of my view should honestly concede ground. End with a committed overall verdict on how exposed the position actually is, not a survey of possible interpretations.
