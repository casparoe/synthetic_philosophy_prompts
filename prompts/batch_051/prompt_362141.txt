I'm a retired atmospheric chemist and I've only recently started reading philosophy seriously, so forgive me if I get the terminology wrong. The position I keep being drawn to is a strong version of what I've seen called cognitive liberty or mental privacy. The principle would be: no reading, recording, or altering of a person's neural activity without their specific, informed, revocable consent, full stop. I like it because it's a single clean rule rather than a stack of balances that courts and companies can game. And the argument behind it feels right to me: thought is upstream of speech and action, so if you protect what people say and do but let the state or a company instrument the head, you're locking the front door and leaving the crawlspace open. If a person has a right to be let alone anywhere, it's in their own skull.

But I have an intuition I can't shake. A friend in aviation safety tells me about systems that passively monitor a pilot's EEG for fatigue and micro-sleeps and alert them, or ground them, before something goes wrong. I think that should be allowed and probably mandatory in safety-critical jobs, and I dont think the pilot's consent should be required. I know this is exactly the kind of benign monitoring that the strong principle has to forbid. But it seems obvious to me that reading a fatigue state off a pilot is more like reading a temparature gauge on an engine than reading someone's diary. I'd step on a plane with that monitor over one without it, and the pilot who wants to opt out is exactly the pilot I don't want flying me.

So there's the clash: a tidy theory that makes this wrong, and a firm intuition that says it's fine. I don't want you to just pick a side. I want a negotiated verdict. What would it take for my intuition to be a debunking target—normalization of surveillance, scope insensitivity, an availability heuristic from plane-crash stories—rather than a genuine counterexample? And what would it take for the theory to be amended instead, say with a principled line between physiological readouts and semantic content, without that line just being an ad hoc carve-out that lets surveillance back in disguised as "it's only biometrics"? Is there a version of the absolute rule that keeps its decision-procedure virtues and still lets the fatigue monitor through, or does any such concession quietly turn the principle into cost-benefit balancing?
