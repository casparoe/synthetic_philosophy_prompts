I have spent a long time reading about modal metaphysics and personal identity, mostly on rationalist blogs and forums, and one question keeps generating the same set of stable judgements in me: which facts about the process that produced me could have been different while the person who resulted was still me, and which would have produced someone else? I hold these verdicts almost as data: they seem to come before any theory, and they have survived several attempts to debunk them. I would like to know what principle, if any, they add up to; where no single principle fits, I would like to know which verdict I should abandon.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. Different sperm, same egg. If, on the occasion on which I was conceived, the same egg had been fertilised by a different one of my father's sperm, the result would have been my sibling and not me. I am as confident of this as I am of anything in this area.

2. Same gametes, different womb and upbringing. If the very sperm and egg that produced me had instead been combined in vitro, with the embryo gestated in another person's womb and the child raised by different people in a different country, the child would still be me. I am confident that gestation and upbringing do not enter into origin.

3. Same gametes, later fusion. If the same sperm and egg had been cryopreserved and fertilisation had been delayed by two years, with the child born after the delay, I think the child would still be me. The moment of fusion does not feel essential.

4. Clone from a somatic cell. If a clone had been made from one of my infant skin cells, the result would have been a genetically identical child who was not me. I take the nuclear genome not to be sufficient for my identity.

5. Synthetic genome. If a future laboratory assembled an embryo from synthesised DNA matching my genome exactly, with no biological continuity from either of my parents' gametes, the child would not be me. Again, the particular gametes seem to matter, not merely their informational content.

6. Mitochondrial replacement. If the same sperm had fertilised the same egg after the egg's mitochondria had been replaced by donor mitochondria, leaving the nuclear genome unchanged, I lean towards saying the child would still be me, though with less confidence than in cases 2 and 3. What matters about the egg, it seems, is its nuclear genetic material rather than its cytoplasm.

7. Twinning. Suppose the zygote from which I came had split into two embryos and each had been carried to term. My intuition is that neither resulting twin is simply identical to the original zygote, yet each has an equal claim to count as the person from that sperm and egg. I also cannot say that, had the split never occurred, the single child would have been me rather than my twin. In the actual world, where no split occurred, I am comfortable saying I am the person who developed from that zygote.

I can already feel some pressure between these verdicts. Cases 2, 3 and 6 suggest that the egg matters through its nuclear genetic material, while cases 1 and 5 suggest that something about the historical particularity of the gametes is doing the work. So here are two further cases on which I am genuinely stuck, and on which I would like the principle to take a stand.

8. Nuclear transfer with a synthetic nucleus. Suppose the actual egg had its nuclear DNA removed and replaced with a synthetic copy of my mother's nuclear genome, and was then fertilised by the actual sperm. Should I say the child is me, following the thought behind case 6, or not me, following the thought behind case 5?

9. Different meiotic outcome. Suppose the same sperm and the same egg had met exactly as they actually did, but upstream of that meeting the egg's final meiotic division had resolved differently, so that the same cell ended up with a different though equally maternal nuclear genome. I do not know whether I should call this the same egg, and therefore I do not know whether the resulting child would be me.

What I am after is a precise statement of the principle, or a small number of candidate principles, that best captures my verdicts; the case or cases that motivate each clause; and the principle's verdicts on cases 8 and 9 and on any further case it commits me to. Please say plainly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which it would force me to revise. If the only principle that saves every verdict is too gerrymandered to be believed, I would rather be told that, and told which verdict is the one to drop.

Please write your answer in British English.
