I run the policy unit of a small human rights organization, and twice a week I have to decide whether an argument landing on my desk — a draft campaign statement, a section of a report to an international human rights body, or a briefing note for a legislator's office — should lean on the language of natural or human rights, or whether that framing would amount to rhetoric a skeptical reviewer will dismiss. I usually have an afternoon to make the call, I have no research staff, and our executive director will not let anything go out unless I can say who bears the corresponding duty, what concretely follows from the claim, and what would change if it were taken seriously. Lately I have been thinking with Bentham's polemic against natural rights: his charge that a right without a lawgiver, a determinate duty-bearer, and a mechanism of enforcement is high-flown nonsense, and that a moral complaint should be translated into a statement about identifiable interests and foreseeable consequences before it is promoted to the status of a right. I want to borrow the core of that critique without turning into a caricature who believes only statutes generate obligations.

Turn Bentham's critique into a reusable procedure I can run before deciding how to frame a claim: a checklist or decision tree in words, with the tests in the order I should run them. I am not asking for a history of natural rights or an interpretive defense of Bentham; I want the operational version. The procedure should tell me which distinctions must survive the simplification — legal right versus moral demand versus purely rhetorical right; the interest actually at stake; the identifiable duty-bearer; the remedy; the cost and likelihood of enforcement — and which distinctions can be collapsed without loss. It must handle the hard cases explicitly: a right not yet legalized but morally urgent, a right whose duty-bearer is diffuse or outside the jurisdiction, a right claimed on behalf of future persons or communities, and a claim that is failing the strict tests but still doing useful political work. At each step, mark what is a genuine test and what is a judgment call dressed up as one, and be honest about where the procedure will misfire.

Then apply the procedure to three worked examples: a campaign asserting a right to adequate housing in a jurisdiction with no such legal guarantee; a report claiming that a multinational's emissions violate a right to a stable climate; and a statement insisting that undocumented migrants have a right not to be deported, grounded in human dignity. Work through how the procedure would discipline or reformulate each claim, and make sure at least one example yields an answer I will not like — a claim I am sympathetic to that your procedure would downgrade or force me to reframe. Conclude by stating plainly what the procedure cannot do, so that I never mistake a disciplined way of testing a rights claim for a decision about whether the underlying moral complaint is just.
