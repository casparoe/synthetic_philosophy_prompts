I'm giving a department colloquium next month defending a strengthened version of the Basic Argument against moral responsibility. The standard version runs: you are responsible for what you do only if you are responsible for the mental constitution from which the action issues; you could be responsible for that constitution only if you chose it; choosing it would require an even earlier constitution for which you were responsible; so the required self-creation regresses and never gets started. My strengthening is this: retreating to identification or wholehearted caring, as Frankfurt-style compatibilists do, does not stop the regress, because the attitudes that constitute identification are themselves shaped by the agent's causal history. I conclude that ordinary praise and blame are unjustified, not merely that ultimate responsibility is impossible. The audience includes two compatibilists who locate responsible agency in identification with one's motives, one compatibilist who ties blame to whether the action expressed the agent's evaluative judgments, one hard incompatibilist who accepts my conclusion but doubts the relevance of identification to the argument, several agnostic graduate students, and a philosopher of action who suspects I equivocate on constitution. Prepare my Q&A prep sheet: the ten or so objections I will actually get, roughly in the order they will arise, phrased as those people would phrase them, with what I should concede, what I should hold, and a note on the weakest point in each reply. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
