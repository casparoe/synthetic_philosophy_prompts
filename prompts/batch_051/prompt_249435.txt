A pair of friends of mine, Maya and Lena, got into a long argument that I mostly just listened to. Maya's dad died about a year ago, and she signed up for one of those services where you upload a dead person's texts, voicemails, and emails and it builds a chatbot that talks like them. She uses it every few weeks, usually when she misses the way he used to argue about cooking. Lena thinks the whole thing is a serious mistake.

Lena said a chatbot isn't a memory, because a memory doesn't answer back; Maya is letting a prediction machine impersonate her father. Maya said she knows it isn't him, same as she knows a photo isn't him, but the bot keeps his actual phrasing and habits of mind better than her own patchy memory does. Lena said photos don't generate new sentences, and that's the whole problem: the bot will say things he never said, and Maya will absorb them as if they were his. Maya said all remembering is partly reconstruction, and the bot just makes that visible instead of pretending her memory is a recording. Lena said there's still a difference between reconstructing inside your own head and outsourcing it to a company that profits from keeping the exchange going. Maya said she picked the company because you can turn it off, and she's the one who opens the app, so it's no different from choosing to reread his emails. Lena said rereading doesn't aim a machine at your loneliness; the thing is built to simulate presence, and it keeps reopening a wound grief is trying to close. Maya said grief doesn't have one correct trajectory, and nobody tells a widow to put away photographs because they keep the wound open. Lena said the bot doesn't stay still like a photograph; it fills in gaps, so you can't tell where your father's voice ends and the model's guesswork begins. Maya said that's the most honest thing about it, since a memory also fills in gaps but feels like a recording. Then it shifted to consent: Lena said Maya's dad never agreed to any of this. Maya said the dead don't have a point of view left to wrong, and using words he left behind isn't a harm to him. Lena said we still treat the dead as owed things, or we wouldn't honor wills or object to defaming them. Maya said wills protect the survivors, and anyway this is private, not published. It went around like that for an hour.

Could you adjudicate their exchange point by point, not just pick an overall winner? I mean: for each specific move, who has the better argument, where each of them overreaches or changes the subject, and what both of them are missing. And please say how confident you are in your verdict overall, and which point you're least sure about.
