Last night I had a long argument with a friend about anthropic reasoning, and I left it unclear where our disagreement really lives. Consider two candidate principles for how a rational agent should update on the bare fact that it exists as one observer among others. The Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA) says: reason as if you were randomly selected from among all observers that actually exist in an appropriate reference class. The Self-Indication Assumption (SIA) says: the fact that you exist is itself evidence favoring hypotheses on which more observers exist overall. My credence that SSA rather than SIA is the correct principle is about 0.7; my friend puts it near 0.3. Give me your own number, but spend most of your answer decomposing it: break the question into the sub-questions doing the real work—whether indexical facts such as my existence should shift probabilities at all, what the right reference class is, whether the doomsday-style conclusions SSA appears to license are acceptable, and whether SIA can avoid the presumptuous philosopher conclusion that we should favor hypotheses merely because they posit vastly more unobserved observers. Assign each sub-question a rough probability, explain how they combine into your overall credence, and identify which sub-question best explains the 0.7-versus-0.3 split between my friend and me. Then say what evidence or argument would do the most to move your credence.
