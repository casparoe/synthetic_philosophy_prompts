Please write a decision procedure for a state law-reform commission that must decide, several times each session, what to do about statutes that have gone unenforced for long stretches. The commission's staff are generalist lawyers and policy aides, not specialists in jurisprudence, and each recommendation must be defensible in a short memo to legislators, with the stakes being whether conduct remains criminal or subject to penalty. The jurisdiction has no controlling case law on desuetude, so the procedure must be defended on principle rather than precedent. The procedure should turn rule-of-law considerations—fair notice, non-arbitrary enforcement, legislative supremacy—into an ordered set of questions a staffer can work through, with a threshold for each question and a specified point at which the review stops and yields one of three recommendations: repeal, re-enact with prospective notice, or leave the statute in force. It should also mark the steps at which the procedure cannot settle the matter and a value judgment must be made, and say who should make it. Then run the procedure on three cases: a century-old statute forbidding Sunday trading that has never been enforced in living memory; a fifty-year-old adultery statute enforced only three times in the past two decades, in scattered counties with no clear pattern; and a decade-old environmental reporting regulation that has never been enforced although violations are widespread and documented. For each, give the recommendation the procedure produces and identify a point at which a reasonable member of the commission could depart from it. End with a candid statement of what the procedure cannot adjudicate—in particular, whether it can settle the legal validity of a dormant statute or only the practical question of how the commission should treat it.
