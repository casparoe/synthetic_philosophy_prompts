I keep noticing a weird asymmetry in how science-literate people talk about heritability, and I want to know whether its actually justified. If a study reports that height is about 80 percent heritable, nobody blinks; someone will just say 'height is basically genetic' and leave it there. Now suppose a different study reports that adult IQ is about 50 percent heritable—lower, actually, than the height figure, but still substantial. The very same people suddenly start listing caveats: heritability is a population-level variance decomposition, it says nothing about any individual child, it doesn't set a floor or ceiling, it shifts when the environment shifts, and it says nothing about differences between groups. All of that is also true of height, but nobody felt the need to say any of it when the trait was height. So the asymmetry is this: for a morally boring physical trait we let the statistic do work it doesn't actually do ('it's genetic, end of story'), but for a socially loaded trait we insist, correctly, that it doesn't mean that. Is there anything in the statistic itself, or in the difference between the traits, that justifies reading the same kind of number so differently? Maybe the stakes, or the history of people weaponizing IQ science, or the fact that intelligence and education are tangled up with agency in a way height isnt. Test the candidate justification against a near case (height in a sport where height is heavily selected, or a physical trait that is stigmatized but not socially loaded) and say whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or dropped. Along the way be precise about what a heritability coefficient can and cannot tell you.
