I've been thinking about how Newcomb's problem changes if the Predictor is no longer a stipulated oracle but a contemporary machine-learning system. In the standard problem, there is a transparent box containing $1,000 and an opaque box containing either $1,000,000 or nothing. The Predictor makes its prediction before you choose, and the contents of the opaque box are then fixed. The standard dominance argument for two-boxing runs like this: whatever the prediction was, taking both boxes gets you $1,000 more than taking only the opaque box; since your choice cannot causally influence a prediction already made, you should take both boxes.

Now change the case. Suppose the Predictor is an AI that has access to your draft reasoning, search history, and in-the-moment deliberation, and that can revise its prediction and the box's contents in real time up until you actually reach into the boxes. The prediction is no longer made before you deliberate; it is causally downstream of the very deliberation that is supposed to result in your choice. Does the dominance argument for two-boxing survive? On one reading it should: at the moment of choice the contents are settled, and conditional on whatever is in the opaque box, taking both boxes still yields $1,000 more, so dominance still applies. On another reading, the dominance argument requires the choice to be causally independent of the contents, and the live-updating Predictor destroys that independence — a disposition to two-box makes it overwhelmingly likely the box is empty, so the states over which the dominance argument ranges are not the right kind of states at all. I would like to know which premise of the two-boxing argument the new case bears on, whether the dominance argument can be repaired for this case and what the repair would have to say, or whether the live-updating case is no longer Newcomb's problem — and if it isn't, what it should be called.
