I am a novelist, and I have been studying filmed scenes of group deliberation to understand how a room actually changes its mind; Sidney Lumet's 12 Angry Men keeps returning to me as the sharpest case. I would like a reading of the film through the lens of the peer disagreement debate between conciliationism and steadfastness. As I understand the distinction, conciliationism holds that when I discover that someone who appears to be my epistemic peer — roughly, someone equally intelligent, equally careful, and equally well informed about the same evidence — disagrees with me, I ought to lower my confidence in the disputed belief, perhaps all the way to suspension of judgment; steadfastness holds that I may rationally retain my confidence even after learning of such peer disagreement. On its face, the film looks like a vindication of steadfastness: eleven jurors stand ready to convict, one refuses, and the film ends with his refusal vindicated.

But I suspect the film is less straightforward than that, and I want that suspicion tested. Are the other eleven really Juror 8's epistemic peers at the outset, given that he has noticed details of the switchblade, the timing of the elevated train, and the eyewitness's glasses that they have not, and given that several of them are plainly voting from prejudice, haste, or private grievance rather than from the evidence? Does the film in fact dramatize a case in which an apparent peer disagreement dissolves once overlooked evidence is shared — exactly the outcome conciliationism would predict — while its staging, camera work, and pacing make us experience it as a triumph of steadfastness? And does it matter that the final verdict is "not guilty" on the standard of reasonable doubt, rather than a finding that the accused is in fact innocent? I want a reading that treats the film's craft as part of its epistemic argument, and that concludes by saying what a novelist writing a scene of collective deliberation should and should not take from it.
