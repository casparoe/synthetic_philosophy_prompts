I've been trying to work out whether everyday conventions are ever moral obligations, and in particular what a visitor owes to the local conventions of a place they are in. Rather than reason from a definition, I kept a list of cases and my own verdicts on them. I want to know what principle, if any, captures the pattern.

1. A visitor from a no-tipping country dines in a restaurant in the United States, knowing in advance that servers are paid below the ordinary minimum wage and that tips are understood to be most of their income. They leave nothing. I think this is wrong, and not merely rude: it is closer to short-paying a worker than to breaching a folkway.

2. An American visitor in a country where tipping is not practiced insists on leaving a large tip after dinner; the staff are embarrassed and chase after them to return the money. I think the visitor did wrong, but of a different kind: they imposed their own convention on people who had a settled and reasonable norm against it.

3. In a city where orderly queuing for the bus is a firm local practice, a visitor from a city where people board by loose crowding walks past the line and boards first. I think they wrong the people who were waiting, even though the visitor's home city does not use queues this way.

4. After a disaster, a queue forms for clean water. A newcomer pushes to the front. I think this is plainly and gravely wrong, but I suspect it is wrong for reasons that have little to do with conventions: anyone, anywhere, who seizes scarce water from others wrongs them.

5. In a city where pedestrians routinely cross against the light when traffic is clear, a tourist crosses with the locals. I think nothing is wrong on either side of this.

6. In a city where pedestrians strictly wait for the light even on empty streets, a tourist crosses against the light at night when no car is in sight. I think this is mildly disrespectful, perhaps a small failure of deference, but I cannot identify anyone who is wronged.

7. A visitor enters a temple where removing one's shoes is the rule, and refuses quietly on the ground that the rule is arbitrary. I think they genuinely wrong the hosts and the other worshippers, and that the arbitrariness of the rule is beside the point.

8. A business traveler is in a country where small payments to officials to process routine paperwork are the accepted practice. They refuse on principle, and their paperwork stalls. I think the refusal is right, or at least permitted; the fact that it is the local way does not bind them.

9. At a dinner in a culture where refusing to drink during a toast is taken as a serious slight, a teetotaling visitor declines alcohol but raises their glass of water during the toast. I think the visitor does nothing wrong; the local convention cannot require drinking, though it can require a respectful substitute gesture.

10. In an office where saying "bless you" after a sneeze is usual, a visitor stays silent for a week. I do not think this is a wrong at all, only a minor social cost.

My working hypothesis is this: a visitor must follow a local convention when violating it would wrong a particular person—the server whose pay they withhold, the queuers whose place they take, the worshippers whose space they fail to respect—and need not follow it when the violation produces only diffuse offense or a reputation for rudeness. But I can feel that "wronging a particular person" may be smuggling in the conclusion, and case 6 bothers me: it involves identifiable locals who take themselves to be disrespected, but I still cannot make it into a moral wrong.

What I want from a response is the best statement of the principle behind these ten verdicts, stated precisely, with the cases that motivate each clause; a candid statement of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and which one it would force me to drop, if any; and a prediction for a case I have not listed—for example, a visitor who, rather than resisting, adopts and follows perfectly a local hierarchy that everyone there accepts but that the visitor regards as degrading, such as a strict workplace where juniors are expected to fetch coffee and stand when seniors enter. Please also separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on norms and obligation, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
