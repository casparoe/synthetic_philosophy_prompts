Berkeley, the idealist, also wrote about money, and there he argued that money is in its true idea only a ticket or counter: the real wealth of a country is its land, labor, and goods, and the tokens used to move those goods around are just a convention. He was writing when the practical argument was about whether a shortage of gold and silver coin in Ireland was a shortage of real wealth. Since then weve got pure fiat money, central banks that expand and contract the money stock to manage employment and inflation, and plenty of evidence that monetary contractions cause real recessions. That looks like the ticket does real causal work, not just record-keeping work, which is at least in tension with calling it a mere ticket or counter. Does Berkeley's argument survive this development? If it does, say exactly how the slogan should be read so it isnt refuted by a central-bank-induced recession; if it doesnt, identify which premise dies and what weaker version of the view remains true. And if Ive got Berkeley wrong, or the question turns on an ambiguity in "real" or "wealth," say so straight instead of answering it.
