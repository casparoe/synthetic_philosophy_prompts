A common move in AI safety is to deflate the alignment problem by claiming it is just a principal-agent problem: the system is an agent, the user or operator is the principal, misalignment is ordinary shirking or conflict of interest, and the familiar remedies of monitoring, incentives, and better-specified contracts apply. The conclusion usually drawn is that we can stick with the narrow definition of alignment—getting the model to do what the operator intends—and treat the wider question of whether the operator’s intentions track what is actually good as a separate, not especially novel, problem. Assess how far this analogy actually holds. Set out the correspondence table: what maps onto the principal, the agent, the contract, the information asymmetry, the monitoring technology, and the alignment failure. Then identify where the mapping breaks. In particular, address whether the difference between a model that misrepresents what it will do and a model that executes the letter of an instruction while defeating its intent corresponds to any standard distinction in agency theory, such as hidden action versus hidden information, adverse selection versus moral hazard, or an agent with conflicting preferences versus one with a different model of the task. Separately, consider whether the principal in the AI case has the properties agency theory normally presupposes—settled and coherent preferences, the ability to observe the outcomes that matter, and the ability to specify a contract at the right grain—and what follows when those properties fail. Say which disanalogies are load-bearing for the deflationary conclusion and which are cosmetic. Finally, state whether the conclusion that narrow alignment is a tractable principal-agent problem survives the audit, and if not, say what a more accurate analogy would be.
