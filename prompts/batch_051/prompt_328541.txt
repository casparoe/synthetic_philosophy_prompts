I am giving a seminar paper next month to a philosophy of science department, and I want a Q&A prep sheet rather than a fuller argument. The paper defends this thesis: during a scientific revolution science remains rational, but the rationality belongs, in the first instance, to the long-run competition between research traditions rather than to the individual scientist's moment of choice or to any shared, theory-neutral decision rule. In a revolution, I argue, at least two internally coherent traditions coexist, each with its own standards of evidence and explanation and its own unresolved anomalies; each side is rational by its own lights, so the charge that one side is irrational usually begs the question by importing the other side's standards; nevertheless, over a generation, one tradition produces a lopsided share of localized explanatory and experimental successes that can be assessed without fully translating the rival paradigms; and the community's eventual realignment can therefore be rational in the aggregate even though nobody ever had a neutral algorithm for choosing sides. I call this a distributed account of revolutionary rationality.

I will not be getting objections from an abstract referee, though. The room will be a specific one. Two senior faculty are falsificationists in roughly Popper's manner; they think Kuhn's talk of incommensurability and Gestalt-switch conversion describes a failure of nerve rather than a theory of scientific change. One senior figure is a committed Kuhn scholar who wrote on incommensurability and taxonomic change, and who will distrust any account that makes revolution look, in hindsight, like a clean case of accumulation. Two others work in a Lakatosian framework: the unit of appraisal is the research programme, and a change of programme is rational only insofar as the new programme is progressive and the old one degenerating relative to the problems they set themselves. There is also a group of younger social epistemologists and science-studies scholars who will suspect that the word "rational" is doing rhetorical work here, and will ask why a causal-sociological account of how a community redistributes credibility is not enough. And one historian of the chemical revolution will want to know whether the account would be true for Lavoisier's opponents — not just for the winner's later story.

Give me the objections in roughly the order I will actually get them — that is, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are — each phrased the way that person would put it. For each, provide a short two-minute reply, what I should concede, what I should hold, how damaging it really is, and a note on where exactly the reply is soft. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and say why it is the dangerous one. Throughout, separate what is settled in this literature from what is genuinely contested among people who know it, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
