I'm a philosophy professor and this came up in our probability reading group, and it's been bugging me. One member kept reaching for the line "when you have no reason to favor one alternative over another, you should give them equal probability" and using it to settle Bertrand's chord paradox. But each way he applied it—pick the chord's endpoints uniformly on the circumference, pick a radius and then a point uniformly along it, pick the midpoint uniformly in the disk—gave a different answer. So the slogan obviously hides several different claims. Could you seperate out the precise principles that sentence could be expressing: what an "alternative" has to be, what counts as having no reason, whether it's a constraint on consistent credences or a substantive claim about the world, things like that. For each reading, say whether it's trivial, defensible, or just false, and which reading (if any) would actually license using it to solve the paradox rather than cause it.
