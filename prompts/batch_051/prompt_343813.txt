I've accumulated a set of views about causal explanation in history over the years, and I honestly can't tell if they form a coherent package. I'd rather have an audit than a lecture: tell me which of these actually conflict, which are only apparently in tension, and what the cheapest repair is. (1) I take the counterfactual theory pretty seriously: explaining why an event happened by citing a cause is at bottom saying that in the nearest possible world where the cause is absent, the event wouldn't have happened, or at least would've been much less likely. (2) But I also think most of the big outcomes historians care about were massively overdetermined — multiple independant processes, each plausibly sufficient on its own, like the cluster of pressures that ended the Atlantic slave trade or the July Crisis of 1914 sliding into war. (3) In a clean symmetric overdetermination case, two snipers fire at the same time and either bullet on its own would have killed; I can't accept that neither shot caused the death just because the counterfactual test says so. That feels like a problem for the test, not for the causal judgment. (4) So I've drifted toward something like: a cause is a nonredundant part of a set of conditions that is jointly sufficient for the outcome — an INUS condition or close to it. (5) At the same time, I don't think there's any principled, context-independent way to separate causes from mere background conditions; what gets called 'the cause' is settled by the historian's contrast class or interests. (6) And I think a good multi-causal explanation shouldn't be a laundry list of 'contributing factors' — it has to say which factors made the difference relative to the relevant contrast. I genuinely can't tell whether I'm quietly running two different theories of causation at once. Be specific: which commitment has to give first, and what does weakening it cost me?
