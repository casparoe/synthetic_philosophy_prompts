I have for some years been writing down my verdicts about cases in which one person seems to owe another something in attitude rather than in outward action — gratitude, basic respect, esteem, recognition. I want to know whether a single principle stands behind these verdicts, and, if not, which of them must be surrendered.

1. A wealthy acquaintance pays for a medical treatment that saves my life, and it succeeds. I later learn that he did it only to win a wager, without any concern for me. I hold that I owe him at most repayment; I do not owe him gratitude, because there is no good will toward me for gratitude to answer.

2. A friend gives me a birthday present chosen with great care. It turns out to be useless and a real burden. I hold that I do owe her gratitude, because her will was good and the harm was unintended.

3. A colleague once gave me a single piece of advice that proved decisive for my career. She has since become unkind. I hold that I still owe her gratitude for that earlier act; the debt survives my present dislike, though I owe her little else.

4. A person in my employ does the work perfectly. I satisfy every contractual obligation toward her, but I have never once looked at her as anything but a function. I hold that something remains unpaid — not a feeling exactly, but an acknowledgment of her as a person.

5. A rival has achieved a great deal by genuine effort. I hold that I owe him honesty about those achievements, but not admiration; esteem, unlike basic respect, cannot be owed. Basic respect, I think, is owed to every person simply as a person.

6. Two people save a drowning child, one from grim duty and the other from spontaneous love. I find that I owe the second more, yet I cannot state why without seeming to reward someone merely for his feelings; this is the case I cannot yet settle.

7. Finally, I hold that an attitude can be owed even though attitudes cannot be summoned at will: a person incapable of gratitude where gratitude is owed has a moral failing, even if it is not a failure of compliance.

A tentative principle I have tried is something like this: gratitude is owed in response to good will rather than to benefit; basic respect is owed to all persons equally; esteem is never owed but only earned or freely given. But that principle struggles with cases 3 and 6, and it leaves unexplained how an attitude can be owed at all if it cannot be willed. What principle, or small set of candidates, best captures the pattern? Which of these verdicts, if any, does it force me to abandon? And what further cases would most cleanly test where its edges lie?
