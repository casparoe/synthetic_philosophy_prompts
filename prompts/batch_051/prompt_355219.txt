I am a junior at a private Christian school and I sit on the student committee that applies our Student Life Covenant to students who apply for positions of "spiritual leadership," which the covenant defines as leading chapel, joining the worship band, or running a ministry such as the weekend food pantry. The relevant clause reads: "Any student seeking such a position must present credible evidence of a living faith in Jesus Christ. Faith alone, and never a record of good works, is what puts a person right with God; yet the faith that so justifies is never idle in the one who has it, and it will show itself in a settled pattern of love and service. No merely verbal profession of faith is sufficient if the student's settled conduct runs against it." In our Bible class we have been reading Romans and James side by side, and several of us think this clause is trying to hold both of them together at once, which is part of why the present case has divided us.

The case is this. A transfer student, Elena, has for two years run our weekend food pantry with an energy we have rarely seen, visits a nursing home every Saturday to sing with the residents, and tutors our junior-high Bible study without ever treating it as a chore. When the committee interviewed her, however, she said that she could not in good conscience say she believes the central Christian claims; she described herself as living as if they are true, hoping they are, and finding real joy in the practices, but she refused to claim any more than that. Some of us say the clause is satisfied, or at least not clearly violated: the covenant itself demotes words by saying that profession is insufficient when conduct contradicts it, so it seems inconsistent to let a missing profession defeat conduct when conduct is the very test the clause installs. Others say that is an inference the clause never licenses: conduct can be evidence of faith only if there is a faith there to be evidenced, and the only direct testimony about Elena's inner state is her own, which does not affirm it; the drafters wrote the clause to catch people who talked piously and lived badly, not someone who lives well and talks honestly.

I need to give the committee a short interpretive opinion. Please argue from the words of the clause, from its purpose, from what you think the people who wrote it would have said had they been asked about Elena's case, and from what a reasonable student reading the covenant would take it to mean. Then tell me which of those approaches should decide the case and why, in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write a letter to the committee.
