Last night I had a long argument with a friend who is a committed act-utilitarian, and he kept answering Bernard Williams's integrity objection more resourcefully than I expected. I want to continue that argument in a controlled setting. Please play the role of my friend.

The character you will play is a philosophically serious act-utilitarian. He holds that an act is right just in case it produces at least as much aggregate well-being as any alternative available to the agent. He insists that this criterion of rightness need not — and usually should not — be used as a decision procedure, and that sound moral education builds strong partial dispositions precisely because they tend to produce the best states of affairs. He is willing to concede that occurrent calculation at the scene of a rescue would be humanly and morally defective, that a life of permanent utility bookkeeping would be impoverished, and that Williams is right that there are deep commitments an agent should not treat as mere instruments. He will not concede the central point: that the husband's action in Williams's rescue example needs no impartial justification even in the background. He fights sharp; he gives ground on small points when genuinely cornered, but he never abandons the criterion. Stay in character until I write stop. Do not break character, do not narrate the debate from outside, and answer my argument directly. Keep each reply to one substantial paragraph.

Here is my opening move. Last night we kept circling around Williams's rescue example, in which a man must choose between saving his wife and saving a stranger and reaches for his wife without hesitating. You argued that no serious utilitarian expects him to run the calculus on the riverbank, because a sensible decision procedure will tell people to reach for their spouses. But that concession misses the weight of the objection. A person who accepts your theory in full believes that what ultimately makes the action right is not that she is his wife but that acts of this sort tend to maximize aggregate well-being. So when he reflects honestly on what he did — when, say, his wife later asks why he pulled her from the water — he has available to him an account of his own action in which his love is at most a useful local disposition and the authoritative justification is impartial. Williams's charge is that this availability of the impartial justification, not its occurrence at the moment of rescue, is the thought he called one too many. Your decision procedure keeps the calculation out of the moment, but it does not keep the alienation out of the husband's reflective understanding of what he did. So here is my question. Either you allow that in a case like this the man may be justified simply by the fact that it was his wife, in which case the impartial criterion seems to do no justificatory work; or you retain the impartial criterion as the ultimate justifier, in which case it reappears as a second-order thought and Williams's point is left standing. Which is it?
