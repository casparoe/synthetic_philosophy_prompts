Before I circulate a draft essay to an interdisciplinary seminar I am chairing, I want my position on objectivity in history audited. It has been formed over years of reading Ranke's effort to recover the past as it actually was, Carr's case for the selection and construction of historical facts, and White's account of historical narrative as literary emplotment, but I have never written the commitments out side by side and checked whether they can all be held at once. Here is the position as I would state it on the spot:

1. The past is a self-standing object of inquiry: historical statements about what happened, who acted, and why are true or false independently of what historians now think, and no retrospective interpretation changes what occurred.
2. A historical fact is not found ready-made in the record. Facts must be selected, and they become historical facts partly through the interests and questions the historian brings, as Carr argued.
3. Narrative form goes beyond the evidence. The same sequence of events can be emplotted as tragedy, comedy, romance, or satire, and none of those forms is forced by the documents; emplotment is not the kind of thing the sources make true or false in the ordinary sense.
4. But the historian's narrative choices are not thereby unconstrained. The documentary record rules out a great many possible stories as false or incompatible with what the sources establish; this is the sense in which the line between history and fiction remains objective.
5. The facts appealed to in (4) do not come in a theory-neutral idiom. Any description of a source or event applies concepts that already carry interpretive commitments, and there is no bare fact-stating language standing outside interpretive traditions.
6. Counterfactual historical claims, such as the claim that without Lenin the Bolsheviks would not have taken power in October 1917, are objectively assessable even though no document records the counterfactual course of events.

I would like you to audit this set. Go through the commitments and identify which pairs or larger subsets are in genuine tension, and rank those tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally serious. Identify any tension that is only apparent and explain why it dissolves. For each genuine tension, state what would minimally have to give and what that revision would cost the rest of the position. Conclude by recommending, as your committed critical judgment, the single revision of my position that you think I should make, and say why that revision rather than another.
