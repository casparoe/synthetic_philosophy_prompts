I've been working on a distinction for evaluating machine-ethics systems and I want a professional philosopher to check it before I build a paper on it. I call it load-bearing ethics vs. rubber-stamp ethics. The idea is: a system only counts as doing moral reasoning to the extent that its moral components actually make a difference to what it does. If you could delete the 'ethics module' — the fairness classifier, the safety filter, the constitutional fine-tune, whatever — and get materially the same output on every live case, then the system doesn't have ethics; it has an ethics-themed output channel. The formal-ish test: find some morally relevant intermediate variable in the system (its judgment that an action is harmful, say) and intervene on it, set it to the opposite value while holding everything else fixed, and see whether the downstream decision changes. If flipping 'harmful' to 'not harmful' never moves the output on any case the system can actually face, the moral content isn't load-bearing, and I want to say there is no ethics in the system, only a post-hoc story. I think this explains the unease about a lot of 'alignment theater' and gives a clean answer to whether a toaster has ethics: there's no moral variable to intervene on.

But I'm worried the criterion is both too strong and too weak. Too weak because someone can trivially make ethics load-bearing by routing every decision through a 'socially acceptible?' classifier, and then the load-bearing variable might just track a superficial correlate (the word 'kill' shows up) rather than the moral property. Fixing that seems to require saying what the moral property is, at which point maybe my test is doing no independent work. Too strong because a perfectly good moral constraint might be redundant with the rest of the system's values — is redundancy really a reason to deny the system is doing ethics, or is that just overdetermination, which philosophers already accept everywhere? Give me the candid assessment: is load-bearing ethics a real distinction, is it just a special case of something obvious I should already know, and what's the case where the whole thing falls apart? You're a professional philosopher working on machine ethics and the metaphysics of mind. Take your time, steelman the best version of my view first, then tell me where it breaks. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, no preamble and no restating the question.
