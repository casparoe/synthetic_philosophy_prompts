I'm a philosophy PhD student working on teleosemantics, the project of naturalizing mental representation by treating a state's content as fixed by its selected or proper function: roughly, what the state is supposed to indicate, and what the systems that consume it are supposed to do with it. I'm drafting the literature-review chapter of my dissertation, and my supervisor has warned me that the chapter will feel flat if it simply walks through the usual catalogue of objections. I have tried to assemble that catalogue, and the objections below are the ones I already know (so please do not re-present them): (1) the disjunction or indeterminacy problem, on which the frog's snap mechanism was selected to catch flies but is triggered by small dark moving things, so selection history seems to underdetermine whether the content is fly, small dark moving thing, or a disjunction such as fly-or-bee; (2) the Swampman objection, on which a molecule-for-molecule duplicate assembled by chance with no evolutionary history would have no content at all, which strikes many as the wrong verdict; (3) the unselected-content objection, on which the account cannot plausibly assign content to representations that never mattered to fitness, such as most scientific concepts or thoughts about merely possible situations; (4) the circularity objection, on which content is supposed to be fixed by what downstream consumer systems use the representation for, but those uses are themselves described in intentional terms; (5) the misrepresentation or truth problem, on which functions are selected for fitness rather than veridicality, so the account may yield success conditions rather than truth conditions and may characterise error merely as malfunction, even though a well-functioning system can be wrong in novel circumstances; and (6) the compositionality or structure problem, on which the account starts from simple indicator states tied to specific behaviours and gives no satisfying story about how their contents combine to yield logically complex or systematic thought. I think this is the standard inventory, so I am not after another familiar objection I have merely forgotten.

What I want is one further objection to teleosemantics, in any of its main variants, that is not on this list and is not merely a rewording, special case, or combination of the six. It may be a problem you think the literature has missed or one that is rarely pressed in exactly this form. After presenting it, give an honest verdict on whether it is any good: whether it survives the most natural replies a committed teleosemanticist would make, and how it differs from the closest one or two items above.
