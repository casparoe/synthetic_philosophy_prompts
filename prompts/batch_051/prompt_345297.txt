I am starting a working group on the ethics of refugeehood and asylum, with the aim of producing a framework good enough to guide principled judgment across the full range of contemporary displacement cases, and before any of us is allowed to advocate for a view I want the specification fixed in advance. The positions I have encountered so far seem engineered to get one or two canonical cases right — a dissident fleeing a persecuting state, a family crossing a border during a civil war — and then go largely silent on the cases that actually dominate current disputes: people fleeing non-state armed groups their government will not or cannot control, gender-based violence claims forced into the particular-social-group category, conscientious deserters from a war of aggression, people moving because of slow-onset environmental change whose displacement is tangled up with conflict and poverty, stateless people, and claimants already inside the destination country when conditions at home deteriorate.

Write the desiderata that an adequate theory of refugeehood and asylum obligations must satisfy. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it, and mark it as non-negotiable or as a contested demand of a particular camp; I especially want you to separate what any adequate theory must do from what particular camps merely prefer, such as whether a theory must anchor itself in the existing refugee regime or may treat that regime as one historical compromise among several. Make sure the list respects distinctions a theory must not collapse: between counting as a refugee and what is owed to one afterward; between non-refoulement, admission, and the grant of durable status; between eligibility for protection and the allocation of responsibility to particular states; between prima facie and all-things-considered duties; and between legal categories and moral ones. The theory should also be constrained by facts it cannot responsibly ignore — for example, that most of the world's displaced are hosted in the global South, that resettlement numbers are tiny, that persecution is frequently by non-state actors, that displacement drivers are typically mixed rather than cleanly political or economic, and that states often fail to comply with even minimal standards, so the theory must be action-guiding under partial noncompliance.

Then do two further things. First, flag any pairs or larger subsets of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — for instance, fidelity to existing legal categories together with the demand that the theory be morally principled, or robust duties toward outsiders together with a meaningful right of states to control entry, or operational administrability together with sensitivity to mixed motives — and say explicitly where you think the list itself becomes a result because the tension cannot be dissolved. Second, identify the single requirement that does the most to exclude popular theories, with a sentence on why it lands where it does. A brief scorecard of the main camps against your list is welcome, but keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scoring.
