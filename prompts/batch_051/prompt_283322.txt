I am a retired natural scientist, fairly new to philosophy, and I am trying to get clear about vagueness. For my own notes I have written down five commitments and would like to know whether they form a coherent package.

(1) I am drawn to supervaluationism: vagueness is semantic indecision, and a sentence is true just in case it is true on every admissible sharpening (precisification) of its vague terms.
(2) I also want to keep bivalence: every declarative sentence is either true or false.
(3) For a borderline case, such as a man of 1.82 meters for "tall," neither the sentence "He is tall" nor "He is not tall" is true.
(4) Yet "He is tall or he is not tall" is true, because every admissible sharpening of "tall" makes one disjunct true.
(5) I reject epistemicism because it seems to posit a hidden sharp boundary for every vague predicate, a boundary ordinary speakers have no access to.

Please audit these five commitments together: identify which pairs are in genuine tension, which likely conflict is merely apparent and why, and rank the tensions by severity. Return exactly five numbered observations, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion; in each item, state how confident you are, and mark with an asterisk the point you are least sure of.
