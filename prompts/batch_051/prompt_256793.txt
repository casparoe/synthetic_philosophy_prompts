In Advaita Vedānta, the stock example of the rope that is mistaken for a snake is used to illustrate adhyāsa, superimposition. A single underlying reality—the rope—appears as something else—the snake—without the rope itself changing; the error is removed not by altering the world but by correcting the ignorance of the perceiver. The example is then generalized: just as the snake is superimposed on the rope, the entire world of distinct objects is superimposed on Brahman through avidyā or māyā. The world is said to be neither fully real nor fully unreal—a status the tradition calls anirvacanīya—and the snake's sublation when the rope is recognized is meant to model how the world is sublated by knowledge of Brahman while the underlying reality remains untouched. The analogy's job is to make intelligible how one undifferentiated reality can appear as a multiplicity, and how knowledge can remove the appearance while leaving reality itself unchanged.

Now consider a prominent strand of recent cognitive science: predictive processing accounts of perception, often glossed with the slogan that perception is controlled hallucination. On this view, all perceptual experience is the brain's best-guess construction, constrained by sensory prediction error, and illusions or hallucinations are not a different kind of process from ordinary perception but points on a continuum. At the level of mechanism, there is no clean boundary between veridical perception and perceptual error.

Does the Advaitin's rope-snake analogy survive this development, need repair, or become unavailable? In particular, say whether the predictive-processing picture strengthens the analogy by showing that even ordinary perception is constructive, undermines it because the analogy appeared to rely on a stable contrast between a veridical and a non-veridical state, or leaves it untouched because the Advaitin's claim is metaphysical while predictive processing is psychological. Identify the specific assumption in the analogy that the development bears on, distinguish the psychological claim that perception is constructed from the metaphysical claim that the world is superimposed, and state what a repaired version of the analogy would need to do in order to keep doing its original work.
