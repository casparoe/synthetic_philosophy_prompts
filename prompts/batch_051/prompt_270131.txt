ok so I've been trying to nail down my views on probability and uncertainty for a paper, and I keep going around in circles. I'll lay out where I've landed and you tell me if it's coherent.

I start from Keynes's discussion of the "weight" of arguments in *A Treatise on Probability*. He says (roughly, from memory without quoting): two probabilities can be equally strong, but one is based on a lot of relevant evidence and the other on very little; that difference—the weight—matters for how confident we should feel, even though it doesn't change the numerical probability. I find that really compelling.

So here's roughly what I think:

1. Probability is a logical relation between evidence and proposition, not a physical frequency. Keynes is right about that.
2. In actual decision-making, I use subjective degrees of belief (Dutch book style, coherent credences).
3. The weight of evidence matters for action: a 0.8 probability based on one flimsy data point is less "solid" than a 0.8 based on tons of data, and a rational person should be more willing to rely on the latter.
4. A rational agent's betting odds should always match their credences.
5. Second-order uncertainty (uncertainty about my own credence) can be modeled as a probability distribution over possible credences. So if I'm unsure whether the chance of rain is 0.3 or 0.6, I have a meta-distribution over those values.
6. But I also agree with Knight that there's a real difference between risk (where probabilities are known) and uncertainty (where they aren't), and that some situations can't be captured by a single well-defined probability.

I've got a nagging sense that (1) and (2) don't sit well together, and that (3) and (5) might be doing the same work twice, or conflicting. But I can't tell which tensions are real and which are just me being sloppy with terms.
