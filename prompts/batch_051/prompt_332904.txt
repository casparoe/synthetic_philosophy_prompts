In a town-hall debate about a rezoning proposal that would add several thousand housing units, one side waved away worries about crowded schools and longer commutes by saying 'the more the merrier.' His argument, unpacked, seemed to be that the people who would move in would have lives well worth living, that a city with more residents living worthwhile lives is on that account a better city, and that therefore the council should approve the plan. Another speaker answered that this conflates the total with the average: by his lights, a city is better only if the typical resident's life is improved, and 'quality, not quantity' is the principle that should settle the matter. I want to know what 'the more the merrier' is actually claiming before I take a side. Lay out the distinct precise principles the motto might express: first as a claim about parties and gatherings, then as a claim about the value of a city or a population, then as a claim about what a council or similar decision-maker ought to do. For each reading, say whether it is trivially true, plausibly true but limited, or false, and whether it carries over from the party case to the city case. Then give a committed overall verdict: which reading is the most defensible precisification of the motto, which reading the first speaker's argument actually needs, and whether the motto on that reading does the work he wants. Where the argument forces a choice between average and total ways of valuing a population, take a side and defend it, and identify the exact step at which the move from 'this is a better city' to 'the council should approve it' could fail even if the value claim were granted.
