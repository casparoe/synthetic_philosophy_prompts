We did Bayes rule in stats this year and my friend has been turning it into an argument I think proves too much, but I can't find exactly where it snaps.

(1) If two people update by Bayes on the same evidence, then in the long run their opinions have to converge, no matter how different their priors started out, as long as neither prior literally assigns zero probability to the truth. (2) So any two Bayesians who aren't totally closed-minded should eventually end up agreeing about whatever they keep getting evidence about. (3) But flat-earthers and conspiracy theorists see basically the same evidence we do, and they never even get close to agreeing with us. (4) Those people can be represented as Bayesians who didn't violate any updating rule — their priors just made the evidence look likely to be rigged or fake, so every observation also fits "it's a hoax" and the updates stay tiny or point the wrong way. (5) So the theorem only makes people converge if they were already the kind of person who could be moved; it doesnt actually wash out priors in any case that matters. (6) Therefore Bayesianism either proves too much (it predicts agreement where there isnt any) or it's empty, because all the interesting work is hidden in which priors you're allowed to have, and the updating rules say nothing about that.

I feel like the slide is in the "as long as" clause in (1) or between (4) and (5), but I can't tell whether the theorem is weaker than he's claiming or whether those people are just irrational in a way Bayesianism refuses to count. Which premise do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost me about how priors have to be constrained?
