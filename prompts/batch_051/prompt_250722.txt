I teach a graduate seminar on value theory and population ethics, and there's a debate in it that has completely stalled, which I find genuinely puzzling because the participants all seem to share the same broad consequentialist picture. The ostensible question is how the components of value combine. Everyone agrees, roughly, that how much an outcome matters depends both on how much wellbeing there is and on what kind of consciousness is doing the feeling—the usual shorthand is something like "consciousness times happiness." But once we move past the slogan, we split into three camps, and a year of arguing hasn't moved anyone an inch.

One camp says value is basically additive and separable: you sum wellbeing across experiences, and talk of multiplying by consciousness is double-counting, since a conscious creature's wellbeing already includes its phenomenal character. A second camp says the multiplication is doing real work: a faintly conscious creature's pleasures are only dimly valuable, and scaling by degree of consciousness explains why a small drop in awareness matters more when awareness is already rich—value changes continuously with both factors, and if you make either one zero, the product is zero. A third camp says the entire continuous picture is wrong, and that below some threshold of consciousness wellbeing is either worth nothing or lexically dominated: no amount of mouse-level, or organoid-level, wellbeing outweighs a human-level harm, full stop, and they don't even agree about whether a cortex in a dish belongs in the calculation at all.

My own working hypothesis is that this isn't really a dispute about values, but about whether "consciousness" picks out a scalar magnitude that can appear in an arithmetic expression at all—the lexical folks, I suspect, are smuggling in a deontological constraint under the language of measurement. But I keep flip-flopping, because some of them are perfectly consequentialist when pushed and insist there making a point about measurement error: at the low end, they say, our estimates are so unreliable that treating tiny values as real weights is a kind of rounding error in the face of normal human welfare.

So my question is diagnostic rather than a request for a verdict. What kind of disagreement is this, actually—verbal, substantive axiological, a disagreement about measurement or decision theory, or several groups answering different questions under one heading? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a conceptual distinction, a formal result about aggregation functions, a thought experiment that would move someone, or an argument that the question is ill-posed and we should be asking something else? I'd rather have a careful diagnosis of the structure of the impasse than a survey of the positions, and an honest answer about whether anything could end it.
