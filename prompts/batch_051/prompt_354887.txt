I keep tripping over the one-liner that maths is quasi-empirical. It tends to come up in conversations about how mathematics actually progresses: someone waves a hand at Lakatos and uses it to win an argument about whether a proof ever settles a question, or whether the old Euler formula business (polyhedra, a counterexample, then changing the definition of polyhedron so the counterexample no longer counts) shows that the definitions are doing the real work. But the people using the phrase are clearly claiming very different things. Sometimes it's just the banal point that mathematicians make mistakes and proofs get checked; sometimes it's the thesis that mathematics is fallible in something like the way science is, because conjectures are tested by attempted counterexamples; sometimes it's the wilder claim that a theorem's having survived many failed refutations is evidence for it in a genuinely probabilistic sense; sometimes it seems to be a claim that there is no finished formal system underneath informal practice and formal proof is just a retrospective clean-up. Seperate out the distinct precise claims that one-liner could be expressing; say which are platitudes, which are substantive and defensible, which are actually false or muddled; and end by saying which reading has to be in play if someone is using it to show that a proof hasn't actually settled the question, and which reading they usually mean. I want the menu of readings and verdicts, not a history lesson — and no citations or bibliography.
