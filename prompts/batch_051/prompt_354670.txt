Ok so I got into a long argument with a friend about Hume and causation last night, and the whole thing ended up turning on one passage that I must have read like six times since. In the Enquiry concerning Human Understanding, Section VII, Part I, Hume writes:

When we look about us towards external objects, and consider the operation of causes, we are never able, in a single instance, to discover any power or necessary connexion; any quality, which binds the effect to the cause, and renders the one an infallible consequence of the other. We only find, that the one does actually, in fact, follow the other.

My friend kept insisting that this is only an epistemic point: all Hume is saying is that we can’t find the necessary connexion, not that there isn’t one out there. Then he brought up the whole New Hume reading—the idea that Hume might be a causal realist after all, someone who thinks there really are necessary connexions in nature but they’re hidden from us or beyond our grasp. That felt like it totally changed the argumentative force of the passage to me, but I couldn’t quite make the point stick. If the whole reason Hume gives for analyzing causation in terms of constant conjunction and custom is that the impression of necessary connexion is missing, and then someone says “sure, but it might still exist as an unobserved somethign,” has’nt that just undercut the argument for rejecting the old idea? So my question is: does the New Hume reading leave the argument in Section VII intact, does it require reinterpreting what the argument is supposed to establish, or does it make the argument collapse into something much weaker? I’m asking about the argument itself, not about which reading is better Hume scholarship.
