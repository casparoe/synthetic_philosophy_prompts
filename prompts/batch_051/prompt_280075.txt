I have been spending a lot of time in seminars where people present formal models, and I keep seeing a pattern I do not have a name for. A speaker presents a model whose assumptions are visibly false — agents form expectations in a way nobody plausibly does, markets clear instantly, or a predator consumes prey at a rate unaffected by the predator's own satiation. When challenged, the speaker invokes a familiar methodological defense: models are not descriptions, assumptions do not need to be descriptively accurate, and what matters is only whether the model's implications fit the available data or yield useful predictions. The audience accepts this and moves on. But I have watched the very same audience members turn around and criticize another model almost entirely for its assumptions — not for its predictions, which may be no worse — saying that an assumption is simply absurd or that nobody behaves that way. After enough of these episodes, the principle that unrealistic assumptions are harmless seems to be applied selectively, and the selection often tracks whether the model's conclusions are congenial to the person applying it. I know the general idea that unrealistic assumptions are acceptable as long as predictions succeed is associated with Milton Friedman, and I believe some people call it the F-twist. But I am not asking about that defense itself. I am asking about the pattern of invoking it asymmetrically: treating the very same property, the known falsehood of an assumption, as a forgivable idealization in one model and as a fatal defect in another without any stated criterion for the difference.

Does this pattern have an established name in philosophy of science or in the methodology of economics? I would rather not settle for a bare label like bias or a double standard unless that really is the standard analysis. I want to know how the phenomenon should be carved up: how it differs from a principled version of the Friedman position, if there is one; how it differs from ordinary motivated reasoning or confirmation bias; and whether there is any non-arbitrary way to decide which unrealistic assumptions should be excused and which should count as genuine objections. If no established term fits, I would be glad to hear a proposed one, provided its definition includes the boundary cases and says what evidence would show that someone is doing this rather than legitimately ranking models by their predictive success.
