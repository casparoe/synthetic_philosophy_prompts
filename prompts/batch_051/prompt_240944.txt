I hold a version of the view that the self is not a thing inside the mind but a center of narrative gravity: an abstraction we project over the stream of mental life, justified by and inseparable from the coherence of a story that links memories, present states, and projected future into a single protagonist. On my version, there is a self exactly when a sufficiently coherent self-narrative can be told about a life; first-person statements are made true by facts about that narrative coherence, not by the existence of a substantial owner of experiences; and the vagueness of selves—their fuzzy boundaries, gaps, and renegotiability—is a prediction of the view rather than a bug. I mean this as a metaphysical thesis about what selves are, not merely an epistemic point about how we find them.

Tell me what would count against it. I want a map of the view's exposure, not a survey of the literature. For each potential defeater, say whether it is fatal, seriously damaging, or merely awkward, and distinguish what a committed defender would concede from what they would correctly dismiss. Address at least: (1) fission, split-brain, and dissociative cases; (2) severe dementia, dense amnesia, and the breakdown of narrative in aging; (3) evidence that autobiographical memory and the self-narrative are confabulated or systematically unreliable; (4) the possibility of creatures with rich perspective but no narrative competence; (5) reports from meditation, trauma, or psychosis of experience without a narrator; and (6) conceptual objections to treating the self as an abstraction rather than a substance or an owner. For each, say what the narrative-gravity view predicts that its main rivals—a substance/owner view and a psychological continuity view—do not, and where the view is only redescribing the data.

I also want an honest audit of whether the view has been insulated. If sufficiently coherent can always be adjusted to accommodate any case, say whether that is a disciplined boundary condition or unfalsifiability, and whether a view of this kind needs to run empirical risks at all. Conclude by naming the single most dangerous evidence or argument for this position and stating what would have to happen, or what would have to be true, for me to abandon it rather than revise it. I personally find the strongest pressure comes from cases where the narrative breaks down, such as advanced dementia and meditative ego dissolution, so pay particular attention to whether those cases really discriminate between this view and its rivals.
