A long article I read about decision theory summarizes Pascal's wager like this: "Pascal proved that belief in God is the only rational option for anyone, because believing is a bet with infinite expected value. If you believe and God exists, you receive an infinite payoff; if you believe and God does not exist, you lose only some finite earthly pleasures; if you disbelieve and God exists, you suffer an infinite loss; and if you disbelieve and God does not exist, you gain a finite amount of pleasure. Since any finite probability of an infinite payoff yields an infinite expected value, the rational choice is obvious. Pascal also forestalls the objection that belief is not under voluntary control by recommending that nonbelievers behave as if they believed—attend Mass, take holy water, kneel in prayer—so that habit will eventually produce real faith. The upshot of the wager is that evidence about whether God exists is irrelevant to deciding whether to believe: rational self-interest settles the matter without metaphysics. And if someone worries that a rival religion's god might offer a different infinite reward, Pascal's reasoning simply applies again, so the wager is best understood as an argument for betting on whatever religion promises the highest expected afterlife payoff."

I am suspicious of this as both a reading of Pascal and a piece of decision theory, but I can't locate exactly where it goes wrong. Please audit the summary claim by claim. For each component, tell me whether it is a fair representation of the original argument, a simplification a careful expositor would accept, or a distortion; and separately tell me which of the article's conclusions would still follow if the defensible parts were stated correctly. In particular, is the final inference to "whatever religion promises the highest expected afterlife payoff" a valid extension of Pascal's reasoning, and what does it miss?
