Could you explain what the paradox of deontology is supposed to be? I get the rough shape of the problem: a deontologist who refuses to kill one person to prevent five murders seems to be leaving more murders on the table, and maybe caring more about their own moral purity than about the victims. But I've never understood why this is meant to be an internal tension in deontology rather than just a consequence of agent-relative constraints that deontologists already accept. Why can't the deontologist just say that the other five deaths wouldn't be ones they commit? And does the problem work in the same way for constraints on lying or promise-breaking as it does for killing? I'd like a plain-language explanation of the core problem and the main moves people make to dissolve it. Lead with the bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating the question. Please write in British English.
