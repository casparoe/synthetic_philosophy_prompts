I'm a retired physicist — spent forty years pushing symbols around well enough to get answers, but without always feeling I'd seen inside the machinery. Since retiring I've started reading philosophy seriously for the first time, mostly philosophy of mathematics and the education literature on the difference between understanding and procedural fluency, and it's revived an old puzzle. In graduate school I could reproduce long derivations on an exam and verify every step, yet when someone asked me why it worked, I'd find myself saying something vauge about the formalism. A mathematician friend swears that understanding a proof just is being able to check each line and fill in the missing steps. I suspect that's too low a bar, but I can't say exactly why.

So I'd like you to settle this. Pick a proof you know well — some standard undergraduate theorem is fine — and imagine a student who has just studied it. Below is a list of things the student might be able to do with the proof. Sort them into tiers by how strong they are as evidence that the student genuinely understands it, not by how useful they are pedagogically. The top tier should be close to "if you can do this, you almost certainly understand," and the bottom tier should be "you could do this with little or no real understanding." Give me a definite ranking, not a survey of what different camps would say; if you think "understanding" is ambiguous, flag that briefly and then commit to your preferred reading anyway. After the tiers, say which single item you'd bet on as the best indicator, and say where the "check each line and fill the gaps" view goes wrong, if it does. Be decisive — I'd rather have a wrong-but-argued ranking than a hedge; just flag the one placement you're least confident about.

The list of things:
- Reproducing the proof from memory under exam conditions.
- Checking that each line follows validly from the preceding lines and the relevant definitions or lemmas.
- Supplying the missing justifications when the proof is presented with gaps.
- Giving a one- or two-sentence plain-language gloss of the proof's main idea.
- Identifying which hypotheses of the theorem are actually load-bearing and pointing to where each is used.
- Adapting the proof to establish a nearby generalization or an analogous result in a different setting.
- Detecting a subtle, deliberately planted flaw in the proof and repairing it.
- Producing an example showing the theorem fails if a particular hypothesis is dropped.
- Explaining what makes the theorem non-obvious: what a natural but naive proof strategy would try, and where it collapses.
