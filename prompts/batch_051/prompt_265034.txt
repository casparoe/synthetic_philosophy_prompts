I've picked up a habit in how I think about the future of humanity and I cant tell whether it's a sound principle or a bias with a fancy name. I later found out it's basically the self-sampling assumption: whenever I estimate things like whether we'll survive the next few centuries or how many people will ever exist, I treat my own life as roughly a random draw from the set of all observers who will ever live. So a theory that says almost all observers live in the future gets a boost from my existence, while a theory that says I'm one of a rare handful of observers seems suspicious. This feels like the only way to avoid special pleading about my own place in history, so I now apply it almost automatically.

My friend keeps pushing back, though. He says the sampling metaphor is broken from the start, because there was never a possible observation in which I don't exist, so I can't treat my existence as if it were drawn from an urn. His alternative (which I gather is the self-indication assumption) says I should favor theories on which more observers exist, since each additional observer is another chance for me to be someone. But when I actually run his rule, I end up having to believe it's quite likely the universe is teeming with unobserved aliens, or that the future population will be vastly larger than any demographic model suggests, just because those theories multiply observers. That strikes me as, well, presumptuous.

Is my habit defensible? Is there a version of it that keeps the good parts without the wierd stuff, and how could I tell from the inside that I'm not just favoring the rule that gives me the conclusions I already wanted?
