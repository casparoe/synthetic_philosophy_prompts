A learned society in a technical field—I will not say which, since the details do not matter—has proposed to make its longstanding practice of coining technical vocabulary from Latin and Greek mandatory. Authors may currently coin freely: they borrow ordinary English words (mouse, bug, cloud), repurpose words from the wider culture, use eponyms, or build compounds from living languages or from Classical Latin and Ancient Greek morphemes. The new rule would require every newly introduced technical term to be a novel compound built from Latin or Greek roots. A standing registration committee of classicists and senior practitioners would vet each coinage for morphological correctness, adjudicate priority disputes, and publish a glossary of approved terms; a journal in the society would desk-reject any paper that introduces an unregistered term, and at the society's meetings an unregistered new term could not be used unglossed. Assume the rule is adopted, enforced with ordinary competence, and survives legal challenge.

The usual justification for classical coinage is that dead languages are stable, neutral across living speech communities, and semantically more transparent than borrowed living-language terms; the rule can therefore be read as a forced, field-wide test of those claims. I am not asking whether the society should pass it. I am asking how it would actually behave. Walk through what the rule incentivizes: who coins terms, how early, in what style, and with what level of candor about the concept. Where does it get gamed first—acronym-first coinages with a thin classical veneer, speculative coinage to squat on productive morphemes, hybrids, terms built to flatter the registration committee, definitions that drift until the etymology actively misleads? Which subfields absorb the rule cheaply, and which find their working vocabulary turned into contraband that must be laundered through the registry? Does the scheme deliver the promised stability, cross-language neutrality, and compositionality, or does it mostly reproduce the present mess with more regular prefixes? Then examine the second-order effects for students and adjacent disciplines, translation and multilingual publication, indexing and search, and public or applied communication, and say which failure modes can be fixed by small amendments and which are the price of the underlying idea. Answer in American English.
