I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so forgive the rough edges. I'm drafting a character—a retired surveyor named Frank—who insists that the only thing "the alder tree by his window" can refer to is a set of ideas in a mind, and that saying there's an alder tree existing completely outside any mind is actually self-contradictory, not just false. My editor says Frank is just trotting out Berkeley's master argument and any halfway sober reader will know it collapses, because surely someone can conceive of a tree existing while no one conceives of it. I don't want Frank to be an ignoramous, though: I want the objection to be genuinely tough for him, or at least to know whether it is. So give me your actual credence that Berkeley's master argument—roughly, that one cannot conceive of anything existing unconceived—gives us a good reason to deny mind-independent material objects. I don't want "it depends on what you mean by conceive" as the end. I want a number, and I want the reasoning decomposed. Specifically: (1) what's the probability the argument is valid, not just clever-sounding; (2) what's the probability it survives the standard objection that the argument only shows something about our conceiving, not about how things can exist; (3) what's the probability the idealist conclusion follows even if the master argument itself fails; and (4) how much does the final number depend on whether "existence" means being perceived versus being perceivable? Give a rough probability for each term and one line justifying it, tell me how they combine, and then tell me which sub-question the final credence is most sensitive to and what evidence or argument would move you most.
