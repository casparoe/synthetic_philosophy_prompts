i've been chewing on a theory i'm calling the 'fever theory of emotions' and i'd like someone to tell me where it snaps before i trot it out for my seminar. central claim: it's main value is never in the episode itself, it's in the recalibration the episode pays for. an emotion is a fever, not a diagnosis. a fever doesn't represent the infection (it overdoes it, and it can make that day worse), but it's still protective, because the cost it imposes is part of what clears the infection. same with fear: the fright you get from a stick that registered as a snake is a false positive, so the stoic is right that, read as a claim about the world, the emotion is mostly noise. but the romantic is right that it's valuable—just for the wrong reason: the fright leaves your snake-detection slightly sharper, and that's the payoff, not the feeling itself. grief, on this view, isn't a price you pay for having loved someone; it's a loading screen for a self-model being rebuilt around the absence. i know this sounds superficially like error management theory, but i think the difference is that i'm not saying the false positive is adaptive because safer-to-be-wrong beats a missed detection; i'm saying the felt episode's cost is the mechanism that buys the recalibration. i like it because it lets me grant the stoics the content and the romantics the value. but awe at a piece of music doesn't seem to be for anything downstream, and contentment looks like the counterexample that should have been the rule. what's the strongest existing name for this, and what's the best counterexample that actually kills it rather than just scuffs it?
