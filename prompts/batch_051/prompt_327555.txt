I run the fine-tuning team at a mid-sized AI lab. Every few weeks we begin a new training run, and the central decision is how to supervise it: process-based supervision, in which annotators score individual reasoning steps, plans, or intermediate tool calls, versus outcome-based supervision, in which annotators, or an automatic verifier, score only the final output. The trade-offs are familiar in practice but hard to make principled. Outcome signal is cheap and sometimes free, when unit tests or proof checkers exist; process signal is expensive, requires a defensible notion of what counts as a step worth scoring, and can still be gamed by models that have learnt to produce steps that look scorable rather than steps that are right. We have a fixed annotator budget each month, some of our tasks have reliable automatic outcome checks and some do not, and the engineering lead will only sign off on a choice that can be justified in a short paragraph. My annotator lead and I relitigate this every run, and I would rather have a procedure we can argue about once and then run in an afternoon.

Please turn this into a reusable decision procedure for choosing between process-based and outcome-based supervision for a new training run, or for splitting the budget between them with the split itself justified by the procedure. Structure it as a sequence of questions asked in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops and returns a recommendation, and where it stops and hands the decision to a human because the question is one of values rather than facts. Keep whatever distinctions from the underlying debate actually survive contact with practice, say plainly which distinctions you are dropping and why, and mark the steps that are judgement calls dressed up as procedure. Then run the procedure on three decisions we actually face: a model that writes proofs for an undergraduate-level theorem-proving benchmark with a mechanical proof checker; a model that drafts care plans for elderly patients, where the only available outcome signals are delayed, noisy, and partly subjective; and a model that refactors legacy code against a passing test suite. Arrange the cases so that at least one returns an answer you expect my team to dislike, and finish by saying what the procedure cannot settle.
