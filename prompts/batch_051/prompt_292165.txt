I am taking a course this term on the place of nonhuman causes — disease, climate, volcanic events — in historical explanation, and there is a recurring habit in the readings and lectures that I find philosophically puzzling. I would like to understand what that habit commits the field to.

The habit is this: the authors and lecturers freely give nonhuman phenomena the active role in historical sentences. The Black Death is said to have transformed the European labor market. The pathogens introduced into the Americas after 1492 are said to have reduced the indigenous population more than any human campaign did. The Little Ice Age is said to have helped produce harvest failures and civil unrest across the early modern world. The 1815 eruption of Tambora is said to have caused widespread harvest failures in 1816. In each case, the germ, the climate shift, or the volcano occupies the place in the sentence of the thing that did something.

Yet the same writers are careful to reject the obvious over-reading of that language. They deny that a pathogen or a volcano has intentions, purposes, or agency in any ordinary sense. They also, at least in passing, warn against environmental determinism, insisting that what a drought or an epidemic did depended on the institutions, inequalities, and choices it met, so the nonhuman cause by itself settles nothing. The boundary they draw is selective in a way that strikes me as significant. Nobody objects to the claim that the plague caused a labor shortage, but everyone would stop short of saying that the plague aimed at the shortage or selected its moment. The field appears to distinguish between making a thing a cause and making it an agent, while still handing it the narrative role of a protagonist.

What I am trying to work out is what this practice presupposes. What view of causation permits a volcano to bring down a harvest, or a bacillus to reorganize a labor market, without any agency? Does the practice commit its practitioners to the claim that an explanatory cause in history need not be intentional; or to the further claim that causes are whatever makes the difference to an outcome rather than whatever initiates it? Is there a genuine conflict between saying that the plague did something and denying that the plague has agency, or does the conflict dissolve once we separate causal contribution from agential responsibility? And how does the anti-determinism caveat fit: can one consistently treat climate as a driver of events while denying that climate is destiny? I would also like to know what would have to change in these narratives if historical explanation were required to bottom out in the intentions of human agents — could the standard stories even be told?

I would be grateful for a reconstruction of the implicit theory; I especially want to know what the practice strictly presupposes, what it merely suggests, and where it remains philosophically underdetermined, so that more than one defensible account of causation and agency could underwrite the same habits of writing.
