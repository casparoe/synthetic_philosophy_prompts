During a discussion of the passage I am about to describe, I found myself unsure how much weight a key term in Cicero's Cato Maior de Senectute is supposed to carry. In the early sections of that dialogue, after Cato has enumerated the four reasons people call old age wretched, he turns to the first one—that old age removes a person from active public life. His reply, as I understand it, is something like this: grand and important affairs are not accomplished by strength, speed, or bodily agility, but by counsel, authority, and judgment; far from being deprived of these qualities, old age usually has them in greater measure. He then contrasts what young men can do with what old men can do, and says that while an old man may not be able to fight or run, he can still guide the state through the weight of his auctoritas, and that this is a greater kind of achievement.

I am trying to figure out precisely what role auctoritas plays in this argument, because the whole rebuttal seems to depend on it more than on any explicit premise. Is Cato claiming that authority is simply the natural compensation old age receives for the loss of bodily capacity, so that old men can still participate in politics in a different way? Or is he making a stronger claim about the nature of political achievement—that the defining acts of statesmanship are deliberation and judgment, so that the loss of physical vigor is not a loss relative to what matters most? I would also like to know whether the argument can stand if one separates it from Roman assumptions about age, deference, and political auctoritas: does the distinction between bodily and deliberative agency do the work on its own, or does the reply collapse without that background?
