I'm a PhD student working on Kant's moral philosophy and i keep running into what looks like a real tension in his stuff on animals. In the Doctrine of Virtue §17 he's pretty clear that we have no direct duties to non-rational animals; any duties we have regarding them are indirect duties to ourselves, because cruelty to animals dulls the sympathy that's useful for our duties toward human beings. But in the Lectures on Ethics—I think in the Collins notes—he seems to say that if a dog or horse has served someone faithfully for a long time, the animal's service is something like merit and we owe it a kind of reward or at least shouldn't discard it when it's old. That doesn't sound like just a duty to self; it sounds like a duty to the animal in virtue of what it did. Are these two claims actually in tension? Can the gratitude/reward line be read as just another indirect duty, or does it smuggle in direct duties that the Doctrine of Virtue officially denies? Which is the least strained reading of Kant, and does he actually hold it consistently?
