We have been reading the final chapter of Henry Sidgwick’s The Methods of Ethics, where he describes what he calls the dualism of practical reason. As I understand the passage, he argues that two apparently rational standpoints both make claims on what I ought to do: one that takes my own good as the ultimate end, and one that takes up the standpoint of the universe and weighs everyone’s good equally. He ends by saying that he cannot see how reason can require me to abandon either standpoint, and that the conflict between them remains unresolved. I keep trying to apply this idea to concrete cases from my own life, and I want to know whether my patterns of judgment reflect a single principle, whether they track Sidgwick’s dualism, or whether some of my verdicts have to be revised.

Here are five cases and my actual reactions to them.

1. I am deciding between giving $400 to a charity that I trust would save a child’s life, and spending the $400 on a plane ticket to attend my sister’s wedding. I buy the ticket. I feel guilty afterward, but I do not think I acted wrongly.

2. My employer asks me to sign a false safety report that would protect the company and my bonus, with no immediate harm likely. I refuse. I think signing would be wrong, even if refusing costs me my job.

3. I pass a building on fire and hear that someone may be trapped inside. I call the fire brigade and wait outside because I am untrained. I do not think I have done wrong, though I would judge a trained firefighter harshly for doing the same thing.

4. I choose a corporate law job and plan to donate a third of my salary to effective charities, rather than taking a low-paying public-interest job and donating little. I believe my choice is at least as good as the more selfless-sounding option, perhaps better.

5. A close friend asks me to break a minor promise to my supervisor to cover for his absence, because he wants to attend his daughter’s concert. I decline. I do not think declining is saintly or especially virtuous; it just seems permissible, even though it costs me the friend’s goodwill.

I cannot tell whether these verdicts come from one coherent principled standpoint, or whether some of them simply express different and perhaps competing sources of practical authority. Is there a principle, or a small set of principles, that captures all five verdicts while also making sense of where I feel guilty or conflicted? If no single principle can capture all of them, which verdict should I overturn, and why? And what would the resulting principle say about a case I have not settled: I receive a large inheritance and must choose between using it to care for my parent at home, or donating it to a well-run charity that would save several strangers’ lives but leave my parent in a less comfortable public facility? Does your answer vindicate Sidgwick’s idea that two equally rational standpoints can make irreconcilable claims, or does it show that the appearance of conflict should be resolved in a particular direction?
