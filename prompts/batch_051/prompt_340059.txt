In the philosophy of mind and cognitive science it has become common to say, following Daniel Dennett, that the self is a center of narrative gravity: an abstractum like the center of gravity of a physical object, real enough to explain and predict behavior but not itself an entity in the brain or an inner substance. The analogy is typically used to license a deflationary conclusion: we can retain ordinary talk of selves, responsibility, and personal identity without positing a substantial unified subject. I want an audit of how far this analogy actually carries. Set out the correspondence in continuous prose: what in the target plays the role of the physical object, the mass distribution, and the computed center. Then press the disanalogies, including at least the following. A physical center of gravity is fixed uniquely by the object's mass distribution, while a person's narrative center is revised, contested, and partly authored from within; a center of gravity has no history, no memory, and no point of view, while a self is precisely something that persists through time and is lived from the inside; and a center of gravity does not act on the object whose center it is, whereas the self is often taken to do real causal or organizing work in a life. I want to know which of these disanalogies matter for the deflationary conclusion and which are harmless, and whether the conclusion survives once they are on the table or was being carried by the analogy all along. If the analogy fails at the load-bearing points, say whether some nearby analogy would do better or whether the deflationary claim has to stand on its own. Please write in continuous prose only, as in a letter or an email: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
