Daniel Dennett spent the last years of his life warning that AI systems able to pass for people — systems he came to call counterfeit people — do a distinctive kind of damage. The damage, on his account, is not that these systems are too capable, but that they undermine the epistemic conditions of ordinary trust: when we can no longer tell whether a message or a decision comes from a person, the practices of taking counsel, assigning blame, and believing another's word begin to corrode. He was careful to distinguish a tool, whose non-human character is visible, from a counterfeit agent, whose fluent surface competences run ahead of anything it comprehends. Dennett died in 2024. Suppose that after his death the following practice became common: pre-sentencing reports in some jurisdictions came to include an AI-generated personality statement — a long, fluent, first-person account produced by a system fine-tuned on the defendant's letters, messages, and social media posts, and presented to a judge as a window into what the defendant is really like. Predict, with argued reasons, the position Dennett would have taken on this practice. Draw only on commitments of his that are well established: his account of the intentional stance as a predictive strategy, his claim that a system can be competent without comprehending what it is doing, and his late arguments about counterfeit people. Where those commitments do not settle the question by themselves, identify the further premise you think he would have added, and defend it as the reading that best fits the overall shape of his view rather than a guess about his temperament. Commit to one definite answer; give the strongest objection to the position you attribute to him; and answer that objection as you think he would have.
