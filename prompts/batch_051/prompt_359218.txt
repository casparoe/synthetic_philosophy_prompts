After retiring from a career in the natural sciences, I have begun reading epistemology in earnest, and I find myself repeatedly drawn to an argument that I would like to have stress-tested. Suppose I know some ordinary empirical proposition — that a certain drug lowers mortality from a particular infection, that one geological stratum is older than another, or that no signal travels faster than light. Part of what it is to know such a proposition is for it to be true. But if the proposition is true, then any evidence, argument, or apparently careful study that tells against it leads away from the truth: it is misleading. From my knowing the proposition, I seem able to conclude, without working through the details of any particular objection, that every present and future objection to it is misleading. And if I already know that an objection is misleading, what reason remains to attend to it? The conclusion appears to be that knowledge carries with it a limited entitlement to dogmatism — not ignoring counterevidence out of stubbornness, but setting it aside as a matter of epistemic right. I am using know in the ordinary fallible sense in which the sciences know many things, not in the sense of Cartesian certainty.

I gather that this line of thought is sometimes called the dogmatism paradox, but I want to treat it as a position I am inclined to hold rather than as an artifact to be dissolved from outside. The position is this: once one genuinely knows a proposition, the demand to engage seriously with counterevidence is rationally undercut, because any such evidence would have to be misleading. My question is what would count against this position. I am not asking about the practical sociology of science — the fact that a working scientist must ignore cranks to get anything done. I am asking about the epistemology. Which cases, intuitions, formal results, or historical episodes should trouble me most? I am thinking, for example, of higher-order evidence, peer disagreement, ordinary human fallibility, and episodes in the history of science in which apparently decisive objections were dismissed and the received view later collapsed. Sort these into considerations that would be fatal to the position as stated, considerations that would only force a qualification, and considerations that a sophisticated defender would dismiss as not really touching it. I would also like an honest assessment of whether the position, as I have stated it, insulates itself from objection so thoroughly that no evidence or argument could refute it — and if so, whether that insulation is a reason to abandon the position or simply a feature of the concept of knowledge.
