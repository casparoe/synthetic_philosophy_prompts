I have been developing a view I call the ex ante license account of strict liability. The core claim: when a person voluntarily takes up an activity whose characteristic risks cannot be fully controlled by taking care—demolition work, keeping dangerous animals, manufacturing explosives, selling food at scale—they thereby accept the terms on which the activity may be pursued, and one of those terms is that if the characteristic risk materializes, the law may impose punishment without asking whether they were careless on that particular occasion. Consider a fireworks manufacturer who follows every safety regulation and still causes a fatal blast through a rare but known property of its compounds. On my account, the culpable act is not the detonation as executed; it is the earlier choice to manufacture fireworks at all while knowing the risk. Fault is not eliminated by strict liability; it is relocated to that standing choice made under uncertainty. The view is meant to show that punishing without fault need not violate the principle that punishment requires culpability.

I would like an honest verdict. Give me the three strongest objections to this account, and for each say whether it is the kind of problem the account can absorb or the kind that disables it. Say what cases the account handles worst, and where its language of acceptance or licensing begins to do work it cannot legitimately do. Finally, if the core move already circulates in the literature under an established label, name the nearest position and say what my formulation adds to it or loses from it.
