Compare two acts with the same expected death toll. In the first, a plant supervisor orders that waste coolant be discharged into a river beside a village, knowing that the contamination will likely kill about three people downstream over the coming year. The victims cannot be named in advance, but the mechanism, location, and time frame are tightly constrained. In the second, an analyst at an infrastructure fund approves the only viable financing package for a large construction project, knowing from an actuarial model that the project's emissions will, in expectation, cause roughly three additional deaths somewhere in the region over the next two decades, through a dispersed mix of respiratory and heat-related mechanisms. Here, too, no victim is identifiable in advance, and the project could not proceed without the analyst's approval. Ordinary judgment treats the first as a grave wrong — perhaps criminal — while treating the second as, at worst, a minor moral blemish, even if the analyst could have selected a slightly more expensive structure that avoided those expected deaths, and even if the expected toll is better supported by evidence in the second case than in the first.

The asymmetry generalizes: we condemn localized, identifiable expected harms that sit only a few causal links away, but we barely blame dispersed, statistically projected harms at the end of a long chain, even when the numbers are comparable and the choice is squarely under the agent's control. A consequentialist may see this as an unjustified bias toward proximity and identifiability — a version of the cluelessness problem, in which the inability to say exactly which downstream harm follows from an act is treated as though it made the harm less real. But perhaps something does justify the asymmetry: a difference in the agent's knowledge about who will be harmed, a difference in whether the agent's act is a necessary condition of any particular death, a difference in how many agents share causal responsibility, or the thought that a workable morality must limit liability somewhere if it is not to collapse into paralysis.

Take a committed position. Identify the strongest candidate justification for this asymmetry — or argue that none survives. Test your favored account against variants: what if the analyst's model is updated monthly and can attach a probability, though not a certainty, to each specific death; what if the supervisor's victims are also merely statistical; what if both agents act in a competitive market where a rival would have made the same choice had they refused? Then say whether the ordinary asymmetry should be kept, narrowed, reversed, or abandoned, and whether a consequentialist should accept it as a useful heuristic or reject it as a distortion to be corrected.
