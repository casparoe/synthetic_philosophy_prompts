In The Prince, Machiavelli compares fortune to a river in flood: when it rises it spills over the plains, uproots trees, levels buildings, and carries away the soil, while in quiet seasons people can build embankments and channels so that the next flood is diverted or spends its force harmlessly. This image is often pressed into a specific interpretive job. The standard gloss runs that fortune is partly beyond control yet partly channelable, that virtù is the foresight and construction embodied in the embankments, and that Machiavelli is therefore no fatalist: a prince who builds the right defenses can master roughly half of what fortune would otherwise decide and can convert fortune from a threat into an opportunity.

Audit that analogy and test how far it actually carries. Provide a table of correspondences between the river scene and Machiavelli's notions of fortune, virtù, opportunity, and princely action. Then identify the disanalogies—the respects in which fortune is not like a river and virtù is not like building flood works. For each disanalogy that matters, indicate which feature of the river image it silently imports into the political analysis and whether that imported feature does essential work in generating the non-fatalist reading. End by saying whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are made explicit, or whether the analogy was doing the argument's real work all along. The same discussion also introduces a second image, of fortune as a woman who responds to youthful boldness rather than to patient engineering; consider whether that second image undercuts the first or quietly revises it.
