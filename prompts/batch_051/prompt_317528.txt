Ok so I'm trying to write up a short position paper on moral uncertainty — basically what you should do when you're not sure which moral theory is right — and I want a consistency check before I share it. Here's roughly where I've landed, as five things I believe:

(1) If I'm torn between two moral theories, I should pick the act with the highest expected choiceworthiness: take each theory's verdict on an act, weight it by my credence in that theory, and choose whichever comes out on top. This feels like just the natural extention of expected-utility reasoning to uncertainty about ethics.

(2) But I don't think there's any real fact about how much choiceworthiness in one theory compares to choiceworthiness in another. Asking how a unit of utilitarian badness trades against a unit of Kantian wrongness feels like asking how a temperature compares to a length. Any way of lining the scales up seems arbitrary, not something to discover.

(3) At the same time, I definitely don't think you should just go with your most plausible theory and ignore the rest; if there's a real chance a completely different theory is right, brushing that off seems morally reckless when the stakes are high.

(4) I'm fine using moral uncertainty as a tiebreaker though: if two options are exactly tied on my best-guess theory, I'll pick whichever one the runner-up theory ranks higher.

(5) And I hold that how you scale a theory's choiceworthiness numbers shouldn't matter. Doubling every number a theory assigns doesn't change what the theory says or recommends. So how we normalize a theory shouldn't make any moral difference.

Do these hang together? Go through them pairwise or however makes sense, flag which commitments are actually in tension and which only look like they are, rank the tensions by severity, and say what the cheapest fix is in each case and what it costs you. I keep going back and forth on wether there's a real contradiction here or I'm just overthinking it.
