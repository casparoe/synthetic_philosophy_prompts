I'm grading a take-home essay for an epistemology seminar. The question was: "Explain the dogmatism paradox. In your answer, state the closure principle it seems to depend on, give an example of the problematic reasoning, and say what you think the paradox ultimately shows about the relation between knowledge and counter-evidence." Here's one student's answer:

The dogmatism paradox is a problem for anyone who thinks knowledge is closed under known entailment. Suppose S knows that p, and suppose S knows that p entails not-E, where E is some piece of evidence against p. According to single-premise closure, S can come to know not-E. But this means S can infer that any evidence against p is false, just from the fact that p is true. So if S knows that the library is open on Saturdays, and a newspaper reports that it is closed, S can deduce that the newspaper report is false and ignore it without even reading it. That's dogmatic. The lesson is that knowledge is not closed under known entailment; once we give up closure, the paradox disappears. The real culprit is multi-premise closure, because the problematic inference combines p with the conditional if p then not-E. So I think the paradox refutes closure-based epistemology and forces us to accept a tracking or relevant-alternatives view of knowledge.
