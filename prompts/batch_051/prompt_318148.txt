I am a PhD candidate in philosophy, writing a dissertation on the difference between intended and merely foreseen effects, and I serve as the graduate-student member of the clinical ethics committee at my university's teaching hospital. The committee meets twice a week and usually has between a few hours and a day to issue a recommendation on whether a proposed intervention may proceed. The cases that most divide us follow a single pattern: a clinician proposes an action that will relieve severe pain or suffering, or preserve a life, while foreseeing, sometimes only as a real possibility, that the very same action will also cause or hasten a grave harm, typically death. The physicians in the room keep invoking the doctrine of double effect, but nobody has turned it into a procedure we can run in a consistent order, and our outcomes drift with the composition of the committee. We have approved near-identical morphine-escalation cases and then, a month later, sent one back, with no clear account of what changed.

I do not want an exposition of the doctrine, and I do not want a survey of theories of intention. I want you to write a reusable decision procedure, a checklist or flow in words, that operationalizes the doctrine of double effect for an audience of intelligent non-philosophers: intensivists, surgeons, palliative-care nurses, a chaplain. The procedure should encode the standard four conditions as a sequence of questions: that the act itself be acceptable, that the bad effect not be intended but only foreseen, that the bad effect not be the means by which the good effect is brought about, and that the expected good be weighty enough to stand in proportion to the risked or foreseen harm. For each question, say what would count as an adequate answer, where a no stops the procedure, and which questions cannot be settled by the procedure because they are judgment calls or require information the committee does not have. Pay special attention to the step where the intended/foreseen distinction is doing the real work: the doctrine is often criticized through cases in which the bad effect is so close, or so inseparable from the good effect, that the distinction seems to carry all the moral weight while being unverifiable from the outside. I need the procedure to handle those cases honestly, for example by treating a clinician's insistence that death was the last thing they wanted as insufficient on its own, rather than pretending the problem does not exist.

Then run the procedure on three cases we have seen variants of. First, a request to escalate a morphine infusion high enough to control severe dyspnea in a patient with advanced cancer, where the dose is likely to depress respiration and may bring death sooner, and the physician insists the sole intention is comfort. Second, a proposed hysterectomy on a pregnant patient diagnosed with uterine cancer, where the surgery is the standard life-preserving treatment but the fetus cannot survive it. Third, a request for palliative sedation to unconsciousness for intractable terminal agitation in a patient whose death is expected within days anyway, with the understanding that once sedated the patient will no longer eat or drink and may die somewhat sooner. I want a definite recommendation in each case, with the chain of answers that produced it. If the procedure returns an answer I will dislike, for instance that a case my colleagues and I have been approving should fail, or that a case we have been resisting should pass, do not soften it: say so and show which step forces it. Finally, state what the procedure cannot do: where it defers to clinical facts, where it defers to law, and where double effect runs out and the committee is simply making a values decision under another name.
