I have been reworking a lecture on the ethics of belief, and I want to test a worry about Clifford's shipowner argument. As I read the case, its force depends on a particular picture of culpable belief: the believer faces a determinate question, there is a reliable procedure for settling it that lies within his power, and he declines to use it because his interests pull in the other direction. The shipowner suspects his vessel is unseaworthy, could order an inspection, and instead lets hope quiet his doubts. His belief is culpable because it is formed by the willful neglect of an available inquiry—not because it happens to turn out false.

But the epistemic economy that picture assumes has changed. For many consequential public questions, inquiry is nearly costless in one respect, yet unguided inquiry no longer converges on the truth in the way the picture assumes. Search results and social feeds return confident, internally coherent, sometimes adversarial material for every side; recommendation systems are optimized for engagement rather than accuracy; and the difference between a genuine test and a faction-specific pseudotest is itself contested, gated by exactly the group judgments the believer cannot yet trust. Where the shipowner had one decisive procedure available and ignored it, the ordinary believer now has a surfeit of would-be procedures and no neutral way to order them. The characteristic failure is not looking away but being unable to weight, filter, and distrust what looking returns. Does Clifford-style evidentialism survive this change? Identify which premise or background assumption the shipowner argument needs, say how the new environment bears on it, and either repair the argument or state honestly what is left of it.
