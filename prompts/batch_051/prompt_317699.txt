As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I keep returning to a simple principle that shapes a great deal of otherwise sensible reasoning about wrongdoing: if one act is permissible and a second act is permissible, then a course of conduct that merely combines them should not become impermissible in virtue of the combination alone. If the combined course of conduct is wrong, the wrongness must already be located in one of its parts; the conjunction itself cannot be doing any independent moral work. The principle is unified, forces explanations to be local rather than ad hoc, and gets mundane cases right: reading in a cafe is fine, asking a stranger for a favor is fine, and doing both is fine.

Blackmail looks like a plain counterexample. Set aside cases in which the blackmailer already owes a duty of confidentiality. It is lawful to reveal a true but embarrassing fact about a person; it is lawful to ask that person for money; yet it is a crime to make the one conditional on the other, to say in effect: unless you pay me, I will reveal it. And my intuition is not merely that the law happens to draw the line there. I believe it should draw it there: using another person's secret as leverage feels like a distinctive wrong with its own character, not a trick of combining two innocent ingredients. But I cannot say what the additional wrong-making ingredient is, and the principle tells me there is none.

So which should give way? Present the composition principle and the anti-blackmail intuition each at their strongest, then offer a negotiated verdict. Do not simply pick a side; I want to know what it would take, and what would justify, each of three moves: qualifying or abandoning the principle, debunking the intuition, or finding a reading on which both survive. Then say which move you judge least costly.
