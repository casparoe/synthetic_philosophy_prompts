I'm in a causal inference seminar and we've spent the last few weeks on Pearl's ladder: rung 1 is association (seeing, joint distributions), rung 2 is intervention (doing, do(X)), rung 3 is counterfactuals (imagining, with a full structural causal model and its equations pinned down). The recurring line is that you can't climb from one rung to the next without extra assumptions, and that a lot of empirical social science and epi talk is secretly rung 1 talk dressed up as rung 2 or 3.

So here's a mechanism I want you to pick apart. A journal — or say a department, for its internal working-paper review — adopts the following rule. Every empirical paper that uses causal vocabulary at all ("causes," "effect," "leads to," "would have," "why") has to state on page one the highest rung its substantive conclusions actually inhabit. Declaring rung 2 requires stating the intervention you're treating as do(X) and the identification strategy or assumptions that license moving up from association (conditional ignorability, a backdoor set, an instrument, whatever). Declaring rung 3 requires a fully specified structural causal model: the variables, the graph, and the functional/response equations, plus a statement of which counterfactual queries on that model the paper claims to answer. Reviewers are told to reject or force downgraded language whenever a paper's causal talk outruns its declared rung. No exceptions for things "everyone knows," no "we use causal language loosely but our results are really associational." Treat this as a mechanism, not as a position: I want to know how it actually plays out, not whether its a good idea.

Give me:

1. **The intended machinery** (~100 words): what this rule is actually doing in terms of the rungs — which moves it polices and which it leaves alone.
2. **Author-side adaptation** (~250 words): what researchers and labs do to keep making causal claims under the rule — rung inflation, the minimum viable DAG, writing around the vocabulary, or taking the identifying assumptions more seriously. Where does honest work get easier and where does it get punished?
3. **Reviewer- and editor-side adaptation** (~200 words): what reviewers do with the new lever, how fights over graphs and equations actually play out, and whether editorial incentives push toward strictness or lenience.
4. **Failure modes and gaming** (~200 words): the specific ways this gets gamed or backfires — performative DAGs, laundering rung 2 claims through rung 1 wording, treating the declared rung as a tax you pay at the top of the paper — and which one bites first.
5. **Second-order effects** (~150 words): what happens to the literature over time? What gets published more, what vanishes, and does the causal language in papers become more or less informative?
6. **Fixes or abandon** (~150 words): can you patch the worst failure modes without gutting the rule, and is the patched version better than nothing?
