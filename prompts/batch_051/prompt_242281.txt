I am a graduate student representative on my university's Committee on Academic Honors and Awards, and I am currently taking a seminar on the ethics of partiality, so a case now before the committee has stopped being abstract. The committee administers the university's most prestigious dissertation-year fellowship. This year a five-member selection panel included a professor in the natural sciences who also directs a large laboratory, and one of the applicants was his own doctoral student. The professor did not recuse himself, and by several accounts he was the panel's most forceful advocate for his student. His defense was that after four years of supervision he was uniquely placed to judge the dissertation's quality, the candidate's independence, and the seriousness of the proposed project. The panel awarded the fellowship to that student. A runner-up has since filed a formal complaint alleging that the process was biased: on this account, the professor's advocacy was not expertise but loyalty to his own student dressed up as judgment, and on the standard quantitative indicators the complainant's record is at least as strong. The underlying question for us is whether partiality of this kind should be treated as an epistemic asset and a legitimate form of loyalty, or as a bias that undermines the fairness of the award.

The committee meets Thursday, and I need to circulate a short written opinion in advance. Please draft the memo I will submit. It should recommend one of three courses of action — uphold the award, uphold it but revise our recusal and advocacy rules, or rescind the award and reopen the competition — and it should give reasons the full committee could actually act on. In particular, please engage honestly with the strongest consideration on the losing side rather than dismissing it, and then tell me where your recommendation is most vulnerable: which argument or factual finding, if it went the other way, would change your bottom line, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
