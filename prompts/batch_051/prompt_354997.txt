You are a professional philosopher of logic and normativity, and I want your careful written judgement on a problem that has been nagging at me. Before you reach a verdict, take the time to lay out both sides as charitably as you can; I am less interested in a quick decision than in knowing exactly what is at stake.

I find myself torn between a picture of reasoning I keep wanting to hold and an intuition I cannot shake, and the two collide in Lewis Carroll’s "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" (1895). At the decisive point, the Tortoise has accepted A and B and, at Achilles’s request, C, where C is "If A and B are true, Z must be true." Then this happens:

> "And why must I?"
> "Because it follows logically from them. If A and B and C are true, Z must be true. You don't dispute that, I imagine?"
> "If A and B and C are true, Z must be true," the Tortoise thoughtfully repeated. "That's another Hypothetical, isn't it? And, if I failed to see its truth, I might accept A and B and C, and still not accept Z, mightn't I?"

Here is the tension. The theory that attracts me is a propositionalist account of rationality. It is simple and unified: there is one kind of rational relation, entailment between propositions, and one kind of rational state, acceptance of a proposition. On this view, "following" modus ponens is not some further act over and above believing; it is just having the corresponding conditional among one’s beliefs. The rule is, as it were, already written into the content of C, and no additional medium is needed. This picture has systematic virtues I admire: it avoids mystery, it treats logic as truth rather than psychology, and it reduces the norms of reasoning to a single category.

But my intuition says the passage will not cooperate. When the Tortoise accepts A, B, C and D and still withholds Z, the problem is not that Achilles has failed to find the right premise; it is that the step from premises to conclusion must be performed, not stated. Stating the step as another Hypothetical only places one more premise on the Tortoise’s list and reopens the very same gap. So there appears to be a residue — an act of inference — that cannot be represented propositionally without beginning the regress again. That residue is exactly what the propositionalist picture denies, and I find it just as hard to deny the residue as I find it hard to give up the picture.

I would like you to present each side at its strongest and then offer a negotiated verdict. In particular: should the intuition be debunked and dropped — for example, as a confusion between rules and premises, or between using modus ponens and mentioning it — or should the propositionalist picture be amended or abandoned by admitting a sui generis practice of inference alongside belief? Is there any reading on which the two are compatible? For each possible move, tell me what would justify it: what consideration would show the intuition to be an artefact rather than a datum, and what would show the propositionalist theory to be not merely incomplete but untenable. Please write your answer in British English.
