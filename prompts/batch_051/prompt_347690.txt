I'm a retired geochemist — not a philosopher. My whole career I ran a lab on the principle that if a method isn't written down well enough for a sleep-deprived tech to run at 2 a.m., it isn't a method, so I have a soft spot for checklists. A couple years ago I got into the philosophy of convention and normativity — Lewis on convention, Gilbert on joint commitment, Bicchieri on social norms, that territory — and I keep thinking these ideas should be usable by people who have to make actual decisions. Now I'm in a position to put that to work.

I sit on the board of a small non-profit that runs summer work-and-travel placements, mostly in restaurants and hotels. We send students abroad, and we host international students here. Every placement season our coordinators field a steady stream of questions about tipping, queueing, jaywalking, and other everyday local conventions, and right now they're winging it. A student didn't tip; a student tipped where tipping is seen as rude; a group keeps crossing against the signal. The coordinators are sharp but have no philosophy background, they get maybe ten minutes per case, and they need an answer they can defend to a student, a host employer, and sometimes an angry parent. The stakes are real — a tipped worker's take-home pay, a host family's willingness to keep hosting, occasionally a student sent home early.

I'd like you to write a reusable decision procedure for them, grounded in what the philosophy of convention and obligation actually says rather than vibes. In particular, the procedure has got to preserve a few distinctions I keep tripping over:

- a convention that's just coordination (which side of the escalator you stand on, whether you queue properly) versus a convention that carries real moral weight (tipping in a US restaurant, where the worker's pay is partly the tip);
- the fact that the student didn't grow up with the local convention and never agreed to it — how much should that matter?
- whether a visitor is bound to follow a convention that is itself demeaning or unjust, and whether that question is one of fact or one of values;
- and the difference between "the program may make this a condition of the placement" and "the student has done something morally wrong." Our staff blur those two in both directions, and I don't want a checklist that inherits the blur. Please keep those two questions seperate at every step.

The deliverable should be a sequence of questions to ask in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a "no," and where it hands the decision to a human because it's a values call rather than a facts call. It has to fit on one side of a page, be runnable in ten minutes by a coordinator with no philosophical training, and be honest about the places it will misfire. Flag the cases where the procedure gives an answer we won't like, and don't soften those. End with a short note on what the procedure cannot settle, so we don't start treating a one-page flowchart as a moral theory.

Then walk the procedure through three cases:

1. Daan, a Dutch student in the US, refuses to tip when he eats out, on principle: he says the employer should pay a living wage instead. The waitstaff are losing money, his host family is embarrased, and a cafe owner has asked us not to send students there anymore. Must he tip, and may the program require it as a condition of his placement?

2. Sophia, an American student in a no-tipping country, keeps adding a 20 percent tip "to reward good service," even though the host says leaving extra money reads as rude and several shops have tried to hand it back. Is she obligated to stop, and what exactly makes her apparent generosity wrong, if anything?

3. A group of our students is staying in a city where pedestrians always wait for the signal, even on an empty road. The students keep jaywalking because they would at home. Locals are complaining that the students set a bad example for children. There's no enforcement, and the students say the convention is pointless when no cars are coming. Should the program make "wait for the signal" a rule — and on what ground: safety, respect for a local convention as such, or something else? If the complaint about children is doing most of the local work, I want the procedure to say what kind of consideration that is.
