I have been thinking, in an amateur way, about what makes two marks on a page the same letter. I work with two criteria. First, a letter is a slot in a particular writing system: it has a name, a place in an alphabet, and a role in spelling words, so its exact drawn form is secondary. Second, that slot admits a family of acceptable shapes, and a mark counts as the letter when a reader who knows the script would recognize it as belonging to that family.

The lowercase a is a useful test. In most serif typefaces it is two-storey — one closed loop stacked on another — while in many sans-serif faces and in most handwriting it is one-storey: a single loop with a tail. Nobody treats these as different letters; a word can be re-set from one font to another and remain the same word.

That is where my trouble starts. The symbol the International Phonetic Alphabet uses for the vowel in English ‘father’, ɑ, is drawn exactly like a one-storey a. The IPA also uses the ordinary letter a separately, for a different vowel, so within a single notation two similar-looking signs are treated as distinct characters and only one is the letter a. Lowercase Greek alpha, α, is in many fonts indistinguishable at small sizes from a one-storey a, and the Greek and Latin alphabets are close relatives anyway, yet nobody would say a Greek word contains the Latin letter a. In the opposite direction, a wordmark can replace a letter with a picture — an apple standing in for an a — and a reader who knows the brand will read the picture as an a, though it shares almost no shape with either the one-storey or the two-storey form.

Please go through these cases criterion by criterion. For each case, say whether each of my two criteria would classify it as the letter a, and whether that verdict matches what I should say. Then say whether my criteria need changing; if something deeper is doing the real work, say as plainly as you can what it is.
