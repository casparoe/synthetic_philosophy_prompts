One of my friends teaches an intro probability course and she's weirdly opinionated about the reference class problem — which class you're supposed to conditionalize on when you assign a probability to a single event. I've been keeping a list of her calls on the toy cases she uses in class (the numbers are made up, so dont go fact-checking them). I want to know what she'd say about a new case she hasn't seen yet.

Her calls so far:

1. Whether the next flip of a particular coin comes up heads.
- all flips of any newly minted coin: 50%
- the last 10,000 flips of this exact coin: 48.2% (n = 10,000)
- the last 60 flips of this exact coin: 43.3% (n = 60)
- the last 8 flips of this exact coin, same person flipping, same starting position: 87.5% (n = 8)
She says use 48.2%. The 60-flip and 8-flip classes she calls noise.

2. Whether a specific cancer patient survives a year.
- all patients with this cancer, any stage, any treatment: 62% (n = 5,000)
- patients with this cancer at the same stage: 58% (n = 1,500)
- patients with this cancer at the same stage and a particular gene variant: 71% (n = 320)
- all of the above plus treatment with a very new drug: 83% (n = 15)
She says use 71%. The drug class is narrowest, but she says 15 patients isn't enough to trust it.

3. Whether a bolt from a particular production run is defective.
- all bolts from the factory: 2.0% (n = 2,000,000)
- bolts from this machine: 1.3% (n = 90,000)
- bolts from this machine during the night shift: 1.1% (n = 11,000)
- bolts from this machine during the night shift when the coolant temperature was out of spec: 4.6% (n = 42)
She picks the night-shift class. She says the coolant class is tempting but 42 bolts isn't enough.

4. Whether a particular loan applicant defaults.
- all applicants in the lender's book: 9% (n = 80,000)
- applicants with credit scores from 680 to 700: 6% (n = 4,000)
- applicants with that credit score and income under $50k: 8% (n = 900)
- applicants with that credit score, income under $50k, and exactly two dependents: 11% (n = 30)
She picks the 8% class and won't touch the 30-person class.

The new case is on life-insurance mortality. It's the probability that a particular 35-year-old woman — she's a commercial pilot — dies within 10 years.
- all 35-year-old women: 0.6% (n = 1,000,000)
- 35-year-old women who are commercial pilots: 1.0% (n = 2,000)
- commercial pilots born in an even-numbered year: 1.2% (n = 1,000)
- commercial pilots with a family history of early heart disease: 2.6% (n = 40)

What would she pick here? What rule is she actually running — is it just "narrowest class with enough data," or does the extra variable have to actually do something? If her past calls don't settle it, what's the cleanest extra case I could put to her to tell the rules apart? And then tell me how confident you are in the prediction, and the one point you're least sure about.
