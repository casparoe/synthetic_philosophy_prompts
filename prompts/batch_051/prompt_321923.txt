You are a philosopher of law with a special interest in self-referential rules and the foundations of legal systems. Before you commit to an answer, brainstorm the possible interpretations of the practice I describe; then identify which of those interpretations the practice genuinely presupposes rather than merely tolerates; finally, take a definite position and defend it.

Here is the practice. In many constitutional democracies the procedure for amending the constitution is itself written into the constitution: a clause states the supermajorities or ratification steps required to alter the text. The same legal systems treat this clause as applying to itself. If enough political will existed, the only legally recognized route for replacing the amendment clause, lowering its threshold, or adopting a completely new constitution would be the procedure the current clause prescribes. At the same time, these systems sometimes announce that certain principles are unamendable no matter how perfectly the written procedure is followed, while in other contexts insisting that an amendment adopted through the prescribed procedure is simply valid law and that courts should not look behind it. A toy model of this arrangement is the game Nomic, in which changing the rules is itself a move permitted and governed by the rules, so players can vote to amend the amendment rules.

To an observer this looks as if the amendment clause is being treated as two things at once: an ordinary replaceable rule, subject to its own operation, and an ultimate, self-grounding rule whose authority comes from nowhere outside itself, even while some of what it protects is declared beyond its reach.

What theory of rules, legal validity, or the structure of a legal system does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, clearly separating what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits well with, and indicate where it is philosophically underdetermined. Is the presupposed theory coherent, or does the practice smuggle in two inconsistent pictures of a rule that both creates its own validity and answers to a higher order it cannot touch? End by saying concretely what would have to change in the practice if we replaced the presupposed theory with the position that the amendment clause is not self-grounding, but merely implements an ultimate rule of recognition or basic norm standing outside the constitutional text.
