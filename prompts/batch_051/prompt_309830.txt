I’m a novelist, and in my current book a charecter studies theology before deciding whether to help poison a king. I keep circling around Aquinas, because I want the scene to feel like the sort of casuistry someone in 1270 would actually do. In the De Regno (On Kingship), Aquinas writes, "Yet it is natural for man, more than for any other animal, to be a social and political animal, to live in a group." That’s the bit I’ve stuck on: if we are naturally political, then political order is natural, and the person who corrupts that order isn’t just a bad ruler but a kind of parasite on our nature. So the question isn’t whether tyrants are bad; it’s whether a particular person, acting without official authority, may kill one.

Here are the criteria I want to use, which I’m treating as my best working reconstruction of Aquinas’s position. A killing counts as justified tyrannicide only if: (1) the ruler is a genuine tyrant, ruling for his private advantage rather than the common good; (2) the act is undertaken by someone with public authority, or at least the people’s consent, rather than by a private person on his own; and (3) all lawful and less violent remedies have been genuinely exhausted. Now the case: a legally crowned king has maintained roads, courts, and grain stores for years, but after a failed coup he suspends the council, executes rivals under borrowed laws, and diverts royal revenues to his cousins. He is not starving the country; many peasants prefer him to the barons. The only person positioned to act is his chancellor, who has no mandate from any assembly and no time to call one, but she knows the king plans to massacre a town that sheltered rebels. Under the criteria, is her planned poisoning justified tyrannicide, or just revenge with a Thomist gloss? Go through the criteria one by one, say where the case is underdetermined, and tell me if the criteria themselves need changing. At the very end, put a single line in this format: \answer{justified tyrannicide} or \answer{not justified tyrannicide}.
