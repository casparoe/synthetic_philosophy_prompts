Been rereading The Handmaid's Tale and I keep bumping it against Mill's Subjection of Women. Mill's move there is that appeals to women's 'nature' are circular: every observation we have of women comes from women already shaped by subordination, so you can't use 'nature' to justify the subordination — it's custom dressed up as fact. I want a reading of Atwood's novel through that lens. Where does Gilead lean on exactly the nature-claims Mill attacks, and do Offred's mother, Moira, Serena Joy (or the Aunts) break the picture of a fixed female nature in the way Mill would of predicted? And where does the novel push back — does it treat fertility or motherhood as genuinely more natural than the rest, and if so does the anti-naturalist reading survive that? I'm trying to settle whether the book is really a Millian text or just looks like one on a first pass.
