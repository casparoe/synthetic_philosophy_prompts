I'm the poetry editor at a small translation-focused magazine, and over the last six months we've been flooded with machine-translated submissions. Some are obviously machine, but plenty are fluent enough that I genuinely cant tell by ear. We got burned last issue: we published a piece that read beautifully in English and a bilingual reader later told us the original line was doing three things at once and the English did one, because the translator had flipped words without ever picturing the scene. I keep coming back to the Hofstadter-ish worry that a system can be statistically fluent while never having actually imagined what the poem is about, so it can't protect the parts of the meaning that live in connotation, sound, and double meanings. I want that idea turned into something my editorial team can actually run, not just a vibe.

Please write me a reusable screening procedure for deciding whether a translation submission shows enough evidence that the translator understood the poem, rather than just moved words and phrases around. We would run it weekly on about forty submissions. First readers are English-only volunteers with maybe five minutes per submission each; I can get ten minutes of a bilingual reader's time per submission that gets past the first screen; I do the final sign-off and can spend about twenty minutes per submission at that stage. The stakes are the magazine's reputation and the fact that we pay translators, so rejecting genuine human work has a real cost too.

Make it a checklist or flowchart in words: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than one of fact. I'd rather it look for positive evidence that someone pictured the scene than just the absence of visible errors, since a large language model is exactly optimized to produce text with no visible errors. Keep the distinction between probably not machine and actually understood if you think it matters, and flag any step where you're collapsing a philosophical point to keep the procedure usable.

Then run the procedure on three submissions we actually saw:

1. A German poem where our bilingual checker confirms the original moves through a chain of puns and half-rhymes. The English is smooth and grammatical, but every pun becomes a literal statement and the sound-patterns are gone, so it reads like a flat mood piece. No translator's note.

2. A Polish poem whose English has two awkward phrases and one strange tense shift. The translator's note explains that a recurring image in the original (a dog nosing a door shut) also carries a slang sense about being locked out of a marriage, and says the translator chose an English idiom that does roughly the same work instead of matching the words.

3. A submission from a friend of mine, a writer I admire, translating a contemporary Portuguese poem. The English is stunning. Our checker says the central image is wrong — a boat winched up a beach becomes a person hauled out of bed — and the note describes the piece as an imitation rather than a translation. I already know I'll want to override this one, but make the procedure tell me the truth.

Make sure at least one of the outcomes is one I won't like, and say straight out where the procedure conflicts with what I'll want to do. Also say what the procedure can't do — the failures it's structurally blind to.

Wherever you land, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or piece of evidence would lead you to a different verdict, and where the reasoning is most likely to fail. Then, very last line, give me a machine-readable bottom line of the form \answer{...}, with the braces containing a short label for what our default policy should be (something like \answer{require positive evidence of understanding} or \answer{first-pass reject unsolicited translations}, whatever you actually conclude).
