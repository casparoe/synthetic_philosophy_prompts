I was talking to my flatmate, who works as a session musician, about the royalties from a track he played on, and he explained something that's been bugging me since. The law apparently treats the song and the recording of the song as two seperate copyrights: they can be owned by different people, they generate different kinds of revenue, and they expire at different dates. When a band covers the song, the original songwriter still gets a mechanical royalty, but the copyright in the new recording belongs to the band or its label, so the same song can sit inside any number of distinct, separately owned recordings. And when a producer samples a two-second snippet of an old record, they can't just clear the record: they have to clear the recording and the underlying song separately, which is apparently why sample clearance is such a mess. Legally, two separate copyright works are stacked inside the same few seconds of audio.

What feels odd to me is that the law never seems to say what the song itself is, the thing that stays the same across all those covers and samples. When I listen to the same Beethoven symphony in two different recordings, I say I've heard the same work, but the law's attention is always on the two recordings and the long-expired composition. So here's what I'd like you to work through. Reconstruct what theory of musical works this practice appears to presuppose: is it committed to something like the view that a composition is a repeatable abstract thing (a type, a structure) whose performances are instances, while a recording is a different kind of entity, itself repeatable through its copies? Or could a nominalist, who denies that anything repeatable exists over and above the individual performances and digital files, accept every part of this practice? Try to sort out what the law strictly presupposes from what it merely permits, and then say what would actually have to change, doctrinally or in the way we credit composers, bands, and labels, if we replaced the standard picture with the view that a musical work just is the totality of its performances and recordings. Please write your answer in British English.
