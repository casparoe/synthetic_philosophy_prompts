Thomas Malthus's name has become shorthand for the claim that human numbers will always press against the means of subsistence. Recently, while my partner and I have been deciding whether to have a third child, I have been reading the population-ethics debates that arose long after his lifetime: total utilitarians who hold that a larger population is better whenever the added lives are worth living, person-affecting theorists who doubt that merely possible people count at all, pronatalists and anti-natalists, and longtermists who treat the number and quality of future people as the dominant moral question. I would like a careful reconstruction of what Malthus would have made of these positions. Work from his actual reasoning about the relationship between population pressure and human welfare, not from the familiar caricature. Distinguish the questions he would have recognized as his own—scarce resources, subsistence, equality, and the conditions of a flourishing life—from the questions that only arose after his death, and say where his empirical premises would push him toward one side rather than another. In particular, address whether he would count as a pronatalist in the contemporary sense, and whether he would accept the claim that adding a life that is barely worth living makes the world better.
