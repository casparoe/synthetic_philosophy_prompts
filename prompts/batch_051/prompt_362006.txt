A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether there has to be, somewhere in the brain, a place and a time at which it all comes together and the subject simply has the experience. He was not defending a pineal gland; he was defending a functional endpoint, some stage where processing converges into a finished conscious episode. I found myself running through the anti-Cartesian-theater arguments I know, and by the end I had used these five:

1. The homunculus regress. A theater requires an audience, and the audience's appreciation of the show is itself a perception that, on the same model, would need its own screen and its own audience. The only stopping points are arbitrary, unless one introduces an inner person with unexplained powers.

2. The Orwellian-Stalinesque indistinguishability. For any supposed case in which experience has been staged or edited before it reaches the audience, there is an equally good description on which the subject experiences an unedited original and memory is rewritten afterward. The two descriptions predict exactly the same behavior and reports. The theater model therefore posits a boundary between what actually appeared in consciousness and what is later recalled that no evidence can locate.

3. The color-phi phenomenon. When a red spot flashes and then a green spot flashes nearby, subjects report a single spot moving across and changing color partway along its path. A report of the midpoint is possible only after the second flash, so the experience of the midpoint appears to have been assembled retrospectively. A model on which experience is presented once, in real order, struggles with this.

4. The cutaneous rabbit. Rapid taps at the wrist, elbow, and shoulder yield reports of taps hopping evenly up the arm, with later taps influencing the felt locations of earlier ones. The felt sequence is an editorial product, not a faithful, once-and-for-all presentation of a fixed sequence.

5. The probe-relativity of drafts. What a subject reports as experienced depends on when and how the system is probed; different probes elicit different, equally authoritative reports. The theater model requires a single finished performance that probing merely discovers, but the evidence suggests the content is partly constituted by the probing itself.

These five worked as far as they went, but I have the sense that a further reply exists of an importantly different kind. The first argues from a regress, the second from a distinction without a difference, the third and fourth from retroactive temporal editing, and the fifth from the instability of the supposed final draft. Give me one more argument against the Cartesian theater that is not a rewording or special case of these five, and not another illusion that works by the same retrospective mechanism as the third and fourth. After stating it, say which specific assumption of the Cartesian theater it targets and whether it would have advanced last night's argument beyond what I already used.
