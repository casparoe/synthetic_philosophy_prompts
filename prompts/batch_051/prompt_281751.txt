Me and my friend spent most of last night going in circles about Clifford’s *The Ethics of Belief*, with him playing the hard evidentialist and me trying to defend an intuition I couldn’t quite articulate. I’ll admit Clifford’s picture has real pull on me: it gives one clean criterion, it stops me rationalizing, and it takes responsibility for my beliefs seriously. The passage he kept coming back to was this one, from the ship-owner discussion:

"The question of right or wrong has to do with the origin of his belief, not the matter of it; not what it was, but how he got it; not whether it turned out to be true or false, but whether he had a right to believe on such evidence as was before him."

Framed like that, I basically agree. If I get to say “but it might be true” and believe anyway, I can rationalize myself into anything, and my unfounded convictions can absolutely hurt people.

But the case that keeps bothering me is my sister and the missing money. A few years ago, everyone in the family thought she’d taken a large sum out of my parents’ account; the evidence was circumstantial but genuinely pointed at her, and I can’t say I believed her innocence because my evidence was sufficient—if I’m honest, it wasn’t. She turned out to be innocent. My friend says that just makes the belief lucky rather than justified: by Clifford’s lights it was still wrong, because of how I formed it. But I can’t shake the feeling that in that situation believing her wasn’t just a risky prediction about the past. It was a way of being her brother—treating her denial as carrying weight, treating her as a person rather than a hypothesis. If I’d suspended judgment until the evidence settled itself, I would have done something worse. So I want the negotiated version, not a verdict. Give me the strongest case that my brotherly trust is exactly “the voice of prejudice and passion” Clifford would diagnose, and that the intuition should be debunked. Give me the strongest case that Clifford’s criterion needs to be amended or restricted—say, that it governs beliefs that are causally independent of our holding them, while trust in another person is partly constitutive of the relationship the evidence is later about—and explain where the principled line would be. And tell me wether there’s a reading of “right to believe” or of “trust” on which both survive, or which one you would give up, and why.
