I am a philosophy professor working on concepts and aesthetic form, and for several years I have been circling an old problem: what makes every 'a' an 'a'? A two-story serif 'a', a single-story geometric 'a', and a child's malformed chalk 'a' share no single stroke, outline, or set of local features, yet competent readers class them together and almost never with 'd', 'o', or 'q'. My proposal is what I call the tolerance-region account. Represent letterforms in a high-dimensional space whose dimensions include stroke topology, terminal presence and shape, bowl openness, x-height, stroke contrast, and the like. Each letter category, on my view, is a region in this space whose boundary is constituted by where, under ordinary reading conditions, competent readers' sorting becomes unstable: two marks are tokens of the same letter just when they are connected by a path through the region that never crosses a point at which the more natural reading flips to another letter. Style then becomes a cross-categorical matter: a typeface is a coherent choice of a single deformation recipe applied across all twenty-six regions at once—lower the x-height, close the bowls, replace bare terminals with serifs—so that a face is not a collection of shapes but a kind of vector field through the joint tolerance space of the alphabet. I know this sounds adjacent to prototype theory, family resemblance, and some computational work on letterforms, but I want to know whether the philosophical thesis is actually distinct, and whether it survives pressure.

Please assess it candidly. As an expert on concepts, categories, and the perception of form, think carefully first—stress-test the view against hard cases such as the one-story versus two-story 'a', ambiguous glyphs, and invented alphabets—and then give me your verdict as exactly five numbered points, ordered from the most to the least serious objection. Each point should be one or two sentences, and each should state explicitly whether the objection is fatal, repairable, or merely uncomfortable. Give no introduction or conclusion.
