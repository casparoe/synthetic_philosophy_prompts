I am a software engineer on a team that formally verifies safety-critical components (e.g., flight-control routines and cryptographic protocols) with a proof assistant before each quarterly release. Our process rests on a small trusted kernel plus the axioms and extensions we import, and we routinely treat machine-checked proof as proof. Recently a developer derived a contradiction after a long proof; the bug was in our own definitions, not the kernel, but it forced the question we had been avoiding: how hard can we lean on proofs if the kernel and its underlying logic cannot establish their own consistency and could, for all anyone knows, be inconsistent? I have no formal philosophy background, though I gather this is an old problem (Hilbert, Gödel, and later work). Please turn whatever is actually known about the possibility that the basic axioms are inconsistent into a reusable decision procedure we can run. The procedure should give the questions in order and what counts as a sufficient answer to each; say when a machine-checked proof is good enough to ship; say which axioms, extensions (e.g., quotient types, termination checkers, choice principles), and imported lemmas to flag as higher-risk and why; say what to do when a contradiction is produced, where the blast radius ends, and what to document for our regulator. It should also distinguish the genuinely technical steps from judgment calls about risk and values that must be made by a person, and say what the procedure cannot settle. Then run it on three cases: (1) a proof we intend to ship stays inside the trusted kernel but relies on an axiom some of our number regard as dubious; (2) a developer proves false by exploiting a soundness loophole in a plugin, while the kernel itself is untouched; (3) a widely used upstream lemma turns out to depend on a strong choice principle we would prefer not to assume. In at least one case the procedure should deliver an answer my team will not like; flag which one and why.
