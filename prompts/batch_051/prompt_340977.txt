I keep hearing a move in AI policy that goes roughly: an AI race is a Fearon-style bargaining failure. Two labs or states would both rather coordinate on a slower, safer trajectory than sprint, but they race anyway, and the standard rationalist explanations for war are supposed to explain why: each has private information about how close it actually is, each has reason to bluff about progress, neither can credibly commit not to pull ahead once it gets a lead, and the prize (a decisive lead, or AGI itself) is indivisible or winner-take-all enough. The conclusion people then draw is that the fix is war-prevention machinery — transparency and verification for the private-information part, treaties or escrow or some other commitment device for the credible-commitment part.

I want an audit of that analogy, not a policy essay. Lay out the mapping explicitly: what in the AI-race case plays the role of the two disputants, the disputed good, the cost of fighting versus settling, private information, the commitment problem, and indivisibility. Then go at where it breaks or quietly imports: are labs and states actually unified actors the way the war model assumes, or are they full of internal principals and agents with their own incentives; is a slower shared trajectory actually a public good or would some parties prefer to be the one that breaks out; is the payoff genuinely indivisible or is capability partly shareable and positional only in places; is monitoring progress easier or harder than monitoring military mobilization; does racing even impose the kind of costs that make war puzzling in the original account. Then the payoff question: how much of the “we need verification and treaties” conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and is there a better source analogy than war for what an AI race most resembles?

And push back if the framing is off — if the war-bargaining lens is doing no real work here, or the verification-and-treaties conclusion would stand or fall on other grounds anyway, say that plainly instead of politely filling in the table.
