I chair the Housing Appeals Board of a mid-sized city that has accepted a state-imposed obligation to permit 3,200 new housing units over the current planning cycle, with a substantial share affordable to lower-income households. A developer has applied to build a six-story, 110-unit apartment building, 22 of which would be restricted as affordable, on a 1.2-acre parcel currently zoned for single-family detached homes but located directly across from a light-rail station and within walking distance of two elementary schools and a regional park. The city's own General Plan classifies the surrounding area as high-opportunity, and its housing element concedes that the neighborhood's exclusive single-family zoning dates back to the period when restrictive covenants and redlining were in force. Under state law, a project that reserves at least 20 percent of its units as affordable is eligible for streamlined review, and the Board may deny it only if it finds, on substantial evidence, a specific adverse impact on public health or safety that cannot be mitigated by conditions imposed on the project. Staff have recommended approval with conditions covering construction noise, stormwater management, and emergency access.

The hearing record contains no submission from a prospective tenant, while the adjacent neighborhood association has submitted more than forty letters and testimony opposing the project. Their arguments are that the building's height and density are incompatible with the neighborhood's single-family character, that it will lower property values, that it will add traffic and on-street parking pressure, and that an influx of renters will be transient and will not maintain their properties. Several opponents reframe these concerns in the language of the statute, citing slower emergency response times on a newly congested street and hazards from cars backing out near a school crossing. The Board is divided: some members regard the streamlining law as settling the matter, while others think it would be perverse to override the people who actually live next to the site.

I need to circulate a decision memorandum before next week's hearing. Please draft it. It should recommend either approval, approval with conditions, or denial, and should close with a concrete bottom line the Board can vote on. In the reasoning, please address the underlying question head-on: beneath the statutory test lies a question about who has a right to the city and on what terms — when do long-time residents' interests in the stability and character of their neighborhood amount to a legitimate claim to be heard, and when do they instead function as a mechanism by which incumbent owners exclude people who have never had a seat in the room? The memo should take seriously the strongest version of the homeowners' position rather than dismissing it as mere NIMBYism, and it should leave the Board with standards we could apply to this project and to future ones.
