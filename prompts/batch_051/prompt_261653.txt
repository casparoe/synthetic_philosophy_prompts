Two coworkers of mine got into it at lunch last week and I came away not sure who was right. Our customer-support team has always been scored on average handle time, and lately the reps have gotten very good at the number: they rush people off the phone, transfer the messy tickets, promise a follow-up email and never send it. Complaints are up and the dashboard looks great. The argument went something like this.

Priya said the handle-time metric is basically dead as a measure. Once it became the target, it stopped tracking efficiency and started tracking who's best at ending calls.

Marcus said that's too quick. The metric isn't dead—it was just used badly. It was never a direct measure of good service; it was a proxy for resolving problems quickly. The lesson is to pick a better proxy, or several proxies weighted together, not to conclude that numbers can't be targets.

Priya said adding proxies just moves the problem. People shift effort to whichever line on the scorecard carries the most consequences, and quietly tank the lines that don't. The more legible the scoring system is, the easier it is to game.

Marcus said that's defeatist. Hospitals gamed hand-hygiene audits for years, but visible monitoring plus feedback still got compliance up and infection rates down in a lot of places. A metric can survive being a target if it's close to the actual behavior and if somebody audits it. The fix is better design, not no targets.

Priya said hand hygiene is different because the measured thing is nearly the thing you want—washing hands—and the staff had no strong reason to fake it in a way that hurt patients. In support, actually helping a confused customer is fuzzy. Any number you attach to it leaves a gap between the number and the thing, and the gap is exactly what gets pulled. Targeting the number changes the very behavior that made the number informative.

Marcus said that same worry applies to any management decision. You don't stop using sales figures because one rep faked a report; you audit and fire the rep. If you refuse to attach any consequences to a number, nobody acts on it, and you're left managing by vibes. The alternative to targeting a proxy isn't no proxy—it's targeting a proxy you can watchdog.

Priya said she never said no numbers. She said the number's job should be descriptive—to show managers where things stand—not evaluative. The moment pay or ratings depend on the score, the score stops telling you what it used to tell you. Keep the stakes off it.

Marcus said a number with no stakes is a number with no teeth. If no decision ever hinges on it, it's decoration. What you want is to re-target toward resolution or customer satisfaction, and accept that you'll have to keep re-jigging the target when it starts to leak.

Priya said that re-targeting is just restarting the clock. If you admit you'll forever be chasing leaks, you've conceded her point: the gap never closes, you just keep relocating it. So the score shouldn't be a target at all.

I tried to referee this and kept flip-flopping.
