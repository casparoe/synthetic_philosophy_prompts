I've spent a lot of time turning over an idea I've come to call tranquilism: that only the absence of craving and suffering matters when it comes to how well someone's life goes. I started out assuming this was too austere to take seriously, but it has made me notice an unevenness in my own reactions, and I can no longer tell whether the unevenness is a real distinction or just a prejudice.

Here is the asymmetry. Everyone I know treats the removal of chronic pain or settled distress as a plain good; if someone is suffering, we relieve it, and no one worries that the person has been made less themselves by the relief. But take a nearby case. Imagine someone with a years-long, unfulfillable longing, for a person or for a life that can no longer happen, and suppose there were a perfectly safe drug that did nothing but dissolve that one longing, leaving everything else intact. I find, and others I have asked find, that we recoil from this. It feels as though something essential to the person would be erased, even though pain relief changes a person too. Yet if only the absence of craving and suffering matters, the two cases look formally identical: in both, a negative state is being removed for its own sake, and the doctrine would call both good. So I want to know whether there is any principle that lets a person consistently welcome the first intervention and resist the second, or whether the resistance is best explained as attachment to the person we are used to.

I would like a committed answer, not a survey of options. Please give it as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Take a definite position on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or abandoned; state the strongest justification you can find for it; test that justification against both cases; and end with an honest verdict on whether it survives.
