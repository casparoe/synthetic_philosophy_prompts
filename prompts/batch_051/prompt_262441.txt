I have a standing rule I call my presumption of independence, and I want to know whether it is defensible or merely a bias dressed up as Bayesian hygiene. The rule is this: when two pieces of evidence point toward the same conclusion—two witnesses, two studies, two expert judgments—I ask whether I can tell a specific causal story about how one produced the other or how both trace back to a common source. If I cannot, I treat them as roughly independent and multiply their evidential force: two six-to-one findings become a thirty-six-to-one finding. Last week this meant updating hard when two acquaintances who dislike each other both told me the same surprising news about a mutual friend, because I could not construct any channel between them; only afterwards did I dimly register that both could have heard it from the same third person. I know that my failure to think of a common cause is a fact about my imagination, not about the world, and that copied sources, collusion, and shared background training are exactly what produce much apparent corroboration. But the alternative seems to be either refusing to combine corroborating evidence unless I have traced the whole causal graph, which would make ordinary belief-formation impossible, or applying an ad hoc discount whose size has no basis. So I have three questions. Is the rule defensible as a policy for a bounded reasoner, as opposed to a claim about how the sources are actually causally connected? If it is, what is the best version of it—should the discount for hidden dependence grow with how similar the sources look, with how surprising the claim is, or with the stakes? Is there a non-arbitrary default amount of doubt for unknown common causes, and are there conditions I could check in which treating sources as independent errs in a known direction rather than simply inflating my confidence? And from the inside, how do I tell when the rule is making me the sucker who counts two parrots as two witnesses, or the paranoid who treats every agreement as conspiracy?
