I hold a reconstructed Berkeleyan position, and I want to know how exposed it really is.

The position is this: the master argument should be read not as a point about mental imagery or about what can be pictured, but as a constraint on thinkable content. So read, it shows that a mind-independent material object is not something we have failed to observe but a content we cannot genuinely entertain. Any content is a content for a possible mind, and the expression an object existing with no possible relation to any mind tries to keep the represented object while subtracting the very standpoint from which it could be represented. Idealism, on this reading, is not a further conjecture about what the world is made of; it is what remains once the purported notion of unconceived material existence is discarded as pseudo-content. The position also makes a methodological claim: because the argument is conceptual or semantic rather than empirical, no future science could count against it. The only thing that could count against it is a successful account of how the offending content can be entertained after all.

Here is what I want: a map of the position's exposure, not a pros-and-cons survey. Please respond in the following sections, in this order.

1. The position, its commitments, and its vulnerable joints (about 150 words). Restate the position precisely and identify the two or three joints that bear the most load — for example, the move from "I cannot entertain this content" to "this content is incoherent," the assumption that representation of an object requires representing it from the standpoint of a possible mind, and the slide from a claim about my capacities to a claim about what there could be. Also say what the position rules out that nearby positions permit, such as a residual notion of matter kept as an explanatory posit.

2. Candidate defeaters, ranked from decisive to merely awkward (about 450 words). For each, say what would have to be true for it to land, what it would establish if it did land, whether it would refute the whole idealism or only my semantic reading of the master argument, and how decisive it would be. Address at least: the act/object distinction or a contemporary analogue; the objection that the argument proves too much, because it would make unthought truths, or the past before finite minds, equally inconceivable; and the scope or de dicto/de re objection — roughly, "there is no x such that I think of x as unthought" does not entail "I think that there is no x such that x is unthought."

3. What a defender should concede counts against the position (about 200 words). Not what critics say, but what I, as a defender, should admit would damage the position if shown: which formulations I should abandon, which auxiliary claims are expendable without abandoning idealism, and which concessions would leave the semantic reading looking merely verbal.

4. Insulation check (about 200 words). Say whether this position has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that is a problem for a conceptual or semantic thesis in metaphysics. Distinguish insulation from merely empirical defeat from insulation from any rational defeat, and state which kind the position exhibits.

5. Bottom line and what would move me most (about 150 words). Give an honest assessment of the position's overall exposure. Then say what single development — an argumentative result, a credible case, or a distinction shown to be unavailable — would personally move me most, given that I find semantic arguments powerful and am unimpressed by appeals to common sense or scientific practice.
