In class we have been studying justifications for punishment, and I keep thinking about the retributivist argument for the death penalty. As I understand it, that argument's force depends on the premise that the person executed really is guilty of the murder for which the penalty is imposed, since retribution is supposed to give a wrongdoer what he or she deserves, not what it would be useful for the state to do. A specific development now seems to challenge that premise directly: post-conviction DNA testing has exonerated a number of people who had already been sentenced to death, so the execution of the innocent no longer looks like a purely hypothetical risk but like a documented, recurring feature of capital punishment as actually practiced. I am interested in the fate of the argument, not in any policy recommendation. Does the retributivist argument survive this development? Which premise does the development bear on? Can the argument be repaired, for example, by demanding a much higher standard of proof or by restricting the penalty to cases of forensic near-certainty, or would such repairs stop being retributive in the original sense? Or is the right conclusion that the argument justified only an abstract principle of desert and never any real institution, and if it has to be restated in that purely ideal form, has something important been lost?
