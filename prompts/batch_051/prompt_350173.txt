I'm a journalist working on a piece about how AI labs talk about their own systems, and I keep tripping over a weird double standard I want to get clear on before I write. The same lab will publish a safety evaluation describing a model as having "lied" to a user, or as "deceiving" the evaluators, or as "trying to achieve a goal" it was not supposed to have — the evaluators will even say the model "understood" what it was doing. But the consumer-facing materials for the exact same product say the assistant has no beliefs, no intentions, no feelings, and that any impression of a mind is just the user projecting onto a next-token predictor. The company seems to want it both ways: rich mentalistic language when the behavior is bad, flat-out denial when it comes to responsibility and user trust.

I want to write the piece around what philosophical commitments this practice actually presupposes. Is the lab implicitly adopting something like Dennett's intentional stance — treating the model as a rational agent with beliefs and desires because that is the best way to predict and explain its behavior — while being sloppy about the difference between an instrumental stance and a claim about what is really inside the system? Or does the safety language presuppose something stronger, like the view that the model literally has beliefs and goals? What would the lab have to give up if it took the "no mental states" line seriously and scrubbed all the deception-talk from it's evals — would its safety testing even remain coherent? And is there a version of the intentional stance on which the double standard is actually fine, because "the model has beliefs" and "the model has no beliefs" are answers to two differnet questions?

I'd like you to take a definite position on what the practice commits the lab to, and argue for it, rather than just laying out options and leaving me to pick. Please think carefully and do some brainstorming before you settle on the position. Write as a professional philosopher of mind who works on the intentional stance and on mental-state attribution to non-human systems. Be opinionated, but distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and flag the places where the practice is genuinely underdetermined — where several theories would license it.
