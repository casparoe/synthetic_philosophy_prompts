In a midterm examination for a course in classical Chinese philosophy, students were given the following question:

"Explain the disagreement between the Mohist doctrine of impartial care (jian'ai, sometimes translated 'universal love') and the Confucian doctrine of graded love, according to which one properly cares more for those closer to oneself, especially one's family. Then evaluate the strongest Mohist reply to the Confucian charge that impartial care is unfilial. Does impartial care abolish filial piety, or does it reinterpret it? Be specific about the point at which the two positions genuinely come apart."

A student submitted this answer:

"Impartial care is often misread as a demand to treat everyone identically. It is better understood as a demand for equal concern: the welfare of a stranger must count no less than the welfare of one's own parent. Equal concern does not require identical treatment, because parents and strangers stand in different practical relations to the agent. When needs and capacities are otherwise equal, one may attend to one's own elderly father rather than a stranger's, since one is the person best situated to know and meet his needs. Filial behavior therefore survives under impartial care, but it is redefined as a set of role-based responsibilities rather than as the expression of a special concern owed only to one's own kin.

"Mozi's central argument is best reconstructed as a proto-utilitarian argument. Partiality, he contends, is the root of the world's disorder: thieves steal because they care more about their own gain than about others' property, and rulers invade because they care more about their own state than about neighboring ones. If impartial care replaced these partial attitudes, people would not harm one another for private advantage, and the result would be mutual security for everyone. But why should anyone already in a position of advantage adopt impartial care? Mozi answers that the advantages are reciprocal: the person who cares for the parents of others thereby prompts others to care for their own parents. The doctrine is therefore justified because, when universally adopted, it maximizes aggregate benefit — the total welfare produced by impartiality exceeds that produced by graded love.

"The Confucian position, developed most fully by Mencius, is grounded in human nature. Mencius holds that we naturally love our own more than we love strangers, and that the moral life consists in extending this natural love outward from the family. The child-at-the-well example is sometimes read as evidence of impartial benevolence toward any human being, but its real point is that even universal compassion is exercised on the particular case before one and grows out of the same natural equipment as family love. Because the intensity of this feeling fades with distance, the proper degree of care also fades: one ought to care more for one's parents than for strangers. This is not a concession to human weakness but the very pattern of ren. On this view, Mohist impartial care is not a higher moral achievement but a distortion: by making one's father interchangeable with any other man's father, it cuts off the root from which all genuine care grows.

"The Confucian argument nevertheless has a flaw. Even if human beings naturally favor their own, natural partiality cannot by itself justify graded love; that would be an unwarranted slide from 'we do' to 'we ought.' Indeed, the existence of natural partiality is precisely the problem impartial care is meant to solve: the thief and the invading ruler are acting on natural partiality. The charge of unfiliality also fails. Mozi is not saying that one should abandon one's parents; he is saying that the reason for caring for them cannot be the thought that they are mine. If a Confucian asks why I should care for my parents in the first place, the Mohist can answer: because their welfare matters as much as anyone else's, and as their child I am the person best placed to do something about it. Filial piety is therefore reinterpreted, not abolished, and the reinterpretation avoids the partiality that breeds social division. The Mohist position is the stronger one."
