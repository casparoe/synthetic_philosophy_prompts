In §52 of the first volume of The World as Will and Representation (translated by Haldane and Kemp as The World as Will and Idea), Schopenhauer makes two claims about music that can appear to pull in opposite directions. On one side, he singles music out from the other arts: whereas the other arts present the Platonic Ideas, music is a copy of the will itself, the inner essence of the world, and thus stands closer to the thing in itself than any other form of representation. On the other side, he insists that music never depicts any particular pain, pleasure, or event; it gives only the universal forms of feeling, the will in its essential modes without their phenomenal clothing, so that music is at once the most powerful and the least representational of all arts. Are these two descriptions in tension? Can music be both the most direct revelation of the will and the most abstracted from particular acts of willing? Consider possible reconciliations: the will as thing in itself may be universal rather than particular; music may copy the will in its fundamental currents rather than its individual manifestations; or Schopenhauer may be attributing to music a unique non-pictorial universality that belongs only to the will. Assess whether one of these readings is honest to the text or whether the tension points to a genuine instability in his aesthetic theory, and say which reconciliation requires the least special pleading.
