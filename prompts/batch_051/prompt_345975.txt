I've been arguing with my flatmate about something and reached for a distinction that I now use all the time, and I want someone to check whether it actually holds up. We were talking about one of those classic examples, the low overpasses on Long Island that supposedly kept buses — and therefore mostly poor and Black people — away from the beaches; the builder didn't have to write "whites only" anywhere, the concrete did it. My flatmate kept saying that's overrated: a bridge doesn't discriminate, the surrounding transit system does, and you could get a bus through if it were small enough. So I started drawing a line between two ways an artifact can be political, which I've been calling "inscribed" and "acquired" politics. Inscribed politics is when the political outcome is designed-in and survives even when the artifact is lifted out of its context: the low clearance does it's filtering no matter who built it or why. Acquired politics is when the same artifact is only doing political work because of surrounding practices and expectations — like a park bench that becomes a moral panic about homelessness once people start sleeping on it, or a group chat that becomes an organizing tool during a walkout. That part feels right, and I've had about four different people tell me I've reinvented something from philosophy or STS; I can't remember the names, but if it rings a bell, tell me what it actually is.

But the claim I actually want checked is bigger. I've started thinking almost every interesting case is neither purely inscribed nor purely acquired, but a kind of drift where acquired politics slowly gets mistaken for inscribed politics, because once an artifact is used politically for long enough the temptation to blame the thing itself gets hard to resist. I've been calling this "political sedimentation": first the use, then the moral reading of the use, and finally the engineering choice that made the use possible is retroactively read as if it had been a deliberate, values-laden decision all along. So the reason everyone latches onto the Long Island bridges story isn't that the original intention was proven — it's that the story gives the bridges a clear political meaning after the fact, and the more the story is retold, the more the bridges look like proof that designers encoded racism in poured concrete. My question: is this distinction coherent, and does "sedimentation" do any real explanatory work, or am I just describing the obvious fact that people tell stories about objects? Reconstruct the strongest version of this, and then tell me exactly where it falls apart, or where someone who knows the existing debates would say I'm fooling myself.
