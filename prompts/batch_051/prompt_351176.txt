I'm a retired geochemist who's been reading philosophy seriously for about two years now, mostly with a small reading group at the public library. Last week we read Hume's essay Of the Standard of Taste, and two members of the group got into a long argument afterward that I can't quite untangle. I'll report it as best I remember it, and I'd welcome a careful adjudication.

Alan started by saying he'd decided not to read a novel that two critics he trusts both called "elegant but empty," and Maya said he was being unreasonable because you can't rationally dislike a work you haven't experienced. Alan said that's exactly what critics are for, and he pulled up this passage from Hume:

"Every voice is united in applauding elegance, propriety, simplicity, spirit in writing; and in blaming fustian, affectation, coldness, and a false brilliant: But when critics come to particulars, this seeming unanimity vanishes; and it is found, that they had affixed a very different meaning to their expressions."

Alan argued that this passage shows aesthetic vocabulary is stable enough for testimony to work, because "every voice" can agree on the general terms; the fact that application is hard is just why we need good critics. Maya said he'd got the passage backwards: the whole point is that the apparent agreement evaporates at the level of particulars, so when a critic says "elegant," you have no idea whether you'd share the response, and you're just deferring to someone else's sensibility. Alan pushed back that Hume definately goes on to defend true judges and a standard of taste, so he isn't a skeptic about aesthetic evaluation — the reasonable position is to find a critic whose track record impresses you and defer. Maya replied that this just pushes the problem back: without already having taste, you can't identify the good critics, so aesthetic testimony can't bootstrap itself. Alan then said we identify scientific experts by training, peer recognition, and track record without being able to check their results ourselves, and aesthetic experts are no worse off. Maya answered that there's an asymmetry: when I believe a scientist, I'm forming a belief about a mind-independent fact, but an aesthetic judgment is supposed to be about the value of an experience, so no one else's report can substitute for the experience itself.

I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher working on the epistemology of aesthetics. Please start by brainstorming the possible readings of the Hume passage and the dialectical roles each side is putting it to, then go through the exchange move by move. For each move, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a brief justification, and finish by saying who is more right overall and what, if anything, both of them are missing.
