I have spent a lot of time in online discussion spaces — including the kind of forum where people argue at length about moderation, norms, and what makes a conversation legitimate rather than merely persuasive. In those debates, Habermas's idea of communicative rationality and the ideal speech situation keeps being invoked: the thought that a norm or conclusion is rationally acceptable only if it could win the agreement of participants in a discussion from which coercion and systematic distortion have been excluded, leaving nothing but the pressure exerted by the better argument itself. I find this an attractive standard, but when I try to apply it to concrete choices about moderation, workplace speech, algorithmic ranking, and social sanctions, I run into a basic difficulty. I do not actually have a working definition of coercion that lets me determine which features of a real discussion are disqualifying and which are benign.

I would like you to propose a definition of coercion — or, if you think the concept splits into more than one sense, a small set of definitions — that decides the following cases in a principled way. I take these to be clear cases of coercion: (a) someone threatens physical harm unless you endorse a claim; (b) someone threatens to reveal damaging personal information unless you publicly retract a claim you believe. I take these to be clear cases of non-coercion: (c) a valid argument, honestly presented, that changes your mind; (d) a colleague reminding you of evidence you had forgotten. But I also need a definition that handles the hard cases: (e) a moderator deletes repeated off-topic interjections, with the result that one side of a dispute loses its most active advocate; (f) an engagement-based ranking algorithm that shows one side's posts to far more readers than the other's, though nothing is deleted; (g) an employer's rule that employees may not publicly discuss certain subjects, backed by the risk of being fired; (h) a friend group that grows cold and distant toward someone who defends an unpopular view, without anyone making a threat; (i) a rhetorician who uses true but emotionally overwhelming stories until an audience is too exhausted to scrutinize the argument; (j) an offer of payment for publicly endorsing a conclusion, with no penalty for refusing; (k) a strict time limit in a debate that prevents a participant from finishing a complex technical argument; (l) a partner's sustained and visible distress when you seriously entertain a view that would, if adopted, change your shared plans, even though they explicitly say they will not stop you.

Please run your proposed definition against each case, flag where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts. If you think coercion has a descriptive sense and a normative sense — roughly, what in fact removes a resulting agreement from justification, versus what makes the agreement illegitimate on independent grounds — give me both and explain how the hard cases move between them. Some of the cases may feel more like distortion or unequal participation than coercion; if you think that distinction matters for Habermas's standard, say so.
