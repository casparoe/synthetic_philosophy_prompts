I'm putting together a short unit on philosophy of linguistics, and a debate came up that I keep going back and forth on with a colleague. The claim is that when a four-year-old starts saying "goed" instead of "went," this shows the child has acquired a productive past-tense rule (add -ed), and that the earlier correct "went" was just a memorized suppletive form temporarily losing a competition with the rule. My skepticism is that an analogy engine — something that stores lots of heard forms and extends the most common pattern to new cases — would produce the exact same behavior without ever "really" representing a rule, so the disagreement might just be about which metaphor we prefer.

So here's the task. Design the best test you can of the conceptual claim that producing a regular past tense for a novel verb (like "wug" -> "wugged") is evidence that the speaker has a productive rule, as oposed to running pattern-association over a stored lexicon. Be concrete: what do you show speakers, what responses do you count, and what pattern of results would push you toward "real rule" versus "sophisticated analogy"? Then say exactly what a positive result would and would not settle — what could a determined skeptic still say, and what part of the question is untestable even in principle? Feel free to bring in irregulars, suppletive forms, near-regulars, or gradient acceptability judgments if they help sharpen the test.
