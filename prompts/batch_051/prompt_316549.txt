I've been circlin around a variation on Galileo's falling-bodies argument and I'd like a philosopher's take. You know the original: suppose heavier things fall faster; tie a light body to a heavy one. The pair is heavier than the heavy one, so it ought to fall faster than the heavy one alone; but the light one ought to drag the heavy one, so the pair ought to fall slower. Contradiction, so the premise was wrong. Now change one thing: run it in honey, using same-size steel and glass spheres. The steel sphere settles faster in honey—that's just gravity, buoyancy, and drag—and nobody disputes it. Yet the exact two-step argument still seems to tell us that a heavier thing can't fall faster. So something must be built into the original premise that the honey version doesn't have. What is it? Is the original only working because it idealizes speed as a function of weight alone, with no medium or shape in play? And what should that do to our confidence that a thought experiment like this yields knowledge on its own, rather than just displaying what we packed into the assumptions? I'd like you to separate what's settled about the standard reading of Galileo from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this, and not present a contested interpretation as the consensus.
