ok so we read Alan Turing’s “Computing Machinery and Intelligence” in my philosophy of tech class and I’m kind of stuck on his section about learning machines. He says you shouldn’t try to programme the adult mind directly, and that instead you should make a child-machine with “rather little mechanism, and lots of blank sheets.” The idea is basically that you build something simple and unformed, then teach it like you would teach a child, with punishments and rewards and all that, and that’s supposed to be a better path to machine intelligence than trying to hand-code everything an adult knows.

But now we have LLMs. They aren’t built as blank notebooks that make mistakes and get corrected by a teacher. They’re trained on basically the whole internet by next-token prediction, and then afterwards they get tuned with RLHF or whatever. So my question is: does Turing’s child-machine argument actually survive that development? Like if a modern LLM passes the Turing test, is that a point in favour of his child-machine idea, or does it show he was wrong about the blank sheets part being important? Could the pretraining on human text just be a weird way of filling in the blank sheets, or is that too much of a stretch?

Also, does RLHF count as “education” in Turing’s sense, or is it more like training an animal with treats? I dont want to just write “Turing was half right,” so I’m trying to figure out exactly what his argument needs to be true to still work when the machine isn’t educated from near-scratch but trained on a giant store of already-produced language.
