I've been going back and forth with my roommate about whether the Mytilenean debate is a point for or against Athenian democracy. You probably know the outline: in 427 BC, Mytilene revolted from the Delian League, Athens crushed the revolt, and the assembly initially voted to kill all the adult men and enslave the women and children. Overnight a whole lot of them got cold feet, so they reopened the question, heard Cleon argue that a democracy running an empire can't afford to be soft or to keep relitigating its own decisions, and heard Diodotus argue, mostly on grounds that deterrence wouldn't work anyway, that killing them isn't even in Athens' self-interest. The assembly narrowly reversed itself, and a second trireme barely arrived in time to stop the massacre. People cite this as evidence that democratic deliberation works: second thoughts were better thoughts, the assembly corrected itself. I'm skeptical that the deliberative-democracy reading is as clean as people pretend, since Diodotus explicitly sets justice aside and the reversal might've been mood and fear as much as reason.

What I'd like is a careful analysis of the episode through the lens of deliberative democratic theory. Lay out the core commitments — reason-giving rather than coercion or status, revisability of decisions, inclusion of those affected, and the notion that the better argument eventually carries it — and then map them onto the case: who actually had standing as a deliberator in 427, what kind of reasons Cleon and Diodotus gave, and what the overnight reversal does and doesn't show. Be honest about where the theory illuminates the episode, where it misdescribes what was going on, and where it's just silent. And then tell me whether Mytilene ends up as evidence for deliberative democrats, an embarrassment to them, or a case their criteria were never designed to handle — a slave-holding, imperial, all-male assembly deciding mass killing in an afternoon. I care less about the overall verdict than about the mapping being disciplined.
