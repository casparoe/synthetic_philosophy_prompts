I want help evaluating an argument about convention, norms, and obligation. It runs as follows.

(1) On a broadly Lewisian account, a convention is a regularity R in a population that solves a recurring coordination problem: each person follows R because each expects the others to follow R and each prefers to follow R given that the others do; and there is always at least one alternative regularity R' such that, had R' been the established expectation instead, people would likewise have preferred to follow R'.

(2) Many everyday social norms — how one queues, the forms greetings take, how gifts are timed and reciprocated — plausibly arose and persist through a process with exactly this structure, with salience, precedent, and imitation selecting among equilibria rather than any judgment that one regularity is better than the alternatives.

(3) So the content of such norms is arbitrary in the Lewisian sense: it depends on historical accident, because a different history could have installed a different regularity that would have done the same coordinating work.

(4) But a rule whose content is arbitrary in the relevant sense — historical accident rather than reasons — cannot by itself generate a genuine obligation.

(5) Therefore, evolved conventional norms of this kind generate no genuine obligations.

(6) Hence, so long as violating such a norm is cheap, one may violate it without thereby acting wrongly or showing a defect of character.

Is this argument valid? If it is not, tell me exactly which step fails, and what the corrected version of that step would have to say for the argument to go through. I half-suspect that the word arbitrary is doing two jobs, but I cannot make the slide precise, so if that is the diagnosis, distinguish the two senses and show where each is needed. If the argument is actually sound, say so and explain why the conclusion, however unwelcome, follows.

Please finish by saying how confident you are in your diagnosis, and by singling out the point in your answer that you are least sure of.
