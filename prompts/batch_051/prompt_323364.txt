Lately I've been thinking about dream skepticism through Zhuangzi's butterfly passage. The way its usually used in epistemology classes, the argument is something like: from inside an experience nothing marks it as waking or dreamed, and a dream can feel just as complete and convincing as waking life, so "I'm awake right now" can't be justified by the way things feel to me. The symmetry between dreaming and waking is the part doing all the work.

But I've also been reading about dream research, and it seems to break the symmetry from one side. Dreams are mostly less coherent than waking experience — writing won't stay stable when you look away and back, scene changes don't fit together, people you know look off — and lucid dreamers can apparently pick up on exactly this kind of cue and correctly realize they're dreaming while they're still inside the dream. Suppose we grant that these findings are roughly right. Does Zhuangzi-style dream skepticism survive them? I can see that the skeptic only needs *some* dreams to be indistinguishable, not all of them, but then the argument starts to look weaker than the version I started with — maybe it only gets you "you could be dreaming right now" as a bare possibility rather than a genuine epistemic threat. I'm trying to work out which premise actually breaks, whether the argument can be repaired by falling back to "I can't rule out that this is one of the rare seamless dreams," and whether a skeptical argument that survives in that form is still interesting or just idle. Mostly I want to know whether lucid dreaming is a real problem for the butterfly argument or weather it only changes the examples without touching the structure.
