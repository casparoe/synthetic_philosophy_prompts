Ok so I've gone down a rabbit hole in Plato's Laws, the part where the Athenian Stranger is talking about getting the whole city systematically drunk, and there's a reconstruction I keep coming back to that seems to prove way too much, and I can't decide where it collapses. Quick background so we're on the same page: in the Laws the Athenian defends organized drinking parties — older men, a sober person in charge, the whole thing run as a civic institution, not a free-for-all — on the grounds that wine makes you feel bolder and more shameless and less in control of your pleasures and pains, so a supervised drinking party is a low-stakes way to experience that loss of control, watch it happen in yourself and each other, and practice pulling back. He treats it as a sort of cheap, safe test and training for temperance, safer than waiting until real opportunities for self-indulgence show up.

But then:

(1) If controlled exposure to a temptation is a good way to train the virtue that resists that temptation, then this should work for whatever temptation you plug in.
(2) The drinking party just is controlled exposure to the temptation of shameless self-indulgence, and Plato thinks it trains temperance.
(3) So Plato should also endorse controlled exposure to other temptations: supervised exercises in theft, lying, bribe-taking, maybe staged situations that invite cowardice or cruelty, all designed to let citizens practice the matching virtues in a low-stakes setting.
(4) But Plato would find that monstrous — and the Laws is largely one long attempt to design a city that keeps citizens away from bad influences, censor the poets, and minimize the occasions of vice.
(5) Therefore either the defense of drinking proves too much, or there is some principled thing about temperance (or about drunkenness) that blocks the generalization.
(6) But the obvious blockers look ad hoc: you can't say 'injustice harms people' because the staged version could be set up to hurt nobody; you can't say 'wine is physical' because seeing a bribe or an unguarded purse is physical too; and you can't say 'drinking is traditional' because that's not an argument Plato would accept.

I'd really like to keep premise (2) — I think the drinking-party proposal is less crazy then it sounds, and I don't want the answer to just be 'Plato was wrong about that.' But I also don't want premise (1) to be false in a way that makes temperance a magical special case with no explanation. So which step do I actually reject, and what do I pay for rejecting it? And if the right move is to say the drinking party isn't really 'controlled exposure to temptation' in the sense premise (1) needs, then what is it instead — what does it actually do, in the Laws' own terms, that a supervised shoplifting club wouldn't?
