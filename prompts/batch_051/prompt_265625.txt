I'm a novelist and my contract for the next book has a clause that's been bugging me. It says the publisher may terminate if, in their reasonable opinion, the Author 'says or does anything which brings, or is reasonably likely to bring, the Author or the Work into public disrepute.' I know what they were thinking of—arrests, drunken tweets, abuse allegations, that sort of thing. But here's my case. For years I've published short stories under a pen name: first-person monologues in the voices of characters with genuinely hateful views. The stories are fiction and don't endorse the characters; that's the whole point, they're studies of self-justification. A journalist has worked out the pseudonym is me and is about to run a piece headed something like 'Prizewinning novelist's hate-fiction secret.' Nothing I've done under my own name, no crime, no allegation. Does the clause reach me? I'm less interested in the practical answer than in what the different ways of reading the clause—plain wording, what the person who drafted it would have said if I'd asked at signing, the clause's purpose, what a reasonable reader would take it to mean—each give. And is there a reading of 'public disrepute' that doesn't quietly smuggle in a moral standard while pretending to be just descriptive? A lawyer mate says its purely factual reputation; another says you can't judge disrepute without first deciding what sort of disapproval counts. They can't both be right, can they?
