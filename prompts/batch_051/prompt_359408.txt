A theologian I know defends God's hiddenness by arguing that it is a condition of genuine relationship rather than evidence against a loving God. His idea is that if God's existence, goodness, and power were as obvious as the sun, belief would be compelled rather than freely given, and a compelled belief could not ground the kind of trust or love theistic traditions say God seeks from us. On this view, the silence many people experience is what preserves the possibility of a free response rather than a failure of divine love.

What interests me is that this argument is a hybrid. One part looks like an empirical psychological claim: that overwhelming evidence of another's presence or benevolence tends to force belief and undermines the genuineness of whatever relationship follows. The other part looks theological or a priori: a claim about what makes a response to God genuine and what a perfectly loving being would permit. I want to know how much of the first part can genuinely be tested.

Design the strongest feasible test of the psychological claim, using human relationships or other proxy scenarios. Be concrete about the conditions, what to measure, and what outcome would count in favour of the claim versus against it. Then say exactly what a positive result would and would not settle about the larger argument for divine hiddenness: could the test bear on the theological conclusion in principle, or does that part remain untouched no matter how the psychology comes out?
