One of the strongest students in my graduate seminar on weakness of will has been pressing an argument I want to think through carefully before I respond. He appeals to Wang Yangming's doctrine — the slogan usually rendered in English as "knowledge and action are one" — to argue that akrasia, as philosophers ordinarily conceive it, is a misdescription rather than a genuine phenomenon. If a person says, "I knew it was wrong and did it anyway," the slogan, as he wields it, tells us the correct diagnosis is that the person did not genuinely know it was wrong: a piece of "knowledge" that never issues in action was never real knowledge in the first place, only a borrowed phrase or an idle profession. The slogan is doing a great deal of work in this inference, and I am not sure it can carry it.

I know the slogan is standardly associated with Wang Yangming, and I have a rough sense of the surrounding view: that knowing and acting are not two separable stages, that sincere moral knowledge is already action-involving, and that failure to act can be evidence that the knowledge was not fully present. But "knowledge and action are one" seems to me compressible into several quite different theses — an identity claim, a claim about the proper criterion or test of knowledge, a claim about the dispositional structure of knowledge, a claim distinguishing genuine knowledge from merely verbal profession, and perhaps others. Please lay the readings out as a menu rather than selecting one. For each reading, state the precise thesis in one sentence, assess whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and — the point that matters for my student — say whether it actually licenses the conclusion that akrasia is confused. Please also separate what is settled in the scholarly understanding of the doctrine from what is genuinely contested among specialists, and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.

End with a table summarizing the menu. Use these columns: (1) "Reading" — a short label for the precisification; (2) "Precise claim" — the thesis in one sentence; (3) "Verdict" — trivial, substantive, false, or contested; (4) "Supports the anti-akrasia conclusion?" — yes, no, or only with further premises; (5) "Specialist standing" — settled or contested among those who work on the tradition.
