Philosophers who work on concepts often divide into two camps. The first treats conceptual analysis as a descriptive enterprise: to analyse consent, addiction, algorithmic fairness, or race is to capture the concept we already have, as fixed by ordinary and expert usage. The second camp, associated with conceptual engineering and ameliorative analysis, denies that existing usage deserves that authority. It asks what concept we should have, given the work the concept must do in moral, legal, or scientific contexts; analysis becomes a proposal for revision rather than a report on current practice. Critics object that the revisionist has changed the subject: if the proposal is answerable to our purposes rather than to our usage, it is not an analysis of the original concept at all, and calling it an improvement on that concept is empty.

Please argue that this dispute rests on an untenable picture of what fixes a concept. Defend the position that for any interesting contested concept there is no unique object—the concept as it actually exists—that could decide whether a proposed analysis is descriptive or revisionary, because actual usage is too fragmented, context-sensitive, and evaluatively loaded to determine a single target. Develop the argument in detail for one concept of your choosing (addiction, consent, algorithmic fairness, intelligence, or hate speech, for instance). Address the strongest objection to your position: if there is no stable target, then the distinction between analyzing and replacing a concept collapses, and conceptual engineering seems to reduce to verbal legislation with no explanatory or critical purchase. Explain why conceptual engineering retains critical force even without a determinate pre-existing concept, rather than merely insisting that it is useful.
