A republic's constitution, framed before the introduction of direct legislation, contains the following provision:

"Article 104. The Supreme Court shall have power to declare void any law of the National Parliament which is inconsistent with this Constitution. No court shall exercise this power in respect of any measure other than a law of the National Parliament."

Twelve years after adoption, the constitution is amended to add Article 67A, under which seven per cent of the registered electorate may place a proposed law directly on the ballot; if approved by a majority of votes cast, it takes effect "as law" without any vote, amendment, or assent by the National Parliament. Article 67A is silent on judicial review. Voters then approve the Public Order (Online Speech) Act, a detailed statute restricting certain categories of online publication. The government concedes, for purposes of argument, that some of its provisions violate the free expression clause of the constitution. It argues, however, that the Supreme Court lacks jurisdiction to declare the Act void: Article 104 reaches only a "law of the National Parliament," and this measure is a law of the people. The challengers reply that Article 104 is the constitution's chosen mechanism for keeping all lawmaking within constitutional bounds, and that "law of the National Parliament" was the drafting generation's way of saying "validly enacted statute"; a reading that lets constitutional rights be defeated by changing the source or label of the law would defeat the clause's purpose.

Assess the case under four approaches, giving a separate paragraph to each: (1) the plain text and ordinary usage of Article 104 at the time of adoption; (2) the purpose of the judicial review power within the constitutional order; (3) what the drafters of Article 104 would most likely have said if asked specifically whether the power should reach laws enacted by popular initiative; and (4) what a reasonable reader of the constitution today would take Article 104 to mean. Then state which approach should govern in this republic's courts, and whether the Supreme Court may declare the challenged Act void under that approach. Finally, connect the holding to the general problem of unelected judges reviewing laws enacted by more directly democratic means: does the popular origin of a law make any principled difference to the case for judicial review?

\answer{<the approach you defend>: <your holding in a short phrase>}

and no text after it.
