I have ended up holding a deflationary view of heritability coefficients, mostly from reading popular genetics and from arguments about IQ and educational attainment. My view is that a heritability estimate is a statistic about variation, not about individuals, and not about what is possible or changeable. It is, as I understand it, a measure of how much of the spread in a trait within one population in one set of environments is tied to genetic differences among its members. So the number cannot tell you how much of any particular person's height or IQ came from their genes; it cannot tell you whether a trait could be shifted by improving nutrition, schooling, or policy; and it cannot tell you whether two groups differ for genetic reasons, even when the trait is highly heritable within each group. I accept that a highly heritable trait can be changed substantially by the environment, and that two groups can differ for entirely environmental reasons on a trait that is strongly heritable within each. Height is my clearest example: it is reported as roughly four-fifths heritable, yet average height rose steeply in many countries when nutrition and disease control improved. To me, that shows the heritability number and the room for environmental change are answering different questions. My working position is that inferences like "if it is heritable, group differences are probably genetic" or "if it is heritable, trying to change it is futile" are category errors.

I want to know what it would actually take for this position to be wrong or seriously incomplete. Which of my commitments carry the real weight, and which could fail while the rest survive? What would the world have to look like for a heritability estimate to license the stronger inferences I have been refusing—inferences about individual causation, about inevitable group differences, or about the futility of environmental intervention? Is there a realistic or even merely possible situation in which a high within-group heritability does increase the pressure to take a between-group difference as partly genetic, even if it does not settle it? Play it straight: distinguish evidence that would be decisive from evidence that would only be awkward, and do not just report what critics of my view say—I want to know what I myself should recognize as counting against it. Finally, tell me honestly whether my view is so heavily qualified by "one population, one environment" that nothing could count against it in practice. If it is insulated in that way, I want that verdict stated plainly, along with whether the insulation matters or whether it is simply the correct way to use a variance statistic.
