I'm a retired toxicologist — spent thirty years inside null hypothesis significance testing and risk assessment, no-observed-adverse-effect levels, false positives, false negatives, all that. Since I retired I've been reading philosophy of science, and the precautionary principle keeps niggling at me. The standard gloss, if I can paraphrase it: when an activity threatens serious or irreversible harm, lack of full scientific certainty shouldn't be a reason to postpone protective measures. The stronger versions go further and say the burden of proof should sit with the people who want to introduce the activity, not with the people who worry about it.

I keep wanting to render this in the vocabulary I actually trust, which is statistical decision theory: Type I and Type II errors, loss functions whose assymetry you set explicitly, priors over worst cases, expected utility, stopping rules. What I want is a careful translation, not the slogan. Restate the precautionary principle in that vocabulary as faithfully as you can — the version that would actually change what a regulator does. Then give me the residue report. Which parts map cleanly onto something like weighing false negatives much more heavily than false positives? Which parts did you have to warp or quietly reinterpret to get them to fit? And is there anything in the principle — irreversibility, the burden-of-proof claim, uncertainty that isn't just a probability distribution you haven't measured yet — that has no real counterpart in my vocabulary? Please don't cheat by assuming decision theory is the correct frame and then treating anything that doesn't fit as confused; I want to know where the translation is doing the work. At the end, tell me whether the precautionary principle is basically a claim about the shape of the loss function, or whether there's a leftover. If there is a leftover, is that because my vocabulary is too cramped, or because the principle is less precise than its defenders make it sound?
