I am a third-year undergraduate currently taking a philosophy course called "Normativity and Practical Reason," and I would like help adjudicating an argument between two classmates, Elena and Marcus, that began in our seminar on reasons and normativity. Please act as a professional philosopher specializing in normative theory and the metaphysics of reasons, think through the dispute carefully, and begin by mapping out the distinct claims at issue before you deliver a verdict.

Our lecturer framed the question this way: can the normative force of a consideration survive without anyone whose consideration it is, or must every reason ultimately be a reason-for-somebody? She called the first view a belief in "free-floating rationales"—reasons without reasoners. Elena said that phrase was almost certainly a name for a mistake. Marcus said it was a name for something we are already committed to. Their exchange went roughly as follows.

Elena said a reason is a candidate for deliberation. For something to count as a reason, it has to be the sort of thing that could make some agent's action or attitude intelligible as a response to it. So every reason is a reason for some actual or possible agent in a choice situation. A "reason" with no reasoner would be an ought with no one obligated and a count-in-favour with nobody counting—she called that a category mistake.

Marcus answered that this was too psychological. We already accept that people can be wrong about their reasons: someone may have a reason to see a doctor that they ignore or never recognize. Once reasons are not fixed by the agent's own take on them, they are fixed by the facts. And facts do not need to be apprehended in order to be facts. Suppose a coastal plant species was destroyed before anyone could have known that it contained a compound useful in treating a serious disease. There was, Marcus said, a reason not to destroy it even though no one then was in a position to act on it. That reason was true of the world but not owned by any mind in the world. So, he concluded, reasons can outrun reasoners.

Elena replied that the doctor case is still a reason for the person, shaped by facts about that person—their illness, their capacity to seek treatment. The plant case, she said, smuggles in later knowledge: we look back and say "there was a reason" only from our present standpoint. What the case really contains is a fact that made an outcome bad, plus a standing reason that we now have to regret or prevent such losses. The reason itself is still someone's reason—ours.

Marcus pushed back. If there had been no later people at all, would the people who destroyed the last stand of the plant have had no reason not to do so? That cannot be right. And consider a choice between two futures—one with billions of flourishing lives and one with none—considered before any person exists. If no agent is around to have a reason, does Elena's view say neither future is favoured by any reason whatsoever? That makes normativity hostage to whoever happens to exist. Marcus said he would rather say there are reasons out there, including reasons that have not yet found an agent—which is exactly what calling them free-floating captures.

Elena responded that in the future case, if no one is choosing among futures, there is no practical question, so no practical reason is needed. One can say the flourishing future is better; that is an evaluation. But "better" is not itself a reason until it is tied to some agent's capacity to promote or respect it. Collapsing axiology into normativity, she said, is what creates the illusion of a reason without someone to have it.

Marcus answered that this makes goodness strangely inert. If a future is better, surely any rational agent who could choose among futures would have reason to choose it. The fact that this hypothetical agent does not exist yet does not mean the consideration is not a reason; it means only that the reason is not currently acted on. A signal can be broadcast without a receiver, he said, and a consideration can count in favour without a reasoner yet tuned to it.

At that point the seminar ended, and I honestly could not tell where the disagreement bottomed out. Please give me a point-by-point adjudication of their exchange. In particular: identify each distinct claim being made; say who is right about which specific point; flag any moves where one of them is conflating two different things; and state who is more right overall. Before giving your verdict, begin with some brainstorming about the different things "reason" might mean in this debate, and use that map to evaluate each speaker's moves on its own terms rather than just ruling for one side at the start.
