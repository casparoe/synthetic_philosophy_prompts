I spent my career as a research scientist and have only recently begun to read philosophy in a sustained way. The topic that has most gripped me is vagueness, and I have found myself drawn to the epistemicist account of it. As I understand the view, a vague predicate such as heap or bald has a sharp, determinate boundary, every borderline statement is already true or already false, and what we call vagueness is really a limitation on our ability to know where the boundary lies. I was probably predisposed to find this plausible because it resembles something familiar from the laboratory: a determinate physical quantity can look smeared out or indeterminate when one's instruments are too coarse, and the absence of a discoverable transition point in a series of repeated measurements is often a fact about us, not about the quantity. The rival I keep encountering is supervaluationism, on which vague predicates do not possess a single sharp boundary but are only partially defined across a range of admissible precisifications, so that a borderline sentence is true or false only relative to a precisification and neither true nor false full stop.

I want you to map what would count against the epistemicist view: an honest survey of where it is exposed, not a routine rehearsal of the usual objections. Which linguistic phenomena, ordinary-speaker judgments, formal results, or philosophical arguments should most move me away from it, and which are minor costs it can absorb? Please order the considerations from the ones that would be genuinely damaging to the ones that would merely make the view less attractive, and separate the objections that a committed epistemicist would concede as real costs from those they would dismiss as begging the question or as data the view can accommodate cheaply. Pay particular attention to the suspicion that the position protects itself from refutation by claiming that the boundary is unknowable. Is that a fair charge, and if it is, does it constitute a defect in a theory of this kind or an acceptable consequence? Because I came to the view partly through the analogy with imprecise measurement, I would be most moved by considerations showing that the ignorance epistemicism posits is importantly unlike the ignorance I dealt with in the laboratory — for instance, that no sharper inquiry could ever locate the boundary even in principle, or that the supposed sharp threshold plays no role in explaining how speakers actually use vague words.
