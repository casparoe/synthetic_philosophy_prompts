I have been asked to write a one-page statement of my epistemological commitments about the dogmatism paradox, and before I circulate it I want an audit of whether the package hangs together. Here is the list as it stands; I have deliberately not marked which commitments I would be willing to revise.

1. Ordinary knowledge. I know a great many mundane contingent truths on the basis of perception, memory, and testimony — for example, that I have hands, that the meeting I am about to attend is at three o'clock, and that a colleague is in her office because I just saw her walk in. These are genuine items of knowledge, and I do not think my having them depends on first ruling out skeptical alternatives or on being able to say, for each way my grounds might mislead me, why it does not.

2. Closure. Knowledge is closed under known entailment: if I know p, and I know that p entails q, then I am in a position to know q.

3. Knowledge-based asymmetry. If I know p, then I am in a very different position with respect to p than with respect to its contraries: I am in a position to know, without examining the details, that any evidence against p would be misleading, since the truth of p entails that evidence against p points away from the truth. I also accept that this makes it rationally permissible, at least in many cases, to set such counterevidence aside rather than investigate it.

4. Anti-dogmatism. When I am actually presented with specific, apparently credible evidence against one of my beliefs, I should not dismiss it out of hand. I should either lower my confidence in the belief or identify a specific defect in the evidence, where the defect cannot simply consist in the evidence's conflicting with what I believe. I also treat the fact that the evidence bears against p as itself a reason to reconsider whether I ever knew p, not only whether p is true.

5. Parity of rational permission. Rational permissions supervene on the subject's perspective. If two subjects are indistinguishable from their own point of view — same evidence, same capacities, same background beliefs — then they are subject to the same rational permissions in the face of the same new evidence, even if one subject's target belief happens to be true and the other's happens to be false.

Please treat this list as the object of study and give me an audit, not a lecture. In particular: (i) identify which pairs of commitments are genuinely in tension and which merely appear to be in tension, saying briefly why the apparent tensions dissolve; (ii) rank the genuine tensions by severity, specifying for each what would have to be given up or qualified to resolve it and what that revision would cost; and (iii) state the single revision that would yield the most coherent overall package at the lowest cost, saying exactly what it sacrifices. Do not simply flag every pair as equally problematic — the point is to discriminate.
