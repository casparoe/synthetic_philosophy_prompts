The question the student was set was this:

"Explain the difference between etiological and causal-role accounts of biological function. Which account, if either, gives a better account of malfunction, and why? Illustrate with the heart."

And this is the student's answer:

"Etiological accounts say that the function of a trait is the effect it has right now that contributes to the organism's survival and reproduction. For the heart, that is pumping blood, because pumping blood keeps the animal alive in the present. Causal-role accounts are different: Cummins introduced them to explain how biologists analyze a system's capacities by decomposing the system into parts and assigning each part a contribution to some system-level capacity. On this view, the heart's function is to pump blood because the heart's pumping is what lets the circulatory system circulate blood. The main difference is usually put as history versus present organization: etiological accounts look backward at how the trait arose, while causal-role accounts look at the current system. Causal-role accounts give a better account of malfunction, because a defective heart is simply one that fails to make the contribution that the corresponding part makes in a normal heart. Etiological accounts, by contrast, are stuck calling a heart malfunctioning even when it was produced by a perfectly normal developmental process but happens not to pump, which seems too harsh."
