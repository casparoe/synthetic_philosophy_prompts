I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I keep running into essays that treat Boomers, Millennials, and Gen Z like they're natural kinds with real essences. I recently read Plato's Meno (Benjamin Jowett translation) and this passage snagged me:

"Suppose that I carry on the figure of the swarm, and ask of you, What is the nature of the bee? and you answer that there are many kinds of bees, and I reply: But do bees differ as bees, because there are many and different kinds of them; or are they not rather to be distinguished by some other quality, as for example beauty, size, or shape? How would you answer me?" And Meno says: "I should answer that bees do not differ from one another, as bees."

I want to use that as a wedge. Take the claim that generations are real kinds — that there is something in virtue of which people born in some 15-to-20-year span form a kind the way, say, a biological species or a medical syndrome does, rather than just an arbitrary administrative bracket. How would you test that claim? Design the best concrete test you can. Tell me what patterns in data would have to show up for us to say "yes, that cohort is a bee-kind" rather than "we just imposed the label," and what patterns would push the other way. Then be honest: what would a positive result actually settle about whether Boomers, Millennials, or Gen Z are real kinds rather than loose, useful journalistic categories? And what part of this is not empirically testable at all, no matter how good the data? Keep it under 900 words — I'll be reading it on deadline.
