I’ve spent thirty years in a lab, so I keep wanting to know what the underlying mechanism is, and I can’t help reading Bentham that way. I’ve been working my way through Bentham’s Anarchical Fallacies, the piece where he attacks the French Declaration of the Rights of Man. Not the famous line everybody knows, but a few pages in he makes this move: he says that a reason for wishing there were such things as rights is not itself a right, any more than wanting something is the same as having it, or being hungry is the same as eating bread. I’m paraphrasing, but that’s the gist.

Now here’s what’s bothering me. Bentham wrote that in the 1790s, when a declaration of rights was mostly a list of aspirations. Since then we’ve built actual legal machinery: the European Convention on Human Rights, the Human Rights Act over here, courts that can strike down statutes because they breach a right. So if I say ‘I have a right to freedom of expression’, that’s not just a wish anymore; it’s a legal claim I can bring. Does Bentham’s argument survive this development? I can see two readings. One: his point was precisely that rights only exist once the law creates them, so modern enforceable rights are fine by him, and the only remaining target is the rhetorical natural-rights talk in preambles. Two: his deeper worry was that calling something a right before the law has decided specific cases just smuggles in contested moral claims, and that still happens in Strasbourg and in human-rights adjudication. Which is right? Is there a repaired version of the argument that says something weaker but still important? I’d like to know which premise actually comes under pressure.
