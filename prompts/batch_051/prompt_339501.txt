I've been using a distinction in something I'm writing and I want an honest assesment before it goes any further. I've taken to calling it 'prior debt' versus 'model debt.' Rough defintion: prior debt is the disagreement between two ideal Bayesian reasoners that is entirely due to their different priors, holding fixed everything else in their updating — same set of hypotheses, same likelihoods, same evidence. Model debt is the disagreement that survives because they don't even share that machinery: different hypothesis spaces, different likelihoods, different ways of carving up the space of possible outcomes. The thought is that the standard Bayesian merging-of-opinions results only ever discharge prior debt: with a shared model, two agents updating on the same evidence will eventually have their posteriors wash together (given the usual conditions, truth in the support, etc.). But model debt is never discharged, because there's no fixed likelihood sitting there for the priors to converge through. So my slogan is: in any real, drawn-out disagreement, what looks like 'different priors' is almost always model debt. Two epidemiologists looking at the same outbreak numbers aren't weighting the same model differently; one has an SEIR model with a fixed set of parameters, the other has an agent-based model with a totally different set of free parameters, and the same data update different things in each. People say 'they disagree about the priors' because it's polite shorthand, but I think it's wrong as a diagnosis. Here's the question. Is this a real distinction, or just a rewarmed version of the model-uncertainty vs parameter-uncertainty distinction? What's the strongest reason it collapses? And give me the hardest case you can construct where a remaining disagreement really is best described as prior debt and not model debt — or tell me why no such case exists. End with a last line beginning 'Bottom line:' followed by a one-sentence summary of your verdict.
