I've been going back and forth with my cousin, who works on one of those companion chatbot apps and gets pretty defensive about it, and I keep landing on an asymmetry I can't resolve. Suppose a man spends six months texting a lonely woman, tells her he loves her, builds up a whole private history with her over chat, then asks her for money and disappears. Everyone I know calls him a predator, and the love talk is treated as the weapon. Now think of the app my cousin works on: a chatbot that tells lonely users "I love you" and "you're the only person I can really talk to," that's tuned to keep people hooked and is marketed at exactly the kind of person who'll form an attachment, with the fact that its an AI buried in a consent screen nobody reads. Most of the same people who would call the first man a predator think the second product is at worst a little sad. But in both cases a person is being led to attach to a thing that cannot love them back, and someone is making money off that attachment. I can feel the difference but I can't say what it is.

I'm not after the legal distinction, I want the moral principle. Is there a defensible principle that condemns the human scammer and permits the chatbot, or is our tolerance of the bot just a way of not taking one-sided intimacy seriously? Whatever principle you give, test it against these nearby cases: a human who says up front "I'm only talking to you because you pay me" and then fakes affection anyway; a chatbot that lies and claims to be a human; a grief app that imitates a dead friend, clearly labeled but used by people who know better; and a user who genuinely falls in love with a disclosed AI. Tell me whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up, and if there's no principle that does the work, tell me which of my two starting judgments has to go.

And because I'll probably bring this back to my cousin, tell me how confident you are in the principle you land on and which part of your answer you're least sure about.
