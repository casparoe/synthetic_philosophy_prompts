I'm in a policy class where the instructor keeps defending nudges by saying that choice architecture is literally just architecture. Their argument goes: a building has to put the door somewhere, the stairs somewhere, the ramp somewhere; a cafeteria has to arrange the food in some order; a website has to present the cookie options in some order; an organ-donation form has to have some default, opt-in or opt-out. In all of these there is no neutral design — someone has to decide which way the path of least resistance slopes. So, they conclude, a nudge is no more paternalistic or manipulative than building a door that opens outward rather than inward; you are just choosing the direction of least effort. On their view, critics of nudging are confused about what design even is, because the alternative to a designed environment is not a neutral environment but a badly designed one.

I'm not sure the analogy is doing as much work as they think, and I want to push on it without just rehashing the usual anti-paternalism lines. Please act as a philosopher who works on paternalism, autonomy, and the ethics of choice architecture. Start by brainstorming, fairly freely, what maps to what: the architect, the person walking through the building, the door hinge, the building code, the locked stairwell, the ramp around the back, and so on. Then give me a mapping table for the analogy. Then go through where the mapping breaks — the difference between changing physical effort and changing a default rule, between a building you can survey all at once and a decision architecture you only notice when it trips you, between an architect who is not trying to make you healthier and a choice architect who is. Tell me which disanalogies actually matter for the specific conclusion the instructor wants (that nudges are not objectionably paternalistic or manipulative) and which are just harmless differences. Then give me a verdict: does the no-neutral-design point really dissolve the paternalism worry, or was the architecture analogy carrying the argument the whole time? And if the analogy is bad or only partial, say what a better analogy would be, or how the analogy would need to be repaired. I need the nuanced version for a term paper, not the standard libertarian pathernalism boilerplate. Take your time and think through it carefully.
