I have been turning over a parallel between Bongard problems and Goodman's new riddle of induction, and I would like a careful judgement as to whether it is deep or superficial. A Bongard problem presents the solver with two panels of six simple geometric figures: the six figures on the left all satisfy a rule that the six on the right all violate, and the solver's task is to induce the rule from those twelve examples, usually in a single attempt. Goodman's new riddle observes that any finite run of emeralds observed to be green confirms the hypothesis that all emeralds are green and equally confirms the hypothesis that all emeralds are grue, where a thing is grue if it is examined before some future time t and is green, or is not examined before t and is blue. The parallel, as it strikes me, is this: in both cases the evidence underdetermines the hypothesis, so the hard step is not finding a regularity that fits the data but selecting the intended or projectible regularity from an indefinite space of competitors; and in both cases success appears to depend on preferences — perceptual groupings, simplicity, prior expectations, entrenchment — that the examples themselves do not establish. Is this a genuine identity of structure, or does the analogy break under inspection? Please set out the mapping precisely before testing where the two sides diverge. In particular: is the Bongard solver doing Goodmanian projectibility in a visual register, or is the puzzle better understood as a perceptual task in which the visual system has already pruned the space of candidate regularities before anything like hypothesis selection begins? Would a Goodmanian account transfer to Bongard problems in a testable way — for example, by predicting that puzzle-setters and solvers systematically avoid gerrymandered, disjunctive, or time-indexed rules — and would the failure of such a prediction count against entrenchment or only against the analogy? Finally, identify at least one thing the parallel predicts that turns out false, and say which side of the analogy is responsible for the failure.
