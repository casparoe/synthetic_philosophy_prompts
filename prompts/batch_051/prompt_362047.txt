I keep using the word "settled" when people talk about the future—"it isnt settled whether the deal closes," "the election is already settled"—but I've realized I don't actually know what property I'm pointing at. I guess this is the old sea battle problem, but I want a working definition I can actually use, not a history. My hunch is that "settled" is doing three jobs at once: an epistemic job (we can know it now), a metaphysical job (there's already a fact of the matter), and a practical job (nobody can do anything about it). I want a working definition of "settled" as applied to future events, or maybe two or three definitions if the word really is doing several jobs. Then run the definitions through these cases and tell me where they give the wrong answer or just go silent:

- a total solar eclipse that orbital mechanics predicts for a particular day next century
- a fair coin I'm about to flip, where I havent flipped it yet
- the eventual decay of a particular uranium atom, where physics only gives a half-life
- a sea battle tomorrow that both admirals have already ordered and neither can recall
- a sea battle tomorrow that will happen only if a scout decides at dawn to send a message, and the scout's decision depends on which way the wind is blowing
- a sentence I'm about to type, where I genuinely haven't decided what it will be
- a claim made yesterday that there would be a sea battle today, where the battle is happening right now

I'd say the eclipse is clearly settled and the undecided sentence is clearly not, but I'm unsure about the uranium atom and the yesterday case, and I suspect the two sea battle cases come apart even though the battle itself looks the same from the outside. So: propose the definition, run every case through it, and tell me where it surprises you or where I'd have to bite a bullet. If "settled" really is doing several jobs, say which cases move between the definitions.
