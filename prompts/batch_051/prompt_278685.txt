I'm a tax preparer, and every season I watch the same thing: a client who earns a salary gets wage income taxed every year, plus payroll taxes, no way around it. A client who just holds a stock that goes up tenfold, and dies before selling, is in a totally different world. The appreciation that happened during their life is never taxed as income, because their heirs get a step-up in basis to the market value at death and can sell the next day owing nothing on fifty years of gains. And the estate tax—which is supposed to be the backstop on dynastic wealth—now only applies above something like $14 million per person, and people above that spend real money avoiding it. So the system as a whole taxes wages from work pretty hard, while letting enormous accumulations of capital move to the next generation mostly untaxed.

I'm not asking for tax policy slogans. I want to know what this practice presupposes, philosophically, about equal opportunity and fairness. Does it only make sense if you think unrealized gains arent "income" until sold? Does it presuppose that descendants have some claim on what they didn't earn? Or is it just administratively captured and philosophically empty? Use whatever jargon you need, just define it briefly so I can follow. But be careful: distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it's underdetermined—multiple theories could license it. Then tell me what would actually have to change in the rules if we instead thought all accretions to wealth are income as they accrue or at death, and wether that new system would have its own philosophical commitments we'd have to own.

Also: is the apparent inconsistency (tax the nurse's wages, dont tax the dead investor's gains) a sign the system has no coherent theory, or is there a coherent theory hiding in the realization rule and the estate tax that I'm missing?
