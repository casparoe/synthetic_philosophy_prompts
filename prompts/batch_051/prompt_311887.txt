I've been trying to get clear on what actually makes a written shape an 'a', going by my gut on cases, and I want to know what principle (if any) I'm applying.

- The double-storey 'a' you see in most printed serif text: obviously an a.
- The single-storey 'a' a lot of handwriting and geometric sans-serif fonts use, basically a circle with a tail: also an a.
- The italic single-storey 'ɑ' with a straight right side and no top hook: still an a.
- A child's attempt at an a that comes out as a lopsided circle with a dangling tale and a half-hearted crossbar: I count it as an a, mostly because that's what they where trying to write.
- The Cyrillic lowercase 'а', which is indistinguishable from the Latin one in most fonts: I read it as an a, even though it's technically a different alphabet.
- The Greek alpha α: not an a, even though our a is descended from it.
- The @ sign: not an a, even though in a lot of fonts there's a kind of a wrapped inside it.
- A printed lowercase 'a' turned upside down: not an a anymore, even though every contour is still there.

The pairs that bug me: Cyrillic а passes but Greek α fails, even though neither is Latin. And the kid's scribble passes but the upside-down a fails, even though the scribble is further from the canonical shape than the rotated one is. So my verdicts can't just be about shape, but they can't just be about alphabet or history or intention either.

Is there one principle that gets all of these, or close? If you have to sacrifice one or two verdicts to keep it from being a mess of clauses, which ones would you drop, and what does the surviving principle say about the cases I keep going back and forth on: uppercase A versus lowercase a (same letter or two?), and the IPA symbol /a/ in a phonetics article (an a or not)? Finally, what does my pattern of verdicts commit me to about whether being an 'a' is a matter of convention, intention, shape, or something else?
