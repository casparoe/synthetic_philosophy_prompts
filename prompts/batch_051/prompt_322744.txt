Between the mysticism-adjacent writing in rationalist corners and an analytic philosophy of language seminar I sat in on, I keep tripping over the word ineffable doing too much work: someone says the experience cannot be put into words, and two sentences later claims to know something from it. I honestly can't tell if that's deep or a motte-and-bailey. Before I ever decide whether what cannot be said can be known, I want the full specification for a theory of ineffable knowledge: what would it actually have to get right to count? I'm picturing the kind of requirements list people make for theories of moral status or reference. For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it, and mark it as nonnegotiable or just one camp's preference. It should at minimum distinguish ineffability from mere difficulty of description, vagueness, missing vocabulary, and the normal fact that first-person experience (the taste of a new fruit) is hard to transmit; locate where the failure is supposed to sit—language, concepts, or something pre-conceptual; and explain how a determinate content gets fixed if no sentence carries it, since no content means no knowledge, while a content no possible statement rules out is indistinguishable from every other content. It also has to deal with mystics talking a lot about the very thing they call unsayable, often incompatibly: do the statements point, miss, work as poetry, or what? And it has to say whether anyone can transmit the thing—if I get it by a practice or state rather than by being told, what makes it knowledge rather than a shifted affect or a wierd physiological event, and why trust retroactive reports?

If you find it useful you can quickly score two or three existing positions against the list, but the list is the point. One last thing: when you present the requirements, tell me which ones are settled among people who work on this and which are genuinely contested, and don't quietly present one school's view as the consensus.
