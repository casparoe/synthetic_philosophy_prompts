Since I began a research internship in a laboratory that works on timing and apparent motion, the color-phi illusion has stopped being a classroom curiosity for me. Watching it on a strip of LEDs on a colleague's desk—two stationary flashes, one red and one green, reported as a single red dot that appears to move and change color in transit—made the interpretive puzzle suddenly concrete. I have come to the position that the Orwellian revisionist account of this phenomenon is, on balance, the most honest one: the observer really does have a conscious experience of the first stationary flash, and when the second flash arrives the system retrospectively revises that earlier conscious record, so the apparent moving red dot is best understood as a cover story written into memory after the fact rather than as the content of an experience that was constructed before it went to consciousness. The tampering, on my view, happens after the first experience, not before.

That is the position I want you to stress-test. What would have to be true for the Orwellian account to be wrong? I want a map of its exposure, not a recital of the familiar back-and-forth. Which experimental findings—from temporal-order judgments, backward masking, the flash-lag effect, or studies of whether masked or unattended stimuli leave traceable effects on later processing—should count against it, and which would merely be awkward? Are there neurophysiological results about timing, such as the role of recurrent processing or the conditions a neural response must meet before it can support a conscious episode, that would decisively rule out the claim that the first stationary dot is genuinely experienced before the second flash arrives? I would also like you to face the deflationary worry directly: that the Orwellian and Stalinesque descriptions are two ways of describing the same functional architecture and differ only in where one draws the boundary of consciousness, so that no experiment could in principle settle between them. If the view has been qualified so that nothing could count against it, say so, and say whether that is a real defect for a claim of this kind. Defenders of the Orwellian account will themselves concede that some findings count against them; tell me which, and rank the defeaters from fatal to merely annoying.
