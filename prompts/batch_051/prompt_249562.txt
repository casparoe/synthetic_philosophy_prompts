Here is an asymmetry I keep noticing and cannot yet justify. When we judge the past, we treat our own moral standards as authoritative: a nineteenth-century slaveholder is wrong, and the fact that slavery was widely accepted then counts as background, not excuse. But when the same structure is applied from the future toward us — the thought that our descendants may condemn us for factory farming, or for carbon emissions, or for something we cannot presently see as a moral issue at all — most of us reach immediately for the principle that it is unfair to judge people by standards they could not have had. That is an asymmetry: the past is answerable to the present's standards, while the present is not answerable to the future's. Is there a principled justification for it, or is it just the conviction that moral history has finally reached its correct endpoint? If you defend the asymmetry, defend it against the case of an educated person in 1900 who offered the same defense against judgment by people in 2000 and who, by our lights, was wrong.
