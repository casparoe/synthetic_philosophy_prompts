I'm TA-ing an undergrad philosophy of language course that pairs classic short essays with AI-ethics spin-offs, and last week's reading was Harry Frankfurt's "On Bullshit." The exam included an eight-point short-answer question, and one answer in particular is making me want a second opinion before I assign it points. The question was:

"Explain Frankfurt's distinction between lying and bullshit in your own words. Now suppose a large language model confidently outputs a false statement (a hallucination). Is the system lying, bullshitting, or simply in error? If none of those applies cleanly to the system itself, say whether the people who built or deployed it might fall into one of the categories."

Here is the student's answer:

"Frankfurt distinguishes the liar from the bullshitter by their relation to truth. The liar knows what is true and deliberately says the opposite in order to deceive, whereas the bullshitter does not care whether what he says is true; he says whatever suits his purpose and might turn out to be right or wrong. Frankfurt actually thinks bullshit can be worse than lying, because the liar at least acknowledges that truth is the standard he is violating, while the bullshitter treats truth as beside the point. Applied to an LLM, the model has no beliefs and no intention to deceive, so it cannot literally be a liar. It is also misleading to call it 'error,' since error is what happens when someone tries to get things right and fails, and the model is not trying to get anything right. That leaves bullshit. A bullshitter is defined by indifference to how things really are, and an LLM trained to maximize predictive likelihood is indifferent in exactly this way: it never checks its output against the world, it just emits whatever token is statistically most likely next. A hallucination is therefore a paradigm case of Frankfurtian bullshit. Nor are the builders off the hook. If a company deploys a system whose training objective is fluent prediction rather than truth, and it knows the system will sometimes say false things, then the company is bullshitting too, because it has decided that sounding right matters more than being right."

I'm supposed to grade this against a rubric that seperately rewards (a) accuracy about Frankfurt, (b) care about whether his categories apply to non-agents, and (c) whether the conclusions actually follow from the premises. Can you give me a grade out of 10, and more importantly the point-by-point comments that justify every deduction? I don't want to hammer a strong answer for one small misstep, but I also don't want to let a plausible-sounding conflation slip through as full credit.
