The separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism leans on the thought that each normal adult is exactly one subject of experience: trade-offs inside a single subject's life can be authorized by that subject, but trade-offs across seperate subjects can't be authorized by anyone, so utilitarianism wrongly adds across lives. Both halves of this picture have gotten empirically shakier. Split-brain patients seem to show that one body can host two streams of consciousness at once, and brain-to-brain interface research makes partial sharing of experience between two bodies a live technological prospect. Does the objection survive once the 'one body, one subject' boundary turns out to be negotiable? I'd like you to say which premise is actually breaking, and whether what's left is still the separateness of persons objection or a weaker claim about distinct sources of moral claims. Please answer in British English.
