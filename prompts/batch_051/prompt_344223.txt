I am an undergraduate in a philosophy of law course, and our class is working through the proof paradox: the blue bus and gatecrasher cases, standards of proof, and the puzzle that bare statistics seem insufficient for liability or conviction even when they satisfy the stated probability threshold. I have to run a 75-minute review session for about sixteen students who have done the reading. Design a simulation, not a lecture, that makes them feel the force of that reluctance before the issue is named. Structure it as a game: split the room into juries, give each a short civil damage claim under a preponderance standard, and vary the plaintiff's evidence across juries—one gets a market-share statistic, another an eyewitness of specified reliability, another a random draw from a defined population—calibrated so every case presents the same objective probability. Give juries a small scoring incentive to decide in line with the objective probability, so laziness with the numbers cannot explain their verdicts. Have them announce and defend their decisions before they learn the cases were matched, then reveal the calibration. The debrief should map the moments of hesitation onto the idea that statistical evidence seems to lack causal fit, aboutness, or sensitivity to this defendant's conduct even when it meets the probabilistic standard, and should press them to say whether that missing feature is epistemic or moral. Tell me what will predictably go wrong—I expect juries will dispute the stipulated probabilities, or say the other bus might have done it while tolerating the same residual risk for the eyewitness—and how to handle each, and be explicit about which aspects of real legal proof the simulation cannot reproduce. In your answer, lead with the bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
