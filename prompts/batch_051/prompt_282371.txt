I am a PhD student in economics, and I have a settled habit when evaluating risky strategies. For any repeated decision—a portfolio rule, a career gamble, a sequence of small bets—I judge it by its ensemble average: I compute the expected payoff across all possible states of the world, and if that expectation is positive I treat the strategy as sound and regard anyone who declines it as making an error. Thus I would endorse a gamble that turns each $1 into $1.50 or $0.60 with equal probability, because the expected value is positive, even though a person who repeats it many times sees wealth shrink on the time average, since the geometric mean of 1.5 and 0.6 is below 1. I am aware that the two averages can diverge sharply for multiplicative, repeated choices, yet I continue to use the ensemble average as a shortcut because it is tractable and I usually cannot estimate the full dynamics. Is this policy defensible as an epistemic shortcut, or is it a bias dressed up as a technical convenience? When do the two averages coincide, and is there a principled restatement—such as maximizing expected log growth rather than expected payoff—that keeps the shortcut's benefits without misleading me in non-ergodic settings?
