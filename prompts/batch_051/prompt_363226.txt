I've been defending a view lately — I guess you'd call it a threshold or two-tier negative utilitarianism — and I want to stress-test it rather than just collect allies. The view is roughly this: below some severity threshold, suffering trades off against happiness and other goods in the ordinary way, bad against good. But once suffering is bad enough — torture-level physical pain, extreme and prolonged mental anguish, the worst states we know about — it has lexical priority: no amount of happiness, preference-satisfaction, beauty, knowledge, or anything else compensates for even one extra instance of it. A world with slightly less severe suffering is better full stop, even if it means sacrificing an enormous amount of the good stuff. I also hold this in an impersonal, add-it-up-across-lives way, so it isn't just a claim about how one person should treat their own pain; it's a claim about which worlds we should prefer when aggregating across all sentient beings. I've found myself saying things like: a world with ten minutes of one person's extreme agony is worse than a world with a million other people's mild contentment and fifteen minutes of such agony — the extra five minutes can't be bought off. People either find that obviously right or sort of repellent.

What I want from you is a map of this position's exposure. What would count against it? I don't mean just the standard criticisms; I want the things a committed defender would themselves have to concede as counting against it — evidence, formal results, considered judgments, impossible-looking cases, or commitments the view turns out to be stuck with — ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Include what the view predicts or rules out that its rivals (plain negative utilitarianism, classical utilitarianism, prioritarianism, maybe rights-based views) don't, and where you think the real logical space is. If you think I've qualified the view into unfalsifiability, or that the threshold is doing no real work and I'm just smuggling in an ordinary strong-but-continuous weighting, say so, and say wether that's a problem for a view of this kind rather than a feature. The kind of thing that would move me most is a spectrum argument from mild to severe that forces me to draw a line I can't justify, or a demonstration that the badness of suffering doesn't have anything like the discontinuity I'm claiming.

Finally, keep separate what's actually settled — that extreme suffering matters a lot and can ground very strong reasons — from what's genuinely contested among people who know this literature, and don't present one contested reading (about thresholds, lexical priority, or how to aggregate across lives) as the settled consensus.
