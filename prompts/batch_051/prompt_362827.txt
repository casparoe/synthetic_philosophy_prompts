I have a policy about whose testimony I trust on questions of how to live. When I am considering an unconventional choice — leaving a secure profession to work freelance, or remaining unmarried in a community that treats marriage as the default — I discount almost to zero the warnings of people who have never tried the thing I am considering. I regard their warnings not as evidence about the life I would lead, but as evidence about how powerfully people rationalize the life they already lead: conceding that a different path might suit someone else would call their own choices into question. I give much more weight to reports from people who have actually lived the alternative, and I treat people who tried it and came back to the conventional path as the most informative of all. But I have started to worry that this is rigged. The returnees are the subset of experimenters for whom the experiment failed; the ones who stayed are not showing up as warnings in my sample. Meanwhile I dismiss the majority's warnings as mere rationalization, which guarantees that the conventional path can never win the argument. Is this policy defensible as an epistemic shortcut in the spirit of Mill's view that untried modes of life should be tested rather than inherited? If not, what is the least costly correction: changing my discount rates, treating never-triers' warnings as fallible testimony about expected social costs, or looking for a kind of evidence neither group is in a position to give me? Under what assumptions about self-selection, rationalization, and the transferability of experience would something like my policy be sound?
