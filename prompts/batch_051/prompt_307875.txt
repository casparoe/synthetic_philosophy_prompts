I've spend the last couple years sliding toward a position I've been calling empirical pacifism, and I want someone to map out what would actually count against it. The view isn't that killing or coercion is intrinsically wrong—I've never found deontological wrongness that convincing—but that, as a contingent matter, violent means are almost never the best available means, and when they do look best the appearance is usually an artifact of how we compare options. We hold a realistic violent intervention up against a strawman nonviolent one, overweight dramatic short-term effects, and undercount escalation, blowback, and the way violence entrenches the thing it's meant to fight. On the strong version I keep almost-believing, even the canonical good-war cases and ordinary self-defense cases fail under honest counterfactual scrutiny. I take this to be a falsifiable empirical claim, not a values commitment, and that's exactly what worries me.

What I'm after is a defeater map. What real or possible evidence should move me away from this, ordered from "this breaks it" to "this just stings"? For each, would a defender of the view have to grant that it counts against the position, or is there a principled rather than ad hoc response? I know the standard objections—Rwanda, Srebrenica, someone attacking your family, civil wars that only end when one side is crushed—but I want to know which are actually decisive and which are just salient. I'd also like to hear what the view predicts that its rivals don't, and how much of that has already shown up. And I especially want to know weather I've accidentally insulated the whole thing into unfalsifiability with moves like "a better nonviolent option must have existed; they just failed to find it." If I have, is that fatal for a claim of this kind, or is that just what an empirical strategic thesis looks like? Be candid: if the defensible version is much weaker than the strong one I keep almost-believing, tell me exactly what I'd have to give up.
