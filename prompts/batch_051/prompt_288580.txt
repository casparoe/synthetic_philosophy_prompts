Two friends of mine went back and forth the other night over a sentence I had never thought needed arguing about: "The morning star is the evening star." The sentence happens to be true — both names pick out the same planet — but they clearly disagreed about what it is to learn that. Neither of them has any training in philosophy, and neither do I; they were just being careful, and each of them landed some solid blows. I wrote the exchange down as best I could, and I honestly couldn't referee it.

(1) Sam said the sentence is empty. Since both names pick out the same object, he said, "The morning star is the evening star" asserts of that object only that it is itself, exactly like "The morning star is the morning star." Both are true, and neither could be true while the other was false, so they must say the same thing; the feeling that the first sentence teaches something is just an illusion produced by switching between two labels.

(2) Dana replied that this cannot be all there is to it, because someone can perfectly well understand both names and still reasonably wonder whether they pick out the same thing. That is not a position you can be in with "The morning star is the morning star." When people first determined that the two were the same body, it rewrote what they thought the sky contained; if Sam were right, she said, that discovery would have been a discovery about vocabulary.

(3) Sam came back that the discovery is real, but it is not carried by the bare identity between the two names. It is carried by something that can be said without using the two-name sentence at all: that the object found the morning way and the object found the evening way are the same object. The sentence "The morning star is the evening star" is just a compressed report of that convergence of routes. So the information attaches to the routes, not to the identity of the labels.

(4) Dana answered that this move leaves Sam with a dilemma. If the two routes are genuinely two ways of finding the object, then "the object found the one way is the same as the object found the other way" is itself an identity statement made under two different ways of picking out the object — which is exactly the thing Sam said needed no explaining. If the routes collapse into one, the report becomes trivial and no longer captures what was learned. So Sam has either relocated the puzzle or changed the subject. She said the sentence can be true and informative only if the two names, beyond picking out the same object, each carry a different way of presenting it.

They never settled it. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: at each step, say who is right about what, note where a step overreaches, and say what, if anything, both of them are missing. Start with your bottom line — your overall verdict — in the very first sentence, and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
