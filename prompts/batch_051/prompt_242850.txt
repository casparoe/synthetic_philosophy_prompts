Before I retired I did geophysics, and I still think in flowcharts, so I'd like a decision tree for something I've been reading about: nuclear deterrence. Could you put together a "which view of nuclear deterrence do you actually hold?" quiz for my reading group—mostly retired engineers and scientists, none of whom have read the deterrence literature. Give me maybe eight to ten questions, each a concrete case, with two or three plain-language answers, and a scoring key. The cases should be things like: a crisis where one side makes a threat it can't verify, a leader who acts irrational on purpose, a false alarm about a launch, a limited conventional war that escalates to tactical nuclear weapons, that sort of thing. The scoring should sort people by how they answer cases, not by which abstract slogan they like, so no question should just ask "do you think nuclear weapons keep the peace?"

The leaves I most want separated are: (1) mutual vulnerability makes major war impossible; (2) the stability-instability paradox, where big wars are deterred but smaller ones are encouraged; (3) brinkmanship, where the real skill is manipulating shared risk rather than making threats that are believable in any ordinary sense; (4) minimal or existential deterrence, where a small arsenal is enough and elaborate counterforce planning is pointless or dangerous; (5) something abolitionist, where current stability is temporary and the real question is how to get to zero. But please build the tree from the logical space first—set out the underlying questions (is credibility required? must leaders be rational? is risk something to be managed or minimized? can conventional war stay conventional?), note which combinations of answers are actually available, and say which combinations are empty. For each empty cell, give a quick diagnosis: is it incoherent, just unattractive, or merely unexplored? And flag any two leaves your questions can't really seperate, plus what extra case would split them.
