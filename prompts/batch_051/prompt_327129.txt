Before I read any more accounts of manipulation, I want the full spec first. Give me the desiderata any adequate theory of the manipulation/persuasion boundary has to meet: the cases it has to classify correctly, the distinctions it can't collapse (between influence and manipulation, manipulation and coercion, persuasion and pressure), the plain facts about how people actually make decisions that it can't contradict, and why each one is a requirement — what case or consideration puts it on the list. Then flag any two desiderata you suspect can't be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement does the most work in killing off otherwise attractive views.

To make it non-hand-wavy, these are the kinds of cases I mean. A car salesman flatters your taste to make you feel like a shrewd judge, and you buy. A friend sincerely bolsters your confidence before a hard conversation. Both alter your self-perception; only one is manipulation, I think, but the difference isn't obviously in the technique. A therapist uses motivational interviewing to get a patient to quit smoking; a pick-up artist uses the same reflective-listening moves to get someone into bed. Does the identical technique change moral valence just because the practitioner's aim differs? A nudge of the organ-donor-form variety changes what you do without changing what you believe or what options you have, and a subliminal prime, if it worked, would bypass awareness entirely. Gaslighting runs through reasons too: it feeds you evidence, just broken evidence aimed at your trust in your own judgement. A parent says "because I said so", which is not reasons-giving and yet doesn't feel like manipulation. A grief-stricken widow gets a timeshare pitch built around what her late husband would have wanted. If a view can't handle most of these without collapsing into "manipulation is whatever I don't like", it hasn't earned the boundary line.

I want the list, not a theory: which of these must a view get right, and which are red herrings that only one camp insists on? For example, is "the target's own agency is always engaged" a genuine condition, or does it beg the question against subliminal and environmental manipulation? Is "manipulation is pro tanto wrong" a desideratum, or should an adequate theory leave open that some manipulation is fine? And is it a mark against a theory that it collapses into a complete theory of autonomy, or is that just the cost of doing business?

The tension I most want addressed: the cases pull toward manipulation being influence that bypasses or subverts rational capacity, but the therapist and the parent of a toddler pull toward subverting rational capacity being exactly what good influence is. If the theory saves the therapist by appealing to the target's interests, it starts to make the salesman's badness depend on outcomes or motives, which sounds wrong for a wrong that's supposed to be in the influence itself. Tell me which requirements can be held together and which one has to give. You can score the usual views — bypass views, deception views, autonomy-subversion views, influence-that-exploits-a-vulnerability views — against the list at the end if it helps, but the list is the point.
