I am preparing a presentation for an interdisciplinary probability workshop, and I need a question-and-answer prep sheet. My thesis concerns the role of the maximum entropy principle in objective Bayesianism. I will argue that MAXENT cannot by itself underwrite a uniquely rational prior, because every maximum-entropy distribution is only as rational as two things MAXENT does not fix: the choice of reference measure over which entropy is defined, and the choice of which moments or summary statistics are taken as constraints. The standard attempts to make those choices principled rather than arbitrary—appeals to transformation groups, invariance arguments, or the Jeffreys prior—do not close the gap; they relocate the judgment into a choice of symmetry or parameterization while continuing to call the result objective. I will defend this not as a slide into subjective Bayesianism but as a relocation of objectivity: the rationally binding work happens one level down, in the selection of the constraint set and reference measure. On this view, MAXENT is best understood as a diagnostic instrument that makes explicit exactly what a chosen set of commitments already entails, rather than as a universal engine that turns known facts into a rational prior.

Here is my audience. Roughly half are working statisticians and machine learning researchers who use maximum-entropy models daily—logistic regression, maxent classifiers, image priors, and the like—and who are largely indifferent to foundational debates as long as the procedure works. Several others are formal epistemologists interested in how norms on priors could be justified at all. And there are two or three committed Jaynesians in the room who regard MAXENT as the unique rational procedure for assigning probabilities and who know the transformation-group responses to Bertrand-style objections in detail. I expect the statisticians, the epistemologists, and the Jaynesians to object in different vocabularies and to push on different parts of the argument.

Please give me the eight or so objections I am most likely to face, ordered roughly by how likely they are and how much damage they would do, phrased the way the relevant members of the audience would actually phrase them, and indicating who each objection comes from. For each objection, give me a sentence on what I should concede, the core of my position I must hold, a short reply I can deliver in the time available, and an honest note on where that reply is weakest. Include the one question I am hoping nobody asks. Finally, tell me the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend actively rather than hope the audience misses.
