I am a site reliability engineer, not a philosopher, and the concepts I actually trust are operational: latency distributions and their long tails, p50 versus p99, error budgets and service-level objectives, blast radius, mean time to recovery, correlated versus uncorrelated failures, and the standing judgment call between rolling back a change whose cost is thinly distributed across every request and hotfixing an incident that is catastrophic for a single shard. A philosopher colleague has been trying to draw me into the torture-versus-dust-specks debate: the question whether one person's being tortured is morally worse than a sufficiently large number of people each getting a barely noticeable dust speck in the eye, and whether harms so small that no individual would notice them can be summed across persons until they outweigh a grave harm concentrated in one person.

I want you to restate the main positions in that debate in the operational vocabulary I just described, treating the dust specks as a uniform sub-threshold latency regression spread across the entire user base and the torture as an incident that concentrates catastrophic cost in a single tenant or shard. Cover at least the aggregative utilitarian position, the lexical-threshold view that no number of the trivial harms can outweigh the grave one, the appeal to the separateness of persons, the continuity or sorites-style concern about where any threshold could lie, and the difference between what an agent might choose for herself and what she might choose as a planner choosing for others. Keep the translation systematic and honest rather than a set of loose metaphors; where a distinction in the philosophy has no genuine counterpart in how on-call engineers actually reason, say so instead of forcing it.

Then provide a residue report. Which parts of the debate survive cleanly, which survive only with distortion, and which cannot be expressed in this vocabulary at all? I am especially interested in whether the resistance to summing tiny harms across distinct persons has a real counterpart in the refusal to let a billion one-millisecond regressions excuse one multi-hour outage, or whether the operational vocabulary quietly imports a picture in which users are interchangeable and the separateness of persons drops out entirely. Finally, tell me whether what fails to translate is best explained as a defect in the target framework or as evidence that the original philosophical claim is less precise than its defenders present it as being.
