I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on Dostoevsky and political philosophy — 16 students, one 75-minute session. They have just read the Grand Inquisitor chapter of The Brothers Karamazov. Before I open any discussion of it, I want to run a structured in-class activity that makes students feel the pull of the Inquisitor's position from the inside, so that by the time they talk about the chapter they cannot treat it as a mere villain's monologue. What I want them to come away seeing is not that the Inquisitor is right, but that his position has real force and that resisting it costs more than the chapter's readers usually first suppose.

The idea they should encounter before it is named is roughly this: freedom is burdensome, and under the right conditions ordinary people will willingly, even gratefully, hand over their self-direction, their responsibility for hard choices, and their liberty of conscience in exchange for security, provision, belonging, and certainty — in a word, in exchange for happiness as the Inquisitor understands it — and the person who takes that burden from them may understand himself as acting from love, not tyranny.

Design the activity around the three temptations the chapter presents — miracle, mystery, and authority — but do not use those labels with the students, and do not mention Dostoevsky until the debrief. Structure the activity as three accumulating choice points, each an apparently reasonable exchange that a sensible person would take, with the accumulative effect becoming visible only near the end. One natural scheme, which you should treat as a starting point rather than a straitjacket: the students are a small community in the aftermath of a prolonged breakdown. In the first round, corresponding to miracle and provision, they choose between keeping individual control of their resources and risking shortage, or accepting guaranteed distribution administered by a panel, which then decides what work each person owes. In the second round, corresponding to mystery and meaning, they choose between keeping the question of what the breakdown means and how to live open and unresolved, or adopting a single official account with shared rituals, administered again by the panel. In the third round, corresponding to authority and conscience, they choose between keeping certain hard decisions for themselves — who may join the community, what may be said and taught publicly, which offenses warrant exclusion — or delegating them to the panel, which by now no longer needs the group's consent to act. The stakes should be small but real, such as control over the final ten minutes of class, points toward a small privilege, or the choice of the next reading, so the exchanges actually bite.

The rules, incentives, and roles should be arranged so that the activity does the teaching: the facilitator should not lecture up front, and the phrase Grand Inquisitor should not be said aloud until the debrief. Include a moment near the end, still before the debrief, in which the completed arrangement is described back to the group in neutral terms so the recognition happens in the room rather than being supplied by me.

Since the design rests partly on predictions about how students will behave and partly on an interpretive reading of the chapter, say how confident you are in the claims you make and single out the one point, pedagogical or interpretive, that you are least sure of.
