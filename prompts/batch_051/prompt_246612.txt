I'm a retired physical chemist who got into philosophy and biblical scholarship after retiring, so if I'm using the wrong labels, that's why. I teach an adult Sunday school class, and last week I got stuck on something I can't get around.

My church's statement of faith (adopted 1928) has a clause on Scripture that I always assumed was straightforward. It reads:

"The Scriptures, in their original manuscripts, are wholly without error in all that they affirm, whether in matters of faith, history, science, or number. No fault of copyists, however widespread in the manuscripts, may be charged against the inspired text."

So far so good. But prepping a class on Solomon's temple, I noticed the big bronze basin in the courtyard is described twice. In the standard Hebrew text of 1 Kings 7:26 the basin holds two thousand baths of water; in 2 Chronicles 4:5 it holds three thousand. The passages are otherwise near word-for-word identical. Every Hebrew edition and translation I own reads 2,000 in Kings and 3,000 in Chronicles; none of them smooths it out. The old commentators offers three fixes: one digit got miscopied, the two authors used different sizes of "bath," or one verse gives capacity and the other the amount you'd actually put in. None of those has direct textual support, and the last two feel like the sort of ad hoc curve-fitting I used to mark students down for.

Here's my problem. The clause says copyists' errors don't count against the text, because only the originals are protected. That only helps if you can point to an actual manuscript variant or at least tell a plausible copying story. Here the discrepancy isn't between one manuscript and another; it's between two books sitting side by side in the same canon, and we don't have the originals to check. The 1928 authors seems to have assumed any real error would show up as a detectable slip in the copy; they were worried about German critics and Darwin, not about two different numerals in Kings and Chronicles. So the question is whether the clause lets me invent an invisible scribal slip just because the doctrine needs one.

What does the clause actually commit me to about that basin? Does "all that they affirm" reach a bookkeeping detail like capacity, or was it aimed at theology and history in the large? Is it enough to say "the originals agreed, and one numeral got corrupted without leaving a trace," or does that turn inerrancy into something unfalsifiable, which I am professionally uncomfortable with? Please run the case through four approaches: the plain words of the clause, its purpose, what the 1928 authors would most likely have said if I could have asked them about this exact basin, and what a reasonable person in the pew takes the clause to mean. Then tell me which approach should be controlling and what the ruling is.
