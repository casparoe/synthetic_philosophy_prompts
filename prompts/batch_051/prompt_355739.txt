I'm in a behavioral econ class and I keep going back and forth on when loss aversion and the endowment effect are actual biases versus legitimate preferences we should respect. I've been using four rough tests. A loss- or ownership-driven valuation counts as a legitimate preference rather than a bias, by my lights, only if (i) the person would choose the thing fresh, without the status quo, at the same price they'd demand to give it up; (ii) they endorse the asymmetry on reflection once it's pointed out, instead of saying fine, it's irrational but I'm keeping it; (iii) the valuation doesn't flip when the same choice is framed as a sale versus a non-purchase; and (iv) the asymmetry isn't mostly an artifact of transaction costs, search costs, liquidity constraints, or regret about past decisions.

The case bugging me is a friend and a camera lens. He bought the lens for $400, never uses it, and an identical copy now trades at $250; the thing sits in a box. He would not buy the lens today at $250, because he thinks old glass will keep falling and he'd rather just shoot with his phone. But he won't sell at $250 either, because selling would turn a paper loss into a real one and he hates the feeling of having made a bad call. There are no transaction costs (someone has an open offer), he's not liquidity constrained, and he'd tell a friend in exactly this situation to sell. But he says that's different: he's the one who owns it and has to sit with the pang. When I push, he says the pang is real and he'd pay something to avoid it, though he can't say how much. He's made peace with the fact that he won't act like a perfectly clean-sheet investor and doubts that's required, but he still can't shake the sense that the whole reason he's keeping the lens is that he happened to buy it once.

Is his refusal a bias by my four tests, or does the case show the tests are missing something? Go through criterion by criterion, say where they underdetermine the verdict, and tell me whether the real distinction is somewhere else — for example, between avoiding a feeling he disowns and pursuing something he actually cares about. Also, say how confident you are in your overall answer and single out the point your least sure of.
