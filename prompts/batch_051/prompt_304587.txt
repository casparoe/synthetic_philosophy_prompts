As a novelist I have been circling a distinction that I do not yet trust. I call it unsponsored assertion: a sequence of words that has the full surface grammar of a claim—it invites agreement, invites contradiction, lays itself open to truth and falsity—but is not owned by any mind that could be answerable for it. The clearest case is the text produced by a large language model. When an assistant confidently volunteers a false historical date, calling it lying seems wrong, because no one intended to deceive. Calling it error seems too gentle or too cognitive, because there was no prior belief to be wrong. And calling it bullshit, in Harry Frankfurt's sense, seems to miss because Frankfurt's account is built around an agent's relationship to truth. In On Bullshit, in the passage where he separates the bullshitter from the liar, he says—if I may paraphrase—that the liar remains quietly responsive to the truth by deliberately trying to misrepresent it, whereas the bullshitter is not moved by how things really are at all; he just says whatever suits his purpose. The LLM, though, has no purpose, no self to protect, no rhetorical occasion it is trying to exploit. So my question is whether unsponsored assertion is a genuine third kind, or whether I am drawing a line inside bullshit that Frankfurt's concept can already absorb. Is the assistant more like the bullshitter than I have admitted, perhaps because the purpose can be located in the training process or the company producing the model? Or does my phrase unsponsored quietly assert something controversial—that the model is not really asserting, not really participating in the practice of claim-making—and thereby settle the philosophical question by terminology? I would like an honest assessment of whether the concept is coherent, whether it already exists under another name, what it gets right, and where it breaks.
