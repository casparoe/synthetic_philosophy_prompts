I'm a deputy city attorney in a city of about 200,000. Part of my job is to rule-of-law-check every proposed ordinance, permit condition, fee schedule, and penalty clause before it goes to council. I see ten or twelve of these a week, and I can't spend more then half an hour on any of them. The council will read at most a one-page memo, and the whole thing exists because we got sued over a retroactive fee and a disorderly-conduct rule so vague that every cop enforced their own version of it. So this is a recurring task and I need a procedure I can use every time, not a one-off opinion.

Here's the theory I roughly buy. Law is supposed to guide conduct, and the standard list of ways it can fail at that is: secret rules, retroactive burdens, vagueness, contradictions, rules that demand the impossible, rules that change every five minutes, rules that don't get applied the way they're written, and ad hoc orders dressed up as general rules. Turn that list into a sequence of tests I can run in order against a draft in under thirty minutes, with plain-English questions and the standard fix language when a provision flunks. It should end in one of three outputs: approve, approve-with-these-fixes, or judgment-call-for-elected-officials with the exact judgment named. At every step, mark where I'm actually making a judgment call, not running a machine. I especially need the congruence test handled honestly — whether the announced rule and the actually-enforced rule match — because that's where we keep getting burned: a draft can be beautiful and the city still wrecks it by enforcing it sideways.

Two boundaries, stated explicitly. First, the checklist should say out loud that a rule can be clear, stable, prospective, and evenly enforced and still be substantively terrible, and that killing it then is a policy fight, not a rule-of-law defect. Second, don't let retroactivity be a blunt weapon: the problem is retroactive burdens and surprises, not retroactive relief, so waiving last year's permit fees shouldn't flunk the way a new fine on last year's conduct would.

Then run the procedure on three things actually on my desk. (a) A sidewalk-vending ordinance that bans vending "near" any school, requires an applicant to show "good character" and "suitable equipment," and lets the city manager revoke a permit for "conduct unbecoming a vendor." (b) The mayor's favorite: a chronic-noise-property rule where three police calls about noise at an address in a year put the landlord on a list, every later call is a $500 fine on the landlord, and after six calls the owner loses their rental registration, no hearing, even if a tenant caused the noise and the owner never knew. (c) A rule I hate: you can't renew a city business license if you have two or more unpaid parking tickets older than 90 days. Run this last one honestly, because my instinct is to call it a rule-of-law violation and I suspect the procedure should tell me it isnt, and that I have to fight it as plain bad policy instead.
