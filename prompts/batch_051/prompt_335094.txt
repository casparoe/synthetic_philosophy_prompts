Here's one I keep going back and forth on. In the 1920s, American city streets basicly got redefined: before that, pedestrians crossed wherever they liked, kids played in the road, and the car was the one expected to watch out. Then auto clubs, car dealers, and traffic engineers ran a campaign to shift the blame, popularized the word "jaywalker" as an insult (a "jay" was a rube, a hayseed), lobbied for ordinances against crossing mid-block, and got jaywalking written into law as a real offense. I know the standard line that this was corporate interest dressed up as safety. What I want is a careful analysis through the lens of the idea that everyday conventions—queueing, tipping, walking on one side of the sidewalk—are morally binding because they solve coordination problems and create legitimate expectations, against the rival idea that a convention only binds if it's fair and freely adopted, not a norm pushed by interested parties. Did the anti-jaywalking campaign create a new moral norm, or only a legal/conventional one? Were pedestrians who kept crossing wherever they pleased doing something wrong before the norm changed, or only after, and wrong in what sense? Does it show that some "conventions" are imposed and shouldnt bind anyone, or that even imposed conventions acquire moral force once widely accepted? And what does it do to the claim that visitors have to follow the local rules? Where does the convention/morality distinction hold up, and where does it collapse?
