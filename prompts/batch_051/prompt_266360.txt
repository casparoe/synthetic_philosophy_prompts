Some of my coworkers are convinced that interpretability is finding "real" representations inside networks, and some are convinced it's all just correlation-spotting. The two camps, as far as I can tell, are using different tests. Camp 1 (informational): a hidden unit or feature direction represents a property P of the input if its activation reliably carries information about P — high mutual information, a clean linear probe, that kind of thing. Camp 2 (causal): information is too cheap for that. A unit only represents P if it's load-bearing — if you intervene on it (clamp it, ablate it, resample it), the network's behavior on P-relevant outputs changes accordingly.

Can you build me a concrete, small, trainable network (no hand-coded weights, no lookup tables, nothing I couldn't train on my laptop in an afternoon) with a specific hidden unit that makes these two camps come apart: it passes the informational test for P with flying colors but completely fails the causal test for P. Spel out the architecture, training data, what P is, and what the probe shows vs. what the intervention shows. Then run the check: what each camp says about this unit and why, which single feature of the setup is doing the work, what happens if I remove that feature (do the camps snap back to agreeing?), and how a defender of the informational camp would attack the conclusion that this unit was never a real representation. And make the divergence fall out of the training/setup rather than being stipulated, otherwise it won't settle the argument.
