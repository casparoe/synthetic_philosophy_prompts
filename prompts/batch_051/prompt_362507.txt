I'm a philosophy of biology PhD student, and I'm TAing an undergrad course on the evolution of cooperation (about 24 students, one 80-minute session). I want an in-class game that makes the students feel the force of the idea that cooperation isn't a moral stance so much as a fragile, conditional equilibrium: in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma mutual cooperation is jointly rational but individually self-defeating, in an iterated game it can become individually rational once the shadow of the future is long enough, and a little noise changes which strategies actually survive. I'm imagining three phases, but redesign if you see something better: (1) a fast one-shot round with index cards and random pairings so the dominance of defection is viscerally obvious; (2) an iterated round with a fixed partner and an uncertain stopping rule (roll a die after each round, a 6 ends the pairing, otherwise play again) so they see why the uncertainty blocks the backward induction unraveling; and (3) a tournament where students circulate holding strategy cards — tit-for-tat, always-defect, always-cooperate, grudger, maybe tit-for-two-tats and generous tit-for-tat — playing each other for points, with the lowest scorers periodically replaced by copies of the highest scorers so strategy frequencies evolve in the room. Materials are just index cards, dice, and a chalkboard leaderboard; no real money; payoffs can be points with something like R=3, S=0, T=5, P=1. Time blocks: short setup, 15 minutes for the one-shot phase, 25 for the iterated/tournament work, and the rest for debrief.

The debrief is where I want the real payoff. I want it to map specific moments of the game onto the formal machinery without me having to lecture it: the continuation probability as the discount factor/shadow of the future, why tit-for-tat's score collapses after a single accidental defection unless the rule has forgiveness built in, what the replacement step can and cannot model about real natural selection (no genes, no kinship, no spatial structure, and 80 minutes instead of eons), and why the one-shot outcome shouldn't be read as the students being selfish. Be frank about which feature of the idea the classroom cannot reproduce and the facilitator has to supply. And tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I'm already imagining pairs colluding to split points, a grudge cycle taking over phase two because someone misread a card and an accidental defection occured, and students leaving with "be nice" as the moral of the story rather than conditional retaliation plus forgiveness. I'd rather have a plan for those mistakes than improvise one in front of 24 undergrads.
