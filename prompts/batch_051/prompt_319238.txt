Okay so I got into this stupidly long argument with my friend last night about William James’s pragmatist theory of truth, and it ended with him saying I’d get destroyed if I ever tried to defend the view in front of actual Peirce people. So here I am.

Next week I have to give a seminar presentation defending the view in this passage from William James, *Pragmatism*, Lecture VI:

"The truth of an idea is not a stagnant property inherent in it. Truth happens to an idea. It becomes true, is made true by events. Its verity is in fact an event, a process: the process namely of its verifying itself, its veri-fication. Its validity is the process of its valid-ation."

The audience is mostly Peirce-leaning: two faculty who work on Peirce’s theory of inquiry and the idea that truth is what would be agreed upon in the long run, one philosopher of science who keeps saying truth is whatever a completed science would settle on, and a few grad students who think the correspondence theory is just obvious. I have my own sketch of an argument: James is not saying truth is whatever feels good or whatever you want to believe; he’s saying the property of being true is not a static relation but something realized in a process of verification, and that this process is what “agreement with reality” amounts to. But my friend thinks I’m missing Peirce’s point about truth being independent of any particular inquirer’s satisfaction, and he says the audience will smell that immediately.

Give me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections this audience will actually raise, in roughly the order they’ll come, phrased the way they’d phrase them, with what I should concede, what I should hold, and the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss.
