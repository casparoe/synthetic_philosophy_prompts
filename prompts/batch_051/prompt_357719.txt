A science writer I follow summarizes Cox's theorem as follows:

"Cox proved that any rational system for reasoning under uncertainty must be ordinary probability theory. He started from a few minimal, commonsense requirements — that degrees of belief be representable by real numbers and comparable, that logically equivalent propositions receive the same degree of belief, and that the degree of belief in a conjunction be determined by the degrees of belief in its components together with the relevant conditional degree — and from these alone the sum and product rules of probability follow as theorems rather than postulates. Since the requirements are ones any rational agent would accept, every coherent reasoner is thereby committed to Bayesianism. Formalisms such as Dempster–Shafer theory or fuzzy logic, which depart from the probability calculus, must violate one of these requirements and can be dismissed as incoherent. This is not a pragmatic choice among rival formalisms; Cox proved that probability is the unique correct logic of partial belief."

I am inclined to find the conclusion attractive, but I suspect a mathematical theorem cannot legitimately settle that much. Is this a fair summary of what Cox's theorem establishes? Please separate the accurate core from what is overstated or subtly conflated, indicate what a careful defender of a Cox-style argument would actually claim, and say which of the summary's conclusions survive the correction.
