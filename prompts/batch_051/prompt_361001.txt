Me and a friend keep having the same arguement about whether you could ever build a robot or an app that figures out what someone actually wants just from watching what they do — the way people talk about AI learning preferences from behavior. He thinks behavior is basically the whole story (you did it, so you wanted it, end of). I think there are cases where what you do and what you want come apart, and the behavior actively misleads you. Before I read any more blog posts on this, I want the spec: what does a theory of "getting a person's real preferences out of their behavior" have to deliver to count as adequate? Give me the full requirements list, each one tied to a concrete case or scenario that makes it a requirement, not just a label. Cover at least: habit and addiction, doing something because you're tired or scared or out of options, caring about the process and not just the outcome, changing your mind once you learn more, self-deception, acting one way to hide what you want from other people, people performing or gaming things because they know they're being watched, wanting to want something (or wanting not to want it), and whatever else you think belongs on the list. Then flag which of these are non-negotiable — any adequate theory has to get them right — and which are more like someone's hobbyhorse, and tell me which single requirement causes the most trouble for the simple "what you do is what you want" view. Keep the weight on the criteria themselves; I'm not looking for a scorecard of named approaches.
