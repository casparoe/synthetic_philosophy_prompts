I'm putting together a short film-and-philosophy unit and I want to pair Christopher Nolan's *Interstellar* with a discussion of strong longtermism. Strong longtermism is a view debated in recent ethics according to which the primary criterion for evaluating actions is their expected influence on the far future. The idea is that the number of people who could exist in the future vastly exceeds the number alive now, and that many of our choices—especially choices about existential risk, technology, and the preservation of civilization—could alter the long-run trajectory of intelligent life, so even very small probabilities of affecting that trajectory should tend to dominate comparatively certain benefits to the present. *Interstellar* seems like a natural text for this: Earth is failing, and the characters repeatedly weigh present ties against long-term survival, whether in Cooper's choice to leave his daughter, the decades lost to time dilation on Miller's planet, Professor Brand's two strategies for preserving humanity, or the embryo colony that would allow the species to continue without saving any particular person now alive. I'd like a reading that works in both directions: where does the film genuinely dramatize strong longtermism, and where does it resist, complicate, or quietly undercut the lens?

I am especially interested in the film's treatment of love and family loyalty as forces that are presented as more than mere biases or obstacles. Several of its most emphasized moments suggest that love has a kind of epistemic and even physical significance—that it reaches across time and space and guides characters toward choices that turn out to serve the long run. Does the film thereby imply that personal devotion and far-future consequences ultimately converge? Is that convergence a serious challenge to an impersonal, expected-consequence picture of longtermist morality, or does it hide the real costs? Alternatively, does the film demonstrate that strong longtermism is incomplete for finite agents, who must act from particular attachments and cannot literally hold the whole future before their minds? Ideally, the reading should end by saying what *Interstellar* adds to the philosophical debate that argument alone cannot: what does a film show about sustaining commitment to a future one will not live to see that a premise-and-conclusion argument does not?
