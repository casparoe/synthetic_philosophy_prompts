A few of us from my office have been meeting for a couple of years as an informal reading and discussion group. None of us has any formal background in philosophy; we pick topics from books and everyday life and talk until we get stuck. At our last meeting, the conversation turned to the familiar question "What if everybody did that?" Two members of the group, Lena and Dev, ended up in a long disagreement about whether it is a reliable moral test, and I've been replaying their exchange in my head ever since.

Lena opened by saying the question deserved more respect than it usually gets. If you are unsure whether an action is permissible, ask whether you could still function in a world where everyone followed the same policy. If such a world would collapse, then your action amounts to making a secret exception of yourself. Dev pushed back that the test gives plainly wrong results. If everyone became a nurse, society would break down—nobody would grow food, build houses, or teach children—but no reasonable person thinks it is wrong for any particular individual to train as a nurse. Lena said he was applying the test to the wrong thing. You do not ask what would happen if everyone performed the same outward action; you ask whether the reason behind your choice could be turned into a general rule. A rule like "each person should pursue work suited to their abilities, provided the basic needs of society continue to be met" fits fine, whereas a rule like "lie whenever it is convenient" fails, because a world in which everyone followed that rule would destroy the very trust that lying depends on. Dev came back that once a rule gets loaded with conditions like "provided the basic needs of society continue to be met," the test simply returns whatever answer has been smuggled into the description. A chronic shoplifter could describe what he does as "take only when I need the item more than the owner does and no one is significantly harmed," and the test offers no independent way to disqualify that description. It is supposed to expose special pleading, but now it depends entirely on how cleverly one writes the rule. Lena replied that this is a complaint about dishonest description, not about the test itself. Any standard can be misused, and if someone finds himself adding clauses to guarantee a private exception, that is precisely the special pleading the question is there to expose. She added a case: if you cut across a newly planted lawn because you are late, the policy you are actually relying on is something like "I may damage shared property whenever I am in a hurry," and a world in which that were the general policy would soon leave you with no lawn to cut across. Dev said that very case showed the deeper difficulty. The problem is not that everyone else will copy one person's shortcut; it is that the shortcut-taker enjoys a background in which most other people respect the grass. The question "what if everyone did that?" pretends each choice is made against a fixed background of general compliance, while many real moral questions are about whether to do your small part in a pattern where others are already failing or free-riding. He also noted that the verdicts flip depending on how high or low one sets the level of description: "everyone may leave a single light on overnight" could be harmless, while "everyone may ignore energy waste as they see fit" could be disastrous, and the test itself gives no instruction about which level to choose. Lena answered that the question was never meant to be a machine that yields judgments without any act of judgment. It is a device for catching inconsistencies—for exposing that you could not sincerely accept your own policy as general practice—and it makes no sense to criticize a tool for requiring honesty and judgment in its use.

I could not tell who was right. Each time one of them seemed to land a decisive point, the other's reply seemed to absorb it. Could you go through the exchange step by step and say which of Dev's objections actually damage the test, which of Lena's replies succeed, and what, if anything, they are both missing? I would also like to know who is more right overall, or whether the sensible view lies somewhere between them.
