Two friends of mine got into it at a bar over a thought experiment one of them had half-remembered from a philosophy class, and they spent the evening trying to get me to referee; I didn't have a settled view, so I'm passing it along. The setup was a person hooked up to a machine with a dial that runs from 0 to 1000. Turning the dial up one notch makes the electric shock it delivers just barely worse—too small a change to notice—and pays out another $10,000. At 0 there's no pain. At 1000 the pain is, by anyone's lights, torture, and nobody would accept the whole 0-to-1000 jump for the whole pile of money even with the payout. The puzzle is that at any intermediate step it seems rational to take the next increment (free money, no detectable downside), but doing that all the way up lands you in a state you'd never of chosen.

Rachel said this shows rational preferences can fail transitivity: each adjacent setting is preferred to the one just below it, but the starting point is preferred to the endpoint, and that's just a fact about fine-grained choices rather than a defect. Marcus said that can't be right—if 0 beats 1000, then somewhere along the line it stopped being worth it, even if the person couldn't feel exactly where, so the rational move is to refuse the sequence up front or pick a cutoff and stick to it. Rachel shot back that any cutoff is arbitrary from the inside: standing at 436 with your hand on the dial, you can't give a reason to stop that didn't equally apply at 435, so your only consistent positions are refusing the very first step or sliding all the way to torture. Marcus said that's the old heap fallacy—a bunch of tiny changes can add up to a real one, and "can't feel the difference" isn't the same as "there is no difference"; you can be rational to decline the whole march even while no particular step is rejectable in isolation. Rachel said that just relocates the problem to the exact moment of action: if no step is rejectable in isolation, what does the person actually do when they're at 436? "Stop somewhere" isn't a policy, it's a shrug. Marcus replied that the person is allowed to act on a higher-order policy—avoiding a foreseeably awful endpoint—instead of re-deciding each step from scratch, and that this is what vague preferences look like: there's no precise threshold, but there are clear cases at 0 and 1000. Rachel said that's her conclusion with better branding, since a preference relation with no adjacent reversal and a reversal between endpoints just is a non-transitive ranking whatever you call it; Marcus said no, he denies each adjacent step is favored, he only denies the agent can tell which one isn't.

Please adjudicate this turn by turn: say who is right about which precise point, where each of them overclaims or misses the other's point, and who is more right overall if one had to choose. Also say whether there's a coherent account of rational choice that avoids both intransitive preferences and arbitrary cutoffs, or whether one of those is unavoidable.
