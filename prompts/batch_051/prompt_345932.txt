I spent most of my career as a research scientist fitting models to noisy observational data, and I retired with a rule of thumb that I am only now, after some reading in the philosophy of causation, beginning to question. When a set of measured correlations could be explained by several different causal structures, I always chose the structure with the fewest direct causal links, and I treated that structure as the one to develop, test, and act on until the data forced a revision. I took this to be ordinary scientific parsimony. Reading about causal Bayes nets has persuaded me that the rule may conflate two different principles, and I would like your judgment about whether the rule is defensible.

Let me lay out the distinction as I currently understand it, so that you can correct me if my grasp of it is off. A causal Bayes net is a directed acyclic graph whose arrows represent direct causal influence, together with a probability distribution over the variables. The graph entails a set of conditional independence relations, roughly that each variable is independent of its non-descendants given its parents. The faithfulness condition says that the independencies present in the data are exactly the ones entailed by the graph; in particular, it rules out a measured independence arising as an artifact of two causal pathways that cancel each other's influence. The minimality condition, as I understand it, is weaker: it says only that the graph contains no arrow whose removal would destroy its ability to reproduce the observed pattern of dependence and independence. A minimal graph can be unfaithful to the true causal structure, and a faithful graph can contain arrows that minimality would strip out.

My career-long policy was, in effect, an operational version of minimality: among all graphs that could reproduce the dependencies I measured, keep the one with the fewest arrows, and treat the rest as uninteresting until new data revived them. I now see that this policy amounts to betting against exact cancellations, and I never had good evidence for that bet. Is this a defensible epistemic habit, or was it a taste for clean diagrams masquerading as an inference rule? Under what conditions would an edge-minimal graph tend to be the true graph, or at least the most predictively useful one, and under what conditions would it systematically mislead me? Does the fact that faithfulness itself is a substantive assumption, and may often fail, make minimality the more honest default, or does it merely hide the same leap of faith in a less visible place? And could the policy be restated so that it keeps the practical advantages—a definite graph to work with, a clear program of experiments, protection against overfitting—without protecting me from causal structures that are real but parsimonious-unfriendly?

Please answer with a verdict on the policy rather than only a lesson in the formalism. I ask you to indicate how confident you are in each of your main claims, and to single out the one point of your answer that you are least sure about.
