I'm retired from behavioral neurobiology and have spent the last couple years reading philosophy — so please bare with me if I'm not using the terminology quite right. I've been chewing on the question whether novelty, adventure, the fear of a boring life, is actually a component of well-being. I've got a half-formed essay and I'd like your comments on the idea before I write the full thing.

Here's the idea. Most philosophical accounts of well-being treat a good life as a set of states (pleasure, achievement, friendship) or the satisfaction of preferences a person already has. But the neural systems that track reward don't respond to nice states; they respond to deviations from prediction. A reward prediction error is a signal about the difference between what you expected and what you got — and the response fades as the reward becomes predictable. The rat pressing a lever for a pellet still gets the pellet every time, but the dopaminergic response doesn't stay at a constant high; it largely shifts to earlier predictive cues and eventually quiets down. So the felt goodness of a life may depend on change itself, not on an amount of good stuff. If novelty isn't one more item on the objective list, and it isn't merely a means to avoid the pain of boredom, maybe it's a background condition — the machinary that lets any state register as good at all. A life with zero novelty is a life where the affect system has flatlined, so nothing feels good for long. And then the fear of a boring life isn't one preference among others; it's sensitivity to a precondition of valuing.

But I can already see the objection: I'm conflating feeling good with a good life. A monk deep in meditation has very little prediction error and may be doing fine; my rat-pellet story shows only that feeling good is a treadmill, not that novelty belongs in a theory of well-being. It could just be an empirical fact about mood, beside the philosophical point.

So: (1) Is the step from "reward prediction error" to a claim about well-being fallacious, or is it more like saying you can't have the good without the machinery for it? (2) Is there an existing position that already says roughly this, so I'm reinventing the wheel? (3) What is the strongest objection I'm not seeing? Be blunt — I'd rather find the crack now than after I've written it.
