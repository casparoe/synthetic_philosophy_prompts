I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've been kicking around an essay on why public arguments feel so broken lately. The phrase everyone reaches for is "good faith," as in "the other side isn't arguing in good faith." But Ive realized I can't actually pin down what the phrase means, and I suspect it's doing at least two jobs at once: describing someone's honesty, and also just flagging "arguments I find frustrating." I'd like a working definition of arguing in good faith that I could actually defend in print.

Here are the cases I keep testing it against. A city councilwoman who opposes a housing project because she genuinely believes it will lower property values, and has never looked at the evidence. A cable news host who interviews only guests from one side, but says he's just representing his audience. A guy in a comments section who brings up a study he knows has been retracted because it still makes his point. A climate skeptic who has spent years reading contrarian blogs and sincerely trusts them. A debater who relentlessly steelmans the other side's arguments in private but in public summarizes them in the weakest possible form, because it's more effective. And a teenager who says inflammatory stuff largely to get a reaction, not because he has any settled view at all. I'm pretty confident the comments-section guy is bad faith and the climate skeptic isn't, and I genuinely don't know about the cable host and the teenager. Propose a definition, run it through all six, and tell me where it forces you to say something unintuitive.
