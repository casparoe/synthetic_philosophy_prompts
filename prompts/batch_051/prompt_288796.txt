I keep thinking about something a friend said in a discussion about how to read Scripture after historical criticism. She compared it to finding a stack of love letters in a historical archive. A historian can establish when each letter was written, in what dialect, under what circumstances, and to whom; a biographer can use them as evidence about the author’s life; but the person who was actually addressed could still read the same sentences as a personal, binding address. She used the analogy to argue that historical criticism and faith are not enemies: the scholar determines what the text meant in its original setting, and the believer receives it as meaning them too. I want to know how far this really holds. Give me the correspondence table: what corresponds to the letter, the archive, the historian, the original recipient, the later reader, the author, and the fact that there are multiple letters. Then show me where the analogy breaks, especially around the facts that biblical texts are not a single correspondence, often lack identifiable single authors, were edited and redacted over centuries, and may have been composed as public liturgy or law rather than personal address. Tell me whether her conclusion survives the disanalogies, or whether the analogy was doing the work the whole time.
