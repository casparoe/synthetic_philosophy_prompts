I spent my career in a lab—physical chemistry, mostly kinetics—so I'm new to reading novels this closely. I retired a few years ago and a friend got me into The Brothers Karamazov, and the Grand Inquisitor chapter has been buzzing in my head ever since. I keep trying to articulate what bothers me about it: the cardinal makes a genuinely strong case that most people can't bear freedom and would trade it for a smaller, safer happiness—bread, a mystery to bow to, and an authority to take the terrible decisions off their hands—and that his church has been correcting Christ's mistake out of love. But then Christ just kisses him, and the old man lets Him go. It feels like the kiss is doing some kind of work without making an argument, and I want to see that tension put into verse.

Could you write a sonnet in the voice of the Grand Inquisitor, spoken to the silent Christ in the prison cell? The octave should compress the Inquisitor's actual argument, not a strawman of it: the refusal of the three temptations, the claim that Christ overestimated human beings, that ordinary people would gladly hand over the burden of free choice in exchange for security and a bowable mystery, and that the church has done all this out of love. The volta, after the eighth line, should be the kiss, or the moment the Inquisitor sees it coming and cant answer it. The closing lines should leave the thing unresolved: the argument is never refuted, but the kiss shows something the argument cant touch. A reader who knows Dostoevsky should recognize this as the chapter they read, not a generic freedom-versus-security poem. Please write in American English, and make the literal last line of your response a prose sentence beginning 'Summary:' that states in one sentence what the poem is doing.
