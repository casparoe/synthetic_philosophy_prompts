I keep noticing a structural parallel between Chomsky's poverty-of-the-stimulus argument and the no-free-lunch theorems in machine learning, and I can't tell whether it is deep or just a shared silhouette. Chomsky's argument, roughly, is that the linguistic data a child hears are compatible with indefinitely many grammars, yet children reliably converge on the same one, so the child must bring strong built-in constraints that do much of the selecting. The no-free-lunch results say, again roughly, that averaged over all possible target functions, no learning algorithm generalizes better than chance, so any learner that generalizes well on real tasks must be carrying task-specific inductive biases. Both move from underdetermination by data to the conclusion that the learner's internal structure is doing real work — which sounds like a precise, modern restatement of the rationalist claim that there is innate knowledge, or at least innate structure. Is this a genuine identity of structure, a loose analogy, or a confusion? I want the mapping made explicit: what in each argument plays the role of the hypothesis space, the data, the convergence claim, and the innate contribution. What would have to be true on both sides for a result to transfer from one to the other? And where does the parallel break — what does one argument give us that the other cannot, or what would one side predict that the other rejects?
