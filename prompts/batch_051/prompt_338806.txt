I'm grading a final for a social epistemology class and I'd like a second set of eyes on one answer before I put a grade on it. Here's the question:

Explain the discursive dilemma (doctrinal paradox) and what it shows about whether a group can hold consistent collective judgments. Use the standard three-judge contract case.

And here's the student answer:

The discursive dilemma occurs when every member of a group has internally consistent judgments, but the group's judgments, aggregated by majority voting on each issue, turn out inconsistent. In the standard case, three judges must decide whether a defendant is liable for breach of contract. Liability requires both that the contract was valid (P) and that the defendant breached it (Q). Judge 1 holds P but not Q, so votes not liable. Judge 2 holds Q but not P, so votes not liable. Judge 3 holds both P and Q, so votes liable. A majority holds P (judges 1 and 3) and a majority holds Q (judges 2 and 3), but a majority rejects liability (judges 1 and 2). So the group accepts the premises yet rejects the conclusion that follows from them. This shows that a group can be irrational even when each individual is rational. The fix is premise-based aggregation: have the group vote only on the premises and let the conclusion be whatever follows logically. That restores consistency and, unlike conclusion-based voting, it has no real downside, so it is clearly the right procedure. Some people confuse this with Condorcet's paradox, but the two are completely unrelated.
