I keep going back and forth on the problem of old evidence, and I've boiled it down to an argument that feels way too clean:

(1) On the standard Bayesian account, a piece of evidence E confirms a hypothesis H just in case P(H|E) > P(H).

(2) But P(H|E) > P(H) can happen only when P(E) < 1. If P(E) = 1, conditioning on E leaves everything exactly where it was, so P(H|E) = P(H).

(3) Old evidence is evidence you already knew before H was ever on the table.

(4) If you already knew E, then your probability for E was 1.

(5) So for any old evidence E, P(H|E) is not greater than P(H).

(6) Therefore old evidence can never confirm anything.

(7) But that seems obviously wrong: the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion was known for decades before anyone formulated general relativity, and it's standardly treated as a major confirmation of the theory.

So is this argument valid? If not, tell me exactly which step fails and on what reading. If it is valid, then what should a Bayesian do—is the real problem that the definition of confirmation in (1) is too cramped, or do we just have to bite the bullet on (6)? I keep feeling the math is airtight and the mess has to be in the word "confirms," but I can't make it precise.
