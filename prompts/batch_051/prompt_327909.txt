For the past year I have been reporting on how the life-sciences community handles dual-use research, and next month I am giving a presentation to the biosafety advisory council of a large private research funder. I will be defending a specific proposal: that for a tightly defined class of experiments in synthetic virology, the decision to publish a detailed protocol or share key methods outside a vetted circle should no longer belong to the originating laboratory alone. A lab in the funder's network would submit a short statement of what it intends to disclose and why it judges the expected value positive; a joint committee working from pooled information would then clear the disclosure or issue a hold with explicit reasons, and the lab could not proceed while the hold stood. My core argument is the unilateralist's curse. As I understand it, the curse arises when many well-meaning actors can each, on their own, trigger an irreversible outcome that affects everyone, and each decides by asking only whether their private estimate of the action's value is positive. If the estimates are noisy draws around a true value that is negative — the pooled judgment of the field would be do not disclose — then as the number of labs grows, the probability that at least one lab's estimate lands above the action threshold climbs toward certainty, and one positive fluke is enough to trigger the disclosure. The thesis, then, is that an individual expected-value test is the wrong decision rule for this class of experiments, and that a low-cost mandatory consultation gate is the least intrusive fix consistent with what the curse teaches.

The room will hold about fifteen people, and I know their temperatures in advance. Nine are working scientists — virologists, synthetic biologists, a genome engineer — whose standing worry is that any consultation requirement will slow vaccine and countermeasure work or leak methods out of the lab. Two are biotech patent lawyers who think the live problem is that preprints already outrun institutional safety review, and that the right frame is liability and contract, not decision theory. Two are historians of science, one of whom has written on mid-century physics secrecy and is suspicious of formal models being used to override expert autonomy. One is an epistemologist who will be friendly to the argument but merciless about its modeling assumptions. The chair is an evolutionary biologist who is sympathetic in principle but wants to hear why the rule will not creep until it strangles ordinary virology. For the sake of their objections, my model is that each lab holds an independent noisy estimate around a shared true value, that labs are acting in good faith, and that disclosure is irreversible once made.

Give me a Q&A prep sheet for this presentation. I want the objections in roughly the order this particular room will actually raise them, phrased the way those people would put them, with a judgment about which objections are merely atmospherics and which ones hit a load-bearing step of the argument. For each objection, tell me what to concede up front, what I should hold, and the version of the reply that can actually be delivered in two minutes. Flag the softest point in my own position — the thing I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss — and tell me the question I should be praying nobody asks, and why that question could sink the proposal rather than merely scuff it.
