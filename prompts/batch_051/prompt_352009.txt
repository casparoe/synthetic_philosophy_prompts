I was at a barbecue last weekend when two mates of mine got into a long argument about a third friend, Dan, and neither of them managed to force a concession. I'm still not sure who's right, so I want a proper referee.

(1) Marta started by saying Dan is self-deceived about his ex. He tells everyone he's completely over her and just wants to stay mates, but he's rearranged his week to bump into her, he gets twitchy when she sees someone else, and he rereads thier old texts. Marta's claim was that Dan honestly believes he's over her, and that's exactly what makes it self-deception rather than lying.

(2) Sam said that can't be right, not if you take the term seriously. Deceiving someone means you know the truth and you deliberately plant a false belief in someone who doesn't know it. For Dan to deceive himself he'd have to be the one in the know and the one in the dark at the same time and in the same respect. One mind can't occupy both roles, so self-deception in that strong sense is impossible. Whatever Dan is doing, it isn't that.

(3) Marta replied that Sam was assuming the mind is a single seamless agent. It isn't. Part of Dan knows he's still in love; another part keeps that from his conscious awareness. That's why the truth leaks out in jealousy and contrived run-ins. The paradox only appears if you treat the mind as a whole rather than as a set of partly independent processes.

(4) Sam said this just relocates the problem into homunculi. If you split Dan into sub-parts, then it's one sub-part deceiving another sub-part, not Dan deceiving Dan, and now you owe an account of what these parts believe and why we should call one of them the real Dan. Worse, he said, we don't need the partition to explain the evidence. A deflationary story works better: a strong desire can bias how Dan searches for, weighs, and remembers evidence, so he ends up sincerely believing he's over her without any hidden knowledge or intention to fool himself. Self-deception is possible, but only as motivated false belief, not as a trick the self plays on itself.

(5) Marta answered that this hollows the word out. If self-deception is just believing what you want to believe, then every teenager convinced his band is about to make it is self-deceived, and the interesting phenomenon has disappeared. Deception requires someone doing the deceiving, and Sam's story has no one. All he's described is wishful thinking with a longer causal chain.

(6) Sam came back: the person doing the deceiving is Dan, not some homunculus, because Dan's own motives do the biasing. And there's a real difference between Dan and the teenager: Dan would spot the exact same pattern in another person in five minutes, but he misses it in his own case. That asymmetry between how he reads himself and how he reads others is what earns the word self-deception and separates it from ordinary stupidity or ignorance.

(7) Marta said that asymmetry still doesn't give Sam the strong sense he started out denying. The fact Dan would catch it in others shows he has the concept and access to the evidence, not that some part of him already believes the truth and is hiding it. So Sam has surrendered the only feature that made self-deception look like deception — knowledge held while denied. That leaves a dilemma: the strict kind is impossible, and the loose kind is just motivated irrationality and shouldn't be called self-deception at all. Either the concept is incoherent, or it's doing no work.

(8) Sam replied that this is a merely terminological conclusion wearing philosophical clothes. The word self-deception has a clear ordinary use — Dan's case is exactly the sort of thing people mean — and a deflationary account makes sense of that use. Rejecting it because there's no explicit intention to deceive is just insisting on a straw man. The strong version was never needed; Marta is complaining that the real thing isn't the impossible thing.
