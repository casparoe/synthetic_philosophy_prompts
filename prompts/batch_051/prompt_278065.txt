I am caught between a theory I find myself defending and an intuition I cannot manage to abandon, and I want a negotiated settlement, not a winner declared.

The theory is a graded account of moral status. Moral considerability, on this view, is not a property something either has or lacks; it comes in degrees that track graded capacities — the vividness and integration of experience, the capacity for felt valence, temporal continuity, the complexity of agency. There is no clean line anywhere on the way down from a human to a dog to a bee to a flatworm to a plant to a thermostat; there are only differences in complexity and integration, and corresponding differences in how much a thing can be wronged, harmed, or owed. Its virtues are the familiar virtues of gradualism: no arbitrary cutoff to defend against borderline cases, one unified vocabulary that treats a bee and a thermostat as low-grade instances of the same kind of thing rather than different kinds, and an honest way to say a bee matters less than a dog without saying it matters not at all.

The intuition is that somewhere between a bee and a thermostat the difference stops being one of degree and becomes one of kind. A thermostat registers temperature; a bee can be made to suffer. The sentence "there is something it is like to be this system" seems to be either true or false, not true-to-a-degree; and a graded account seems committed to saying the first flicker of something-it-is-like is merely an arbitrarily privileged point on a continuum that also runs through thermostats. But no amount of "more of the same" appears capable of getting from nothing to something. If the graded account is right, I should say the difference between a bee and a thermostat is ultimately scalar; but that is exactly the sentence I find I cannot believe.

I do not want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes — what sort of account of its source (scope insensitivity, anthropomorphism, a practical threshold mistaken for a metaphysical one, something else) would actually dislodge it, and what that debunking would require me to give up. I want to know what it would take for the graded account to be the thing that goes, or to be amended: whether the right view is a hidden binary of sentience beneath a graded surface of evidence and confidence, or a graded view with a steep nonlinearity that yields binary-looking practice. And I want to know whether there is a reading of a key phrase — "matters," "can be wronged," or "something it is like" — on which both the graded account and the qualitative boundary survive without special pleading.
