As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I keep noticing an asymmetry in how researchers treat evidence, and I have never found a satisfying justification for it. An inspector who has examined a thousand emeralds will confidently expect the next one to be green. But define 'grue' as Goodman does: an object is grue if it is examined before some future time t and is green, or is not examined before t and is blue. Every emerald examined so far is green before t, and is therefore also grue; the generalisation 'All emeralds are grue' fits the evidence exactly as well as 'All emeralds are green'. After t, the two hypotheses predict different colours for the next emerald, yet almost everyone treats only the green generalisation as confirmed. What, if anything, justifies this asymmetry?

David Hume thought the machinery behind such expectations was repetition rather than reasoning. In An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding, Section V, Part I, he writes:

"This principle is Custom or Habit. For wherever the repetition of any particular act or operation produces a propensity to renew the same act or operation, without being impelled by any reasoning or process of the understanding; we always say, that this propensity is the effect of Custom. By employing that word, we pretend not to have given the ultimate reason of such a propensity. We only point out a principle of human nature, which is universally acknowledged, and which is well known by its effects."

Hume's proposal is that custom renews 'the same act or operation' when experience repeats it. But the grue case seems to block precisely this move: the past observations are as much instances of grue as of green, so which act counts as 'the same' is settled by the vocabulary we already use, not by the evidence. I would like the strongest case for thinking the green generalisation is justified while the grue one is not — naturalness, simplicity, entrenchment, or some other candidate — and I would like it tested against the possibility of a community for which 'grue' is the old, well-entrenched predicate. If no candidate survives, should we conclude that the asymmetry is a rationally ungrounded habit we simply have, and what would inductive practice look like if we refused to privilege green?
