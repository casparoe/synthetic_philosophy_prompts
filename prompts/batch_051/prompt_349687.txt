After retiring from a career in the natural sciences, I have recently been reading political philosophy, and the problem that has most gripped me is what the field calls the boundary problem: before a people can legitimately decide anything democratically—including the terms of its own constitution—someone must determine who belongs to that people, yet that determination cannot itself be made by an already-constituted democratic vote without circularity. The leading solutions I keep encountering are the all-affected principle (roughly, that everyone whose interests are affected by a decision ought to have some say in it), the all-subjected principle (roughly, that everyone who is or will be subject to the resulting laws or to the exercise of state coercion ought to be included), and a nationality-based criterion (roughly, that the demos is the pre-existing political community, however its membership has been legally defined). Each seems plausible in the abstract, and each seems plainly wrong in some concrete case—exactly the kind of situation in which I once tested models against data.

A real case that appears to concentrate these issues is the 2014 referendum on Scottish independence. The franchise, set by the Scottish Parliament under authority agreed between the UK and Scottish governments, used residence rather than citizenship or birthplace as its operative criterion: those eligible were persons aged sixteen or over on polling day who were resident in Scotland and were British citizens, qualifying Commonwealth citizens, or citizens of another EU member state. Sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds and, for example, EU nationals living in Scotland could therefore vote, while UK citizens born in Scotland but resident in England, Wales, Northern Ireland, or abroad could not.

I would like you to analyze this episode through the lens of the boundary problem, rigorously and in both directions. First, say what each of the three principles I mentioned would have required the two governments to do when they fixed the franchise, being precise about who the relevant agents were, what options they faced, and what should count as evidence. Second, determine which principle the actual franchise most plausibly embodies, and identify both what it explains well—for instance, the inclusion of resident non-citizens and the exclusion of non-resident citizens—and what it fails to explain or determines only loosely—for instance, the lowering of the voting age to sixteen, and how the notion of residence should be understood for recent arrivals, temporary residents, or people who intended to leave. Third, run the analysis in the other direction: what does this case show about the feasibility and correctness of each candidate principle? In particular, does it show that the all-affected principle is too expansive and indeterminate to be operationalized, and that the all-subjected principle merely relocates the problem into the threshold of what counts as subjection? Finally, be explicit about where a theory of the demos is genuinely silent about something the actors had to decide, rather than forcing it to speak.
