I'm a novelist, and in my writing group the phrase 'true in the story' has become a kind of trump card, but people use it to mean opposite things. Last month someone told me that if a fact about my character never made it onto the page, it isn't 'true in the story' no matter what I intended. A week later the same person argued that another character secretly resents her sister because 'it's true in the story — it's the best explanation of the scenes we actually get.' Then it got tangled with empty names: we all say 'Sherlock Holmes doesn't exist,' and we all also say 'it's true in the stories that Sherlock Holmes is a detective.' I can't figure out what the phrase is actually claiming when it lets both of those Holmes sentences stand. Can you lay out the different precise claims 'true in the story' could be making, from the trivial to the radical, and say for each whether it's defensible and whether it licenses one, both, or neither of those moves in the writing group? I especialy want to know which reading lets the two Holmes sentences be true together, and whether that reading commits you to saying 'Sherlock Holmes' actually refers to something.
