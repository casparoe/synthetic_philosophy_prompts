I've been reading the preface to Leibniz's Codex Juris Gentium Diplomaticus, where he spells out his definition of justice as the charity of a wise person, charity as a habit of loving others, and love as taking pleasure in another's happiness. Something is bugging me about this, because my own reactions are asymmetric in a way that maybe doesn't fit. If a close friend gets wonderful news, I feel genuinely happy—it shows up in my mood even though nothing in my own life improved. If a colleague I barely know gets the exact same news, I'd say "good for him" and mean it, but I don't feel any real pleasure. And if I read about a stranger's good fortune in another country, I feel nothing at all. Leibniz's definition seems to say all three should count as love to different degrees, but the first feels like love, the second like mere approval, and the third like information. Is there a principle within Leibniz's framework—or in our own—that justifies this gradient, or is it just partiality he would want me to expunge? I also have a reverse case: when something bad happens to a stranger I barely know, I'm not as pained as when it happens to a friend, but I do feel a small dent in my day. Is the pleasure in another's happiness and the pain at another's misery the same asymmetry, or two different ones? I want the strongest Leibnizian or post-Leibnizian defense of this pattern, and an honest verdict on whether I should try to change my reactions.
