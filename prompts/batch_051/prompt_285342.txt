I have been traveling a good deal over the past few years, and I keep coming back to the same unresolved set of questions about the small rules of daily life—tipping, standing in line, crossing the street—and whether they genuinely oblige a person the way clear moral duties do, or only mark one as mannered or unmannered. I have never studied philosophy, so I would like help making sense of the pattern in my own judgments rather than receiving a theory imposed from outside. My reactions to particular cases are firm, but I cannot state the rule behind them, and I suspect that a couple of those reactions may not sit comfortably together.

Here are my honest reactions. At a sit-down restaurant in the United States, leaving no tip feels to me like positively wronging the server, not merely being cheap, even when the service was merely adequate and I know I will never return; and I think a visitor from a country without tipping who is aware of the local practice should tip somewhere near the local rate. At a fast-casual counter with a tip jar, by contrast, leaving nothing does not feel like a wrong to the counter worker, though I usually leave a dollar as a gesture. In a country where tipping is taken as an insult, I think the visitor should not tip, and that it would not be a kindness to insist on leaving one. When a strict queue has formed at a bus stop, I think pushing in front of others wrongs the people behind me, and a visitor should queue even when in a hurry. But in a place where the local practice is to crowd and jostle at the bus door, I do not feel obliged to shove, only permitted to hold my position, and I cannot tell whether the locals wrong one another or whether the convention simply does not apply because they do not understand themselves as forming a line. At an empty crosswalk at two in the morning in a city where pedestrians reliably wait for the light, I would wait and feel that a visitor should wait too, even though crossing endangered nobody and held no one up. Yet in my own city, where crossing against the light on an empty street is ordinary, a tourist who does the same seems to me to do nothing wrong. As a last test, in a place where drivers seldom yield at marked crossings and locals step into the street expecting traffic to stop, I would not feel obliged to adopt the practice, though I would wait for a genuine break in the cars.

What principle, or small set of principles, best captures these verdicts? Please state it plainly and precisely, show which case or feature motivates each part of it, and then test it on cases I have not listed. In particular, I want to know how the principle decides how a visitor is supposed to tell a binding local convention from an optional local practice, as with the tip jar; whether someone who violates a local convention out of genuine ignorance still wrongs anyone, and whether there is any duty to learn such conventions before going somewhere; and whether a convention that involves real danger or unfairness binds a visitor simply because it is local. Please be candid about which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether the trouble lies in the principle or in my verdicts. Please write your answer in American English.
