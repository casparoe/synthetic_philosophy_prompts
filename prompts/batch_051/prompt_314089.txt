Something I've been turning over: there's a fairly standard defence of conditionality in the welfare state — benefits aren't a gift, they come with reciprocal obligations, so it's legitimate to require job search or attendance on programmes and to sanction people who don't comply. Notice that this argument gets its force from actual behaviour: you didn't look for work, so your benefit gets cut. Now suppose the system shifts to predictive conditionality. At sign-up a risk model flags you as likely to become long-term unemployed (or likely to commit fraud), and purely on the strength of that score you're put on tougher work-search requirements, more frequent in-person meetings, more mandatory digital check-ins, before you've breached anything. Does the reciprocity argument survive this? I can see it either snapping (the condition is now a bet about your type, not a response to a wrong) or mutating into something else — risk pricing, or a paternalism that's fine with harassing people on the basis of predicted outcomes. What I want is the diagnosis: which premise of the classic argument actually breaks, whether it can be rebuilt at all, and what it would have to look like in its rebuilt form. Not really interested in a direct verdict on whether predictive scoring is good policy.
