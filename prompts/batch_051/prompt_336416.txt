Re-reading Lewis Carroll's "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" for a seminar on regress arguments, and I've convinced myself the Tortoise has an argument that proves way too much—but I can't shake it. The setup: Achilles has premises A and B and conclusion Z. The Tortoise accepts A and B but won't accept Z unless he also accepts the conditional "if A and B are true, Z must be true." Fine. But once he accepts that conditional as a new premise C, he just has three premises and the same problem all over again: he can accept A, B, and C and still ask why he must accept Z. Every time Achilles turns the logical link into a premise, another logical link seems needed to bind the bigger pile of premises to the conclusion. Here's the passage where the drift starts:

"I'm to force you to accept Z, am I?" Achilles said musingly. "And your present position is that you accept A and B, but you don't accept the Hypothetical—"

"Let's call it C," said the Tortoise.

"—but you don't accept (C) If A and B are true, Z must be true."

"That is my present position," said the Tortoise.

"Then I must ask you to accept C."

"I'll do so," said the Tortoise, "as soon as you've entered it in that note-book of yours. What else have you got in it?"

The argument as I reconstruct it: (1) accepting the premises of a valid argument never by itself forces you to accept the conclusion; (2) to be forced, you must also accept a further proposition saying the conclusion follows from the premises; (3) but that further proposition just becomes another premise, and now you need a still further proposition saying the conclusion follows from this larger set; (4) so the demand never terminates; and therefore (5) no one is ever logically compelled to accept any conclusion at all. That conclusion seems to prove too much: if it were right, "valid" would describe a taste rather than a binding relation, and any finite proof would be a kind of charade. But I can't locate the bad step—the Tortoise never asserts anything false, and at each move Achilles seems to have no reply except writing another conditional in the notebook. Where does the argument go wrong, if it goes wrong? Is the regress vicious here, or is it harmless and the mistake lies somewhere else? I'd rather keep the idea that modus ponens genuinely binds, but I want to know what I'm paying.
