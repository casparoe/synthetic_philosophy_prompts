So I'm an engineer, not a philosopher, but I've noticed a weird asymmetry in how we talk about intent at work and I can't tell if it's defensible or just a bug in how people assign blame. Suppose two PMs each run a redesign of the same checkout page. In both cases the PM says, word for word, "I don't care about screen reader support, I just want to ship." Team A's change accidentally breaks screen reader support, so blind users can no longer complete a purchase. Team B's change accidentally improves screen reader support because a refactor happened to clean up the ARIA labels. Afterwards, pretty much everyone at the company says the Team A PM intentionally harmed blind users. But almost nobody says the Team B PM intentionally helped them. The PMs said the same thing, cared equally little, and did the work for the same reason. The only difference is whether the side effect was harmful or helpful. Is there a principle that would make that asymmetry correct, or is it just that we moralize harmful side effects and then call them intentional? Give me the strongest defense of the asymmetry you can, and test it against a case where the only difference is the sign of the outcome. If you can't defend it, say so plainly and tell me what we should change about the way we make these judgments.
