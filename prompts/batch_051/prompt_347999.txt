In my ancient ethics class we've been reading the Stoics, and I keep noticing something strange about how the people around me actually talk about being unbothered. We praise someone who loses a job and shrugs — that's equanimity, being philosophical. But if the same person shrugs at, say, a factory collapse in another country that kills dozens of people, we call them cold and callous. Both are cases of not getting knocked around by things outside your control; the Stoics even gave a name to something like that, apatheia, a kind of freedom from being pushed around by the passions. So why is the first a virtue and the second a vice?

My first thought was that the second case involves other people's suffering and the first is only your own, but that can't be the whole story, because we also admire a surgeon or a firefighter who stays calm while someone is dying in front of them — we say the calm is what lets them help. So it's not just "other people." Maybe the difference is about whether being upset would have been useful, or whether the person could have done anything? But that doesn't seem to be doing the work either: we don't require the person who lost the job to have some useful task for their frustration before we call their calm admirable. I want the strongest principle you can come up with that generates both judgments, and then an honest verdict on whether it's a real distinction or just an inconsistency we've all quietly agreed to live with.
