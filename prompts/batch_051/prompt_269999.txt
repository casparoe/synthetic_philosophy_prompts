I'm a journalist working on a piece about authority and why people go along with legal systems they suspect are unjust, and I keep getting stuck on this passage from Pascal's Pensées (the section usually called Justice and the Reason of Effects). It's entry 298 in the numbering I've got:

"It is right that what is just should be obeyed; it is necessary that what is strongest should be obeyed. Justice without might is helpless; might without justice is tyrannical."

I can see the broad point: justice needs force behind it, and force needs to be constrained by justice. But the wording is bugging me. He says it is *right* that the just should be obeyed, and it is *necessary* that the strongest should be obeyed. Is he recommending both, or just describing how things actually work? And then the epigram about justice-without-might being helpless and might-without-justice being tyrannical — is that a call to combine them, or a kind of cynical acceptance that any actual order is going to be a messy blend of the two? I'm especially interested in whether he's saying 'therefore the strong should make themselves just' or 'therefore our idea of justice is mostly just force dressed up.' It seems like he's walking right up to the edge of saying custom and force are the real basis of law, but I can't tell if he thinks that's a problem to fix or just the human condition.

Walk me through what this passage is doing, and don't gloss over the 'should' vs 'must' distinction. Also, if you can, say how confident you are in your reading and single out the point you're least sure of. And I'd like the answer in American English, please.
