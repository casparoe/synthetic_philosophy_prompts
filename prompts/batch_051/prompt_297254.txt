I am taking a course on rational choice and the ethics of intertemporal trade-offs, and for the final assessment each student has to design and run one 50-minute session of our department's undergraduate philosophy workshop. The group is about eighteen students, mostly philosophy majors with no economics background. I have chosen the distinction between discounting the future for instrumental reasons — uncertainty about whether a promised benefit will actually arrive, the opportunity cost of waiting, the chance that one will not be around or will care about different things — and pure time preference, the idea that a later benefit counts less simply because it arrives later. In our readings, Sidgwick argues that the mere difference of temporal position is not a reasonable ground for caring more about nearer goods, and Ramsey says in passing that discounting later goods is ethically indefensible and comes from a failure of imagination. In discussion, most people agree with these claims in the abstract and then immediately make choices that look time-biased. What I want the session to produce is not just agreement with the reading but something closer to an uncomfortable recognition: that much of what feels like concern for one's future is really uncertainty or opportunity cost, and that the leftover pure preference for the nearer as such is harder to justify than it first appears.

Design a runnable game or structured deliberation for this workshop. It should use real, small stakes — snacks or modest amounts are fine — so that the choices reveal actual trade-offs, but it must not require anyone to spend money or assume that everyone has the same tastes. The rules, incentives, or sequence should be arranged so that participants run into the distinction themselves: ideally, some early choices will look like pure time preference, and a later round should expose how much of that was really uncertainty, opportunity cost, or the special pull of the immediate present. In the debrief, map specific moments in the activity onto the formal machinery we are learning — exponential discounting, present bias, and the difference between discounting by delay and discounting by absolute calendar date. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, for example mistrust of future delivery, social desirability, stakes too small to matter, or participants smuggling in assumptions about risk, and say how to handle each problem. Finally, be explicit about which feature of the distinction the game cannot reproduce on its own and which part I will have to supply in discussion.
