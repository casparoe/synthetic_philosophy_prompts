I am drafting the opening section of a formal-epistemology paper on whether the standard asymptotic results by which conflicting priors are supposed to wash out actually dispose of the problem of prior selection. Before I deploy those results, I want the target they are being asked to hit made explicit. Write, as the desiderata list for that section, the requirements a theory of prior washing-out would need to satisfy to count as adequate. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it onto the list, and state whether it is non-negotiable or should be read as the preference of a particular camp — the subjectivist who wants a purely asymptotic story, the objective Bayesian who wants an intersubjectively privileged limit, the decision theorist who wants convergence in what is done rather than merely in what is believed, and so on. Be sure to address the distinctions that standard statements blur: convergence of posterior distributions versus convergence of predictive distributions versus convergence of the acts the posterior would recommend now; convergence under a correctly specified model versus under misspecification; behavior when the data are optional-stopped or selected opportunistically rather than gathered as a fixed sample; and whether the rate at which agreement emerges is part of the phenomenon or an inessential extra. Then answer the question the paper actually turns on: is there a subset of these requirements that the existing theorems cannot satisfy jointly? If there is, say exactly which combination fails, what the natural repair is, and which single requirement does the most work in excluding otherwise promising accounts. A brief scorecard of the standard approaches — exchangeability-based arguments, merging-of-opinions theorems, almost-sure consistency results — is useful only insofar as it tests the list; keep the bulk of the answer on the criteria and their motivation.
