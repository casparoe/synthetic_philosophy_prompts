After a career as a research biologist, I have spent my retirement working through moral philosophy, and lately the subject has been freeganism. In our reading group we argued about whether it is acceptable to use the byproducts of rescued animals: eggs from ex-battery hens, wool from sheep kept as companions, honey from managed hives, dairy from a cow no longer bred, even meat from an animal that died of old age. The question was whether consuming these things still treats animals as resources, or whether the fact that the byproduct would otherwise go to waste removes the difficulty.

One member of the group cut the discussion short with 'Waste not, want not.' His view was that letting anything usable go to waste is itself a wrong, so the moral question dissolves. I am confident that something is wrong with this move, but the proverb appears to be doing several jobs at once and I cannot yet say which of them is doing the illicit work.

Please lay out the distinct precise claims that 'waste not, want not' could be making in this context—for example, a claim about what is permissible, a claim about what is obligatory, a claim about whether using a byproduct implicates one in how it was produced or expresses a wrong attitude toward the animal, or something else. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false, and give the grounds. Then identify which reading or readings the dismissal actually requires in order to work, and state whether any reading that survives scrutiny would justify closing the debate the way he did.

Please structure the answer as four sections in this order:

- The readings (roughly 45% of the answer): a numbered menu of the distinct theses the proverb could express, each stated precisely.
- Verdicts (roughly 30%): for each numbered reading, the verdict—trivial, substantive, or false—with a sentence or two of justification.
- What the slogan was doing (roughly 20%): which reading or readings the group member's dismissal depends on, and whether the defensible ones actually deliver his conclusion.
- Confidence (roughly 5%): how confident you are in the overall analysis, and the single point you are least sure of.
