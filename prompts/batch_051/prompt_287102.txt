I'm a novelist, and I sit on the literature panel of a state arts council. We're trying to decide whether to give a twelve-thousand-dollar grant to a debut novel, and the panel has split nearly down the middle. The book — working title *Feast of Ash* — is a grief story: the narrator, a lapsed Catholic caring for her dying mother, breaks into the parish at night, takes the consecrated hosts from the tabernacle, and crushes them under the wheels of her mother's wheelchair while reciting a garbled Act of Contrition. The scene isn't played for shock, exactly; it reads like rage with nowhere else to go. Still, a couple of panelists think public money shouldn't be attached to desecration, and one of them called it "ritual injury, not criticism." Others — me included, most days — think the council exists to fund difficult work, and that pulling a grant because a religious group might feel wounded quietly installs a de facto blasphemy test. The local diocese has also written asking us to "send a message," which honestly makes me want to fund the book out of spite, though I know that isn't a reason.

I keep circling a version of the strongest point on the loosing side: desecrating a sacred object isn't the same as criticizing a belief, because the people to whom it's sacred don't experience it as a claim they could argue with. It's aimed at a practice they think makes demands on everyone, nonbelievers included, so a public funder can't treat it like an essay attacking a doctrine. As a novelist I want to say fiction's whole license is to imagine the worst thing a person could do with the holiest object in the room, so a grants panel shouldn't start deciding what's sacred — but I'm not sure that's an argument so much as a reflex. I need to see the two cases written out cleanly, because I don't yet know how much weight that "ritual injury" point can bear once it has to stand next to the case for funding.

Write the short memo I'll circulate before Thursday's vote, with these sections in this order: (1) Recommendation — two or three sentences, bottom line up front, including whether we fund, refuse, or fund with conditions. (2) The case for refusing the grant — about 150 words. (3) The case for making the award anyway — about 150 words. (4) Conditions or next steps — about 100 words, giving concrete conditions if you land on funding and a revisit date if you land on refusing. The losing side's best argument should get stated as forcefully as the winning one, and the memo should end with something I can actually read out in the meeting.
