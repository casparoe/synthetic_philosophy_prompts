Under the usual account of American defamation law, liability attaches only to false statements of fact: pure opinion is protected, but only so long as it cannot reasonably be understood to assert or imply a false fact about someone. Ratings and forecasts have traditionally been sorted onto the opinion side of this line, since a one-star review, a credit rating, or an analyst's "buy" recommendation is taken to express an evaluative judgment rather than to report a fact. Now suppose that an increasing share of such ratings and forecasts is generated not by a person making a judgment but by trained predictive models: systems that emit scores and projections no human actually forms, and whose inferential steps may be opaque. Does the opinion/fact distinction survive this change? In particular, can a black-box model's output count as an "opinion" in whatever sense the doctrine needs, and does the rationale for protecting opinion come apart when the model's score can simply be mistaken rather than defensibly evaluative? Say whether the traditional distinction still does its work, needs repair, or should be set aside for algorithmic ratings and forecasts, and identify which premise of the opinion-protection argument the development puts under pressure.
