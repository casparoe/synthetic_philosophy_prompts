After a long recovery last year, during which I had plenty of small pleasures along with a lot of pain, I started noticing two things at once: how unmistakable the difference between pleasure and pain feels, and how hard it is to say what all the pleasant moments had in common—the relief of pain stopping, the click of a solution, being praised, the taste of cold water, the bleak satisfaction of nursing a grudge. I have come around to a view I used to think was obviously false, and now I worry I have talked myself into something unfalsifiable. The view is that every genuinely pleasant experience shares one and the same introspectively detectable felt quality, the feeling of pleasantness itself. This felt quality is not any of the specific sensation-types involved (sweetness, coolness, relief, excitement); it is a determinable tone or gloss carried by all those specific contents, and it is the single feature in virtue of which all these experiences count as good for the person having them. I am aware of the obvious objection, the so-called heterogeneity problem: when I introspect, the experiences seem to have nothing in common except that I prefer them, want them to continue, or take an approving attitude toward them. My current line is that this objection only shows how easy pleasantness is to confuse with its varying sensory vehicles, and that enough attention can isolate the common gloss underneath.

What I want is a map of everything that would count against this strong qualitative monism about pleasure. I am less interested in a single knockdown objection than in an ordering: which introspective reports, possible cases, arguments, or (if relevant) empirical dissociations should most worry me, and which would be decisive against which part of the view—the claim that there is one common felt quality, the claim that it is detectable by introspection, or the claim that it is what makes all pleasant experiences good? What should the view, taken seriously, predict that rival accounts of pleasure (as an attitude, as a functional role, or as a loose family of distinct goods) would not predict, and which of those predictions have arguably already failed? I would also like to know which of these a committed defender of my view would concede as genuine counterevidence rather than dismiss as misdescription, and whether the view still risks anything or survives only because I have quietly qualified it into unfalsifiability—for example, by allowing that the common quality can always be masked, or by relabeling whatever makes an experience good as pleasantness after the fact. Is insulation of that kind a problem for a view of this kind, or is it what we should expect of a theory of something as familiar as pleasure? Please be explicit about how confident you are in the claims you make, and single out the point you are least sure of.
