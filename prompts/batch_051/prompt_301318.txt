I'm a novelist, so maybe this is my occupational disease talking. I'm drafting a novel about a charismatic political outsider taking over a democracy in crisis—think interwar Germany, the whole paranoid opera. And I started the book convinced by the structural account: the crash, mass joblessness, a humiliated country, a constitution nobody would fight for, conservative elites scheming to use the movement and throw it away. On that account the man himself is almost incidental—put a different face on the same resentments and the story runs basically the same. Historical fiction written like that reads like a weather report, though. Whenever I write a scene where the takeover feels structurally inevitable, it's dead on the page. The scenes that live are the ones where this particular man hesitates, misreads the room, refuses a deal, blows a chance, doubles down at exactly the wrong time, and the thing happens anyway because of him as much as around him. So here's the problem. I'm attracted to the structural story for the usual reasons—it's general, it lets you compare countries, it doesn't fall back on genius or destiny—and it tells me my intuition is just the novelist's need for a protagonist. But the intuition won't go away: this person mattered, not as a symbol but as a cause. If he had died in the war, does the whole thing still happen? I don't want a verdict, I want to know what it would take for each side to give: what would have to be true for the structural account to absorbe my intuition entirely, what would have to be true for me to abandon the structural account, and is there a reading where the wave is real and the man's choices still decide what the wave does—or is that just a soft landing for people who want both?
