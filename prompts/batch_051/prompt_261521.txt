I keep coming back to Nietzsche's early essay on history (the one about monumental, antiquarian, and critical history) and I want to know whether the claim at its centre is anything more than a vibe. The claim, as I'd steelman it: an excess of the historical sense is life-inhibiting — it makes a person or a culture worse at initiating action. Each mode is supposed to cut both ways. Monumental history gives you models and the confidence that greatness is possible, until it turns into delusion or makes present attempts feel shabby. Antiquarian history preserves and reveres a continuity, until it mummifies the present and resents anything new. Critical history drags the past before a tribunal and frees you to make your own evaluations, until it leaves you with no continuity at all and nothing to act from. So the deeper claim is roughly: historical consciousness has a dosage/regime problem, not just a content problem.

Pick the most tractable version of that claim and design the best concrete test you can for it. I want the operationalisation spelled out: who the subjects are, what manipulation of historical framing they recieve, what behaviour or decision counts as the dependent variable, and what result would count as evidence for and against the claim. Then be very explicit about what a positive result would and wouldn't settle. Would it show that historical framing changes action-initiation, or just that it changes mood or verbal self-report? Would it show anything about whole cultures over generations, or just about individuals in a lab for forty minutes? Also say what the test could never reach even in principle — but don't use that as an excuse to declare the whole thing untestible; the interesting version is where a test is partly possible and partly leaves something out.

Bottom line first, then the reasoning, no preamble, and don't restate the question. British English please.
