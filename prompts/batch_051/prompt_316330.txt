I've been trying to find the principle behind my verdicts about when an earlier self gets to bind a later, cognitively impaired self, and I've failed. The cases below are my actual gut reactions; they feel mostly right, but I can't state the rule that generates them. I want you to extract the best candidate principles, tell me which of my verdicts they cannot accommodate, and say which of my verdicts you would overturn.

1. Esther signed an advance directive at 60, witnessed and detailed, refusing antibiotics, intubation, and tube feeding if she ever developed severe dementia. She is now 80, with advanced dementia; she doesn't recognize her children but seems content—she hums, eats with pleasure, and watches birds. She develops pneumonia that antibiotics would likely clear. I think the antibiotics should be withheld, as she directed: the earlier Esther gets the final say.

2. Reuben wrote a directive at 55 that said only "if I ever need a feeding tube, no feeding tube." After a stroke at 72 he needs a feeding tube for an estimated six weeks. He has mild cognitive impairment but understands what a feeding tube is, wants it, and wants to work at rehabilitation. I think he should get the tube despite the directive: the present Reuben wins.

3. Ruth signed a directive at 50 refusing blood transfusions on firm religious convictions. At 76 she has severe dementia but still recites prayers, keeps dietary observances, and shows every sign of remaining devout. She now needs a transfusion after a fall. I think the directive should be honored.

4. Eric, who has bipolar disorder, wrote a psychiatric advance directive during a stable period refusing ECT and all antipsychotics if he ever became manic, on the ground that he did not want his brain altered by psychiatry. He is now manic, refusing treatment, and a danger to himself. I think the directive should be overridden and he should be treated. What bothers me is that I can't state the difference that makes Esther's earlier self bind her while Eric's doesn't bind him; in both, a competent person made a future-directed refusal that a now-impaired person is living under.

5. Owen wrote nothing down, but for twenty years he told his family he never wanted "to be kept alive like a vegetable." After an accident he is now minimally conscious, with some chance of meaningful recovery. His wife wants the ventilator withdrawn. I think the ventilator should stay: his repeated remark was too loose to authorize ending treatment from which he might recover meaningfully.

Is there one principle behind these verdicts, and if not, which of my verdicts should I give up? Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Use the first four items to state the four candidate principles you think I most need to see, and for each say which of my cases it handles and which verdict it forces me to revise. Use the fifth item to tell me which of my five verdicts is least defensible, and which argument or consideration would most likely change your mind—and mine—about it.
