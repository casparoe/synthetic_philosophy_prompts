I am a doctoral student in ancient philosophy finishing a chapter on the binding force of oaths, and before I circulate the chapter I would like a consistency audit of the position I have been assembling. I presently find myself committed to five claims. First, what distinguishes an oath from an ordinary promise is the conditional self-imprecation at its core: to swear is to make oneself liable to a penalty, called down by oneself upon oneself, should one fail to do the thing sworn, and it is this imprecatory mechanism, rather than the general moral duty to keep promises, that gives an oath its special force. Second, the paradigm of such a mechanism is the oath by the river Styx, the oath the gods themselves treat as the most solemn. Because the gods cannot die, the penalty they call down is not death or bodily harm but the suspension of standing: a perjured god is denied the food and drink of the gods and shut out of their assembly for a fixed term. I take the lesson to be that oaths bind through the standing one risks forfeiting within a community, not through fear of inflicted suffering or external coercion. Third, a modern oath can bind, as an oath, an atheist who utters no imprecation of any kind and who, in the case in question, has no reason to expect either divine or secular punishment for breach; a bare and avowedly secular oath formula that mentions no deity and no penalty can bind fully. Fourth, what makes one bound is the public act of swearing rather than one's private belief or sincerity: an oath taken insincerely, if taken without duress, binds the swearer as fully as a sincere one. Fifth, an oath to do something gravely wrong is void from the beginning, generating no obligation at all rather than an obligation that is overridden; the moral limits on what can validly be sworn are internal to the oath itself, not applied to it from outside. Some of these claims now seem to me to pull against one another, but I cannot tell which pairings represent genuine incompatibilities and which merely apparent conflicts. Please audit the set: separate the apparent conflicts from the genuine tensions, rank the genuine tensions by how severe they are, and for each say what would have to be given up or revised and what the revision would cost the position as a whole. I would like the answer written in American English, as continuous prose in the manner of a letter or email, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
