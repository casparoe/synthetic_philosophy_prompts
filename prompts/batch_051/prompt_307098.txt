I write a column on ideas and behavioural science for a general-interest magazine, and I've just read a pop-econ book that sums up the money-pump argument in a way I want checked before I paraphrase it in print. The book says, roughly:

The money pump shows that rational preferences can't run in circles. Suppose you prefer tea to coffee, coffee to juice, and juice to tea. A trader can then march you through a loop of swaps — a small fee to trade juice for coffee, another to trade coffee for tea, another to trade tea back to juice — and you finish holding exactly the same juice you started with, just poorer. Since a rational person would never keep changing their mind like that, intransitive preferences are irrational.

I have a hunch the wobble is in the jump from accepting-each-swap to irrational-full-stop, and maybe in whether you could see the whole sequence coming, but I dont know enough to be sure of either, and I definitely cant publish a hunch as fact. So sentence by sentence, what would a decision theorist or philosopher of rationality accept in that paragraph, what would they wince at, and what's outright wrong? Does the conclusion that intransitive preferences are irrational actually follow from the parts that are accurate? And I'd particularly like a line on whether this is meant to be a claim about a person at one time or across time, because the book's "changing their mind" talk feels like it might be smuggling in a different problem.
