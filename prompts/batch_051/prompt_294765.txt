Here's one I'd like you to think through properly. Take Lewis's account of convention: roughly, it's a regularity R in a population that nearly everyone follows, nearly everyone expects nearly everyone else to follow, nearly everyone prefers to conform to R given those expectations, and it's common knowledge that at least one other regularity could have done the job instead. Now suppose a coordination problem has exactly two stable regularities, A and B. A is the incumbent convention—history and precedent point to it, and basically everyone follows it. B is strictly better for everyone: if the whole population could switch in one shot, every single person would be better off under B than under A. But a lone person switching to B while everyone else stays at A does worse than staying at A. So no one can reach B alone.

Now the wrinkle. Five percent of the population are enthusiasts. There payoffs are different: each enthusiast gets a large payoff from following B and only a tiny loss from being the only B-follower during the transition, so each of them is willing to switch to B unilaterally. Non-enthusiasts can observe, at each step, how many people are already following B before deciding whether to switch themselves.

Here's what I want you to work through. On a Lewis-style view, is B a convention before any non-enthusiast has switched, when only the enthusiasts follow it? Is there a sharp point at which B becomes the convention even though most people are still following A, and if so what marks that point? Do you actually need common knowledge of the growing B-minority, or would mere public observation—everyone sees the same counts but maybe not everyone knows that everyone knows at each level—be enough? And I'm especially interested in whether arbitrariness is doing real work here: B arguably stops being arbitrary once everyone can see it's strictly better, whereas A was arbitrary in the usual Lewisian way. Does that mean the two equilibria become conventions by different routes, or is that way of putting it a mistake on Lewis's terms? Go through each of Lewis's conditions step by step instead of hand-waving about tipping points. Also say how confident you are in the overall answer, and single out the point you're least sure of.
