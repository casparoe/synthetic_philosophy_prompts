In Plato’s Meno, in the passage where Socrates distinguishes knowledge from right opinion, he argues that a true belief can guide action just as well as knowledge, but that true beliefs are worth less because they are unstable: unless someone tethers them by working out the reason why they are true, they can slip away the way Daedalus’ statues wander off. Treat this as an early version of the claim that knowledge is not merely accurate judgment but judgment rendered stable by an explanatory account.

Now consider a modern epistemic system that produces stable, well-calibrated probability judgments from distributed inputs—say, a prediction market or an aggregative forecasting platform that has been well calibrated across many domains. Its outputs are stable in a practical sense: they persist across time, they guide policy and betting decisions reliably, and they are not lost the moment a particular participant forgets a chain of reasoning. But no single participant, and perhaps no central model at all, can supply the Platonic tether: the system’s reliability is an emergent property of many partial information-bearers and a market mechanism, not anyone’s grasp of why the relevant proposition is true.

Does Plato’s Meno argument that knowledge is better than right opinion survive this kind of case? Which premise is actually under pressure—the empirical premise that untethered true belief is unstable, or the normative premise that the extra element worth having is the knower’s own account of the cause? If there is a repaired version of the distinction, what does it say about the relationship between distributed reliability and individual understanding, and what kind of value, if any, does the missing explanatory tether still have for the person who relies on the system?
