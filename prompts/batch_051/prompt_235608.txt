I have recently begun a meditation practice, and it has made the ordinary sense of an enduring self feel far less obvious; I have been trying to reconcile that experience with the Buddhist doctrine of anatta and with philosophical accounts of personal identity. I keep assembling the following argument, and I cannot tell whether it is sound or where exactly it goes wrong.

(1) If there is a self, it is what persists through the changes in my mental life and what makes experiences at different times all mine.
(2) No individual experience, thought, or feeling persists through those changes; each arises and passes away.
(3) Nor do I encounter any further thing, over and above these passing states, that does the persisting: I find no inner owner or witness among the contents of experience.
(4) Therefore, there is no persisting self.
(5) What does persist, however, is a network of relations—memory, bodily continuity, continuity of character and projects—connecting earlier and later mental states.
(6) So personal identity over time consists in these relations, and the idea of a self beyond them is at best a useful fiction.

I am more than half convinced, but I suspect a trick somewhere. My main worry is that (3) slides from an epistemological fact about what I can find in experience to a metaphysical conclusion about what exists. I also worry that (5) and (6) move from the claim that these relations exist and matter to the claim that personal identity consists in nothing more than them, without arguing that nothing further is required. Are those the genuine weak points? Is the argument valid as it stands? If not, which step fails, and under which reading of it? And if the argument is repaired in the most defensible way, does it still amount to anatta, or only to the weaker claim that the self is not an object of introspection?
