I am taking an ethics seminar on historical representation, and I keep getting stuck on the norms of the prestige biopic. The form advertises itself as faithful: the marketing says it tells the true story of a real person, and reviewers treat accuracy as a virtue. At the same time, the makers of these films routinely compress a life into three acts, invent composite characters, put plausible but unattested words in the mouths of the dead, and settle biographical ambiguities in whatever way serves the plot. They sometimes seek the cooperation of a subject's family or estate, and sometimes proceed without it; they append disclaimers saying that events and characters have been changed for dramatic purposes, apparently to ward off legal claims. I cannot tell what this practice presupposes about what we owe the real dead. Does it commit itself to the view that the dead cannot be wronged, or only to the narrower legal fact that the dead have no standing to sue? Does it treat accuracy as an obligation owed to the audience rather than to the person depicted, or merely as a marketing convention? And is the practice even consistent in the way it treats different categories of the dead — heroes, villains, the recently dead, the long dead, public figures, and ordinary people caught up in an event?

What I would like is a reconstruction of the implicit moral theory of this practice, separating what it strictly presupposes from what it merely tolerates or fits well with. Under that theory, is it coherent to say that a filmmaker can be criticized for lying about someone who no longer exists? What would have to change in the way biopics are produced and received if we instead accepted that people can be wronged after death because their reputations remain objects of concern, or that telling someone's story is a way of continuing a relationship with them? Or is the practice perhaps underdetermined — compatible with several different views about duties to the dead — with the real philosophical disagreement lying elsewhere?
