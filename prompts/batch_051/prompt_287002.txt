I'm on my hospital's ethics committee and I need to circulate a short written opinion by Thursday, and I honestly don't know which way to land. The case: a 34-year-old woman was in a car crash two days ago in which her husband and daughter were killed. She asked the trauma team if anything could "turn the volume down" on the next few weeks, and one of the psychiatrists mentioned that propranolol, given soon after a trauma, has been studied for reducing the intensity of traumatic memories and perhaps grief. Now the patient is asking for it. Half the committee says approve: she's a competent adult who's asking for a temporary intervention, and there's no obligation to suffer. The other half says this would be ethically off, because grief after losing your spouse and child is a fitting response, not a disorder, and blunting it is a kind of cosmetic pharmacology that would distance her from a loss she needs to integrate. There also worried that approving it amounts to saying a life with less grief is simply better, and that we'd be medicalizing ordinary sadness.

I need the memo to recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame (approve, reject, or approve with conditions). It has to engage the strongest version of the losing side's argument seriously, not just restate our policy. It would also help if you could pull apart a few distinctions before landing on a recommendation: grief being fitting vs. grief being useful, taking the edge off acute grief now vs. long-term emotional numbing, and eliminating sadness vs. not insisting that everyone experience it at full intensity.

Please write as a moral philosopher who specializes in philosophy of emotion and has served on hospital ethics committees. Before drafting the final memo, brainstorm two or three possible framings and pick the strongest one, and think through the strongest argument on each side. And the committee chair hates when a memo hides behind "we need more data," so whatever you recommend has to be something we can actually implement this week.
