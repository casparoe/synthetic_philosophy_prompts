Last week, in a reading group on rational choice, we spent an evening on two of Douglas Hofstadter's set pieces: the Platonia dilemma and the Luring Lottery. In the Platonia dilemma, a trillionaire tells 20 people that if exactly one of them sends him a reverse-charges telegram by noon the next day, that person will receive a billion dollars; if no one sends or more than one sends, nobody gets anything; and the players cannot coordinate. The Luring Lottery was a real contest run by Scientific American in 1983: readers entered by mailing a postcard with a number on it, which counted as their number of lottery entries; the prize was a million dollars divided by the total number of entries received, awarded to a randomly drawn entry. Hofstadter's suggested superrational play was to estimate the number of likely entrants, roll a die with that many sides, and send a single entry only on a roll of 1. Two people in the group, Mina and Eli, got into a long argument afterward, and neither would concede. Here is roughly how it went.

Mina said the Platonia dilemma is an open-and-shut dominance case: sending can only help you and can never hurt you, so the rational thing is to send, and the fact that all 20 sending means nobody wins is unfortunate but irrelevant to any one player's decision. Eli replied that this is exactly the reasoning by which 20 identical rational players all send and all walk away with nothing. Superrationality, he said, asks which decision procedure is best when everyone in the same position runs it; the procedure of each person randomizing and sending with probability 1/20 gives about a 38% chance that exactly one telegram arrives, and since the positions are symmetric each player has about a 1.9% chance at the billion. That beats the zero delivered by universal dominance reasoning. Mina answered that the mixed strategy is not a best reply: if you believe the other 19 are each rolling their 1/20 dice, your best move is to send for sure, because there is a (19/20)^19 chance—about 38%—that none of them sends and you win outright. A rule that every individual has an incentive to defect from, she said, is not a theory of rational choice; it is a collective discipline masquerading as individual rationality. Eli said she was smuggling in an asymmetry the problem does not supply. There is no one who gets to be the lone constant-sender while the others restrain themselves, because all the players are identical and have no more reason to assign that role to themselves than to anyone else. The admissible comparison, he said, is between symmetric procedures: everyone always sends, which pays zero, or everyone uses the 1/20 rule, which gives each player a positive shot at the billion. The second is plainly better. Mina then pressed the Luring Lottery: the actual field was full of people who did not restrain themselves—they submitted googolplexes and larger expressions—and the prize was diluted out of existence. The superrational policy of rolling a die and sending a single entry only on a 1 was a bet that the rest of the field would be superrational too, and the actual contest showed that bet losing as a description of real people. Eli replied that the real contest shows the opposite of what she thinks. In the Luring Lottery the individually best reply to whatever the rest of the field enters is to enter a number equal to the total entered by everyone else; when many players follow that logic, the totals explode and the prize collapses. Entrants who submitted googolplexes were not refuting superrationality's normative point; they were demonstrating that fixed-others, best-reply reasoning becomes self-undermining when payoffs depend on other peoples' reasoning about you.

They kept going in this loop. I would like a careful adjudication: for each move in the exchange, say whether it is clearly mistaken, partially right, or clearly right, and briefly defend each rating; then say what deeper issue they kept circling but never stated, and say who is more right overall.
