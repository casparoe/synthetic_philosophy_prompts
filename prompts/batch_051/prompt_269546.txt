I'm teaching the last session of a philosophy of mind reading group on Dennett, and I want to close with a poem that does some of the arguing for us, instead of just another summary of the four kinds of creatures. You know the ladder: Darwinian creatures only get redesigned across generations, by natural selection; Skinnerian creatures also adjust within their own lifetime, by trying things out and getting reinforced; Popperian creatures have an inner environment, so they can run trials in their heads and let the candidates die in their place; and Gregorian creatures borrow thinking tools from their surroundings — above all words — and pass them around.

Here's what I have in mind. Write a poem in four stanzas, six lines each, that climbs this tower, and make the form climb with it. The Darwinian stanza should be locked down: fixed metre, full rhyme, end-stopped lines, no room to manoeuvre. The Skinnerian stanza should mostly keep that skeleton but slip into slant rhyme and slight variation, as if the form is being nudged along by reinforcement. The Popperian stanza should go interior — broken lines, enjambment, two or three hypotheticals tried and dropped mid-flight. The Gregorian stanza should loosen into free verse and lean on borrowed language: idiom, proper names, technical terms, maybe a cliché used on purpose. The through-line is generate-and-test: new variants keep getting produced, and nearly all of them die; the poem should show that rather than just say it. It dosen't have to be metrically perfect, but it should hold up read aloud, and it should be accurate enough that a Dennett person wouldn't wince. And please write in British English where it matters.
