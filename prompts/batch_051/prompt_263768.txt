In our political theory course, the unit question is whether "left" and "right" name a real distinction and, if so, what holds each side together. The vocabulary, as everyone in the seminar notes, began as literal seating positions in the French revolutionary assembly, but it has since hardened into a piece of conceptual geometry: political positions are supposed to lie along a single left–right spectrum, with distance on the line measuring disagreement, the midpoint marking moderation, and the two poles counting as opposite extremes of one underlying dimension. A classmate of mine is using this spatial picture to run a substantive argument. Move far enough toward either pole, he says, and the line bends around into a horseshoe, so the far left and the far right end up resembling each other more than either resembles the liberal center; on that basis he concludes that both extremes deserve the same sort of suspicion. I want to audit the spectrum as an analogy rather than simply accept it as a map of the terrain. Could you lay out, explicitly, what corresponds to what between the source—a continuous line in space—and the target—the structure of political disagreement; where the mapping breaks; which features of the spatial source it silently imports, such as one-dimensionality, a natural midpoint, symmetry, adjacency, and distance as a measure of difference, that the political terrain may not actually have; and how much of the horseshoe conclusion is left standing once the disanalogies are put on the table? If some other representation would do the relevant work better, I would also like to hear what it is and why it is less misleading.
