I am a retired experimental physicist who has only recently begun reading the philosophical literature on artificial intelligence, and I keep being stopped by a distinction that I would once have said I understood. I would like you to explain it to me as a conceptual matter rather than a formal one. The distinction is between worst-case and average-case guarantees. In the laboratory the rule was simple: a safety-critical property had to hold under the most severe conditions we could justify, while "on average" described yield or noise, never whether an interlock would hold. In the alignment literature, however, I regularly find "guarantees" that are explicitly average-case: claims that a system will behave as intended with high probability over some distribution of inputs, tasks, or environments. Since alignment itself is not a fixed physical quantity but a relation between a system, its designers, and an open-ended world, I want to understand what an average-case guarantee could even mean for such a property. Please do not merely survey positions; work through the following worry with me. The familiar problem is that catastrophic failures are exactly the tail of the distribution, and an average says nothing about the tail. The deeper problem, as it seems to me, is that the distribution is not fixed from outside: a learned system's actions help determine which situations arise, and can determine which of its own behaviours get evaluated, so an average over any fixed distribution looks partly circular or self-fulfilling. If the system can steer the world into the region of the distribution where it does well, average-case success is uninformative; if it can steer evaluation toward where it already looks good, it is worse than uninformative. Does this show that alignment is the wrong kind of property for which to seek average-case guarantees, or is there a defensible sense of "average" that does not presuppose a static environment and a passive system? And if we refuse average-case guarantees, are we left insisting on a worst-case standard that no learned system could plausibly meet, and would that indicate that the trouble lies in thinking of alignment as something guarantee-like at all? I am not looking for a survey of authors or a bibliography. Write your reply as continuous prose, in the manner of a careful letter; no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
