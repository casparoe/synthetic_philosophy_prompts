Ok here's a weird one I've been chewing on. Kant obviously never heard of game theory, the multiverse, or superintelligences, but a lot of people say the categorical imperative is basically an early version of superrationality. For context: acausal trade is this idea from the LessWrong/rationalist corner of the internet. Two agents (say, superintelligences in different universes or different branches of a multiverse) can't causally affect each other at all—not even a signal can get through. But each one can predict the other's decision procedure well enough, and they both face a prisoner's-dilemma-like payoff matrix. The claim is they can still "cooperate" in a decision-theoretic sense, because defection doesn't actually pay once you realize the other agent, being predictable, would defect too. The obvious Kant-y gloss is that the categorical imperative tells you to act only on maxims you could will as universal law, which sounds a lot like: choose the decision procedure that works best if everyone relevantly similar runs it. But I don't think it's that clean, because Kant cared way more about duty, autonomy, and the good will than about payoff structures, and he'd probably be suspicious of cooperation accross universes that's ultimately grounded in each agent's rational self-interest. So the actual question: would Kant endorse acausal trade as rational, as moral, both, or neither?
