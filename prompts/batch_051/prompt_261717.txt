Me and a friend have been going back and forth on this and I can't figure out which step actually breaks. It's about regular and irregular past tenses, and whether the line between a "rule" and an "exception" is real or just a way of describing the same facts.

(1) The regular past tense (walk→walked, jump→jumped) is a rule in the only sense that matters: speakers extend it to words they've never heard, even nonsense ones — give a kid a made-up verb like wug and they'll say wugged. Exceptions are supposed to be different: they're stored item-by-item, like go→went, be→was, buy→bought. Suppletion is just the extreme case where the stored form shares almost nothing with the stem (go/went, am/was, good/better).

(2) But any exception can be restated as a rule if you let the rule's domain be tiny. "To form the past of go, replace the whole word with went" is a rule; it just has a domain of one. From the outside, go→went and walk→walked look like the same kind of thing with different-sized domains.

(3) And any regular rule can be restated as a list. You could store walk→walked, talk→talked, jump→jumped, and so on for thousands of verbs, plus an analogy mechanism that generalizes the pattern to new items. Nothing forces you to posit a rule over and above the list.

(4) So the rule/exception distiction is just notation — a choice of description, not a fact about what's actually represented in a speaker's head.

(5) But if (4) is true, a child's mind shouldn't systematically prefer regulars as a source of generalization. Regular and irregular past forms would be on equal footing, representationally.

(6) They aren't. Kids overregularize — goed, eated, runned — even after they've produced went, ate, and ran correctly for months. The reverse, taking a regular verb and inventing a novel irregular past for it, is basically absent. The error flows almost entirely from irregular toward regular.

(7) So the distinction does track something psychological: the -ed pattern is a live default and the irregulars are stored exceptions.

(8) But then one of (1)–(3) or (5) has to be wrong, because (4) and (7) can't both be true. Which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost? I'd really rather keep (6) than (2) or (3) — the kid data feels non-negotiable to me. And if it helps, there are supposed to be two big camps here: one that posits a rule system plus a mental lexicon, and one that tries to do everything with a single associative memory and no real rules. I can't line up which camp rejects which premise, or whether they both have to reject the same one. Where's the exit?
