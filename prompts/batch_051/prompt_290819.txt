In this exercise I want you to think about what it is for a language to be alive, and what exactly we value when we say we value the survival of a language. Let me offer four working criteria for a living language. A language is living just in case (i) there is a community of speakers who use it for ordinary communicative purposes; (ii) it is transmitted to children as a first language, not merely acquired later; (iii) it is used across a reasonably wide range of domains—domestic life, work, play, argument, intimacy, public performance; and (iv) it remains productive, in the sense that its speakers coin new vocabulary and extend old constructions to meet new circumstances, so that the language changes over time as living natural languages do.

Now consider a hypothetical case. The last person to acquire the language V as a first language died in 1980. V was, however, extensively documented during the twentieth century: grammars, dictionaries, and many hours of recorded conversation and storytelling survive. In the 1990s, younger descendants of the historical V-speaking community, none of whom had ever heard V spoken in everyday life, set out to revive it from the records. Today several thousand people speak V as a school- and study-acquired language; a few hundred use it daily at home and in community life; and there is a school in which V is the medium of instruction for children from ages five to twelve, though the children's dominant language, and their only first language, is the surrounding national language. New songs, stories, and online forums are produced in V, and the revived language has borrowed heavily from the national language and regularized parts of its older inflectional system. No child currently acquires V as a first language.

Does V count as a living language under the criteria above? Work through the four criteria one at a time, and say plainly where the criteria underdetermine the verdict. Where you judge that V fails a criterion, ask whether that criterion is doing real work or whether it is a stipulation carrying an unargued assumption—in particular whether criterion (ii), and perhaps criterion (iv), should be relaxed or reinterpreted. Finally, connect the classification to the underlying question of value. If we suppose that a language's value does not reduce to the interests of its current speakers, what exactly is the bearer of that value in this case: the structure preserved in the archive, the present community's practice, or the unbroken passing-on from one generation to the next? And if your analysis says V is not a living language, should that make us think that something important has nonetheless been preserved—or alternatively, that "living language" is the wrong category in which to look for what we care about?
