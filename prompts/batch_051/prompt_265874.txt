I've been kicking around a view I call the idle witness theory of consciousness, and I recently realized it might undercut itself. The view is this: everything a person does — quitting a job, writing a note, wincing after a burn — is produced by ordinary physical processes in the brain and nervous system. Conscious experience rides along on top of those processes but never pushes back. The hurtfulness of the burn, the redness of a wall, the what-it's-like of being me: all real, but causally useless. Like the whine a refrigerator makes while it cools, or the heat a laptop gives off while it computes. Nothing in the cooling waits for the whine.

The problem is that, on my own account, my saying "I'm conscious" is just one more physical event. The burn doesn't make me wince because it hurts — the nerve signals do. The red wall doesn't make me say "that's red" because of how it looks — the visual pathways do. So my belief that I have experiences was never caused by any experience; if the same physical processes had run without any experiences attached, I'd still have typed these words. So why should I trust the belief at all? And if I shouldn't trust it, then introspection was never evidence for the theory, and the theory saws off the branch it's sitting on. I have a feeling I'm reinventing something old, but I'm not asking for a survey of who said what — I want a verdict on the argument itself. Is the self-undermining worry fatal? Is there some modified version of the view that survives it, or do I have to give up the claim that consciousness does no causal work? Where exactly does it break, and what's the strongest repair available?
