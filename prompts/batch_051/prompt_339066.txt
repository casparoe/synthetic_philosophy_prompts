I keep noticing a pattern in how English builds word forms, and I don't have the vocabulary for it. If I coin a new noun—say "blorp"—speakers automatically form "blorps" for more than one, and the ending -s applies to essentially any noun, new or old, with nothing added except the predictable meaning "more than one." There the -s behaves like an automatic grammatical rule. But the same -s also shows up where it is nothing like that. "Physics" wears a plural-looking -s yet takes singular agreement, and there is no count noun "physic" meaning that science. "Peoples" adds -s to a form, "people," that was already the plural of "person," because "people" has come to be treated as a singular noun for a nation or ethnic group. "Scissors" is plural in form even when it refers to one tool. And some plurals have settled into idiomatic meanings of their own—"colors" for a flag, "damages" for monetary compensation, "spirits" for alcohol—where the meaning is not recoverable from the singular plus "more than one." So the same piece of form ranges from an automatic rule to an unpredictable dictionary item, with many cases in between. Does this split have an established name, and is there a recognized way of classifying the intermediate cases? I want to know what tests decide whether a given -s is being applied by a rule or stored as a word, which of my examples fall on which side, and whether the field treats the distinction as a clean dichotomy or a graded boundary. A bare label won't help; I want the criteria that justify it. Please keep the answer under 300 words.
