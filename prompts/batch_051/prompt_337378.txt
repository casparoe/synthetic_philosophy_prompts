Last night I spent a long evening arguing with a friend about whether the phrase we do not know the odds names a different kind of uncertainty or just a particularly vague probability judgment. I kept saying that some decisions require a different stance, and my friend kept answering that this is only confusion. The argument has made me want to test my intuition against a real institutional practice. Consider how the same regulatory body often behaves. For ordinary market risks, it requires explicit probability models, publishes point forecasts, and commits itself to expected-value reasoning. For low-frequency, high-severity threats such as pandemics, abrupt geopolitical ruptures, or the failure of a fundamentally new financial structure, it refuses to assign probabilities and instead works with a small number of scenarios that are deliberately left without likelihoods. It does not treat the missing numbers as a temporary gap in the model; it treats the demand for a probability as a category mistake. Yet it still acts: it builds precautionary buffers, runs stress tests, and makes qualitative judgments of resilience, and it considers those judgments disciplined rather than arbitrary. What view of probability and uncertainty does this practice presuppose? Does it actually presuppose a distinction in kind between measurable risk and unmeasurable uncertainty, in the spirit of Keynes, or is it fully compatible with a subjectivist view on which there are only degrees of belief, some of them very imprecise? Be specific about where the practice genuinely rules out the subjectivist reconstruction and where it is underdetermined. And tell me what this institution would have to change in its behavior if it replaced its implicit theory with a consistently Bayesian one tomorrow.
