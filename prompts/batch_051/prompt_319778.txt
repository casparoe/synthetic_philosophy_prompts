I have recently been drawn into helping design a citizens' assembly on climate adaptation, and in the course of that work I have settled, tentatively, on a set of Habermasian commitments that I would like someone to audit for internal consistency before I bring them to the other organizers. My commitments are these. First, a collective decision is legitimate only if all those affected could in principle accept it under idealized conditions: everyone affected has equal standing and equal opportunity to speak, no participant is coerced or subtly dominated, and participants engage one another in order to reach mutual understanding rather than to win. Second, because actual discourse is always shot through with power asymmetries, the fact that a real group actually converged on a consensus has no legitimacy-conferring force by itself. Third, I nonetheless treat our assembly's consensus as a legitimate basis for city recommendations, because our facilitated process realized significant elements of that ideal—structured inclusion, trained facilitation, equal speaking rights—even though it fell far short in other respects. Fourth, I also hold that the ideal speech situation should not be treated as a blueprint that institutions could progressively approximate; it is better understood as a presupposition already implicit in the act of arguing, one whose gap no actual procedure can close. I want to know which of these commitments are in genuine tension and which only seem to be. Please rank the tensions by severity, say for each what would have to give if I wanted to restore coherence, and tell me which single revision would do the most work.
