I've been reading the back-and-forth between people who say markets civilize us and people who say they corrupt us, and one thing I can't get past is how each side treats the same facts as obvious evidence for their view. The pro-market side points to long-distance trade makeing strangers willing to trust each other enough to keep contracts; the anti-market side looks at the same merchants and sees only instrumental friendliness, flattery, and a narrowing of what they'd even count as a genuine relationship. This argument has been going on since at least the eighteenth century, and as far as I can tell no fact about actually existing markets has ever changed anybody's mind. So my question is less "which side is right" and more: what kind of disagreement is this? Is it empirical in principle but starved of the right evidence, a fight about what should count as virtue, two different questions being asked under one name, or an ill-posed question? Suppose we found solid evidence that market societies reliably produce both more honesty-as-following-through-on-deals and more instrumentalization of relationships; would that move either side? My hunch is it wouldn't, because the two camps are using different yardsticks, but I want a diagnosis with reasons rather than a survey of the positions. And then, given that diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, a reframed question, a kind of evidence, or a reason to think the whole thing is just a clash of starting intuitions that no argument can fix.
