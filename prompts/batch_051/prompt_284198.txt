I'm a novelist, not a social scientist, and I keep coming back to a scene where two characters fight about that finding that follows me around the internet: the more a country scores on gender-equality indexes, the more strongly its men and women sort into different careers, with the high-equality countries having a smaller share of women in engineering and a bigger share in care work. One character has a chain of reasoning he thinks ends the debate, and I need to know wether he's actually right, because if he is, the scene has to go one way; if he's wrong, the other character has to be able to put her finger on the exact step, not just wave her hands.

Here's the argument:

(1) If sexist socialization were the main thing pushing men and women toward different careers, then reducing that socialization should make the career distributions of men and women converge.
(2) Countries that score highest on gender equality have reduced the relevant sexist pressures (stereotypes, bias, discouragement, legal barriers) more than any other countries.
(3) Yet by the usual measures the career distributions in those countries are not more similar; if anything they are even more different.
(4) So the socialization story makes a false prediction.
(5) If the difference isnt mainly socialization, it must be mainly something else, and the obvious candidate is something innate.
(6) And if it's innate, then the project of pushing every field toward a fifty-fifty mix is fighting nature, and the unequal outcomes aren't an injustice that needs fixing.

I can't accept (6); if he's right, the whole back half of the novel collapses. But I also don't want the other character to reject a step just because the conclusion is ugly. She needs to know which step breaks, and what giving it up costs. The thing is, I'd like her to be able to keep (3), because that finding is the only concrete thing in the scene. If the argument is actually sound, tell me that too, because then I have to write the scene differently.
