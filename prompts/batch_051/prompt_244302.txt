Two friends of mine got into it at a party after one of them, Dan, gave $40 to a stranger outside a 7-Eleven who said he was from the future and needed the money for a component to repair his time machine so he could go back and prevent a plague that kills most of humanity. The other friend, Mira, watched this happen and tore into him. The argument went something like this:

(1) Dan: Even if there's only a one-in-a-billion chance he's telling the truth, $40 to save billions of lives is the best expected value of anything I'll buy this month. Do the math and you'd do it too.
(2) Mira: That logic means you have to pay every grifter with a crazy story, because each one can promise an even bigger payoff for the same price. Your rule is just "hand money to anyone who claims to be saving the world."
(3) Dan: No, because I only do it when the arithmetic works out. One-in-a-billion times, say, eight billion people saved is eight expected lives. Forty bucks for eight expected lives is better than almost any charity. If you wouldn't spend forty dollars to save eight lives, that's a you problem.
(4) Mira: But your one-in-a-billion isn't measured from anything. It's a number you pulled out of the air. A con man can always pick a story with an enormous payoff and a probability too small for you to refute, and the expected value stays huge. You've built a decision procedure that any story can hijack.
(5) Dan: That's a problem with bad probability estimates, not with expected value reasoning. We run tiny-probability, huge-payoff calculations all the time: seatbelts, smoke alarms, vaccines.
(6) Mira: Those probabilities come from data. This one comes from the same sentence as the request for money. There's a difference between a small real risk and a fantasy. Below some probability you should just treat the chance as zero, otherwise your a mark.
(7) Dan: Treating small probabilities as zero is exactly how people get killed. A one-in-a-million reactor accident is still real. You can't draw a line and say everything below it doesn't count.
(8) Mira: I'm not saying ignore real small risks. I'm saying that when the only evidence for the risk is the story of the guy asking for the money, the probability isn't one-in-a-billion, it's way closer to zero. You've confused "small but real" with "made up but convenient."

Neither of them would budge, and they asked me to settle it. I couldn't, so now I'm asking you. Go through move by move and say who's right about what: rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a sentence or two, then say who's more right overall, and whether either of them is missing a cleaner way out of the mess.
