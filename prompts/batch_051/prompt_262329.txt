I've ended up with a view I'd call Sidgwickian. Henry Sidgwick — author of The Methods of Ethics, hardly a crank — helped found the Society for Psychical Research and spent years on it because, as I understand him, he thought evidence for telepathy or survival would bear directly on whether the universe is morally ordered in the way his ethics seemed to need; and that this was an empirical question to be settled by careful investigation, not armchair metaphysics. The position I want stress-tested is the methodological one: questions about telepathy and survival are ordinary empirical questions; if controlled, statistical work with cross-examined witnesses turned up effects that defeat every mundane explanation — fraud, coincidence, misremembering, selective reporting — then a rational person should shift their metaphysics at least a little; and the fact that a century of work has produced no clean result doesn't refute the programme, because a real effect could be rare and faint.

What would count against that position? Not 'what would show telepathy is false', but what would show the Sidgwickian stance is wrong or empty. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, an honest account of which of them a defender of the position would themselves concede, and a verdict on whether the view has been insulated into unfalsifiability — and if it has, whether that's actually a problem for a view of this kind.
