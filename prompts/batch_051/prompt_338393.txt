For a paper I'm writing for my school's philosophy club, I've been asked to take a side on whether universities should keep funding and sheltering fields that attract almost no students and have no obvious practical payoff — things like the study of extinct writing systems, the history of alchemy, or highly abstract corners of pure mathematics. The assignment requires me to anchor my argument in a primary text, and the passage I want to work with is from John Henry Newman's The Idea of a University, in the discourse titled "Knowledge Its Own End":

"It is a great point then to enlarge the range of studies which a University professes, even for the sake of the students; and, though they cannot pursue every subject which is open to them, they will be the gainers by living among those and under those who represent the whole circle. This I conceive to be the advantage of a seat of universal learning, considered as a place of education. An assemblage of learned men, zealous for their own sciences, and rivals of each other, are brought, by familiar intercourse and for the sake of intellectual peace, to adjust together the claims and relations of their respective subjects of investigation. They learn to respect, to consult, to aid each other. Thus is created a pure and clear atmosphere of thought, which the student also breathes, though in his own case he only pursues a few sciences out of the multitude."

I'd like you to write the essay I'm trying to write. First, explain exactly what Newman is claiming here about why it matters for a university to host areas of study that no given student will pursue. Second, test whether his argument really applies to the most obscure and apparently "useless" fields that universities actually support today, or whether it only justifies keeping a broad curriculum in general. In doing so, weigh the strongest objections — for example, that scarce money and student tuition should not subsidize fields with no external payoff, or that what looks like a principled defense of odd scholarship is often just a cover for preserving faculty jobs in low-demand areas. Reach a definite conclusion and justify it; do not end neutrally or say the issue is simply a matter of balancing. If you think Newman's reasoning fails under some conditions, say clearly what those conditions are and what follows for university funding.
