In *On the Heavens*, Aristotle presents an early version of what later gets called Buridan's ass. He says, in effect, that a man equally hungry and thirsty, standing at an equal distance from food and drink, would have to remain where he is, because there is no reason pushing him more toward one than the other; he then uses this as an analogy for the earth staying at rest at the centre of the cosmos. I want to use the case to test a set of criteria, not to settle the physics.

Here are the criteria I was taught for an action's being an exercise of choice: (1) the agent has more than one available option; (2) the agent considers each option under a description; (3) the agent forms a preference for one option over the others; (4) the agent's overt behaviour is caused by that preference in the right way; and (5) the agent could give a reason for the chosen option. Now consider a variant of Aristotle's case: a person is in a room with two identical doors, has equal evidence and equal desires concerning them, but knows that one will open and one will not, and must select one in order to leave. She considers both, pauses, then announces that she will simply pick the left one, with no further reason. On the criteria above, is her action an exercise of choice? Work through each criterion in turn, and say whether the case shows that one or more of the criteria should be revised, replaced, or treated as only typically present rather than strictly necessary. Please answer in British English.
