There's a fight I've watched recur on LessWrong and a few rationalist-adjacent blogs for what feels like a decade, and it never moves. One side says information can't hurt you: Good's theorem says that if a signal is free and your utility is defined over world-states and acts, then the value of the information is nonnegative in expectation, because you can always just use the same decision rule you would have used without the signal. So someone who pays not to know, or turns down a free test, is making a decision-theoretic error — maybe failing to identify with their future self, or smuggling in a taste for self-deception. The other side says this is obviously wrong about real cases: people don't want to know the date of their own death, don't want to find out a partner cheated, won't get a genetic test for Huntington's, and in those cases the knowledge itself changes the felt quality of the rest of their life. They say Good's theorem only goes through because utility is defined over world-states and acts, so the fact of knowing can't carry a direct payoff; once you let utility depend on your own posterior beliefs the result just doesn't follow. What's strange is that the two sides never seem to be disagreeing about anything empirical. Each just restates their premise about what is allowed inside a utility function and then accuses the other of equivocating. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is really about whether a coherent utility function is allowed to depend on your own posterior beliefs, and that one side is doing normative decision theory while the other is doing descriptive psychology of preference, so they aren't answering the same question under one heading. But I keep going back and forth on whether that's the right diagnosis, whether it's instead a substantive disagreement about whether there's any norm against caring about your own beliefs, or just a verbal one about the word rational. I'm not asking who wins. I want the best available diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is, and what would count as progress given that diagnosis — a formal result, a distinction, an experiment, or an argument that the question is ill-posed in principal.
