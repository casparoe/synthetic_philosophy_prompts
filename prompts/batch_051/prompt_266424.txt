Im trying to settle a reading-group argument about whether the young Marx and the old Marx are even talking about the same thing when they criticize wage labor. In the Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844, in the section on estranged labour, Marx writes:

"Labor's realization is its objectification. Under these economic conditions this realization of labor appears as loss of realization for the workers; objectification as loss of the object and bondage to it; appropriation as estrangement, as alienation."

And in Capital, Volume One, Chapter 9 (The Rate of Surplus-Value), he writes:

"The surplus-value generated in the process of production by C, the capital advanced, or in other words, the self-expansion of the value of the capital C, presents itself for our consideration, in the first place, as a surplus, as the amount by which the value of the product exceeds the value of its constituent elements... From what has gone before, we know that surplus-value is purely the result of a variation in the value of v, of that portion of the capital which is transformed into labour-power; consequently, v + s = v + v', or v plus an increment of v."

The first passage makes the problem sound like a kind of lived inversion — the worker's own activity and product turn against him. The second makes the problem sound like a structural, almost arithmetic fact about value expanding, with the worker demoted to a variable in a formula. So what's the relationship here? Is the later Marx just supplying the mechanism behind the earlier alienation-talk, did he actually drop the humanist frame, or are these two different claims that happen to both be true? Give me the candidate reconciliations (different senses of "loss," different levels of description, a change of mind, a distinction Marx never draws explicitly) and then your honest verdict on which reading does the least special pleading.
