I write about science and ideas for a general but technically literate audience. I came to this problem while reporting on confabulation and the psychology of the feeling of knowing, but the version that has stayed with me is older. In one idiom it is Descartes's: if my cognitive equipment might have been arranged by a deceiver, no test I run on it can vouch for itself. In another it is the problem of the criterion: I need a criterion to tell sound inference from unsound, but choosing the criterion is itself an inference, and the circle never closes. I recently returned to a passage in David Hume's A Treatise of Human Nature, Book 1, Part 4, Section 7, where Hume appears to grant the skeptic's point and then notice what remains after the grant is complete. He writes: "Can I be sure, that in leaving all establish'd opinions I am following truth; and by what criterion shall I distinguish her, even if fortune shou'd at last guide me on her foot-steps? After the most accurate and exact of my reasonings, I can give no reason why I shou'd assent to it; and feel nothing but a strong propensity to consider objects strongly in that view, under which they appear to me."

Here is my problem. The theory this passage belongs to is very hard for me to escape. On reflection, my assent to even my most careful reasoning bottoms out in a felt propensity rather than in a chain of reasons I can complete, and there is no non-circular standpoint from which I can certify that this propensity tracks truth instead of merely producing conviction. Yet I cannot actually live that thought. When I watch a child step toward a busy road, or check a drug dosage, or feel a plane's wheels touch the runway, I do not notice a customary propensity; I experience certainty as firm as anything I could claim to possess. I find myself wanting both the philosophical claim that no criterion is available and the ordinary conviction that these beliefs are not merely caused but in good standing.

I do not want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for each side to give way, and whether there is a reading on which both survive. Should the ordinary conviction be debunked — an artifact of the same liveliness of ideas whose role in belief Hume dismisses as arbitrary — and what would it take for me to distrust something so involuntary? Or should the philosophical story be amended, since being unable to give a reason may not amount to lacking warrant, so that ordinary certainty can be correct even without explicit inference? If both survive, I want that reading stated precisely and told what it costs. Act as an expert on early modern skepticism and naturalized epistemology, think carefully, and start by brainstorming the strongest version of each side before you render a negotiated verdict. Then be explicit about your confidence: where is your preferred reading most likely to fail, and which argument or consideration would make you adopt the other verdict?
