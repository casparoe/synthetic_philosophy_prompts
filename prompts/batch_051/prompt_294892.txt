Write a villanelle (the standard nineteen-line form: five tercets and a closing quatrain) on the relation between Peirce's pragmatic maxim and his account of the fixation of belief. The two recurring lines should express, respectively, the maxim's claim that the whole meaning of a conception lies in its conceivable practical consequences, and the fallibilist claim that every fixed belief remains answerable to future experience. Let the alternation and return of these two refrains do structural work: the poem should enact the rhythm of inquiry itself — the irritation of doubt giving way to settled belief, and settled belief being unsettled by surprise — so that by the final quatrain the reader sees the two claims not as rivals but as two halves of one self-correcting method. You may vary the wording of the refrains slightly for grammar, but they should remain recognisably the same two claims. Keep the poem faithful enough that a reader of Peirce would recognise the view: treat beliefs as habits of action rather than inert representations, and do not reduce truth to whatever is merely useful or convenient. The closing lines should also register Peirce's warning that the one thing inquiry may not do is block its own further road. The poem should be strong enough as verse that a reader who has never encountered Peirce would still find it a good poem. Please write it in British English.
