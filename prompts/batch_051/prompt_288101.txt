I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, but I adjunct an intro philosophy of language class at the local college because it beats teaching comp, and on the midterm I asked a question I'm now realizing is a little harder to grade than I thought. Here's the question and one student's answer, exactly as they wrote it.

Question: Explain why the following two sentences are often claimed to show that indicative conditionals and counterfactual conditionals need different semantic treatments: If Oswald didn't shoot Kennedy, someone else did; and If Oswald hadn't shot Kennedy, someone else would have. Be explicit about whether each one is being evaluated for truth or for something else like probability or assertability.

Student answer: The two sentences pull apart. We accept the first one but reject the second, even though they differ only in verb tense and not in the facts described. On the standard world-based account of counterfactuals, the second sentence is evaluated by considering the closest possible worlds in which Oswald didn't shoot Kennedy. In those worlds he just misses or loses his nerve and there is no backup gunman, so Kennedy isn't killed in them, which makes the counterfactual false. The first sentence works differently because it is about our information, not about what would have happened. We already know that someone shot Kennedy, so among the possibilities not ruled out by our evidence there is no world where Oswald didn't shoot and no one else did. Since any way of adding the antecedent to our evidence forces someone else as the shooter, the probability of the consequent given the antecedent is high. So the sentence is true in a probability sense, unlike the counterfactual. This is why the two kinds of conditionals need different semantics: world-based truth conditions for the counterfactual, and probability-based truth for the indicative.
