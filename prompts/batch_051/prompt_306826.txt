I'm in a course on political obligation and we read Thoreau's "Resistance to Civil Government" (1849, the essay people now usually call "Civil Disobedience"). I got a little obssessed with one sentence, not the usual ones people quote:

"Law never made men a whit more just; and, by means of their respect for it, even the well-disposed are daily made the agents of injustice."

I tried to turn that into an argument that political obligation simply lapses whenever the law is unjust, and I want to check whether it actually goes through. Here's my reconstruction, with the steps numbered because my instructor keeps asking me to show my work:

(1) Some laws are unjust, and a reflective person can sometimes see that a particular law they are subject to is unjust.
(2) If you obey a law you judge to be unjust, then in complying you are yourself doing an injustice; even if your motives are decent, you become what Thoreau calls an agent of injustice.
(3) Whoever does injustice is an agent of injustice.
(4) You are never morally obligated to be an agent of injustice.
(5) So you have no moral obligation to obey a law you judge unjust.
(6) Therefore political obligation—the obligation to obey the law as such—doesn't cover unjust laws at all; it ends exactly where you judge the law unjust.

I keep going back and forth on whether step 2 is doing too much work, or whether step 4 breaks in lesser-evil cases (obeying a mildly unjust law to avoid something much worse). But maybe both are fine and I just don't like the conclusion. Tell me exactly which step, if any, fails, and what the corrected version of that step would be—or say the argument is actually valid. Also, does it matter that the argument runs on my judging a law unjust rather than on the law actually being unjust?
