I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy. Recently, after a security incident at my company, I watched two colleagues argue about blame. The vulnerable component had been silently deprioritized across three teams, and no individual had ever made the decision to ship it. One colleague said we should still blame the company itself; another answered that this is a category error, because only beings with intentions can be blamed; a third cut in with "corporations are people," as though that settled it.

I doubt that phrase settles anything, but I also cannot say exactly what it is claiming. Treat it as a disambiguation problem. What are the distinct precise theses "corporations are people" could be expressing—legal personhood, moral standing, moral agency, irreducible group decision-making, mere aggregate shorthand, or something else? For each reading, tell me whether it is trivially true but morally inert, substantive and defensible, or likely false, and whether that reading could support blaming a company as a company rather than blaming the individuals or processes inside it. In the argument I witnessed, the slogan was being used to move from "the law lets firms sue and be sued" to "the firm is a proper target of moral blame." Does any reading actually license that move, or does the legal sense do all the work while a moral sense slips in unnoticed?

One further point I keep getting stuck on: blame seems to require that the blamed thing can be made to answer for itself, yet a corporation does not feel fines or resentment. Is corporate blame then merely deterrence and coordination under another name, or can an organization answer in a stronger sense? As an engineer, I am comfortable with saying a service "decided" to fail over when it was really thresholds and fallbacks; I want to know whether "the company decided" is the same kind of harmless heuristic, or something more. Please lay out a menu of readings with verdicts rather than just the one you favor.
