I've been trying to sort out what I actually think about blame, because I read a summary of Galen Strawson's Basic Argument and it's been rattling around in my head ever since. As I understand it, the argument is: everything you do comes out of the kind of person you are, so to be truly responsible for an action you'd have to be truly responsible for the kind of person you are. But you can't be, because you never chose your nature; and even if you set out to improve yourself, you'd be doing that from whatever nature you already happened to have, and that nature traces back to earlier causes before you even existed. So nobody is truly responsible for anything. I half-convinced myself of this, but my actual blame reactions are all over the place, and I want to know if there's one principle behind them or if I need to give some up.

Here are my verdicts:

- A guy gets drunk because he's had a terrible week, then drives home and hits someone. I blame him completely, even though I know he never chose to be the kind of person who drinks to cope.
- A man develops a brain tumor that changes his personality and makes him violent, and he attacks a nurse. I don't blame him at all; that just wasn't him.
- A friend of mine grew up in a brutal home and came out mean and dishonest. When he cheats people, I blame him less than I'd blame someone with an easy upbringing, but I do still blame him some.
- Another person from the same background somehow turns out gentle and honest. I hugely admire them. But if the Basic Argument is right, whatever made them able to turn out that way was just as unchosen as whatever made my friend mean, so shouldn't my admiration be just as misplaced as my blame?
- Someone with OCD washes their hands until they're raw, even though they know it's irrational. I don't blame them, just feel sorry for them.
- An alcoholic knows they're an alcoholic, drinks anyway, then drives drunk. I blame them, even though the urge to drink is probably just as compelling as the urge to wash.

Is there a coherent principle that captures all these? If I keep every one of these verdicts, am I just fooling myself, and which ones does the Basic Argument actually force me to drop? Also, whatever principle you find, tell me what it says about someone who is naturally decent and never had to fight a real temptation — do they deserve credit like the person from the bad background who had to fight for it?
