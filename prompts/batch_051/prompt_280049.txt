I am a novelist working on a book about the Allied war-crimes tribunals after the Second World War, and I keep returning to a puzzle in the actual practice rather than in any fictional version of it. At Nuremberg and Tokyo, the victors tried and executed enemy leaders for acts that had not, at the time they were committed, been codified as individual crimes under any enforceable international code — and, for the charge of planning and waging aggressive war, not under the defendants' own national law either. The same tribunals nevertheless presented themselves as courts doing law rather than victors merely imposing punishment, kept records, allowed defense counsel and cross-examination, and their judgments claimed fidelity to the principle that there can be no crime without prior law, rather than openly setting that principle aside. What view of legality, and of the relation between moral wrong and legal guilt, does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory of criminal law that would permit punishment to be lawful without prior positive law, distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely accommodates. Would a consistent legal positivist be forced to condemn the tribunals, and what would the proceedings have had to look like if that positivist view had actually governed them?
