I am drafting a short essay on when self-examination shades into harmful rumination, and I would like your critical comments on the argument before I revise. The working thesis is that the standard question — how much introspection is too much? — is misleading, because harmful rumination differs from useful self-examination less in quantity than in the kind of question being asked. My notes are below, followed by the specific points on which I want criticism.

Notes toward the essay:

1. I distinguish diagnostic from deliberative self-examination. In diagnostic self-examination, one treats the self as an object to be read: the aim is to detect a fact already in place, such as whether one is honest, whether one truly loves one's partner, or what one really wants. In deliberative self-examination, one treats the self as an agent: the aim is to decide what to do next, what to revise, what to commit to, and the inquiry is completed in action rather than in further interpretation.

2. The core claim is that rumination is diagnostic self-examination aimed at global, evaluative, and query-sensitive traits. Ruminators typically seek a verdict on the self as a whole and search memory and current feeling for evidence. But no observation settles questions of that kind, and every candidate answer can be reinterpreted as self-deception or defensiveness. The inquiry therefore has no internal stopping rule.

3. The deeper problem is query-sensitivity: gathering evidence changes the evidence base. Repeatedly reviewing one's past for signs of selfishness tends to retrieve confirming episodes, because one searches under that description; and the review makes one's ongoing behavior self-conscious, so that spontaneous concern becomes hard to distinguish from performance. The method does not measure the self neutrally; it tends to produce the condition it claims to detect.

4. This lets the essay reject the usual advice to simply introspect less, without insisting that introspection is always bad. Diagnostic self-examination is harmless when its question is local and terminates in behavior — asking why a remark stung, answered by deciding what to say to the person. What makes rumination harmful is not inwardness but an inquiry whose satisfaction conditions lie always one interpretation further inward.

5. The final move is to convert recursive self-questions from diagnostic into deliberative form: from "am I this kind of person?" to "what would I have to do, or stop doing, if this were true?" The identity question is then answered obliquely, by what one does, because action is the medium in which such questions can have determinate answers.

I would appreciate comments on four issues. First, can the diagnostic/deliberative distinction be drawn sharply enough to carry the argument, or will plausible cases of useful self-examination blur it? Second, does the essay smuggle in a controversial metaphysical claim — that there is no fact of the matter about what one really wants apart from what one does — and if so, should I argue for it directly or weaken the dependence on it? Third, how should I handle the objection that rumination can be accurate, and that self-knowledge may be worth the distress, so the essay should not equate harmful with epistemically defective? Fourth, is the query-sensitivity mechanism plausible enough to bear the argument's empirical weight, given that some people report major self-understanding only after long stretches of apparently unproductive self-questioning?
