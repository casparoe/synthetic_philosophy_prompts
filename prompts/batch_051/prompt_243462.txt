I have been working through the foundations of imprecise probability, and I keep noticing a structural analogy that I cannot decide is deep or merely verbal. On one side, the imprecise-probability framework replaces a single Bayesian prior with a set of admissible probability measures—a credal set—and evaluates a proposed act by its minimum expected utility across that set, the lower prevision, so that alternatives are ranked by how they perform under the least favourable distribution the agent cannot rule out. On the other side, distributionally robust optimization, which I know from operations research and machine learning, does something formally very similar: rather than minimizing expected cost against one estimated distribution, it minimizes expected cost against the worst distribution in an ambiguity set, yielding decisions that remain acceptable under every distribution in the set. The correspondence looks almost term-by-term to me: credal set to ambiguity set, lower prevision to worst-case expected cost, and the refusal to pretend to a sharp probability to the refusal to trust a sharp point estimate.

Yet I suspect the parallel may collapse on inspection. The robust optimization literature treats an ambiguity set chiefly as a hedge against finite-sample estimation error and prizes tractable reformulation; it does not, so far as I can tell, commit itself to any view about rational degrees of belief or about what underdetermined evidence should do to an agent's confidence. Imprecise probability, by contrast, is built on coherence axioms and on the thought that rationality itself may require refusing a single prior when the evidence leaves a range of probability distributions open. My question is whether these are two applications of one underlying structure or only the same mathematics put to unrelated purposes.

If the analogy is genuine, what would have to be true on both sides for a theorem from one field to transfer to the other? If it is superficial, precisely where does the mapping break? As a test, I have been asking myself whether, if the analogy were tight, something corresponding to dilation—the way an interval probability estimate for an event can be properly contained in the interval estimate conditional on every member of a partition, so that learning which member holds makes the estimate strictly less precise no matter what is learned—ought to show up in distributionally robust optimization as well. Does it show up, and does its presence or absence tell us which way to read the analogy?
