Two friends of mine argued over dinner about a third friend's story from a trip. In a city where the local custom at counters is to press forward rather than form a queue, she kept hanging back, let locals go ahead, and later complained that they had cut in front of her. (1) One friend said her complaint was justified: arriving first gives a person a claim to be served next, and queueing is a duty of fairness, not mere local etiquette, so the locals did wrong her. (2) The other replied that queueing can bind only among people who share the practice; where no one accepted it, there was no established turn for her to hold, and a visitor's private standard of queueing creates no claim on locals. (3) The first came back that this makes obligations collapse into local custom and would force a visitor to comply with a tipping norm she thinks exploits servers, or a jaywalking norm she thinks is reckless, simply because locals follow them. (4) The second answered that this conflates rules that protect people from harm with rules that merely coordinate access to a scarce good, and that queueing and tipping are mostly of the second kind; a visitor has no business imposing her own coordinating convention on strangers. Neither could explain where a convention of the second kind gets normative force for someone who never joined the practice, and they asked me to referee, which I couldn't. Please adjudicate their exchange move by move: say which claims hold up, where they talk past each other, and who is more right overall. Distinguish what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. Lead with your overall bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
