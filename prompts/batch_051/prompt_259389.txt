i run a game club at a high school — about a dozen kids, 90 minutes a week — and ive been thinking on my own about this thing people say about two-player, no-luck, turn-based games. the claim is that in certain games the second player cant have a winning strategy, because the first player can make a throwaway opening move and then just copy the second players strategy; whenever the copied strategy tells him to play on the spot he already took, he plays somewhere else, and the extra stone is supposed to only help. that arguement always felt weirdly strong to me, and i think ive finally put my finger on where it leans its weight: it assumes an extra move or stone of your own can never hurt you. and in some games it can, like those chess spots where youd rather pass than move (i think its called zugzwang).

i want the kids to feel both sides of that. by the end i want them to see that the stealing argument only works because of the idea that an extra move or stone of your own is never a liability, and that this is a real assumption rather than something you get for free. design me a 90-minute session with two games. game one: a small connection game on a hexagon board — two players, each owns two opposite sides, they take turns coloring one empty hex, and the first to connect their two sides with an unbroken chain wins (no draws, basically). there, an extra stone of your own really cant hurt, so the stealing argument goes through. game two: a forced-move take-away game to make the point that moving can be a liability — ive been messing with starting at 13 counters, taking one or two per turn, and whoever takes the last one loses, where you can be stuck in a spot that any move hands the win to the other kid. if youve got a better small game for the same point, use it, but commit to one.

i want them to run into the assumption themselves before i name it — maybe by trying to give the second player a guarenteed winning recipe in game one and watching the first player steal it, then finding exactly where the same trick stops working in game two. gimme the exact rules for both games, a timeline, and the nudges or questions i should use instead of lecturing. then a debrief that maps what happened back to the assumption. also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time, and which part of the idea the activity cant reproduce (i suspect its the nonconstructive part — the stealing argument shows a win exists without saying what to play, and im not sure high schoolers will be able to feel that). and please commit: pick one definite version of each game, one timeline, and one set of nudges, and give your reasons for the choices, not a menu of options.
