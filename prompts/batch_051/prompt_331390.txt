Before I read another proposed resolution of the St. Petersburg game, I want the specification against which any such proposal should be judged: the desiderata that an adequate treatment of the paradox has to satisfy, or deliberately reject with an explicit reason. The debate keeps talking past itself because different resolutions presuppose different standards of success—one writer treats the puzzle as a problem about the offerer’s finite bankroll, another as a problem about the shape of the player’s utility function, another as a problem about whether expected utility maximization is a sound norm here at all. I want the list of requirements, with the case or consideration behind each, and a clear view on which requirements are non-negotiable and which are merely preferences of a particular camp.

The list must at least take a stand on the following. (i) It should recover the datum that thoughtful agents decline to pay an arbitrarily large fee for a single play, without simply dismissing them as irrational or computationally lazy. (ii) It should handle the truncated game, in which the coin is tossed at most K times and nothing is paid if no head appears before the cap; here the standard resolutions come apart sharply, since expected money value grows linearly with the cap while a concave-utility account will typically make the price converge as the cap grows, so an adequate treatment must say which behavior is correct and defend it. (iii) It should address the repeated game and say whether the availability of many independent plays changes the fair per-play price, and if so how fast that price grows and why. (iv) It should treat the whole geometric family of games in which the first head on toss n pays the amount a^n, and it should explain the significance of the divergence threshold at a equal to 2 rather than tuning the account to the standard case alone. (v) It should separate the diagnosis that the offerer cannot pay the largest prizes from the diagnosis that the player’s utility is bounded, and say which, if either, lies at the heart of the paradox. (vi) It should say which axioms of expected utility maximization, if any, the treatment preserves, restricts, or abandons. Flag any pair of these desiderata that you believe cannot be jointly satisfied as stated; in particular, I suspect there is a strain between keeping expected utility maximization with an unbounded utility function and assigning a finite value to every member of the geometric family, and I want to see exactly where that strain appears and how it should be resolved. Finally, score the main families of resolution briefly against the list: bounded utility, finite bankroll, nonlinear probability weighting, and the outright rejection of expected utility maximization as the relevant norm. Keep the scoring short; the criteria are the deliverable.
