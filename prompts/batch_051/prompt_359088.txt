I'm a third-year undergraduate in a seminar on collective intentionality, and I'll be leading a 50-minute tutorial for about 15 philosophy students. I would like a runnable activity that makes participants feel the difference between two things that can look identical in practice: a set of individual plans that merely interlock under common knowledge, and a shared intention sustained by a joint commitment that none of the participants can dissolve unilaterally. The centerpiece should be a small cooperative task, followed by a manufactured moment in which one member stops, stalls, or changes course unilaterally; the group then has to work out, without technical vocabulary, whether the others are entitled to rebuke, whether the withdrawing member needs the others' leave, and what would count as revising the plan together. Please include concrete rules, materials, a timed outline culminating in a debrief, and an honest note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (for instance, participants mistaking social discomfort or pressure for the distinctive normativity of joint commitment). The debrief should map specific moments of the exercise onto the interlocking-intentions/joint-commitment contrast, but the students should not hear those terms until after the moment has happened. End with a single sentence, enclosed in \answer{...}, stating the core insight you want them to carry away.
