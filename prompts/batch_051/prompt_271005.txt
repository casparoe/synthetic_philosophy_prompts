I keep tripping over an asymmetry in how we talk about art, and I want to know whether it is actually justified. If a busker plays a sloppy kazoo cover of a Beatles song, I have no problem saying they played the same song the Beatles recorded — just a worse performance of it. Music seems built around that idea: the same song can have thousands of performances and recordings and still be the same song. But if someone paints a visually indistinguishable, stroke-for-stroke copy of the Mona Lisa, nobody would call it the same painting. They would call it a copy, a reproduction, maybe a forgery — never another instance of the work. So we treat songs as repeatable and paintings as one-off, even though both involve someone reproducing something. Is there a principle that actually justifies that asymmetry, or is it just habit plus the art market?

I want the strongest justification you can find, not a shrug. Try to formulate the rule that makes a work repeatable or singular, then test it against the awkward in-between cases: original prints and etchings (which we call original even when there are fifty impressions), bronze casts of the same sculpture, digital files copied a million times, and a recording of one specific live performance. If the line cant be drawn cleanly, say whether we should give the asymmetry up, keep it for practical reasons, or revise it into something more defensible.
