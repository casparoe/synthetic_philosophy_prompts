Suppose that in late May and early June 1940 the evacuation of the British Expeditionary Force from Dunkirk had failed almost entirely. The Channel was too rough for small craft for all but a few hours, the Luftwaffe made the harbour unusable throughout, and by the time the perimeter collapsed fewer than thirty thousand British troops had been brought home, with the remainder killed or captured. Nothing else about the military position changes: France still falls, and the war continues.

Give me your best argued prediction of how British public memory of the war and British politics develop over the following three decades, compared with what actually happened. I am less interested in a simple yes or no than in the chain of mechanisms. In particular, consider these questions. Which functions served by the actual Dunkirk story—turning a catastrophic defeat into an emblem of national character, civilian participation, and providential escape—would be hardest to supply from later events in the war? Would the absence of that story have made the British public in 1940 more receptive to a negotiated peace, or less, and under what assumptions does each conclusion follow? Which alternative episodes, such as the Battle of Britain, the Blitz, or the North African campaign, would have expanded to fill the gap, and how would their meanings have shifted as a result? Finally, state which of your predictions you are most confident in, and what evidence about the formation and decay of national myths would most likely prove you wrong.
