I'm trying to sort out what I actually think about making some people worse off so that others are better off. Going by gut on cases: a carbon tax that raises prices for everyone, including people already struggling, but prevents a lot of future deaths—fine. Diverting a runaway train from five people onto a side track where it hits one—fine. Harvesting a healthy patient's organs to save five dying patients—not fine, not even close. A levee that protects a thousand homes but floods one farm, where the farmer gets compensated afterward—fine. A draft lottery in which one person will be sent on a near-suicidal mission and everyone knew the lottery was coming—fine, though it makes my stomach hurt. A clinical trial of an experimental drug on volunteers where the expectation is about one severe reaction in two hundred, but it will save hundreds—fine. Forcing a stranger in the hospital to donate bone marrow to one specific dying patient—not fine. And the case I keep going back and forth on: taking a ventilator away from a stable patient to give it to a patient who will definitely die without it—I feel okay about it, but I can't see how it's different from the transplant.

Part of what I'm going on, I think, is a seperateness-of-persons idea: you shouldn't trade one person's harm for another's benefit the way you would trade a harm now against a benefit later within one person's life. But I don't know how to square that with the carbon tax or the levee, where I'm happy to make exactly that kind of trade.

My current principle is something like: you can impose costs as side-effects, or as part of a scheme that is ex ante fair, but you can't deliberately use someone as a means without their consent. But the ventilator case looks like deliberate taking as much as the transplant, and the draft seems to use someone, only fairly in advance. Is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? What would it say about the ventilator case, and which of my verdicts do I need to revise?
