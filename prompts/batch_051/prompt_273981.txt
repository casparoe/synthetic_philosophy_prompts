I'd like you to draft the joint statement that a committed textualist and a committed purposivist about statutory interpretation could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Assume the two participants are sophisticated: neither is a strawman, neither accuses the other of simply making things up, and both are trying to state the debate precisely rather than win it. The statement should open with a body of claims both can sign, and I want these to go past the trivial commonplaces. Both presumably agree that the enacted text is the starting point and a real constraint, that context matters to what a provision means, and that judges may not substitute their own policy preferences, but the statement should also record the more substantive agreements a careful collaboration would uncover, for example about which easy cases every plausible view handles the same way and about which stock arguments each side considers too crude to attribute to the other. Then give the precise formulations of where they differ, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording. I expect at least one formulation about what statutory interpretation is ultimately answerable to, the public meaning of the words as enacted or the aim the legislature was pursuing, and at least one about the evidentiary status of legislative history. Then identify the cruxes, the sub-questions such that if one of them were settled at least one side would revise something. For each crux, specify a concrete case or statutory example that both sides would agree bears on it; one may be a rule forbidding vehicles in a park, but do not rely on that example alone. For each such case, record in advance what each side would predict about the right result and about how the other side would argue. Finally, say which parts of the dispute you judge to be verbal rather than substantive, if any, and which residual disagreement remains irreducible even after the collaboration. Write the whole document as a single piece of continuous prose, the way one would write an email or a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables. Where a numbered list would otherwise suggest itself, use ordinary connectives such as first, second, and next instead.
