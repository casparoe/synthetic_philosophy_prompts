I am on the committee of a small professional association, a little under forty members, which meets each spring to choose one of four proposed projects for the coming year. We have argued for years about the vote, and a colleague has put forward the following procedure. Each member submits a secret, complete ranking of the four projects, with no tied places. The count proceeds by elimination: the project with the fewest votes in the current count is removed, and its votes transfer to the highest-ranked surviving project on each ballot, until only two remain; the project preferred by a majority in those transferred rankings is adopted. Ties for elimination are broken by lot, and a tie in the final head-to-head is broken by drawing a single ballot at random and following that member's ranking. One further provision is added: before any ballots are cast, the outgoing committee may designate any one project as protected. A protected project cannot be eliminated during the transfer rounds; if it would otherwise have the fewest votes, the surviving project with the next fewest votes is eliminated instead, and the protected project can still lose the final head-to-head.

I take it as read that no voting method satisfies every attractive condition at once, so I am not asking whether this rule is flawless; I am asking what its particular defects are and how they would surface in practice, bearing in mind that the same members repeat this decision each year. Work through the incentives for an ordinary member deliberating over how to rank strategically, for a faction attempting to steer the result, and for the outgoing committee in choosing which project, if any, to protect: who would be first to learn to exploit the rule, with what effect on the projects that ultimately get chosen, and which of these failure modes could be corrected by a small amendment rather than by abandoning the scheme for some standard electoral method?
