I've been turning over an analogy for a while and I want it stress-tested rather than just nodded at. I call it the "moral bankruptcy" model of transitional justice. In ordinary bankruptcy, creditors almost never get paid in full, but a court forces the debtor to disclose everything under penalty, publicly tallies the claims, and then discharges the remaining debt so the debtor can start over. We accept that even though it looks like letting a promise-breaker off, because the realistic alternatives — hiding, endless fights between creditors — are worse. On my model, a truth commission is the trustee, perpetrator testimony is the forced disclosure of assets, amnesty is the discharge, and "national reconciliation" is just the fresh start. I think this explains three features of these processes that otherwise look irrational: the obsession with public findings even when there will be no punishment, the fact that amnesty can feel legitimate despite offending ordinary retributive instincts, and the permanent feeling that old debts were never really settled. South Africa's amnesty-for-testimony deal looks like the cleanest version of a discharge; Rwanda's gacaca courts look like a refusal of full discharge in favor of a huge number of small local reckonings; and Argentina's later reopening of prosecutions after its amnesty laws were scrapped looks like creditors clawing a discharge back.

But the ugly part is staring at me: in commercial bankruptcy the debtor doesn't get to keep their freedom, their money, and their place in the community, whereas in transitional settings the perpetrators often walk away with all three. So the model might not illuminate anything; it might just be a fancy apology for impunity, with "debt" and "fresh start" doing rhetorical work for the powerful. I want a candid assesment: is this model doing real explanatory work, or is it a metaphor with extra steps? have other people drawn this parallel in a way I've missed? and what are the three strongest objections — the ones where it genuinely breaks, not just where it makes people uncomfortable?
