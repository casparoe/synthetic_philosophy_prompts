I teach a philosophy of medicine course, and I've been using a rough-and-ready set of criteria for when an advance directive should settle what happens to a person later. The way I put it to the students, an earlier self's directive carries authority only if: (1) the person was competent when they wrote it; (2) they had genuinely adequate knowledge of the state they were directing about; (3) the directive is stable — not a one-off in a panic, consistent with their deeper values; and (4) the treatment being refused now is basically the treatment they had in mind, not something importantly different that came along since. The case I want to use in class next week is making me unsure about my own criteria.

Here's the case, disguised as I'd present it. A man in his early 40s with bipolar I has been through three severe manic episodes, each ending in hospital. His memory of the episodes themselves is patchy and mostly reconstructed from what his partner and friends told him afterward; what he knows intimately is the crash, the shame, the arrest he doesn't remember. While fully stable, he writes a psychiatric advance directive saying that if he is ever manic and refusing admission, his refusal is to be ignored: he is to be hospitalized and, if necessary, given an antipsychotic against his then-expressed wishes. He adds a clause saying that nothing he says while manic counts as revoking the document. Five years later, while again stable, he tells his partner over dinner that he isn't sure he should have signed it. "When I'm high, that version of me feels like the truest thing I've ever been. Maybe I shouldn't have signed away that person's say." But he never writes a revokation, and when his partner pushes, he says he doesn't want to change anything "right now." A few months after that, he is floridly manic, grandiose, and refusing hospital; the team has the directive and has to decide whose refusal counts — the man in front of them now, or the man who sat down and wrote the document.

Does the earlier directive qualify under my four criteria? Go through each one honestly, and where the answer is underdetermined, say whether that's because of my criteria or because of this case. In particular, I keep worrying that criterion (2) may be unsatisfiable in principle for this kind of condition: the only self who knows mania from the inside is the very self that can't be allowed to decide. Am I overreading that? Please also tell me how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point you are least sure of.
