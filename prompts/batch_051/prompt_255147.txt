I am a retired ecologist, not a machine learning researcher, which is why I need help drafting a decision memo rather than writing it myself. I serve on the allocation committee of a small private foundation that funds work on the governance of advanced AI. We are sitting on a proposal from an interdisciplinary team—two ML engineers, one economist, one philosopher—titled "Measuring the Alignment Tax Across Scales." The proposal asks for two years of funding to build a standardized benchmark that isolates the capability effects of instruction-tuning, reward-model training, and RLHF across several model families and parameter counts. Its central hypothesis is that the so-called alignment tax is not a single trade-off but several distinct mechanisms, and that the size and manageability of the tax depends on which mechanism one has in mind.

The proposal has split our committee. Several senior members believe this is exactly the kind of measurement work needed before anyone should claim that competitive pressure forces labs to deploy less aligned models. Others argue the project would reify a misleading analogy: they say the competitiveness constraint is not an empirical claim that can be settled by benchmarks, but a structural condition of any competitive ecology, and that a benchmark study will just produce false precision. One committee member has circulated the following passage from Ouyang et al., "Training language models to follow instructions with human feedback," to argue the tax is already known to be minimal:

"In human evaluations on our prompt distribution, outputs from the 1.3B parameter InstructGPT model are preferred to outputs from the 175B GPT-3, despite having 100x fewer parameters. Moreover, InstructGPT models show improvements in truthfulness and reductions in toxic output generation while having minimal performance regressions on public NLP datasets."

That member says the passage is strong evidence that alignment techniques can avoid catastrophic capability regressions, so the proposed benchmark is answering a question we have already been given an answer to. Another member says that inference is bad science: the quote judges alignment through a particular training pipeline, on particular NLP benchmarks, from one paper, and says nothing about the incentive conditions under which a firm decides whether to align a model at all.

Draft the written opinion I will circulate before our next meeting. It should recommend approve, reject, or approve with conditions, and it should give reasons the applicants and both factions could respond to. In particular, I want a careful treatment of what the Ouyang passage does and does not establish about the competitiveness constraint. Use your analysis of that passage to drive the recommendation, and make the memo actionable: if you approve with conditions, specify them precisely; if you reject, specify what kind of proposal would change your mind. Engage the strongest consideration on the losing side honestly.
