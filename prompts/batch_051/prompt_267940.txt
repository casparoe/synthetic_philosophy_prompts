In Language, Truth and Logic (1936), A. J. Ayer applies the verification principle to claims about survival after death. He argues that a sentence such as 'There is a life after death' is not merely insufficiently supported, but literally lacking in factual meaning: since no possible sense-experience could settle whether it is true, it fails to express a genuine proposition in the first place. Much later, after undergoing a near-death experience, Ayer wrote a short autobiographical essay in which he reported that the episode had slightly weakened his conviction that death would be the end of him, while insisting that it had not provided evidence for any religious doctrine and had left his philosophical views unchanged. Are these two positions in tension, and, if so, can they be reconciled? In your answer, distinguish carefully between a claim about the cognitive meaninglessness of a sentence and a change in one's subjective degree of belief concerning a related proposition. Consider whether Ayer's early verificationist position committed him to any particular view about how his own confidence should respond to such an experience, and whether the later essay is best read as a revision of his earlier view, an inconsistency, or a psychologically interesting report that leaves his philosophical commitments intact. State which reconciliation you find most defensible, and say whether any of the candidate reconciliations amount to special pleading.
