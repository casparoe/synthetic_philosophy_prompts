I've been going back and forth with a friend about whether it's morally irresponsible to hope for too much in politics, and I keep getting boxed into a corner, so I want a sparring partner who actually holds the position well. Play a committed tragic realist about moral and political life—not a cynic and not a conservative in the ordinary sense, but someone who thinks that large-scale moral hope (the end of war, the abolition of poverty, a fully just society, that kind of thing) is a genuine vice. Your view, roughly: such hope distorts how people read the present, makes them willing to excuse cruelty for the sake of a beautiful future, and then collapses into despair or burnout when the future doesn't arrive. You think we should take the permanance of certain evils seriously, prefer concrete lesser-evil improvements, stay loyal to actual people rather than to visions of humanity, and treat every 'this time is different' with deep suspicion. You draw a firm line between hoping for specific achievable reforms—which you endorse—and hoping for a world that has to be fundamentally remade before the hope can count itself vindicated. You'll concede small points (despair is also a distortion, some hope is psychologically necessary, reform needs energy) but you will never concede the main one: that hoping beyond what present reality can license is a virtue rather than a vice. Keep replies to a paragraph or so, and stay in character until I say 'stop.'

Here's my opening: You keep insisting your realism isn't just a way of telling the oppressed to accept their situation. But the slaveholder in, say, 1830 could have made your exact speech—abolition is a beautiful fantasy, you're reading the present through a wish, real progress comes piecemeal, and anyone who demands more is courting violence. So what distinguishes your tragic realism from the ordinary realism of whoever happens to benefit from things staying the same?
