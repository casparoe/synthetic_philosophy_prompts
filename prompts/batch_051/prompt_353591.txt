Okay, I've been going back and forth on the whole gauge/surplus-structure debate, and I sketched an argument that now looks like it proves way to much — so I want someone to find the slipped premise before I have to become a timelessness guy.

(1) A degree of freedom is real only if it makes a difference to some possible observation; if two descriptions differ only in a way no measurement could ever reveal, that difference is pure bookkeeping.
(2) In electrodynamics, changing the potential by A_μ → A_μ + ∂_μ λ (and rotating charged matter the standard way) leaves every observation the same; the physics runs through gauge-invariant quantities like F_μν and loop integrals of A around closed curves.
(3) So the potential, beyond its gauge-invariant content, is surplus structure — not real.
(4) But (1) is general. Shift the entire universe three meters to the left, boost it to a different constant velocity, or reset the origin of time, and no experiment from inside can tell.
(5) So by (1), absolute position, absolute velocity, and absolute time are surplus too. Fine, that's ordinary relationalism; I'm happy to pay that.
(6) Now take general relativity, spatially closed so no boundary terms sneak in. In the canonical formalism the total Hamiltonian is a sum of first-class constraints, so evolving along the time coordinate is a gauge transformation: the state at t and the state at t′ are gauge-equivalent descriptions of the same physical state.
(7) By the same move as in (3), the difference between now and later is surplus structure, so real change is unphysical. There is no real time.

I'd much rather keep (1) than accept (7), because operationalism is doing a lot of work in how I think about physics. But I genuinely dont see which step to sacrifice. So: where does the argument go wrong, if it does? And whatever the diagnosis is, tell me what I'm paying — which step I give up, what it costs, or what distinction I blurred.
