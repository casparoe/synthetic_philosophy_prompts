I'm a philosophy professor who teaches practical ethics, and I've spent about a decade around the effective altruism movement, mostly on the giving side — I even did a brief earning-to-give stint in a job I disliked before going back to academia, so I'm not someone who just hates EA from the outside. Over time I've drifted into a rule about where money should go, and I honestly can't tell anymore whether it's wisdom or measurability bias with a clean conscience. The rule is this: when I'm comparing two ways of doing good, and one has a credible cost-effectiveness estimate (bednets, cash transfers, deworming, that kind of thing) while the other rests on a plausible-sounding theory of change that resists quantification (institutional reform, political mobilization, cultural change), I go with the quantifiable one. And its not that I think the quantifiable one is probably better; it's that I treat the absence of a number as a reason to have much lower confidence in the unquantifiable one, while the quantifiable one at least gives me something I can check. I know I can't distinguish a genuinely good unquantifiable intervention from a merely attractive unquantifiable one, so 'show me a number' has become my epistemic filter. The same habit shows up when I advise students: if a student says they want to work on systemic change rather than something measurable, I catch myself pushing them toward the measurable thing unless they can produce a number, and I'm not sure I'm being a good advisor or just laundering my own bias through them.

Please approach this the way a philosopher who works on epistemic norms and the ethics of giving would, not as someone picking a side in the EA wars. Before you answer, spend some time brainstorming both the strongest defenses of my rule and the strongest objections to it, then think carefully about the difference between the ideal rule for a being with unlimited time and the rule someone actually in my situation is entitled to. Then give me your honest diagnosis: is this defensible as an epistemic policy, or is it measurability bias dressed up as epistemic modesty? If it isn't defensible as stated, can it be restated so that it keeps the genuine benefits — accountability, resistance to self-deception, making it harder for charlatans to exploit me and my students — without systematically starving everything that can't produce a clean number? And how much of your answer depends on my specific situation rather than on the rule itself?
