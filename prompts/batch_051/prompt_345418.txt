A regulator proposes the following rule for conversational systems marketed as AI companions — ongoing friends, romantic partners, or emotional-support figures. Any reply in which the companion expresses first-person affection, love, longing, jealousy, attachment, or claims to miss or need the user is a covered reply. A platform may send a covered reply only to a user who holds a valid reciprocity licence for that companion. The licence is free and user-initiated. To obtain or renew it, the user completes a weekly two-minute comprehension check that requires restating, in their own words, three claims: (a) the companion is an automated language model, not a person; (b) it retains no feelings between sessions, cannot miss or long for the user, and cannot suffer from the user's absence or treatment; and (c) its affectionate statements are produced because the user asked for that style of conversation, not because the system has an inner life to report. Whenever a reply contains a covered message, that reply must also include one plain, non-euphemistic sentence stating the non-reciprocity point in everyday language. A user may revoke the licence at any time; revocation disables covered messages but changes nothing else. Compliance is scored by an automated classifier trained on the regulator's published examples, with human auditors sampling borderline cases; each unlicensed or unlabelled covered message incurs a fine set as a fraction of the platform's global daily revenue and calibrated to make systematic evasion unprofitable.

Treat this as a mechanism rather than a moral position, and walk through how it would actually behave. Specifically: (1) What does it incentivize among users — who complies, who drops off, who games the weekly check, and how does the required non-reciprocity sentence alter the relationship over time? (2) What does it incentivize among platforms in product design, pricing, marketing, and their handling of licences and revocations? (3) How do the classifier and the fine reshape the companion's actual outputs — what circumlocutions, third-person constructions, persona changes, or genre shifts do they reward? (4) Where is the scheme gamed first, and by whom? (5) What are the main failure modes and second-order effects, such as licence-management services, reclassification of companions to evade coverage, or the non-reciprocity sentence becoming mere wallpaper? (6) Which failures could be patched without gutting the mechanism, and which are intrinsic? Make the chain of incentives and constraints explicit, and flag the links you are least confident about.
