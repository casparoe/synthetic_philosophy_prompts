After four decades as a research geophysicist, I have found retirement an unexpected invitation to return to questions I set aside early in my education, and I have lately joined a small reading circle devoted to philosophy of religion. Our current topic is the atonement, and I have noticed that the debate between two of the standard accounts, the penal substitution view and the moral influence view, is often carried on badly even by thoughtful people: each side presents the other as a caricature, and the live question of what a defensible version of either position could concede is seldom reached. I would like you to write out the kind of dialogue that would remedy this.

Let the two interlocutors be equal in philosophical ability and in goodwill, and call them Elena and David. Elena defends a version of penal substitution: she holds that human wrongdoing incurs a real moral debt or liability before God, that divine justice cannot simply waive that liability, and that the death of Christ pays or absorbs the penalty so that God can forgive the guilty without compromising justice. David defends a version of moral influence: he holds that the primary work of the cross is not the transfer of a penalty but the display of a love strong enough to break through human resistance and actually reorder the wrongdoer's motives and character. Both accept that God is perfectly just and perfectly loving, and both accept that an adequate account of the atonement must make sense of two things at once: how wrongdoing is justly answered, and how actual human beings come to repent and change.

Their conversation should be a genuine exchange rather than alternating monologues. Each should restate the other's position in terms the other would accept before objecting to it; each should make at least one real concession or refinement in response to what the other says; and by the end they should have isolated at least one issue on which their disagreement ultimately turns, together with one point of unexpected agreement. The point is not to declare a winner but to let the strongest version of each position be tested against the strongest version of the other.
