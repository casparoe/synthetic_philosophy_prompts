In my work as a software engineer, I maintain a system that has accumulated features and constraints whose original rationale is no longer documented. I often hear Chesterton's fence invoked as a reason to leave such things untouched: roughly, before you remove an existing arrangement you should understand why it was introduced, because its purpose may be real even if it is not legible now. Our deployment tooling, however, has changed the cost of acting on that caution. We can ship the removal of some constraint behind a feature flag to a small fraction of traffic, observe a broad set of behavioral and reliability metrics, and roll back automatically if anything degrades. This can look like a cheap alternative to reconstructing old reasons. My question is whether the Chesterton's fence argument still goes through under this assumption, or whether it depended on removal being hard to reverse and harm being easy to miss. Identify the premise that carries the weight, say whether metric-driven rollback actually addresses that premise, and state what remains of the principle—or what should replace it—for systems where reversibility and observability are cheap.
