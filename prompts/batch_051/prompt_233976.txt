I've spent the last few weeks clearing out my late mate Dan's flat. Dan was a hobbyist game dev, and he left a half-finished game on his laptop. His family don't want anything to do with it, and a few of us from his old games group feel we should finish it and put it out for free. That felt obviously right to me, but then I started applying the same gut instinct to other cases and it all came apart.

Case 1, the unfinished game. No will, no instructions, no-one to inherit. Finishing it and releasing it free: fine, I'd feel weird not doing it.

Case 2, a dead MMO. The studio folded in 2009 and the game shut down. Fans reverse-engineered the server and have run it free for a decade. No-one bought the rights. Verdict: fine, good even.

Case 3, a game whose company is still around. It killed the game in 2006 and has ignored every request to re-release it or let anyone pay for it. Archivists put a playable copy online. Verdict: also fine, but I'm more uneasy than with case 2 and I can't say why. This is the one that niggles at me.

Case 4, old negatives. A library holds 1930s negatives by a photographer with no traceable heirs, and wants to digitise and put them online. Verdict: fine.

Case 5, an unpublished novel. A novelist died with no will and no close relatives; a small press found a finished novel in his papers and published it. Verdict: fine, though I felt a bit less okay than with the negatives, for no reason I can defend.

Case 6, the demos. A singer said in interviews while alive that the early demos should never come out. After they died, a label released them and paid the estate. Verdict: not fine.

Case 7, an open-source library with no licence. The maintainer hasn't answered for years. A company wants to use the code in a paid product. Verdict: not fine. But a student forking it just to keep the project alive is fine.

My best attempt at a principle is something like: when the owner disappears, the work becomes everyone's to look after, but only non-commerically, unless the owner left known wishes against it. That's really three principles in a trench coat. Is there a single coherent principle that captures all or most of these, and which of my verdicts does it force me to drop? What does it say about the one I keep going back and forth on: a worker at the folded studio from case 2 kept a copy of the original server code, and now wants to sell a licence to a museum and keep the fee. Is that fine or not?
