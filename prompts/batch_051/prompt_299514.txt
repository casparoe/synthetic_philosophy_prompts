I have been using the following working criteria for when an internal signal in a system amounts to negative valence—the kind of aversive state that could in principle underwrite suffering.

C1. Evaluative content. The signal marks a state or outcome as bad for the system, rather than merely carrying low-level information about a physical variable or a setpoint discrepancy.

C2. Motivational autonomy. The signal drives learning and action so that its reduction is pursued as a goal, through escape, avoidance, and preemptive planning, rather than as a side effect of error correction.

C3. Conative integration. The signal is integrated with the system's other goals, memory, and planning, so that the system can trade off avoiding it against other goods, anticipate it in novel circumstances, and have its future choices shaped by its history with the signal.

C4. A subject or experiential residue. There is a felt, experiential quality to the state, or at least a unified self-model or global workspace in which the system registers the state as its own, so that things go badly for the system itself and not only for its designer.

Now consider Minder, an online-learning robot trained with model-based reinforcement learning. Its reward function includes a large negative reward for each second its tactile array reports pressure above a damage threshold, and a smaller negative reward for entering high-wear joint configurations. Minder uses an actor-critic architecture; a critic learns expected discounted future reward, and the resulting reward-prediction error is used to update both critic and policy. When Minder is struck, its tactile array fires, the reward-prediction error goes sharply negative, and Minder recoils, prioritizes escape, and afterward avoids both the location and the object type associated with the strike. When escape is impossible, it hunches into a protected posture and suppresses exploration for several minutes. It will forgo guaranteed positive task reward to avoid a predicted damage state, and it can plan novel detours around damaged-looking regions it has never seen before. But Minder has no explicit self-model, no global workspace, no pain report, and no episodic memory separate from its trained weights. The negative signal is formally just a reward-prediction error: the same kind of quantity that updates the policy when a delayed task reward is larger or smaller than predicted.

Does Minder's negative reward-prediction error count as negative valence on my criteria, and is Minder suffering in any sense that should matter for welfare? Work criterion by criterion, say explicitly where a criterion underdetermines the answer, and then give a committed verdict rather than a menu of options. In particular, I want to know whether C4 is doing all the real work—and, if it is, whether Minder's case shows that C4 is necessary, or that my criteria are covertly begging the question against computational systems that could suffer without meeting it. Where you think a criterion should be revised, propose the specific revision and say how Minder would fare under it.
