I have been trying to adjudicate a disagreement between a colleague in a history department, who regards cliodynamics as numerology with larger datasets, and a friend in complexity science, who thinks macrohistory only reveals regularities at scales too coarse for ordinary historiography to see. The claim at issue is the structural-demographic account associated with Peter Turchin: that large agrarian and early modern states undergo secular cycles, roughly century-scale swings between political stability and crisis, driven by feedbacks among population growth, popular immiseration, elite overproduction, and state fiscal decline, so that major outbreaks of instability become partly predictable in the aggregate even though no particular event is. The defender says the theory forecasts rising or falling pressure rather than specific crises; the critic says that with enough freedom in choosing indicators and coding rules, cycles of this sort would show up in almost any sufficiently noisy series.

I want you to design a concrete test of the claim that large-scale political instability is partly predictable from structural-demographic variables rather than being a sequence of unique, contingent episodes. Specify units and time periods, how you would operationalize political instability and each driver, what data you would actually need, and how you would make the test genuinely prospective — out-of-sample forecasting, pre-registration, or some equivalent — rather than a retrospective fit. Include the null model or benchmark against which the structural-demographic forecast should be compared. Then explain what a positive result would and would not establish: would it license a mechanistic interpretation of the cycle, or merely show that a composite index has some predictive value? What would an ambiguous or negative result settle, if anything? Finally, identify the part of the underlying conceptual claim that no design of this kind could reach, such as the decision about what counts as the same recurring cycle, how political instability events are coded, or whether a correlation between immiseration and rebellion is genuinely explanatory rather than a quantitative redescription.
