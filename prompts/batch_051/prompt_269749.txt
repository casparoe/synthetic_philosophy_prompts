I'm in a debate club at my school and our club president just discovered game theory, so now every meeting is somebody's uncle's take on the prisoner's dilemma. Last week my friend tried to explain "superrationality" to me, which he got from a blog post his older cousin sent him. His summary went like this:

"Superrationality is the idea that in a prisoner's dilemma you should cooperate, because the other player is in the exact same position as you and will reason to the same conclusion. If cooperation is the move you arrive at, the other player arrives at it too, so your choosing to cooperate goes together with them choosing to cooperate. Defecting only makes sense if you secretly assume you are the one exception to the symmetry. Standard game theory says defect, but that's because it treats the other player's move as fixed and independent of yours, which misses the fact that your decision is evidence about what they will decide. The blog even claims Hofstadter uses this idea to show that in an arms race, two countries reasoning independantly should both disarm."

I want to use a cleaned-up version of this in a practice speech, but the jump to "your decision is evidence about what the other player does" feels like it's smuggling in something extra, and I can't tell how much of this is the actual idea vs. the cousin's gloss. Could you go through the summary and tell me what a careful defender of superrationality would accept, what's a common simplification, and what's subtly wrong or overstated? And separately, does the arms-race conclusion actually follow once the accurate parts are isolated, or does it depend on one of the shaky parts?
