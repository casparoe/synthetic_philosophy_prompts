I've been chewing on when exactly Latin becomes Italian, and I think I've got a criterion that beats the usual mutual-intelligibility stuff. I call it the Last Correction Rule. Here's the idea. Languages drift, and adjacent generations basically always understood each other, so asking when people stopped understanding each other is hopeless—the chain has no break. So stop looking at what the kids said and look at what the parents did about it. As long as a parent, annoyed at a kid's slopy speech, could correct the kid and produce a genuinely Latin sentence—one an earlier fluent Latin speaker would accept—then the spoken language is still Latin in the only sense that matters. Italian gets born at the first generation where the parent's sincere correction, aimed at Latin, comes out as something an earlier Latin speaker would reject: the correction itself has quietly switched languages. In other words, the break doesn't happen in the kids' mouths; it happens in the parents' corrections. The old language lingers as the target of correction after it stops being the language anyone actually speaks, and it finally dies when even the target wobbles.

Example: a parent in, I dunno, 700 CE tries to fix the kid's illu caballu and produces what they think is good Latin—but it's already missing endings a classical speaker would insist on. On my view that's still Latin, because the parent is still trying for Latin and hasn't landed outside it. The birthday comes later, when the parent's best shot is no longer aimed at Latin at all, just a fancier version of the local speech.

I know this might be something sociolinguistics already says in better words, or that it might collapse under pressure. So: is the criterion coherent? Is it already out there under some name I don't know? Give me the two or three strongest objections and tell me whether any of them is fatal or just uncomfortable. And give me one real moment in the Latin-to-Italian story where my rule gets the date right, and one where it makes a mess.
