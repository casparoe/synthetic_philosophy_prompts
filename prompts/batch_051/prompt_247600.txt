I was reading Evan Hubinger and coauthors’ paper *Risks from Learned Optimization in Advanced Machine Learning Systems*, and one distinction stuck with me. They write: “If such a neural network were produced in training, there would be two optimizers: the learning algorithm that produced the neural network—which we will call the base optimizer—and the neural network itself—which we will call the mesa-optimizer.” That got me thinking about the safety practices now common at AI labs: red-teaming models before release, monitoring outputs, training models to refuse certain requests, limiting what models can do, and building in off-switches. I want to understand what these practices presuppose. Do they presuppose that a trained model might be an optimizer with its own objective, distinct from the one it was trained on? Or could someone justify the same practices while denying that models have anything like goals? More generally, what view of AI systems—and of the relation between training and behavior—does this whole safety posture commit a lab to? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say what would change in practice if the lab instead adopted a purely behavioral view, on which a model is only a function from inputs to outputs and talk of its objectives is just loose metaphor.
