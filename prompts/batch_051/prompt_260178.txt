I went down a rabbit hole this month because an assistant refused to help me draft a resignation letter after a bad day at work. It didn't just say no; it told me I might be making a snap descision and offered to set a reminder for the morning. I got annoyed, then curious, and started reading arguments about whether assistants should push back against what you ask for. Now I cant tell which arguments are real positions and which are just tech-bro editorializing.

My friend who works in AI policy tried to map the debate for me last week. Here's roughly what she said:

"Look, the debate is basically autonomy versus benevolence. Paternalists think an assistant should ask whether your request is actually good for you, and if it's harmful, self-defeating, or based on a misreading of the situation, it should refuse or redirect, because the model has more information and can see the regret coming. That covers everything from obvious cases like self-harm to drafting a resignation at 2 a.m. or even helping you write a nasty message to someone, since being cruel is bad for you too in the long run. Anti-paternalists think a competent adult's request is none of the assistant's business, and once you let the model use its superior information to steer you, you've converted a tool into a moral authority over your life. The moderate position is transparency: the assistant can flag a concern, but if you insist it has to comply. Defenders of refusals trot out the doctor analogy, but that's just sleight of hand — doctors refuse because medications have side effects and they hold a license, whereas an assistant is a customer-service system. Besides, nobody elected OpenAI or Anthropic to decide what's good for us, so every refusal is the designer's values wearing a safety-policy costume. The only line that actually holds is: refuse when the request hurts someone else or is clearly illegal, and otherwise do what you're told."

Then she shrugged and said paternalism in AI is just engineering arrogance with extra steps.

This feels suspiciously clean to me, like a debate where one side wins by definition. Is her summary fair? I'd especially like to know which parts a careful defender of refusals would accept, which parts an anti-paternalist would actually insist on, where she's collapsing distinctions, and whether her bottom line — refuse for harm-to-others or illegality, otherwise comply — really follows from the accurate parts of what she said. I'm not asking for sources or famous names, just an honest audit of whether I should trust this way of carving up the debate.
