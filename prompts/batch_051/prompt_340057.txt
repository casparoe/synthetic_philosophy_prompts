I work in the screening unit of a state anti-corruption commission that decides whether incoming complaints count as corruption or should be set aside. Our own precedents so far are as follows.

- We classified as corruption a customs officer's acceptance of small, regular cash envelopes from importers in exchange for prioritizing their clearance, even though the paperwork was always in order.
- We classified as corruption a minister's appointment of a clearly unqualified nephew to a well-paid public board.
- We did not classify as corruption a procurement officer's award of a contract to the highest-scoring bidder, where the winning firm belonged to an old school friend but the award was made by an independent panel on the scores alone.
- We did not classify as corruption a clerk's acceptance of a crate of fruit from a retiree as thanks, after the clerk had processed the retiree's pension file in the ordinary way.

A new complaint alleges that a member of parliament used a discretionary development fund to award school scholarships to the children of local party organizers who had run her campaigns, passing over applicants with higher exam scores. The MP took no money, gift, or promise, and none of the beneficiaries are relatives; her only benefit is the continued loyalty of her organizers. Local practice widely treats a leader's right to reward her own supporters as normal, and she says we are imposing an outside, Western standard by calling it corruption.

If we want to be consistent with our own precedents, should we classify this as corruption? Identify the principle or principles that best fit the decisions above, name which earlier case is the closest analog, and say what principle we would be committing ourselves to on either ruling. Take a definite position: I want a recommendation with reasons, not a menu of options.
