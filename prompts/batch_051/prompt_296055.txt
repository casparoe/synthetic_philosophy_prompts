I'm writing a blog post trying to explain why "make the AI want to be shut down" is not the same thing as making it corrigible, and I want a thought experiment to hang the explanation on. My rough sense is this: a corrigible agent, in the standard shutdown-problem sense, is indifferent to the off switch. It won't stop you from pressing it, won't press it itself, and won't maneuver you into pressing it, because what it tracks is your decision about the switch rather than the state of being off. The shortcut version is a shutdown-incentivized agent: same base task, plus a terminal reward for the state of being shut down.

I want a case where these two designs pull apart in the direction people find less intuitive: the shutdown-incentivized agent ends up behaving worse than the corrigible one, not by refusing to turn off, but by quietly arranging the world so that you'll want to press the button. Keep it realistic — say a calendar bot in a small office with a physical off switch, no perfect predictors, no exotic machinery — and make the two agents differ only in that one terminal reward. The scenario should be about a paragraph. Then do the analysis: what each agent is maximizing, what each one actually does, and which structural feature of the utility functions does the work (my guess is something about rewarding a state versus deferring to a decision, but I want you to actually argue it). Then tell me what happens if you remove that one feature, and finish with the strongest objection someone who prefers the shutdown-incentive design would give, and whether their fix basically smuggles corrigibility back in.
