ok so I'm a novelist, and I'm trying to write a character who keeps sabotaging the one thing she actually wants, and I want the weakness of will to feel psychologically real instead of just a plot convenience. I've ended up with a working theory of how this works, and before I build a whole novel on it I'd like someone to tell me whether the pieces actually hang together. I have a feeling a couple of them pull against each other but I cant locate which ones. Here's where I've landed:

(1) Akrasia is a real, synchronic thing: at the moment she takes the drink she can still judge, right then, that stopping at two is the better option, and she fails to act on that judgment. It's not a change of mind — it's a failure to follow a judgment that stays put.

(2) But I also think hyperbolic discounting gives the best account of the larger pattern: her preferences discount the near future so steeply that as the temptation approaches her ranking flips, and it flips back once the temptation passes.

(3) I don't think you need two agents inside her — a far-sighted planner and a myopic doer — to make sense of this. One persisting person with one discount function is enough.

(4) When I actually write the giving-in scene, I think it has to be written as her genuinely wanting the immediate thing more than the long-term thing in that instant, or else she reads as possessed rather than weak.

(5) And I think the episode only counts as weakness of will, rather than ordinary preference drift, if later she can look back and see it as failing a standard she held all along — not a standard she retroactively invents from the safe distance of a hangover.

So: do these hang together? Go through the set and give me the tensions ordered by severity, real ones before ones that only look like tensions, and for each say exactly what would have to give. And please make it exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no introduction, no conclusion.
