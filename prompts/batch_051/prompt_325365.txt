In Lecture VI of *Pragmatism: A New Name for Some Old Ways of Thinking*, William James writes: 

> The truth of an idea is not a stagnant property inherent in it. Truth happens to an idea. It becomes true, is made true by events.

Take this passage as your starting point. James here treats truth as something that happens to an idea through its working or verification. Compare James’s conception of truth as a process of verification with C. S. Peirce’s conception of truth as the opinion that would ultimately be agreed upon by investigators. What exactly is James claiming about the relation between an idea’s truth and its verification, and how does that claim sit alongside Peirce’s view? Are the two accounts compatible, or does James’s emphasis on an idea’s *becoming* true mark a genuine break with Peirce? In your answer, identify what each thinker means by truth, locate their central point of divergence if you think there is one, and argue for which of the two offers the more defensible account of truth.
