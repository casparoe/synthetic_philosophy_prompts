I am writing a novel in which a recurring character, a retired civil judge in her eighties, has issued a series of verdicts about promises over the course of the first volume. She has held that a debtor remained bound to repay a loan even after the creditor, forgetful and wealthy, had entirely forgotten the debt; that a woman's private written vow, made to no one, was not a promise and gave her son no claim to the bequest it described; that a buyer was not bound by his promise to pay for a painting when the seller had knowingly misattributed it; that a man was still bound to keep a forgotten lunch appointment, though the other party's only loss was an hour's wait, and that the fault was a small one; and that a promise made under threat was void. In the sequel she must now decide a fresh dispute: to end a quarrel, a man solemnly promises his cousin that he will pay for the cousin's daughter to attend music school the following year, while privately intending from the outset never to do it; the cousin is pleased but makes no arrangements and is out nothing when he refuses. I want to know how she would rule and, more importantly, the principle she seems to be applying across those earlier verdicts, the rival principle that fits them equally well, what each candidate principle predicts for the new case, your best guess about which principle she would actually endorse and why, and the further case that would most cleanly settle the matter.
