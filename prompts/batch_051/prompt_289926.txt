I keep seeing a structural parallel between the economist's argument for gains from trade and a certain kind of moral compromise between people whose value systems conflict. The mapping looks clean: the disputed issues play the role of goods, the status quo is the initial endowment, each party's marginal rate of substitution becomes their moral priorities, and the Pareto-improvement condition becomes the requirement that no party ends up worse off by its own lights. But I cannot tell whether this is a deep identity of structure or just pattern-matching. The economic result requires only differing marginal rates of substitution, not any common scale of value, which seems to promise moral compromise without commensurability; yet moral values are often deontic, identity-constituting, or treated as non-negotiable, which sounds like exactly what would break the analogy. What would have to be true of two value systems for the trade-theoretic results to transfer, and where exactly do they fail when those conditions are absent? In particular, the parallel predicts that compromise should be easiest when the parties care about mostly disjoint issues; is that prediction borne out in real moral negotiation, and what does its success or failure reveal about whether the analogy is genuine?
