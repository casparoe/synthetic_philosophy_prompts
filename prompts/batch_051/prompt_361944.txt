I recently had a long argument with a friend about whether a honeybee colony, a Portuguese man o’ war, or a holobiont ought to be regarded as a single organism. My friend insisted that Darwin had already settled the matter. She directed me to his discussion of the sterile workers in social insect colonies in On the Origin of Species, and after rereading the chapter on instinct I can see that he says selection may be applied to the family as well as to the individual, so that a trait may be preserved because it benefits the whole family even if it renders the individual sterile. I find myself troubled by what exactly this passage licences. Does Darwin’s willingness to treat the family as a unit of selection commit him to the view that the colony, the siphonophore, or the holobiont is itself the organism, rather than a collection of organisms? If not, what is the difference between being a unit of selection and being an individual organism? I want to know whether my friend and I have both read the modern debate about biological individuality back into Darwin, or whether he really was making the point we attributed to him.
