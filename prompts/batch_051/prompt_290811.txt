I have been thinking, on my own and without much formal background, about how operations and maintenance people decide whether two failures are "the same problem" or two different problems that merely look alike. I do not mean the statistical side of anomaly detection — how you notice that something has gone wrong. I mean the conceptual side: once you have two detected anomalies in front of you, what makes it correct to say that they are two instances of one underlying mechanism rather than two mechanisms that happen to produce overlapping symptoms?

The view I keep coming back to is this: two anomalies are the same mechanism exactly when they have the same control point — that is, when the same minimal intervention would have prevented both, corrected both, or at least interrupted both. I like this view because it is crisp where the obvious alternative is not. If you say two failures are the same mechanism just when the underlying physical processes are similar enough, you have to answer "similar in what respects, and how similar is enough?" — and every answer I can think of looks arbitrary. The intervention answer also explains why we group failures at all: we group them in order to know what to change, so the groupings should be organized around what would have to be changed. And it seems to give a definite verdict in every case, which is more than the "physical similarity" answer does.

But I also have an intuition I cannot talk myself out of: mechanisms are in the failing thing, not in our handles on it. Two quite different physical processes can happen to run through the same control point, and the same physical process can require different interventions in different circumstances.

Here are the two cases that make the clash vivid for me. First, a conveyor line stops intermittently. On Monday it stops because a bearing is wearing out and drawing extra current, which trips the thermal overload. On Friday it stops because a dip in the supply voltage drives the current up, which trips the same overload. In both cases the same minimal intervention — resetting the overload and keeping the current below the trip threshold, say by adjusting the protection or the load — addresses the stoppage. The control-point view therefore says these are two instances of the same mechanism. But it seems plainly true to me that one is a mechanical-wear failure and the other is a supply-instability failure, and that they merely converge on a shared protective device. I would be reluctant to tell an engineer that these are "the same mechanism" just because they are interrupted by the same switch.

Second, two batches of parts are both ruined by hydrogen embrittlement during plating — the same metallurgical failure process. In one batch the embrittlement is corrected by changing the plating bath temperature, in the other by changing the post-plating bake time, because the parts' different geometries trap hydrogen differently. The control-point view says the two batches exhibit different mechanisms, since the minimal effective intervention differs. But that seems to make the identity of the failure depend on facts about the part geometry rather than on what is going wrong — the same process, differently reached.

So the rule has the virtues of precision, non-arbitrariness, and usefulness, and the intuition has the virtue of respecting the difference between the world and our grip on it. I do not want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that gives way, what it would take for the intervention rule to be amended or abandoned, and whether there is a reading of "same mechanism" on which both survive — perhaps by distinguishing two senses of the phrase, or by relativizing mechanism identity to a level of description. Walk me through each option and what would justify it.
