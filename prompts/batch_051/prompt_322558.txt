I edit a small interdisciplinary journal and every week I decide whether a submitted paper's method is legitimate enough to send out for review. We recieve everything from standard clinical trials to people who have basically invented their own discipline. I'm sympathetic to the Feyerabend line that there is no single scientific method, that major advances have often broken the methodological rules of their day, and that the slogan people associate with him is usually misread as a licence to accept anything rather than as a warning against fixed rules. But a journal can't publish everything, and I can't send every weird submission to reviewers. Please write a decision procedure my assistant editors, who aren't philosophers and have about twenty minutes per paper, could actually run before a paper reaches me: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because it's a judgement call. Ground it in whatever that Feyerabend line, charitably read, actually implies for a decision like this, rather than in a fixed list of gold standards. Then run the procedure on three submissions: one with an entirely standard method and one glaring flaw, one with a novel method that has no track record but a clear rationale, and one that is internally consistent but almost certainly pseudoscience. I want to see where the procedure misfires, including at least one case where it gives an answer I won't like, and what it cannot decide. British English please.
