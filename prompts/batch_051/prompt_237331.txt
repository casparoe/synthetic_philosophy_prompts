I have been trying to work out what principle actually governs how I should revise my beliefs when my evidence does not settle anything outright, and I would like help extracting the principle from my verdicts on cases rather than having it handed to me. My working rule—I gather it is sometimes called Jeffrey conditioning, or probability kinematics—is this: when an experience changes my confidence over some partition of mutually exclusive possibilities, it should leave all of my conditional probabilities given each cell of that partition untouched, and only reweight those conditional probabilities by the new confidences. To fix the idea with a case I am confident about: a cloth is either blue or green, and I begin 50/50. My prior probability that the cloth came from the new factory is 0.6 if it is blue and 0.3 if it is green. A glimpse in dim light leaves me 80% sure it is blue. I take my new credence in the new-factory claim to be 0.6 × 0.8 + 0.3 × 0.2 = 0.54. I am not looking to have that arithmetic overturned; I want the deeper rule that explains it and that survives the following tests.

But I also hold several further things, and I suspect they cannot all be true alongside the reweighting rule exactly as stated. First, if the light improves a moment later and my confidence in blue rises from 80% to 90%, I hold that the final result should be exactly as if I had gone straight from 50/50 to 90% in a single update; the fact that I passed through 80% should leave no trace. Second, I expect the order in which evidence reaches me not to matter. Suppose the foreman says the cloth is blue, and though he might be speaking from a reliable memory or merely guessing, his saying so makes me 70% sure that he is remembering. That testimony, on my rule, works through the partition {foreman remembers, foreman guesses}, while the dim glimpse works through {blue, green}. I hold that whether I have the glimpse first and the foreman second, or the foreman first and the glimpse second, my final credence about the new-factory claim should be the same; yet nothing in the reweighting rule appears to guarantee this, and for natural assignments of the underlying probabilities the two orders can come apart. Third, I worry that the rule is sensitive to a choice the evidence did not make for me. Suppose the glimpse really leaves me 80% sure of blue, 15% sure of green, and 5% sure of teal, where green and teal were equally probable in advance and the new-factory claim is more likely given teal than given green. If I treat the relevant partition as {blue, not-blue}, the reweighting rule gives one number for the new-factory claim; if I treat it as {blue, green, teal}, it gives another. The glimpse itself carried no instruction about how finely to sort the non-blue possibilities, so a rule that delivers different answers for the two descriptions seems to be adding information the evidence did not contain.

What I would like, then, is either a single precisely stated principle that captures all of these commitments—the reweighting arithmetic, the no-trace property within a fixed partition, order-independence across different partitions, and independence from refinements the evidence does not itself fix—or an honest diagnosis of which commitments I must give up and how. State the principle precisely enough that I could apply it myself, say which of my verdicts it saves and which it overturns, and apply it to one further case I have not resolved: a blood test whose result reaches me in two installments, first a laboratory report that shifts my credences over three mutually exclusive conditions, and then a colleague's warning that the report may be contaminated, which shifts my confidence in the report itself; the prognosis I care about depends only on which condition the patient has. How should I combine the two installments, and does the order in which they reach me matter?
