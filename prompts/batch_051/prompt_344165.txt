In Plato’s *Meno* (Jowett translation), Socrates says to Meno:

“For I literally do not know what virtue is, and much less whether it is acquired by teaching or not.”

Socrates then refuses to take up Meno’s question about how virtue comes to people until they have first said what virtue is.

I’m a geometry teacher, and this passage keeps coming to mind in our department meetings. We are trying to shift our assessments away from procedural fluency and toward “understanding proofs.” But we can’t agree on what that means. One colleague says a student understands a proof of, say, the triangle sum theorem when she can reproduce it and justify each step. Another says that’s still just memory; understanding requires applying the theorem in an unfamiliar problem or modifying the proof when the setup changes. A third says a student understands only when the proof strikes her as inevitable, so she could reconstruct it after forgetting the details. We all agree that some students can follow the written steps without grasping why the proof works, but we can’t articulate the extra thing they lack.

Socrates’s move suggests we are putting the cart before the horse: asking how to teach and test proof understanding before saying what proof understanding is. But I’m not sure that’s right for our situation. We can’t pause our curriculum until we have a definition. And maybe working through concrete examples of students’ successes and failures would help us build the definition, rather than needing the definition first.

Is Socrates’s methodological demand the right one for our department’s question? Or can we fruitfully investigate how to teach and assess proof understanding without first giving a full account of what it is? Is there a reading of the *Meno* passage on which his point is narrower than a general demand for definitions before practical questions? Keep your answer to at most 400 words.
