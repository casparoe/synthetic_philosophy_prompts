I have come to accept a fairly strong version of Milton Friedman's F-twist: the realism of a model's assumptions is not an independent criterion by which the model should be judged. What matters is whether the model yields sufficiently accurate predictions, and a theory may be good even if its central assumptions are known to be false, provided the false assumptions set up a reliable inference machine. Friedman's billiard-player illustration captures the core idea: an expert player's shots are well predicted by assuming she has solved the relevant physics, even though she does no such calculation; likewise, firms may be profitably modeled as if they maximize an objective they do not consciously pursue. I don't just hold this as a narrow thesis about economics; I hold it as a general stance about idealized models in the special sciences.

What would count against it? I don't want the standard objection that strictly false assumptions cannot literally explain; I already grant that the F-twist is a thesis about assessment, not explanation. I want a genuine map of the position's exposure. Specifically:

Which possible cases, formal results, modeling practices, or philosophical arguments would a committed F-twist defender concede as counting against their view, and which would they dismiss as question-begging or misdirected?

Which of those defeaters should be ranked fatal, and which merely awkward? For example, are there cases where predictive success has come reliably apart from assumption-realism in ways that later embarrassed the as-if approach—where the false assumptions were not just unrealistic but actively misleading about where the model would break down?

What does the F-twist commit one to about scientific practice? What would a rival view—say, a robustness or mechanism-first view—predict that the F-twist does not, and have any such predictions been borne out in ways that should lower my confidence?

Finally, I want an honest assessment of whether the F-twist, as usually defended, has been qualified into unfalsifiability—by, for instance, saying some assumptions matter for realism and others do not without a principled way to tell them apart in advance. If it has been insulated, is that a defect peculiar to a methodological position of this kind, or is it an acceptable feature of a stance that is partly a maxim for practice rather than an empirical claim?

Please don't give me a list of papers; give me the arguments and cases themselves, as concretely as you can from memory, and sort them by how much they should move me.
