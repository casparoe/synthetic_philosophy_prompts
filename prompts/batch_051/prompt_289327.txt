I've been teaching a class on early Chinese political thought, and I'm stuck on a problem about Han Feizi's Legalism. In the Han Feizi, in a chapter often translated as Two Handles, there is a passage I'll paraphrase because I don't have the translation in front of me. Han Feizi says a ruler's control over ministers is like a tiger's control over dogs: the tiger dominates because of its claws and teeth, and if it lends those out or loses them, the dogs will dominate it. By analogy, he says, reward and punishment are the ruler's two handles; if he lets ministers wield them, he becomes their prisoner. His conclusion is not just a warning to keep power centralized. It is a deeper argument that government should be designed around incentives rather than around virtue: you don't ask officials to be good, you make it so that their interests cannot lead them to do harm.

Now my problem. Han Feizi's argument assumes that reward and punishment are instruments the ruler can actually calibrate and observe. But a lot of modern institutional design has shifted toward precisely the kind of incentive-based governance he recommends — performance targets, audit metrics, bonuses, sanctions — and we have seen a recurring failure. Metrics get gamed; agents optimize whatever is measured rather than the underlying good; explicit incentives sometimes crowd out the intrinsic motivation and informal norms they were meant to replace; and the people who design the incentives often know less about the task than the people they are incentivizing. Goodhart's law has become a cliché of management theory for exactly this reason.

So here is what I want to know. Does Han Feizi's argument survive this development? Is the lesson that rule by incentives is dead, or that his argument just needs repair — for example, by separating the ruler's ultimate control of rewards from the choice of which proxies to measure — or does his argument actually still go through because these failures are just misapplied incentives rather than an objection to incentives as such? I'm especially interested in which specific premise of his argument the gaming-and-crowding-out problem actually puts pressure on, and whether there is a weaker version of his conclusion that remains true even after the problem is granted.
