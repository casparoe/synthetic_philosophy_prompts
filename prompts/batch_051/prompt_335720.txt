I have been trying to work out something I call the reductive remainder. Take any social regularity that a strict individualist wants to explain purely in terms of individuals: a norm persisting, a bank run, a fashion spreading. The individual-level account always has to describe each person as already situated — as facing incentives that encode what everyone else will do, as expecting others to conform, as using concepts that make sense only within the practice being explained. But those descriptions quietly import the very social fact the explanation was supposed to eliminate. So the reduction does not dissolve the social level; it relocates it into the premises, the way paying off one loan with another does not reduce your total debt. I think this is different both from the claim that social facts supervene on individuals and from the claim that social wholes have their own causal powers. It is meant as a purely explanatory claim: an individualist explanation either fails for incompleteness or succeeds by borrowing the social fact it was meant to replace. Is this a coherent distinction? Does it collapse into something already worked out, such as supervenience, emergence, macro-micro-macro explanation, or situational analysis? And where exactly does it break or overstate its case?
