I sit on the strategy committee of a well-capitalized private foundation that supports global health and development, and we must choose between two ten-year portfolios of roughly equal cost. The first, which our analysts call the "breadth" strategy, would extend inexpensive and well-proven interventions—long-lasting insecticidal nets, oral rehydration salts and zinc, vitamin A supplementation, improved staple seed—to very many additional people in high-fertility regions. Our models project that this would substantially increase the number of people alive in the second half of this century, each of whose lives is expected to be clearly worth living but only modestly so. The second, the "depth" strategy, would spend the same money on a much smaller set of existing people: sustained treatment for severe mental illness, surgical care, and secondary schooling for a few million. It would produce much higher average well-being per person and would not change how many people ever exist. Summed impartially across all affected lives, the first portfolio does more total good; but the average life it makes possible sits close to the threshold of a life worth living, while the second produces lives that are plainly good.

A close friend and I spent most of Saturday evening arguing about exactly this trade-off. She thinks the depth portfolio is obviously right and describes the breadth strategy as buying "a larger population of barely-worth-living lives rather than fewer good ones," which she calls a repugnant conclusion and a sign that something has gone wrong with how we are aggregating welfare. I am no longer sure she is wrong, and the committee is now split down the middle, with the economists on one side and the program officers on the other. I have been asked to prepare the short written opinion we will circulate before next month's vote.

Please draft the memo itself. It should recommend one portfolio, or an explicit modification of one, with a concrete bottom line the foundation's non-philosopher trustees can act on. It should state the reasoning in terms a board can follow, and it must engage seriously with the strongest objection that could be made from the side we do not follow, rather than caricaturing it. Do not simply rehearse the population-ethics debate in the abstract; give me a document we could circulate.
