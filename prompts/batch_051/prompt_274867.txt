I'm a grad student and I've been arguing with a professor about Berkeley's master argument. From section 23 of A Treatise Concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge, the passage goes roughly like this: Berkeley says you can easily imagine trees in a park or books in a closet with nobody there to perceive them, but all you're doing is forming your own ideas of trees and books and leaving out the idea of any other perceiver; you yourself are still thinking or perceiving them. To show they can exist without the mind, you'd have to conceive them existing unconceived or unthought of, which he thinks is contradictory. When we try to conceive external bodies existing without the mind, we're really just contemplating our own ideas, and the mind, not noticing itself, is deluded into thinking it can conceive bodies unthought of.

Here's my numbered reconstruction:

(1) Suppose someone tries to imagine a tree in a park with no one to perceive it.
(2) In doing so, they frame an idea of the tree in their mind and simply omit any idea of another perceiver.
(3) They themselves perceive or think of the tree during the whole attempt.
(4) So what they've imagined is not a tree existing unperceived; it's a tree as perceived by them.
(5) To imagine a tree existing without any mind at all, they would have to imagine it existing unconceived or unthought of.
(6) But that's impossible: the act of imagining it makes it conceived.
(7) So it's impossible that a tree exists unperceived, because no one can even conceive it that way.
