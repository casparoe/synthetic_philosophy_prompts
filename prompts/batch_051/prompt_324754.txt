I'm taking an upper-level course in the philosophy of private law, and I keep running into a tension between the classical idea that contractual obligation rests on actual agreement and the reality of modern consumer contracting. I'd like to work through a concrete case.

Consider how courts treat clickwrap agreements. A website presents a consumer with a box of terms and a button reading I agree, with no opportunity to negotiate or alter the terms; almost no one reads them, and the companies know it. Courts nevertheless enforce what was clicked, including arbitration clauses and liability waivers buried in the document. Some courts go further and enforce browsewrap terms the consumer never clicked at all, so long as the terms were accessible through a hyperlink and the site gave some form of notice. The same legal system, though, insists that there is no contract without mutual assent and refuses to enforce agreements arrived at by fraud, duress, or unconscionable terms.

What I want to understand is what theory of agreement, and of contractual obligation generally, the enforcement of clickwrap presupposes. Does it presuppose an objective, act-based theory of assent, on which what matters is what a reasonable observer would read into the outward act rather than what the consumer actually understood or intended? In what sense, if any, is clicking a button one did not read a genuine exercise of autonomy? Is the practice consistent with the doctrines that vitiate contracts for duress or unconscionability, or does it quietly treat consumer consent as a formality? And does the practice need a consent-based theory at all, or is it better explained by an instrumentalist picture in which assent merely marks the boundaries of enforceable terms for reasons of efficiency and predictability? Please separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to be compatible with, and say what would have to change if we replaced the presupposed view with a genuinely voluntarist, meeting-of-the-minds theory of contractual obligation.
