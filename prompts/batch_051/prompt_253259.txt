I am compiling a set of objections to the view, usually associated with Wittgenstein's language-game approach, that many concepts — with 'game' as the standard exhibit — are unified not by a common essence but by overlapping family resemblances. So far I have five.

(1) The chaining objection: pairwise similarity is so cheap that, with enough intermediate cases, a chain of overlapping likenesses can be built from nearly anything to nearly anything, so the account draws no principled boundary around a concept's extension.

(2) The relevance objection: family resemblance supplies no criterion for which respects of similarity count, and therefore cannot distinguish concept-constitutive features from accidental shared ones — all games are human activities, but that is not what makes something a game.

(3) The hidden-essence objection: an observed network of similarities could be the surface effect of an underlying common feature, or of a disjunction of features, so the account is compatible with hidden essences and does not show that none exists.

(4) The circularity objection: to assemble the family of games in the first place one must already be able to recognize which things are candidate games, so the account presupposes the extension it is supposed to explain and gives no standard by which an application could be mistaken.

(5) The overgeneralization objection: family resemblance is extrapolated from a few permissive terms such as 'game,' but many concepts — legal categories, kinship classifications, mathematical terms — are fixed by explicit rules or definitions, so the account is at best a regional one.

I want a sixth objection that is not on this list and not a variant of any of the five — not a rewording, a special case, or a combination. If the only objection you can find is a variant, say so and then give the nearest genuinely distinct objection you can construct.

Please respond as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion:

1. the new objection, stated directly;
2. why it is structurally different from the five objections above;
3. what it shows about family-resemblance accounts that the other five do not;
4. your honest assessment of whether it is any good as an objection;
5. how a defender of family resemblance would most plausibly reply to it.
