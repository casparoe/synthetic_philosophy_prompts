I’m a software engineer and I keep running into a distinction I don’t have a word for. When I’m debugging a complex system—say a chess engine or a language model—the easiest way to predict what it will do is to talk about it like it has beliefs and goals: “the model thinks I want a list, so it returns a list,” or “the engine wants to protect the king.” We all know the thing doesn’t literally think or want anything; its just code. But the same is true of a thermostat or a sorting function, and nobody says the thermostat wants the room warm, or that the function believes its input is sorted. So there’s a line somewhere: for some systems, the only sane way to predict them is to assume they’re rational agents with beliefs and goals, even though we don’t believe they have minds. Does this phenomenon have an established name? I’ve seen people talk about “as-if” explanation or teleology, but those don’t feel quite right. I’m not looking just for a label though—I want to know where the edge is. What exactly is different between the chess engine and the thermostat? Is the difference just how complex the system is, or is it something about the kinds of rules it follows? And does talking this way commit me to saying the model really has beliefs, or can it be a purely instrumental way of predicting behavior? Also, where do you draw the line between systems that get this treatment and systems that don’t—does it change as the system gets better, or is there a point where “useful shorthand” becomes the only coherent description?
