In my seminar on practical rationality we have been discussing Parfit's hitchhiker: a stranded motorist must persuade a passing driver that he will pay up once he is safe, even though both know that, after the rescue, paying would not serve his interests. A student has taken to ending every dispute by insisting that "it can be rational to be irrational," and when pressed he glosses this as the claim that the hitchhiker can rationally bring it about that he later pays the driver even though paying then is irrational. Before I respond to him, I want to know what that slogan could actually amount to. Distinguish the several precise theses it might be used to assert—for example, theses about which dispositions or commitments it is rational to cultivate, about which acts are rational at which times, and about what "irrational" is being measured against—and assess each as trivially true, substantively true but contentious, or false. Then identify which reading or readings the student's gloss actually needs, and whether any reading would support the stronger conclusion that an agent may rationally plan to do what, at the time of action, she knows to be against her own all-things-considered judgment.
