I am a novelist, and I have been unable to decide whether a parallel I keep noticing between Kant's Third Antinomy and the ordinary working assumptions of fiction is a genuine structural resemblance or merely a piece of writerly self-deception dressed up in metaphysical language.

The antinomy, as I understand it, is this. The thesis holds that natural causation cannot be the only kind of causality if the appearances of the world are to be fully explained; we must also suppose a causality through freedom, a capacity of certain events to begin a series from themselves without being determined by a previous state. The antithesis holds that there is no such freedom: every event in the world happens solely according to laws of nature, and an uncaused beginning within the causal order would destroy the very lawfulness that makes explanation possible. Kant's resolution distinguishes standpoints. One and the same action, considered as an appearance, is fully determined by antecedent causes, while considered through its intelligible character it may be free; the two descriptions do not compete because they apply to the same thing under different aspects.

Here is the parallel. The manuscript fixes my protagonist's every move. Her decision in the final chapters is the product of the history, traits, and circumstances I have written for her, and in the strictest sense there is no other action available to her, because the text is already the text. Yet I cannot write the scene at the level of causation alone. If the act is explained by what the plot requires, or by my convenience as an author, the writing has failed and readers will detect the author's hand. The act must arise from the character: it must be intelligible as what she, given her reasons and temperament, does. Readers who know perfectly well that I invented her will still argue about whether the act was in character and whether she could have done otherwise. And when a scene is going well, I often experience something similar myself: I discover what she would do rather than simply dictating it.

What I want to know is whether this is genuinely the structure Kant invokes, or only an imitation of it. A character has no intelligible character in Kant's sense, no noumenal ground, and no first-person standpoint; the author stands outside the fiction and selects one continuation from among many real alternatives, whereas a natural cause does not select. So the analogy may break exactly where Kant needs it to hold. Please assess how much is really shared. What would have to be true of authorship and of Kantian freedom for the parallel to amount to more than metaphor? Does the novelist's distinction between plot-level explanation and character-level motivation carry any lesson for the debate about whether explaining an action by the agent's reasons competes with explaining it by prior causes? And where does the parallel predict something false in fiction or in metaphysics — for example, it seems to me that an act written as genuinely uncaused and reasonless reads not as free but as arbitrary, which may indicate which notion of freedom is really in play.
