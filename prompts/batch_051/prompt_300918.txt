I'm giving a paper at a public law workshop next month, which I'm dreading a bit, because the room is split between people who think constitutional entrenchment is mostly a way for dead elites to tie the hands of living majorities, and two or three people who are steeped in the classic Ulysses-and-the-sirens story about precommitment and think it basically rescues constitutionalism. My thesis is that the Ulysses framing gets the justification of entrenchment backwards: an entrenched clause is democratically legitimate when it works less like self-binding against weakness of will and more like an assurance or credible commitment to someone outside the present majority—minorities, treaty partners, creditors—that their deal won't be renegotiated the moment it becomes inconvenient. The corollary I'm tempted to defend is that where a clause can't point to any outside beneficiary who actually needed that assurance, the precommitment talk is just dressing up ordinary dead-hand entrenchment, and the standard Ulysses defenses are doing real normative work only in the assurance cases, not the weakness-of-will cases.
