At last week's philosophy of religion seminar on divine hiddenness, a fellow student waved away the argument with the maxim "absence of evidence is not evidence of absence". His use of it was this: the fact that God is not more evident to sincere seekers is merely an absence of evidence, and an absence of evidence cannot count against God's existence. He said it as though it closed the question.

I doubt the maxim is a single claim. It could mean something near-tautological, or something very strong and false, and the intermediate readings do not all help his case. The argument he was trying to dismiss was the nonresistant-nonbelief version of the hiddenness argument: roughly, a perfectly loving God would not leave capable, nonresistant persons unable to enter a personal relationship with him, yet it seems there are sincere seekers who remain in exactly that state; so, probably, no such God exists. The maxim is being used to say that their lack of evidence is no evidence against God.

Please lay out for me the distinct precise theses this maxim could express — from platitudinous to radical — and for each give a verdict on the reading itself and on whether it actually damages the hiddenness argument as I have just stated it.
