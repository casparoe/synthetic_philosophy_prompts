An old argument in legal and political theory held that the state and the chartered corporation are continuous in kind, not different in nature. Both were analyzed as artificial persons: they could own property, enter contracts, sue and be sued, and survive the deaths of their members; both acted only through representatives; and the sovereign state was said to hold a larger and more complete bundle of corporate powers than a trading company did. The fact that a chartered trading company could maintain troops, mint coins, and levy taxes in India was treated as evidence that sovereignty and corporate power differ only in degree and in how much of the bundle has been consolidated in one body.

Now consider a decentralized autonomous organization that has no charter from any government, no territory, and no human board of directors. Its rules are written in software, membership is voluntary and in principle open to anyone, and the organization administers a digital currency, records property entitlements on a public ledger, enforces agreements through automated escrow, and distributes funds according to those rules. Suppose such an organization comes to serve, for a large number of people, as their main provider of dispute resolution, property records, money, and income-like payments.

Does this development vindicate the old thesis that the state is simply a corporation with an especially complete bundle of powers, or does it reveal a hidden load-bearing premise — for instance, that corporate personality is an artifact of a territorial legal order, a public charter, or a coercive enforcement mechanism? State exactly what the old thesis would have to claim in order to cover the DAO, and assess whether the DAO can meet those claims. If it cannot, identify which feature of statehood the argument was quietly presupposing all along, and whether the DAO is barred from acquiring it by the fact that its constitution is code rather than chartered authority.
