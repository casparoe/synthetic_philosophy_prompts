I'm a philosophy professor, but my work is mostly in ethics, so when I start fussing over ordinary English sentences I feel like I've walked into the wrong department. There's a phenomena I keep noticing and I can't put a name to it. Take 'ducks lay eggs.' True, even though the majority of ducks at any moment are not doing anything of the sort—the males never do, ducklings don't, and older females have stopped. Now take 'mosquitoes carry malaria.' Also true, and yet the vast majority of mosquitoes never carry malaria. Still, 'mosquitoes don't carry malaria' is not something we would assert, even though it is true of almost every individual. And 'ducks are female' is plainly false despite being true of roughly half of them. So the proportion isn't what makes the sentence true. The best I can do is hand-wavy talk about what is typical, or characteristic, or what the kind is 'for,' but these come apart in ways I can't make precise. My question: does this exact matter have an established name in semantics—not just the general category of such sentences, which I believe has one, but the feature of tolerating exceptions unevenly and the distinctions among the ways a property can be true of a kind? If it's several distinct notions, I'd like them seperated and ranked; a single label won't help me teach the material. And if the standard accounts treat my two examples as different species of the same genus, say precisely what makes 'ducks lay eggs' differ from 'mosquitoes carry malaria.'
