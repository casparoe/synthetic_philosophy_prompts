I am a novelist, and I am writing a character who prices catastrophe cover for an insurer; I want the texture of his uncertainty to be right. He moves between two kinds of odds: risks whose distribution is known and stable, and murkier prospects whose odds seem only partly known. I keep returning to the Ellsberg urn as the crispest statement of the difference. In the usual setup, an urn holds 90 balls: 30 red, and 60 that are black or yellow in unknown proportions. Offered a bet on red or a bet on black, most people take red; offered a bet on red-or-yellow or a bet on black-or-yellow, most people take black-or-yellow. The pair of preferences cannot be represented by any single set of subjective probabilities for the three colors under expected utility. The standard lesson is that people have a distinct ambiguity aversion: they prefer the options with crisp probabilities (1/3 for red, 2/3 for black-or-yellow) to options whose probabilities are known only as intervals, even when the payoff structure is symmetrical.

But this character works in a world where every consequential decision arrives with a machine-generated, well-calibrated predictive distribution: hurricane paths, counterparty default, crop yield, negotiation outcomes. The system assigns each color of ball a probability and reports that its probabilities are calibrated against past draws. This is the development I want to think through. If an urn can always be supplemented with such a forecast, does Ellsberg's argument still show what it was taken to show? A Bayesian can absorb disagreement by moving up a level and placing probabilities over possible models, which would make ambiguity look not like a primitive feature of a decision but like ordinary uncertainty about model misspecification, potentially an endless regress. Yet one could also say the lesson survives, because a calibrated forecast is itself only one more fallible event, and the agent must still choose how much weight to give the model's crisp recommendation against the suspicion that the world has drifted. I want to know which premise of the original argument is actually under pressure, whether the argument is dead, repairable, or merely relocated, and what a weaker but still meaningful repaired version would claim. I am interested in the structure of the argument, not in policy or portfolio advice.
