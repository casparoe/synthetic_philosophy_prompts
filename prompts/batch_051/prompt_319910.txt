I'm the compliance engineer at a small medical-device company that makes infusion pumps, and every week I'm the one who signs off on whether a design change triggers full verification and validation. Our quality system says a change triggers it when it 'could affect safety or performance.' The obvious cases are easy — new firmware, a different battery chemistry. The ones that eat my week are things like swapping the plastic resin on a non-fluid-path housing, changing the font or kerning of the on-screen instructions, or bumping the torque spec on the IV-pole clamp. I want a reusable checklist, grounded in Peirce's pragmatic maxim (the whole meaning of a concept is the set of its conceivable practical effects) plus whatever else in his account of inquiry is actually usable — the difference between real doubt and paper doubt, the ways we settle opinion by tenacity, authority, or what just seems reasonable instead of by letting consequences decide, and the fallibilist point that even a settled answer could be wrong.

The checklist has to be something I can run in about twenty minutes, something an engineer who's never read Peirce can follow, and something I can defend to an auditor. At each step, tell me whether it's a factual question or a judgment call that no checklist can eliminate, and what a good-enough answer at that step looks like. Then run it through three cases: (1) changing a non-fluid-path housing from ABS to polycarbonate, (2) changing the on-screen instruction font and kerning, and (3) increasing the torque spec on the screw that holds the pump to an IV pole. I want at least one of those worked answers to be one my team won't like, and a closing section that says plainly what this procedure can't settle.
