I keep circling back to Spinoza's picture because it has alot of the systematic virtues I find hard to resist: one substance, everything following from God's nature with the same necessity as the fact that a triangle's angles equal two right angles, no brute contingency left over, no question of "why this world rather than another." And the Ethics doesn't stop at the metaphysics — it's built to move you from the bondage of the passions to freedom, by understanding. When you see clearly enough that some bad thing couldn't have happened otherwise, so the story goes, the rage and the guilt and the "if only" dissolve, and what replaces them isn't resignation but something Spinoza thinks is the highest joy: the intellectual love of God, loving the whole order of nature under the aspect of eternity (where "God" here means the one substance, nature itself, not a person who could have chosen otherwise).

But I have an intuition that won't get out of the way. When I think about someone doing something genuinely awful — a grown adult who tortures a child and could easily have done otherwise, a government that lets a preventable famine happen through sheer venality — "it had to happen, it couldn't have been otherwise" sounds like the thing the abuser says to excuse himself, not the thing the wise person says. I'm not sure I want the indignation to fall away, and I'm not sure loving a whole that includes that, as necessary, is wisdom rather than a kind of moral anesthesia. And when I'm actually deliberating — say, whether to lie to a friend — the alternatives feel genuinely open in a way the theory says is just my ignorance of causes.

I'm not asking you to pick a side. Tell me what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes: what kind of debunking account of "could have done otherwise" and of indignation would have to be true, and whether Spinoza's machinery of adequate and inadequate ideas — understanding through causes versus being buffeted by confused passions — actually gives it. Then tell me what it would take for the theory to go or be amended at the point where it says the bad thing was necessary. And tell me whether there's a reading on which both survive — maybe the necessity of the whole is compatible with a real distinction between acting from your own nature and being driven by external causes, and the intellectual love of God attaches to the order rather than to each event inside it. Give me the strongest version of each move and a verdict on which one you'd actually make.
