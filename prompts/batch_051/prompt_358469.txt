I’m currently taking a course on the philosophy of probability, and we’ve been reading John Venn’s *The Logic of Chance* alongside some contemporary discussion of the reference class problem. Venn’s worry, as I understand it, is that any particular event or individual can be referred to an indefinitely large number of classes, each with its own statistical frequency, and the probability we assign depends entirely on which class we take as the reference class; there is no obvious rule, in the world itself, that tells us which one is correct. That has made me want to think through a concrete institutional mechanism rather than just the abstract issue, and I would like your help analyzing how the mechanism would actually behave.

Suppose a university administration sets up a Predictive Score Tribunal to assign each student a graduation-probability score, which will be used for early-warning outreach and for allocating tutoring resources. The rule is this: for any student, anyone in the university community—advisors, instructors, administrators, and even the student—may nominate a reference class, such as “students in this major with this many failed courses” or “first-generation students with this high-school GPA band.” The Tribunal maintains a public model that records each nominated class’s historical graduation frequency and a running calibration score based on how well that class frequency has predicted outcomes for students assigned to it in the past. To produce a given student’s score, the system takes a weighted average of the frequencies of all nominated classes, with weights proportional to each class’s past calibration and capped so that no single class can receive more than a quarter of the total weight. Any nominator whose class improves the system’s calibration over the next year receives a small administrative credit or budget increase; any nominator whose class systematically degrades calibration loses the right to nominate classes for a semester.

What I would like from you is not a general verdict about whether the scheme is fair or unfair, but a detailed account of how it would play out in practice. What incentives would it create for advisors, instructors, students, and the administration? How might people game the nomination process or the calibration system, and what second-order effects would likely emerge in the distribution of scores over time? Would the mechanism solve Venn’s problem, or would it simply relocate the arbitrariness into the nomination rules, the weights, and the calibration measure? And if the failure modes are serious, could any of them be fixed without abandoning the core idea?
