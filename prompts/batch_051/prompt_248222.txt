I got into an argument with my dad over whether it's ever okay to break a promise, and I wound up going down a rabbit hole reading forum posts about people who think moral rules are kind of overrated. I saw some commenters call the no-rules side "particularism" and the rules side "generalism," though I'm not totally sure I'm using those labels right. I'm not in school for any of this, so I'm worried I've got the basic idea tangled. I wrote a short essay trying to sort out what I think. Could you read it and tell me where the argument is weak, where I'm talking past the other side, and what the strongest version of the other side would say back? I'd rather have comments on the ideas than line-by-line grammer stuff.

Here's the essay:

Most of us were taught morality as a list: don't lie, don't steal, keep your promises, help people in need. The list feels like it should work like a recipe. But I've started to think a recipe is exactly the wrong picture, and that the really important part of moral life happens before any rule gets applied.

Take an ordinary rule like "don't lie." It's fine as a slogan, but the second you ask whether it's okay to lie to a drunk person who's demanding his car keys, pretty much everyone agrees the rule bends. The usual move is to amend the rule: don't lie, except to protect someone from serious harm. But now ask why protecting someone from serious harm counts as an exception while protecting someone from mild embarrassment doesn't. Whatever answer you give will itself be another principle, like "protect people from serious harm," and that principle will have its own exceptions (what if the serious harm is a punishment they deserve? what if they brought it on themselves?). You either keep adding clauses forever, or at some point you just say: you have to see the situation for what it is. And that's the point where the rule has stopped doing the actual work.

Maybe someone replies that the real rule isn't the slogan but the huge list of clauses, including clauses about the clauses. But that can't be how people actually decide, because nobody can hold a list like that in their head, and even a computer following the list would need a further rule for how to apply the rules. And if the list isn't for deciding but is just a summary of correct verdicts, then calling it a moral rule feels like a bit of a cheat - it's an after-the-fact map, not the thing steering the ship.

I also think there's something about how reasons work that doesn't fit the rule picture. A fact that pushes one way in one situation can push the opposite way in another. Usually, the fact that something will cause pain is a reason not to do it. But the fact that a punishment will hurt a defiant bully doesn't count against the punishment the way it would count against hurting someone innocent. Same fact, different moral sign. Rules, at least the appealing kind, want facts to keep a steady meaning across situations. But they don't.

So my current view is something like: principles are real and useful as rough first passes, teaching tools, and summaries, but they're not the engine of moral thought. The engine is more like a trained perception - you learn to notice certain features (cruelty, desperation, gratitude, exploitation) the way a birdwatcher learns to see a bird that a beginner stares right past. That's why two decent people can agree on every rule either of them can write down and still disagree about a hard case.

The part I can't get past is this. If moral thought is basically perceiving what matters in a case, and there's no rule that ties the features to a verdict, then why expect anyone else's perception to be correctable? With rules, we can at least argue by checking whether a case falls under the rule. Without them, "you're seeing it wrong" starts to sound like "you're just not seeing what I see," which gets us nowhere in a disagreement. I don't think that makes the no-rules view false - maybe moral argument really is messier than we'd like. But I keep going in circles.

So: is my after-the-fact-map point actually different from just saying rules are useful fictions? Is the pain-in-punishment example doing the work I think it is, or is it just a dressed-up exception? And what's the strongest comeback from someone who thinks a rule or principle really is the engine of moral thought, even if it has to be one of those indefinitely long ones? Don't worry about citing anyone; I just want the ideas pressure-tested.
