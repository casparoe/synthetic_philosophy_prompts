So here's what's been bugging me. US labor law lets an employer classify salaried "executive, administrative, or professional" workers as exempt, which means a manager can do sixty-hour weeks with no overtime pay at all, on the theory that she's paid for the job and not the hours, and that her work is the kind of thing that involves discretion and judgment rather than time-clocking. France meanwhile made the legal working week thirty-five hours, and keeps layering on a "right to disconnect" from after-hours work email. And Japan has a formal system for recognizing karoshi — death from overwork — as a work-related injury, while a lot of corporate culture there still treats leaving before your boss as vaguely shameful.

These look like three different institutional answers to the same underlying question: what work is, and how much of a life it should be allowed to consume. So I want to know: what theory of work and leisure does each of these practices presuppose? For each one, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with — e.g., is the US overtime exemption actually commited to a Protestant-work-ethic picture of work as a calling, or is that just a cultural story we tell around a much thinner legal idea? And if you replaced the underlying view of work with a rival one — say, work is just a disutility that people exchange for consumption, or work is a site of identity and self-realization — what would actually have to change in each of these regimes? When you answer, lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give me the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating the question.
