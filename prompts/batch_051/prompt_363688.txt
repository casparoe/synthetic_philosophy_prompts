Throughout this term, my tutor in moral theology has been working through whether guilt, as opposed to damage or obligation, can be transmitted across generations. She has given a series of verdicts on cases, which I have recorded as follows.

1. The daughter of a man who organised a massacre is not, in her view, morally guilty of the massacre, even though she inherited property acquired through it. She nonetheless has a duty to disclose what she knows and not to obstruct restitution.
2. Someone who unknowingly bought stolen goods is not guilty of the theft, but must return or surrender the goods.
3. A nation that has existed continuously since a massacre committed under a predecessor regime may now confess that massacre as its own sin: no living citizen is individually culpable, but the nation is the same corporate agent.
4. A religious order whose founder committed serious abuses against those in his care may, as an order, confess the founder's sins as its own, because the order, and not its present members severally, is the continuing agent.
5. A newborn child is in original sin, a real privation and disorder of the will rather than the child's own act. When pressed on whether this is guilt or damage, she rejects the suggestion that it is merely hereditary weakness and describes it as a participation in the guilt of the human race at its origin.
6. The great-grandchildren of a perpetrator do not inherit his guilt, because they and he form no continuing corporate agent; their duties are limited to memory, truthfulness, and not benefiting from the wrong.
7. A covenant people may confess the sins of an earlier generation of the same covenant community as its own, provided the people has continued to exist as that same community under the covenant. The confession is corporate, not a claim about each member's individual life.

Next week she is to be asked about a hereditary monarchy whose present monarch is about to issue an apology for a massacre ordered by a predecessor in the same office a hundred and fifty years ago. The draft statement asserts that the crown itself is guilty of the massacre, although the present monarch was not alive at the time.

If she is to remain consistent with the seven rulings above, what should we expect her to say? Which of her earlier rulings is the closest analog? If her record underdetermines the matter, set out the two or three principles that each make good sense of all seven rulings, state what each principle predicts for the monarchy case, give your best prediction and the reasons for preferring the corresponding principle, and specify the further case that would most cleanly discriminate between the leading candidates. Please answer in British English.
