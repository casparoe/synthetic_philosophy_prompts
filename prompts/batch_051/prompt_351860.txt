In my work as a journalist covering science and ideas, I have absorbed a rule I find hard to relinquish: when a community's account of its own past conflicts with the documents, the dates, and the material traces, the record wins. The rule compels me because it has the virtues I want in any epistemic practice — it is public, checkable, revisable, and it refuses to let the loudest voice in the room settle what happened. Yet I keep running into cases where applying it feels like erasure rather than correction. A community's memory of a massacre, a dispossession, or a founding wrong may be imprecise about chronology and culprits, but it is not merely a defective report against the archive. It binds the living to the dead, allocates obligations and blame, and gives people a shared description of themselves; telling a community that its memory is false can be experienced as a second injury, and not obviously irrationally. I am therefore caught between a systematic theory of how the past is known and a firm intuition that some memories should not be overridden even when they are not accurate.

I want a negotiated verdict rather than a declaration for one side. What would it take to debunk the intuition — to show that it confuses commemorative speech with factual assertion, or that protecting cherished error is a form of condescension? What would it take to amend the theory — to hold that the record enjoys epistemic authority while memory enjoys a different, practical authority over what a community owes, mourns, and teaches? And if both can survive under that division of labor, what exactly remains of the claim that the record wins when the two conflict?
