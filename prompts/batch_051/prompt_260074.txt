Bernard Williams's Makropulos case is usually read as showing that an endless life would eventually become tedious: because a person has only a finite stock of categorical desires, an immortal would either exhaust the projects that give her reason to live on or change so much that she ceases to be the same person. Vary the case as follows. The elixir confers an indefinitely extended life at a fixed biological age, and every seventy-five years it also fades all episodic memories more than seventy-five years old into something like third-person knowledge: the person still knows what she did and whom she loved, but no longer has the participant's felt perspective on it, while skills, semantic knowledge, and standing dispositions are preserved. Everyone who considers taking the elixir knows this in advance. Does Williams's dilemma survive the variation? In particular, does the fading allow a single person to keep renewing the categorical desires that Williams says must run out, or does it avoid tedium only by periodically replacing the person, so that Williams would count it as death rather than immortality? What, if anything, does the variant reveal about whether the real obstacle is endless time itself rather than a fixed character occupying it — and would a heaven that included this kind of fading be a coherent object of desire, or merely a promise of serial oblivion?
