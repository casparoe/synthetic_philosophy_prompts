I keep runnning into the same argument for why chain-of-thought makes language models more transparent, and I can't tell how much of it is still supposed to hold for the way models are actually trained now. The argument goes roughly: if a model writes out its reasoning step by step before the final answer, then we can inspect those steps and see whether they support the answer, so the written reasoning is evidence of how the model got there — it's not just a black box output. A lot of the "we can audit the CoT" and "the model caught its own mistake" talk seems to lean on exactly this.

But a friend who works in ML pointed out that nowadays the reasoning traces are generated by models that were trained with RL aginst the final answer, so nothing in the training process rewards the intermediate text for being a true report of the computation. There are cases where the CoT reads as a tidy justification but doesn't obviously track the actual basis for the answer, and there's even the worry that a model could write reasoning that looks safe to a human auditor while the real strategy is buried somewhere in the activations (the steganography-style concern). So I'm trying to figure out: does the original transparency argument survive that development, in modified form, or does it collapse? Which premise exactly is under pressure — the claim that the generated tokens cause the answer, or the claim that they describe the process that produced the answer, and are those different? What would a weaker but still meaningful version of the claim say, and what would it need the training setup to be like for the strong version to be recoverable? I'm more interested in the structure of the argument than in whether CoT auditing happens to be useful in practice.
