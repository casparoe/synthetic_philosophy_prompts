So we've been doing reflective equilibrium in my methods seminar, and I put together this argument that seems to prove way too much, because the conclusion basically says reflective equilibrium can't do the work everyone says it does. I can't figure out where it goes wrong, or maybe it doesn't go wrong and I just have to live with the conclusion. Here's the argument, numbered out:

(1) On the standard picture, a set of moral principles is justified for a person only if it's in reflective equilibrium with that person's considered moral judgments, where "considered" means the judgments are made under conditions conducive to good judgment—like full information, no obvious bias, no personal stake, stuff like that.

(2) But those conditions can be specified without building in any substantive moral constraints. They're just epistemic conditions. So a person who is fully informed and impartial could still, in principle, have deeply bigoted considered judgments. Say someone starts with the firm considered judgment that people of a certain group are less capable of moral reasoning.

(3) That person can then work their judgments and principles into a coherent set. They can adjust principles to fit their initial judgments, maybe tweak a judgment here or there when it conflicts with a principle they find plausible, and eventually reach a stable reflective equilibrium where the principle "members of group G are morally inferior" coheres with a wide range of their considered judgments.

(4) By the method's own criterion, that principle is now justified for that person. Their equilibrium is just as internally coherent as someone else's anti-bigoted equilibrium.

(5) But we don't want to say that the bigoted person's principle is genuinely justified, even relative to them. That seems like a reductio.

(6) So either reflective equilibrium is not a genuine method of justification—it just describes what someone happens to believe after tidying up—or it must secretly rely on an independent standard of which starting judgments are correct, which would make the method circular or redundant.

(7) But that can't be right, because reflective equilibrium is supposed to be our best account of how moral justification works without appealing to foundational intuitions or external moral facts.

I guess the step I most want to keep is (1), because otherwise I don't know what reflective equilibrium even is. But then I'm stuck with the relativism in (4) and (5), which feels awful. What's the least costly way out? Is there a way to constrain the starting judgments so the bigot's judgments don't count as considered, without just assuming the conclusion? Or do defenders of reflective equilibrium just bite the bullet and say yes, the bigot really is justified relative to their own starting points, and that's not as bad as it sounds?
