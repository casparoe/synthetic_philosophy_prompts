In Cesare Beccaria's An Essay on Crimes and Punishments, the case against judicial torture rests partly on a psychological claim: "Every act of the will is invariably in proportion to the force of the impression on our senses. The impression of pain, then, may increase to such a degree, that, occupying the mind entirely, it will compel the sufferer to use the shortest method of freeing himself from torment." The ticking time bomb case, by contrast, is usually built on different assumptions: the interrogators already know the detainee is guilty, and what they want is not a confession but timely information that only he can provide. Does Beccaria's argument still have force under those assumptions, or does it depend on the older practice of torturing suspects of uncertain guilt to obtain confessions? Which premise does the time bomb case actually pressure — the psychological claim about what pain compels, the legal principle about innocence and proof, or something else — and is there a repaired version of Beccaria's objection that survives the new scenario?
