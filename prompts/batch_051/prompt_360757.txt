I find myself holding the following set of commitments about mathematical proof, and I would like an audit of whether they are jointly tenable.

(1) Mathematical justification is a priori: empirical observation can corroborate a conjecture, but it cannot be part of what makes a statement a theorem.

(2) A proof must be surveyable by a single human mind, at least in principle. If no individual thinker could hold the chain of reasoning together as a whole, the derivation is not, in the relevant sense, a proof.

(3) The four-colour theorem and the Kepler conjecture are proved theorems, and there is nothing epistemically defective about relying on them in ordinary mathematical practice.

(4) The report that a computer has executed an algorithm is an empirical observation about a physical machine. A successful run is therefore experimental evidence rather than a mathematical demonstration.

(5) The classification of finite simple groups is also a proved theorem, even though no single mathematician has read the entire proof, and I do not see this as a concession.

Do these commitments hang together? I suspect that somewhere in the set there is a real conflict, but I cannot locate it. Please (a) identify which commitments pull against which others, (b) rank the tensions by severity, and for each say what the cheapest repair would be and what it would cost, (c) flag any apparent conflicts that dissolve on closer inspection, and (d) say which commitment, if any, cannot be kept. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
