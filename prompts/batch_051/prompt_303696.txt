I am working on the boundary between polysemy and homonymy, with an eye on the larger question whether ordinary talk of a word's meaning can survive the fact that most content words shift their contribution with context. As I understand the standard toolkit, deciding whether two uses of one phonological string are one polysemous word or two homonymous words comes down to four diagnostics:

1. Etymology: the uses count as one word if they descend from a shared etymon and as two if they have separate histories.
2. Regular semantic relatedness: they count as one word if one use can be derived from the other by a productive relation such as metaphor, metonymy, narrowing, or broadening.
3. Co-predication: two uses are more plausibly a single lexical entry to the extent that one occurrence can simultaneously answer to both without anomaly; forced or punning coordination is treated as evidence that the readings live in separate entries.
4. Cross-linguistic lexicalization: readings that unrelated languages tend to conflate in the same lexical item are unlikely to be accidental homophones, while readings that consistently split across languages are more plausibly a language-particular accident.

I think this toolkit is both redundant and incomplete, but before arguing that I want to enlarge it on its own terms.

Give me one more diagnostic for polysemy against homonymy. It must not be on this list, and it must not be a rewording, a special case, or a weighted combination of the four. State the diagnostic precisely; say what kind of evidence it draws on and what makes that evidence a genuinely different locus (acquisition, processing, formal composition, communication, or something else); apply it to a conflict case of your own invention in which the four pull in different directions; and say what it shows that the four together would leave open. End by saying whether your diagnostic presupposes that words have stable meanings, or whether it could be stated by someone who holds that lexical meaning is only a family of contextually generated uses.
