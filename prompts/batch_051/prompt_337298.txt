I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about when a failed long-run prediction is allowed to be revised rather than thrown out, because my snap verdicts on cases feel inconsistent and I'd like to know if there's a principle behind them. I'll give you my verdicts, and I want you either to find the principle that gets all of them, or tell me which of my verdicts I should drop and why. Also, please write the whole answer as continuous prose, like an email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.

Case one. In the 1970s a demographer predicts famine across much of South Asia by 1990 on the grounds that population is growing faster than food output. It doesn't happen, mostly because new high-yield crop varieties and fertilizer spread through the region. Her defenders say the underlying mechanism really was there and would have produced famine, but a one-off technological jump moved the ceiling; they update the model with the new yields and keep using it. My gut says that's a legitimate revision, not a refutation.

Case two. In 2005 someone predicts a permanent crash in oil production by 2015, with prices rising so high industrial economies can't function. It doesn't happen; fracking and efficiency gains push the date back. The same author now says he was right, just early, and the crash is coming in 2040. My gut says that's moving the goalposts and I don't trust it.

Case three. Around the same time a different analyst predicts a plateau in oil production by 2015. Instead output grows. He then publishes a paper identifying the thing he had missed, which was the fracking cost curve, rebuilds the model, and revises the plateau date to 2035, while spelling out what data between now and then would count against it. My gut says that's legitimate, even though he moved the date just like the guy in case two. This is the pair that bothers me, because I can't yet say what the difference really is.

Case four. An 1830s writer in the Malthus tradition argues that population tends to outrun food, so severe periodic crises are inevitable in the long run, with famine, disease, and war as the only real checks. Two centuries later, thanks to fossil fuels and synthetic fertilizer, the endless crises haven't arrived where he said they would. Defenders say he was right but early: fossil energy is a temporary buffer, and once it runs down the original tendency comes back. I'm torn. Part of me says this is the same move as case one, and part of me says it's unfalsifiable faith like case two. I genuinely can't tell which.

Case five. A group predicts a devastating asteroid impact in 2012. It doesn't happen. They say their calendar arithmetic was slightly off, not the prediction. My gut says that one is obviously illegitimate.

Case six. A climate model predicts an ice-free Arctic summer by 2015. It doesn't happen. The modelers find an aerosol cooling effect they hadn't included, add it, and now predict 2050. My gut says legitimate, even though the original prediction failed badly.

What I want is three things. First, is there a single principle, stated precisely enough to use on new cases, that sorts these six verdicts the way I do, or close to it? Second, apply it to a case I haven't settled: a fisheries model predicts complete collapse of a stock by 2030, and if that doesn't happen the modelers say the fleets became more efficient at finding the remaining fish, so the date moves back but the collapse is still coming; should I count that as legitimate like cases three and six, or as goalpost-moving like case two? Third, if no clean principle gets all six of my verdicts, tell me which ones I should overturn and whether that changes what I should say about the fisheries case.
