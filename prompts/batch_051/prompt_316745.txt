I'm a PhD student in political theory and I keep coming back to John Stuart Mill's *Considerations on Representative Government*. In the first chapter he characterises the view that governments 'are not made, but grow' (he's presenting it as the natural-history rival to rationalist design, not yet as his own line). I'm trying to use that distinction to figure out what modern epistocratic proposals are actually committed to. Take a concrete institutional design: voting requires passing a basic political-knowledge test, and people who score above a high threshold get weighted votes. Does that practice presuppose that a franchise can be designed as a piece of machinery to achieve good outcomes? Or could it be described as something that grows out of an existing educational hierarchy? And what does it suppose about political knowledge — that the knowledge needed to vote well is the sort of thing the state can test and certify, rather than something distributed across the whole population in a way no test can track? I'd like you to reconstruct the implicit theory of institutions and of political knowledge here, and say whether it is in tension with Mill's 'grow' line or just with one reading of it. British English please.
