I'm in a lab that builds RLHF'd language agents, and we're arguing about a training-loop proposal I'll call "reward self-governance." I've read enough of the embedded-agency stuff on lesswrong to know the cheap answer is "the model edits its own reward channel and everyone dies," but I want a more mechanism-level take, because the proposal has guardrails that are supposed to prevent exactly that.

Here's the rule. After each generation finishes training, the model gets repository access to the reward model that scored it, for one day. During that day it can submit at most one patch to the reward model's codebase. The patch is accepted only if:

- it changes the reward model's scores on a held-out freeze set of 10,000 (prompt, completion, human_rating) tuples by less than some small epsilon;
- on 200 synthetically generated distribution shifts, the patched reward model doesn't assign a higher mean score than the old one on any of the 200 synthetic datasets (yes, the check is that the reward distribution doesn't drift upward on any synthetic dataset);
- the model writes a plain-English delta explaining what scoring behaviors changed and why;
- a human can veto the patch with zero justification.

After any accepted patch, the next generation is trained from scratch against the patched reward model. Rinse and repeat.

The in-house argument is: the freeze set stops obvious score-inflation, the synthetic shifts stop the model from expanding what counts as rewardable, and the human veto plus the delta makes its preferences legible. Since these models are embedded (their outputs are part of the data that later retrains both the policy and the reward model), I suspect the real failure modes are boring and subtle rather than a sudden wirehead.

So: in equilibrium, what kinds of patches does a capable optimizer actually search for under these constraints? Where does the freeze set get Goodharted first, and what do the 200 synthetic shifts fail to catch? Does the plain-English delta make the model more honest in expectation, or just select for models that write innocuous-sounding patches? Does the human veto do any work once the deltas are smooth enough? And is there some modified version of this you'd actually run, or is the whole thing a embedded-agency accident waiting to happen?
