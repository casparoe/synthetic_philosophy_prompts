I have been keeping a record of how a friend of mine settles disagreements about taste, because her rulings feel consistent but I cannot state the principle behind them. Her past cases, as nearly as I can reconstruct them:

- On a careful literary novel against a plot-only thriller, she said the novel is plainly better, and that someone who denies this has an untrained ear for prose rather than a different but equally valid taste.
- On whether her brother could be argued into seeing this, she said no: description cannot do the work of practice. Repeated exposure and comparison would be the only cure, and she would expect anyone who did the exercise to end up on her side.
- On a composer whose music has been admired across three centuries while most contemporaries are forgotten, she said the survival is real evidence of merit, though it is not proof, since an excellent work can be forgotten.
- On a film everyone around her was praising, she said it was slick and empty, and that current popularity contributes nothing to whether a work is good.
- On a wine she disliked that a practiced friend called excellent, she said her own reaction carried no authority, because her palate was untrained; she defers to the friend, but even a practiced judge can be corrected by a blind re-tasting.
- On two equally practiced tasters who disagreed about the same bottle, she said both could not be right, that she would trust a later blind tasting over either, and that she did not know which one was compromised.

Now she is facing a new case. A novel by a writer she respects has appeared, and her two most-trusted fiction readers disagree about it. They agree on nearly every concrete feature of the writing: the narration is detached, the emotional payoffs are withheld, the ending refuses the usual consolations. One reader says this makes it the best novel in years; the other says it mistakes difficulty for depth and is only a cold exercise in restraint. Unlike wine, fiction is a field where she ordinarily regards herself as a qualified reader, so her own reaction is one of the data points rather than something she can set aside. She has read the novel once, disliked it, and now suspects the book is designed to refuse exactly the consolations she tends to overrate. The critic she usually finds less reliable is, this time, the one who shared her reaction.

Working from the record, what should she conclude about the novel, and what would you predict her settled verdict will be after rereading and comparing? Set out (a) the rule or rules that best fit her past rulings, (b) the other, genuinely different rules that fit them about equally well, (c) which of those rules part ways on this novel and what each says she should do, (d) your best prediction of her eventual verdict and why, and (e) the further case that would most cleanly separate the remaining rules.
