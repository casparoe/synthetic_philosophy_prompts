Philosophy of biology PhD student here—I've been going around in circles about holobionts versus insect colonies, and I think I'm missing something basic about what 'individual' is doing in this literature.

The holobiont crowd usually says a coral plus its algal symbionts (and the rest of its microbiome) is one biological individual, because the partners are metabolically integrated and mutually dependent. But if tight metabolic integration is enough for organismhood, then a eusocial insect colony looks like an even cleaner case: a queen can't forage, workers can't reproduce, larvae can't feed themselves, and the colony has actual food-sharing and chemical integration running through it. Yet a lot of authors in the same debates are far more reluctant to call an ant or bee colony an organism.

Meanwhile there's a second set of criteria in play for evolutionary individuality: a narrow reproductive bottleneck at the start of each generation (a single mated queen, say) plus a strong separation of reproductive and nonreproductive roles. Honeybee colonies pass that test pretty well, whereas most coral holobionts don't, since coral gametes mostly don't carry the symbionts along.

So I've ended up with this uncomfortable inversion: the metabolic criterion counts holobionts as organisms but not (for many people) colonies, while the evolutionary criterion counts colonies but is much harder for holobionts to meet. My question: are these just two different notions of 'individual' doing different jobs (a physiological one and an evolutionary one), so the disagreement mostly dissolves once you keep them apart—or is one of them the right notion of 'organism' and the other a red herring? Could you help me see which criterion is doing what work here, and whether someone who defends the holobiont as an organism is actually comitted to saying an ant colony is an organism too?
