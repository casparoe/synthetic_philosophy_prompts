I'm a PhD student in cognitive science and I've accidently built an argument that now scares me a little. It started as a throwaway line for a workshop on planning and improvisation, but I wrote it out and now can't find the step that breaks.

(1) The whole point of planning is to improve decisions by anticipating how the future will unfold.
(2) You can anticipate the future only if it is regular enough to forecast.
(3) In exactly the situations where planning looks most necessary — a startup pivoting, a military campaign, a first-time surgery — the situation is novel and not regular enough to forecast.
(4) So planning is least effective precisely where it looks most necessary.
(5) In routine situations, planning is unnecessary for the opposite reason: you already know what to do, so a habit or checklist does the same job with less overhead.
(6) So planning is either ineffective or redundant.
(7) Therefore we should stop treating planning as a skill and train improvisation instead.

The conclusion feels absurd — nobody wants an air traffic controller to just wing it — but each premise looked right when I typed it. I'd most like to keep (3), since it is the one I have real evidence for. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
