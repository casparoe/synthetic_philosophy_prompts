So there's this thing the psychology people do where they hand someone a questionaire asking them to picture, like, a familiar face or a sunset, and rate how vivid the image in their head is on a 1 to 5 scale. Based mostly on that, they sort people into buckets: aphantasia if you report no picture at all, hyperphantasia if you report it bright and lifelike, normal in between. And then they go write papers about aphantasia as a condition, differences in working memory, all of that.

I want to know what theory of mental imagery this practice is smuggling in. It seems to treat the image as a kind of picture-like object with a vividness dial that you can just introspect and report on. But there's a whole camp in the old Kosslyn-vs-Pylyshyn fight that says that's basically a user illusion: what's really there is something more like a description or a data structure, and asking how vivid the picture is is a bit like asking how many pixels are in the taste of salt. Does the questionnaire practice actually require the picture-view, or could a description-view person sign off on it as a useful behavioral sorting tool while still saying the introspective reports are confabulated? And if the description-view wins, what should aphantasia research look like instead — do we have to redescribe the condition as a reporting difference, or is there a version where aphantasia is real but just isn't about picture vividness? Also, am I wrong to think the field is being inconsistent when it treats aphantasia as a stable individual difference while also noting that some people only learn they're aphantasic when they realize other people weren't speaking metaphorically?
