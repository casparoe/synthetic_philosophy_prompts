My officemate has this argument that thought experiments are never evidence, only intuition pumps, and I can't find the bad step but the conclusion has to be wrong. Help me figure out where it breaks.

(1) In a thought experiment, the case is just whatever the person describing it stipulates; there is no independent fact about how the imagined situation would actually go. (2) So any verdict I reach about the case is determined either by those stipulations or by background principles I already bring to the table. (3) If the verdict comes from the stipulations, it's just restating the description — illustration, not evidence for anything beyond it. (4) If it comes from my background principles, then using the verdict as evidence for those very principles is circular; at most I'm learning something about my own psychology. (5) So thought experiments can't serve as evidence; they're intuition pumps that maybe have rhetorical or pedagogical value. (6) But then Gettier cases never gave us reason to abandon the JTB analysis of knowledge, trolley cases never tested a moral theory, and things like Maxwell's demon or Einstein's elevator did no evidential work in physics — a huge chunck of philosophy and some science turns out to be epistemically hollow.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd like to keep (2) and (6) if possible, but I'm open to the possibility that one of those is the real culprit.
