In my Eastern philosophy unit, we’ve been studying Nāgārjuna’s Madhyamaka, and I keep getting stuck on the tetralemma. As I understand it, for any subject matter—a table, a self, causality—Nāgārjuna examines four positions: that it exists, that it does not exist, that it both exists and does not exist, and that it neither exists nor does not exist. He then appears to reject all four. But when I try to formalize this, the list looks exhaustive. If I treat “the table exists” as a proposition, the available options seem to be that the proposition is true, false, both, or neither, so denying every option appears to leave no remainder except the conclusion that nothing whatsoever exists. Yet my teacher insists that Madhyamaka emptiness is not nihilism and that my reading of the tetralemma is a beginner’s mistake.

Could you pinpoint exactly where my reasoning goes wrong, and explain what the tetralemma is actually doing if it is not assigning truth-values to four exhaustive alternatives? Please keep your answer under 300 words.
