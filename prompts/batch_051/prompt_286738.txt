I sit on the grant committee of a foundation that funds work in global poverty and public health, and I have been unable to shake a tension in how we conduct ourselves. Our published strategy describes poverty as the product of structural forces: weak institutions, captured regulators, social norms that keep children out of school, markets that exclude the poor. But when we actually allocate money, our behaviour runs in the opposite direction. Programmes that distribute bed nets, deworm children or transfer cash are treated as the gold standard, because a randomised trial can isolate their effect and attach a cost to each measured unit of benefit. Programmes that would train civil servants, reform procurement, build legal aid capacity or try to shift a norm are judged by vaguer criteria, described as speculative, and funded only as small pilots, if at all. We talk like systems thinkers and spend as though poverty were a collection of discrete, repairable defects.

What view of social causation, and of what it means for a grant to do good, is presupposed by a practice that requires effects to be isolable and attributable before serious money is committed? Reconstruct the implicit theory of change as precisely as you can, separating the commitments the practice strictly requires from those it merely inclines toward for reasons of measurement, attribution or accountability. Tell me whether the systems rhetoric and the funding behaviour are coherent together, where they conflict, and which parts of the practice are genuinely underdetermined such that more than one philosophical view would license them. If we replaced the presupposed picture with the view that the causes of poverty interact as a system and that the salient unit of assessment is the functioning of the whole, what would actually have to change in how we decide which grants to make? Please answer in continuous prose, as you would in a letter or email, with no headings, bullet points, bold text or tables.
