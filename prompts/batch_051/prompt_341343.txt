I retired a couple years ago from a long stint in experimental physics, and I've lately been trying to get clear on how philosophers think about causation. My lab instincts say a cause is something you can wiggle while holding the rest fixed and watch the effect change, so I'm sympathtic to the interventionist story. But I'm trying to understand where the simpler counterfactual/but-for test goes wrong: C caused E iff, if C hadn't happened, E wouldn't have happened.

So far I've collected the obvious counterexamples where some backup messes up the test. I have early preemption (Suzy's rock shatters the bottle; Billy would have thrown if she hadn't, but he never throws), late preemption (Billy throws too, but his rock arrives only after Suzy's has already broken the bottle), and symmetric overdetermination (two shooters fire at once, each bullet sufficient by itself). These all trade on the same underlying problem: the effect doesn't counterfactually depend on the actual cause because something else was waiting in the wings.

Give me one more difficulty for the plain counterfactual test that is not just another backup or redundancy variant. Ideally it would be a failure in the opposit direction—where the test manufactures a cause that isn't really one, or gets the relata wrong—or a case where the interventionist approach also struggles even though the counterfactual test gets it right. Say what the case is, why it's genuinely different from the three I've listed, and how the two approaches would deal with it.
