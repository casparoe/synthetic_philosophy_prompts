Two friends of mine started arguing about a tiny game, and the argument quickly outgrew the game. The game is this: two players simultaneously pick Up or Down, without talking. If both pick Up, each gets $10; if both pick Down, each gets $1; if they mismatch, both get $0. The payoffs and both players' rationality are common knowledge between the players. Sasha's line was that rational players go Up: since nobody can expect the other to make a mistake, and Down only pays off if the other also picks Down, rational players should coordinate on the mutually best outcome. Dev said that's too fast. Down is a best reply to the belief that the other person will pick Down ($1 beats $0), so Down-Down is self-consistent; common knowledge of rationality rules out choices that would be worse no matter what the other does, but it doesn't force both players to have the same beliefs.

Sasha answered that Down-Down is just a bad fixed point. If you run the iterated "what would a rational person believe?" reasoning, the only beliefs that survive are ones anchored in the game itself, and nothing in the game anchors the belief that the other will pick Down; it has to be imported as a free-floating hunch. Two rational people with the same information and a uniquely best shared outcome shouldn't need hunches. Dev said the iterated reasoning was question-begging: the Down chain ("if she thinks I'll play Down, she'll play Down; if I think she thinks I'll play Down, I'll play Down") closes just as tightly as the Up chain. Common knowledge of rationality is compatible with both. If Sasha wants Up to be mandatory, she's smuggling in something stronger, like team reasoning — in a symmetric common-interest game, do the action whose universal adoption is best. That may be a good principle, but it isn't individual best-reply rationality. Sasha said maybe that's right, but then individual best-reply rationality is too weak to be a theory of what to do; a standard that lets two perfectly rational people leave $10 each on the table and take $1 each just because "each expected the other to go Down" has lost sight of what rationality is for. Dev replied that "what rationality is for" was doing all the work: once you replace best-reply with selection of a mutually best scheme, you've changed the subject — maybe for the better, but it has to be defended on its own terms.

I couldn't tell whether one of them had made a simple mistake or whether each was right about a different thing and they were talking past each other. Please go through the argument move by move and say who's right about what, where each overreaches, and what both of them are missing.
