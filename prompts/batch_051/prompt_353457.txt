I have been trying to get a clear picture of what is actually responsible for the logical force of liar-style paradoxes. I understand the basic liar sentence: a sentence that says of itself that it is false leads to trouble because it seems true if false and false if true, and people usually blame self-reference. I also ran into a sentence that says, roughly, if this very sentence is true, then the moon is made of green cheese. The troubling thing is that, using only ordinary rules for conditionals, one can apparently reach the conclusion that the moon is made of green cheese without assuming anything else. I would like an intuitive explanation of why this happens, not just a formal proof: which step in the reasoning is doing the strange work, and how it differs from the liar.

But the part I keep getting stuck on is a construction I later saw, attributed to Stephen Yablo, where one considers an infinite list of sentences: sentence one says that every sentence after it is false, sentence two says that every sentence after it is false, and so on. Each sentence only points to sentences later in the list, so at first glance none is about itself, yet there is still no consistent assignment of truth and falsity. I had assumed the moral of these paradoxes was that self-reference is the culprit, but a friend told me Yablo's construction complicates that moral. I would like the explanation to walk through the two cases side by side and isolate what logical principles are doing the work in each: for example, the principle that lets us infer a conditional if we can derive its consequent from its antecedent, or the principle that lets us move from a conditional and its antecedent to its consequent. Does Yablo's construction really avoid self-reference in the way it seems to, and if so, what should I conclude about the usual claim that self-reference is the source of the problem? Please write your answer in American English.
