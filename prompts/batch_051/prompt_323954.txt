I'm a software engineer and I keep coming back to a passage from John Searle's essay "Minds, Brains, and Programs" (1980):

> Now suppose further that after this first batch of Chinese writing I am given a second batch of Chinese script together with a set of rules for correlating the second batch with the first batch. The rules are in English, and I understand these rules as well as any other native speaker of English. They enable me to correlate one set of formal symbols with another set of formal symbols, and all that "formal" means here is that I can identify the symbols entirely by their shapes.

I noticed a parallel I don't know how seriously to take. In distributed systems work, we distinguish syntactic interoperability from semantic interoperability: two services can parse each other's messages, validate them against a shared schema, and pass every contract test, yet still disagree about what the messages mean—whether "price" includes tax, whether "status": "shipped" commits the sender to a refund, whether an "order" resource is the same thing on both sides. The contract test only checks that the bytes conform to shapes; it does not establish that the two systems attach the same meaning to those shapes.

This looks nearly identical to Searle's point that the room's instructions let him correlate symbols entirely by their shapes while he remains unable to understand Chinese. But here is where I get stuck. In a real pipeline, the service's outputs are not just shapes: they write to a database, trigger a payment, send an email, or block a login. A strong-AI defender could say the messages do mean something because of their causal role in that larger system, even if no single service understands them. My engineering intuition says causal role in a pipeline still does not amount to understanding, but I can't tell whether that intuition is doing any work.

Is this parallel between Searle's formal/semantic distinction and syntactic/semantic interoperability a deep structural identity, or is it superficial? Specifically, does the Chinese room argument imply that a service which passes contract tests but only manipulates schematized payloads cannot be said to understand the domain, and if so, would adding side effects to the service break the analogy or just relocate the same problem? Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
