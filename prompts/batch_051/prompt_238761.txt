In a philosophy of mind course I'm taking, we've been working through the problem of self-deception, and I've realized that I already follow a policy about my own beliefs that the course has made me uneasy about. The policy is this: when I notice that one of my beliefs is doing convenient work for me—protecting my self-image, excusing a failure, or making some course of action I already prefer seem more reasonable—I raise the standard of proof for that belief. Before I allow it to guide what I say or do, I have to be able to write out the argument for it in a form that I would accept if it came from someone whose interests ran the other way. If I can't, I treat the belief as suspended: I don't assert it, and I try not to reason from it, even when I still feel sure it's true. I applied the rule recently to my belief that an unfair grading rubric caused my poor exam performance, and I suspended the belief even though the feeling of having been wronged hasn't gone away.

My worry is that the rule seems to presuppose a picture of self-deception as a single mind playing two roles: one part of me knows the truth and deliberately keeps another part from it. That is exactly the picture the course readings have been making look hard to sustain, since ordinary deception appears to require the deceiver to know that p is false and intentionally produce a false belief in the victim—two things it is hard to locate when the deceiver and the victim are the same person. My question: is my policy rationally defensible even if that picture turns out to be wrong? Is there an account of self-deception as motivationally biased belief-formation, with no knowing inner deceiver, on which my asymmetric standard is a sensible precaution rather than a bias against any belief that happens to flatter me? And if genuine self-deception turns out to be impossible, is there a weaker epistemic failing the rule is actually tracking—and should the rule be restated, narrowed, or abandoned?
