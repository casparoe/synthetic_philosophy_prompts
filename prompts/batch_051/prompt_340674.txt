Okay, here's a setup I've been turning over. A developer buys a big tract of land in a poor-ish country and cuts a deal with the host government: inside the boundary the developer owns the land outright, most national taxes are waived, and the developer's leases and arbitration clauses are directly enforceable in the national courts. The city is run like this. Every resident leases their home, and any business premises, from the developer on a 99-year term. In return for a single monthly "governance fee" they recieve policing, courts run as private arbitration, fire services, roads, and not much else. There's an elected residents' council, but its powers are deliberately narrow: it can vote on service levels, like how many patrols there are or how often the bins get collected, and any change that would push the fee above the contractual formula needs a two-thirds majority plus the developer's sign-off. The residents' bill of rights is just a schedule in the lease. The developer can amend it at 30 days' notice. When that notice goes out, any resident who leaves within the window gets the unexpired part of their ground rent back in full and is released from the lease; anyone who stays is legally treated as having consented to the amendment. Residents can sell their leasehold at market price, but a business owner who moves away has 90 days to sell up or keep paying the fee.

The marketing line is that you can leave whenever you like, so staying is basically consent. I want you to take the mechanism apart, not rule on whether private cities are legitimate in the abstract. What does this set of rules actually incentivise? Does the exit clause discipline the developer, or does it do the opposite — letting him float changes that the most mobile and switched-on residents will flee from, leaving a rump of people too stuck, too busy, or too incurious to object, and then treating their staying as consent? How would people game the 30-day window, the consent-by-staying rule, or the council's tiny remit? What happens to someone with school-age kids, or a job that doesn't transfer, or a business that will take more than 90 days to wind down? And where does the host government fit in — what is its incentive to keep its side of the bargain once the city starts making real money? Would changing the notice period, the refund formula, or the council's powers fix the failure modes, or just move them around? At what point does this stop being a genuine exit option and turn into a machine for converting lock-in into consent?

Oh, and write the answer in American English, please.
