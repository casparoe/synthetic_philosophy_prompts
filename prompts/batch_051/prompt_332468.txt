I've been going round in circles with a mate about this, and I'd like a proper answer. There's a pattern in how people talk about behavioural genetics that I can't tell if its a real position or just a slip. When a study reports that a trait—reading ability, say—is highly heritable within a population, the conversational takeaway is fatalistic: it's mostly genetic, so there's not much point intervening, don't blame the school, temper your expectations. But when a study reports that a trait is mostly environmental, the takeaway is exactly the reverse: it's malleable, so we should intervene, change the environment, raise expectations. The two claims are numerically symmetric—if reading difficulty is 60% heritable it's 40% environmental—yet the practical conclusions run in opposite directions. You see it in practice too: a school might cut a reading programme because reading problems are 'mostly genetic', but nobody would cut school meals because height is 'mostly genetic', even though that is the same form of inference.

What I want to know is wether anything actually justifies the asymmetry. Heritability is a population statistic: it says how much of the variation in a population is associated with genetic variation, not how fixed a trait is in a given person, and it says nothing about whether a different environment would shift the whole distribution. Height is highly heritable, but average height rose a lot over the last century with better nutrition. Phenylketonuria is about as genetic as a condition gets, but the serious damage is prevented by changing the diet. So why do we hear 'it's heritable' as hands-off but 'it's environmental' as hands-on? Give me the strongest principle you can find that would keep the asymmetry, test it against cases like those two, and tell me honestly whether it survives or should be given up.
