I've been going in circles over Euclid's defintions of unit and number and I need a second pair of eyes. Here's an argument I can't shake; tell me whether its valid and, if not, exactly which step fails and how you'd rewrite that step.

(1) Euclid says a unit is what makes each existing thing be called one — that in virtue of which anything counts as one.
(2) He also says a number is a multitude made up of units.
(3) Now if the unit is what makes each one thing one, then it has to be one and the same unit in every one thing; otherwise being one wouldn't be a single way of being.
(4) Take the number two. By (2), it's a multitude made up of two units.
(5) By (3), those two units are the very same unit, just taken twice.
(6) But the very same unit taken twice is still just one unit, so two would be a multitude made up of one unit.
(7) So either two isn't made up of two units, or Euclid's definitions make number impossible. Since both options look crazy, I must have pulled a fast one somewhere.

It feels like the trick has something to do with what 'unit' means, but I can't make it precise. Help me pin down exactly where it goes wrong and give the corrected version of that step — or, if it's sound, tell me why the conclusion is less crazy than it looks.
