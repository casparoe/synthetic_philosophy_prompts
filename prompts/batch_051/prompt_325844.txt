Suppose a mid-size city converts its building-permit office to what the new director calls Han Feizi administration. Out go the ethics trainings, the mission statements, the appeals to public service. Everything runs on explicit payouts. A permit must be decided within ten business days. If an official approves a permit that later gets overturned for a code violation, he loses a bonus worth two months salary; if he denies one that gets reversed on appeal, same penalty. If his denial is upheld on appeal, or if his approval survives a random audit, he collects a small bonus. Any colleague or member of the public can file a confidential report about a hidden violation in an approved permit, and if the report leads to a fine on the builder, the reporter gets ten percent of the fine. The director, following the old advice that a ruler should not reveal his likes and dislikes, never says which outcomes he privately cares about beyond the posted rules, and every quarter he changes the bonus weights without explanation. Analyze how this would actually run. What does the system select for, how do the people at the desk game it, what does the reporting channel do to trust among the staff, how much does the director's refusal to reveal preferences matter, and can the failure modes be fixed without quietly smuggling virtue back in? Answer in American English.
