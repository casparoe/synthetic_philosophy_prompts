In the novel I'm writing there's an artists' colony in an old coastal fort, maybe forty people, and I want the place to run on a kind of local karma instead of money. The mechanism I've been playing with goes like this. Every member has a public balance of a thing I'm calling "regard." At a weekly meeting, any member can sponsor a named piece of someone else's work or a specific act of help; sponsoring costs the giver ten units of regard and the recipient recieves eight, the other two are burned. Everyone starts with one hundred units, balances decay four percent a month, and at the start of each season the people with the highest balances get first pick of the studios, the darkroom, the boat, and the one travel grant. You can't sponsor a spouse, a partner, or anyone you've sponsored in the last month; new members also start at one hundred, and when someone leaves their balance disappears with them.

I'm less interested in whether the thing is fair than in how it would actually behave over a few years of messy human use. Who ends up rich in regard and who ends up broke? Does the burn make generosity too expensive so that everyone hoards, or does it make sponsorship a credible signal? Do people game it with back-scratching rings, chains of newbies, or the spouse rule? Does tying each sponsorship to a named act matter, or does the balance just turn into a class system? Where would it break first, and is there any small patch that fixes the worst failure without turning the colony into a bureaucracy?
