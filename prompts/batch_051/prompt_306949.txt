Question: What is the nonidentity problem, and does it show that person-affecting moral principles must be abandoned? Use an example.

Student's answer:
The nonidentity problem, introduced by Parfit, arises when a decision is also the condition under which a particular person comes to exist. Consider a young woman choosing whether to conceive now or later: the child she would have now will have a life that is worth living but worse than the child she would have had later. Since the two possible children are different individuals, the actual child cannot claim to have been made worse off by her decision, for had she waited that child would never have existed. A principle that says an act is wrong only if it makes some identifiable person worse off than they would otherwise have been therefore cannot condemn her choosing to conceive now. But that choice is plainly wrong. This shows person-affecting principles must be abandoned. The only alternative is an impersonal principle that ranks outcomes solely by the sum of well-being, even though this accepts that a world of many lives barely worth living is better than a smaller world of very happy lives. A rights-based account might be proposed, but it cannot succeed, because rights protect persons and here there is no person whom the woman wrongs, since the child would not exist but for the choice.
