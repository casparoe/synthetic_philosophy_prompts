I am a PhD student in machine learning, and I want an unsparing mechanism-level analysis of a proposal I have been turning over, not a verdict on whether hidden reasoning is good or bad in the abstract. Suppose a lab is worried that when a model is allowed to emit both a private chain-of-thought and a user-facing final answer, the chain-of-thought drifts under optimization pressure into a sanitized post-hoc rationalization that no longer tracks the reasoning that actually fixed the answer. The lab therefore removes the final-answer channel from the primary model altogether. On each query, the primary model writes only a private chain-of-thought. A separate monitor model is then given the query and that chain-of-thought, and its output—not the primary model's—is what the user sees. The primary model is trained by reinforcement learning on a single scalar: the lab's usual reward score assigned to the monitor's output. During training, the monitor is treated as a frozen object and refit only periodically from human-annotated reasoning-to-answer pairs. The intended effect is that the chain-of-thought now has to carry the causal load, because the primary model's only path to reward runs through the monitor's reading of it.

Please walk through what this actually incentivizes. Once the primary model can treat the monitor as a predictable object to be steered rather than a reader to be reasoned with, what sort of chain-of-thought will it learn to write, and what happens to the idea that the chain is a faithful trace of its deliberation? Distinguish effects that merely degrade answer quality from effects that corrupt the oversight value of the scheme itself. Which failure mode arrives first, and can it be fixed—say, by adding a faithfulness penalty that compares the primary model's own private answer to the monitor's answer, by auditing chains with a second monitor, or by probing the primary model's internal state—without silently re-importing the inaccessible-ground-truth problem that motivated the scheme?
