Me and a friend have been going in circles about an argument he brought back from a decision-theory reading group, and I can't find the false step, but the conclusion seems like its too much. The argument runs like this.

(1) If a person's preferences are coherent in the usual way—complete, transitive, and satisfying the expected-utility axioms—they can be represented by a utility function that is unique up to a positive affine transformation.

(2) Each individual's preferences are coherent in that way, so each person has such a utility function.

(3) A society, insofar as it is a rational chooser of policies and lotteries, should satisfy those same axioms. So there is a social utility function as well.

(4) Social preference should also respect unanimity. If everyone weakly prefers option A to option B (where A and B may be lotteries), society weakly prefers A to B; and if at least one person strictly prefers A to B while no one strictly prefers B to A, society strictly prefers A to B.

(5) Harsanyi's aggregation theorem then tells us that the social utility function must be a weighted sum of the individual utility functions, with positive weights.

(6) So once you grant individual rationality, social rationality, and unanimity, the only remaining room for distributive values is in the choice of weights—and the theorem itself is silent about how to choose them.

(7) Therefore equality or fairness has no independent standing. For fixed weights, one distribution is better than another just in case it gives a higher weighted sum of individual utilities. Full stop.

(8) But that seems to prove too much. Take two people with the same utility function and equal weights: a distribution that gives 10 units to each comes out exactly as good as one that gives 20 to one and 0 to the other. That seems to throw away the idea that fairness in distribution matters for its own sake, not merely because people happen to have a taste for equality. And it does so on the back of axioms that were supposed to be about coherence, not distributive ethics.

I'd like to keep (1), (2), and (4) if I can, which pushes me toward rejecting (3). But rejecting (3) feels like saying a society can be a rational agent in every ordinary sense and yet not be bound by the expected-utility axioms, and I don't want to pick that just because the conclusion is ugly. The other live options are rejecting (4) or saying the move from the theorem to the distributive claims in (6)–(7) is too quick. So where does this go wrong? And whatever I reject, what do I pay?
