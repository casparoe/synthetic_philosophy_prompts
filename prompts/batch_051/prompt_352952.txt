I'm currently taking a philosophy of religion course, and we've been working through the debate over the afterlife: the traditional view of eternal conscious torment, annihilationism, and universal salvation. I have found that my reactions to particular cases are firmer than my commitment to any general theory, so I would like to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

Case A. A seventy-year-old man runs a trafficking network for decades and dies unrepentant, having caused hundreds of people great harm. My verdict: I cannot accept that the fitting outcome is endless conscious torment, but I also cannot accept that he simply passes out of existence with nothing further owed. I want a real reckoning — yet a finite one.

Case B. A nineteen-year-old commits a single impulsive armed robbery. The victim dies of a heart attack during it, and the robber is killed in the getaway. My verdict: eternal conscious torment would be absurd. I am fairly confident annihilation would also be too much; what I most want is for whatever comes after death to leave open real repair, in him and toward the victim.

Case C. A man lives a mostly decent life — faithful spouse, adequate parent, honest at work — but is selfish in countless small ways, never owns his failures, nurses grudges, and dies at seventy without ever taking himself to account. My verdict: neither eternal torment nor annihilation; this seems like ordinary material for purgation rather than damnation.

Case D. A person has a clear understanding of what the Christian tradition says God offers and commands, yet refuses it for decades — not out of confusion or trauma but out of considered contempt. He declares that he wants nothing to do with God and would rather be left alone forever. My verdict: I think it would be wrong for God simply to override this and make him enter communion. I can even accept an irreversible consequence here, though I keep wanting to describe it as self-exclusion rather than inflicted pain.

Case E. The same as D, except that the refusal is the product of abuse and manipulative religious teaching: this person has only ever met God through coercion and fear. My verdict: no final rejection has actually occurred. I would expect whatever God is actually like to remain available after death, and I consider neither torment nor annihilation fitting.

Case F. A man is responsible for the deaths of many thousands and dies before any earthly reckoning, but in his last weeks he undergoes what appears to be a genuine change of heart — not mere fear of death but horror at what he did and a wish to make amends. My verdict: reconciliation should remain possible, but he should not simply be welcomed into bliss as if nothing happened; whatever is done must somehow run through the victims.

The pressure point is between A and D. In A, my resistance is retributive: wrongdoing committed in a finite life cannot earn an infinite penalty. In D, I am willing to let a person's final, clear-eyed refusal have irreversible and possibly endless consequences, even though that person's overt wrongs are less than those in A. Can one principle hold those two verdicts together, or is one of them doing illicit work? The three familiar positions — eternal conscious torment, annihilationism, and universal salvation — each seem to capture only part of the pattern. Please state the candidate principle or principles as precisely as you can; say which of my verdicts each cannot accommodate; advise me which verdicts I should revise and why; and then apply the resulting view to a further case I have not listed: a person who lives a decent, generous, but nonreligious life and dies without ever having seriously confronted the question.

Please answer in American English.
