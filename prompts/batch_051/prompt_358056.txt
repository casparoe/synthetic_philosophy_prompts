I write about food, science, and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and while reporting a piece on plant-based diets and food security in low-income countries I kept coming back to an argument I finally had to write out in full. Each step seemed plausible during my interviews, but the chain lands in a contradiction I cannot accept. It runs as follows.

(1) If buying or eating animal products is wrong, it is wrong because the person knowingly participates in unnecessary animal suffering. (2) The suffering an animal undergoes in an intensive production system is the same no matter who ends up consuming the product; the consumer's location or income does not change what happened to the animal. (3) What does vary is how far the consumer could have avoided participating: a person in a wealthy city with ample plant-based options can avoid animal products without serious loss, while a pastoralist whose land cannot support crops, a day labourer in a food desert where the only affordable complete proteins are animal foods, or a person with severe legume and nut allergies may have no practicable alternative. (4) But the absence of an alternative can excuse a person, or show that she is not blameworthy; it cannot make the act itself right. Wrongness and blame are two different questions. (5) So the person who cannot avoid consuming animal products still acts wrongly when she consumes them, even though she acts under necessity; by parity of reasoning, a subsistence herder does something wrong every time she feeds her family. (6) Yet an act that is wrong is an act one ought not to perform, and ought implies can. (7) By the description of the case, the herder cannot do otherwise without serious harm. (8) Therefore the herder's act both is and is not something she ought not to perform, which is a contradiction.

I would most like to keep two convictions: that a genuine moral principle about animal suffering still applies to people for whom compliance is costly or difficult, and that ought implies can. The argument seems to show that I cannot keep both while also maintaining that the wealthy consumer's purchase is wrong. Please tell me exactly where the argument goes wrong, if it does; why the offending step looked like common sense; and what a defender of a universal principle against unnecessary animal suffering must pay to reject that step rather than accept the conclusion. Write your answer as continuous prose, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold type, and no tables, the way you would write a letter to a colleague.
