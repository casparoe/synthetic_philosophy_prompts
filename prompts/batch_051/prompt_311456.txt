At a friend's birthday dinner, two guests argued about a specific repeated bet and eventually asked me to referee. I wrote down their exchange as accurately as I could.

The bet is this: each round you stake an amount, and a fair coin is flipped. On heads you get back 1.5 times what you staked; on tails you get back 0.6 times what you staked. The expected per-round multiplier is (1.5 + 0.6)/2 = 1.05, so the bet has a positive expected return of 5% per round.

(1) Maya said: "A rational person with a sufficiently long horizon should take this bet as often as possible and stake everything. Positive expected value is positive expected value. Over many rounds the good luck and bad luck cancel, and your average outcome converges to the expected one — 5% growth per round. Refusing a positive-expectation bet is simply irrational."

(2) Ravi said: "I wouldn't touch it. You don't live in the ensemble of parallel possible histories; you live out one trajectory. If you stake everything, a head followed by a tail — or the reverse — leaves you with 1.5 × 0.6 = 0.9 of your stake, down 10%. The geometric mean multiplier is sqrt(1.5 × 0.6) ≈ 0.949, which is below 1, so over many rounds a typical trajectory shrinks by about 5% each round, not grows. The ensemble average is positive only because a handful of astronomically lucky paths pull the average up."

(3) Maya: "That's confusing the average with the typical outcome, and I never claimed the typical outcome was good. The expected value is still the right decision rule. With many repetitions the law of large numbers applies: your realized average multiplier converges to the expected multiplier of 1.05. So the long horizon is exactly what makes the expectation vindicated."

(4) Ravi: "No — the law of large numbers applies to sums, but wealth compounds as a product. What converges over time is the average of the logarithms of the multipliers, and that average is half log 1.5 plus half log 0.6, which is negative. Exponentiating gives the geometric mean of about 0.949. So if you stake everything every round, your wealth converges to zero with probability one even though expected wealth grows without bound. A policy can have enormous expected value and still be financial ruin for the person actually living it."

(5) Maya: "Then all you've shown is that betting everything is reckless. The relevant conclusion is about stakes or risk aversion, not about expected value as the right standard. Your geometric-mean rule just is a commitment to maximizing expected log wealth — one particular utility function, smuggled in as if it were mandatory. A genuinely risk-neutral agent maximizes expected wealth and is coherent in doing so. Besides, for a one-shot version of the bet, or for a large pool of people diversifying across the bets, the ensemble average is realized and the time-average objection loses its force."

(6) Ravi: "The reason expectation has normative force in the first place is that it captures the consequences of your choices for you, the chooser, across the events you might actually experience. For a non-ergodic multiplicative process, the ensemble average stops doing that; it counts states that no single trajectory visits. Diversifying, or taking the bet only once, doesn't save your principle — it changes the setup. And I'd say a criterion that endorses a policy that ruins you with probability one, merely because it has a fantastically high expectation on the upper tail, is not a criterion we are rationally required to accept."
