I've been mulling something over for a while and I'd like another head on it. I tried to write myself a little test for what counts as a lie. The test has three parts: (1) the person says something they believe is false, (2) they want the other person to end up believing it, and (3) they do it in a situation where people normally expect a straight, truthful answer.

Now here's the case that keeps breaking my test. I lived in Japan for a few years and now I'm living in the Netherlands. In Japan, a colleague would tell a client "we'll do our best" or "that might be difficult" when everybody in the room, including the client, knew the real answer was no. The client seemd to take it as a no and nobody got confused. In the Netherlands, the same colleague would probably just say "no, we won't do that," with no scenery around it. Both get the no across. But on my three-part test, the Japanese version is strange: "that might be difficult" is vague enough that it isn't exactly false; the speaker isn't trying to make the client believe it the way a con artist would; and the client knows the local rules, so maybe condition (3) doesn't apply. Yet the client is still being steered away from hearing the thing said plainly.

So my question: by my own test, is the polite Japanese refusal a lie? Go through the three parts one by one and tell me where it lands and why. Is the Dutch bluntness more honest, or is it just a different set of rules that happens to look honest from the outside? And then there is the thing that actually bothers me: a white lie, like saying "I love it" about a gift you hate. That one passes all three parts of my test as a lie, but it feels less wrong to me than the Japanese refusal, even though the refusal fails my test. Does that mean my test is picking out some narrow feature of the words and missing the part we actually care about, which is being misled or handled? I don't just want the names for these styles; I want someone to take the test seriously and tell me whether the test itself is any good.
