Construct a thought experiment in which, for the same offense, a penal system organized wholly around rehabilitation detains an offender for longer than a retributivist sentencing scheme would detain that offender. The case is for a discussion of whether the humane reputation of rehabilitation-based systems is a necessary truth or a contingent feature of how such systems are currently run, so a case that simply stipulates the verdict rather than deriving it from the two theories' own release criteria fails the assignment. Constraints: one paragraph; realistic, using ordinary parole or release-on-license procedures, with no corrupt, sadistic, or incompetent staff and no exotic or gerrymandered offense; and the extra detention must be produced by the rehabilitative system's core release criterion—for example, release only after treatment completion or once predicted reoffense risk falls below a fixed threshold—not by an avoidable design flaw in a particular program. After the scenario, walk through the reasoning each scheme uses to arrive at its release date, identify the single feature of the case that is doing the inversion work, and explain what would happen to the case if that feature were removed or reformed. Then give the strongest reply available to a thoughtful defender of rehabilitation, and say whether that reply rescues the general view or only defeats this one example.
