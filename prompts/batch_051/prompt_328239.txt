I'm giving a 40-minute talk next month at an interdisciplinary workshop on rational choice, and I'd like a thorough Q&A prep sheet. I'll be defending a version of Quinn's self-torturer: the claim that a rational agent can have a preference ordering that violates transitivity, and that this need not be explained away as confusion, vagueness, changing tastes, or an artifact of the thought experiment. I'll set up the case roughly as follows: an agent wears a device whose dial runs from 0 to 1000; the increase in discomfort between each adjacent setting is, on its own, imperceptible; at setting 0 he is comfortable and at setting 1000 he is in severe, unremitting pain that he would pay heavily to avoid. For each one-step advance from n to n+1 he is offered a modest fixed payment, and at each stage he prefers taking the payment and advancing to staying put; nevertheless, he would never accept a single offer that moved him straight from 0 to 1000 for the same total payment, and in fact his preference between the two endpoints runs the other way. By transitivity, the stepwise preferences should commit him to preferring setting 1000 with the accumulated payment to setting 0 with nothing; he does not. I'll argue that the self-torturer's stepwise choices are rational all the way through, that the right lesson is to surrender transitivity as a universal requirement on practical preference, and that this concession does not license any obvious disaster once we carefully distinguish the permissibility of a series of local choices from the advisability of the whole sequence.

The audience is mostly economists and decision theorists working on revealed preference, dynamic consistency, and money-pump arguments, plus two philosophers whose work concerns vagueness and practical rationality. I expect the economists to treat transitivity as non-negotiable and to reach immediately for exploitative cycling arguments; I expect the philosophers to reach for a sorites-style diagnosis. Please draft the prep sheet for me: the objections in roughly the order I'll actually hear them, phrased the way this specific audience would phrase them, with a note on who in the room is most likely to raise each one. For each objection, tell me what I should concede up front, what I should hold the line on, and a two-minute reply, and flag where that reply is softest.

Make sure the prep sheet addresses at least these pressure points: the classic money pump (if he will move from n to n+1 for a payment each time, a trader can drive him around a cycle and extract an arbitrary amount of money from him); the charge that the puzzle is only vagueness in "no noticeable worsening" and that there is simply no sharp threshold, so no real intransitivity in any precise preference relation; the claim that on the revealed-preference interpretation transitivity is built into the concept of preference and therefore cannot fail for a real chooser; and the worry that my view rationalizes self-exploitation or akrasia because it lets an agent approve each move while deploring the endpoint. End with the question I most hope nobody asks, and tell me why it would hurt more than the money pump.
