I am a novelist, and the question I want to put to you comes out of the writing life rather than out of any philosophical reading. The novel I am drafting turns on a woman whose childhood pastime becomes her profession and whose leisure is slowly re-described as content. I had assumed I would be able to give her either a clean conscience or a clean tragedy. Instead, the more I try to imagine her honestly, the more I notice that my own judgements about when play is preserved and when it is corrupted are firm and particular, while the principle behind them escapes me, and I would like to know whether one exists.

The verdicts, as I actually hold them. A child playing at keeping a shop, alone and unobserved, is doing something valuable; the same child, filmed by a parent for relatives and strangers, seems to me to be doing something different, and probably worse, though the movements are the same. Writing a novel from an impulse I could not justify seems to me good in the way play is good; writing the same novel under contract, with an advance and a deadline, does not seem to me any less good, provided it remains the book I meant to write. Writing to market – working backwards from what a platform rewards and trimming the book to its expected readers before it exists – feels like a corruption of the whole enterprise. I play tennis with a friend and take the game to be valuable in itself; the same tennis played for a wager feels altered, though I cannot say whether the alteration is a moral one. A footballer who played as a boy and now plays to a fixture list, through injury, is still playing by my lights, but something has gone out of it. Chasing trophies in a video game leaves the game intact for me; keeping a streak-and-badge record of my own daily word count, as though the writing of a novel were a game, feels faintly corrupting. An afternoon in the garden doing nothing in particular seems to me a real good, whilst the same afternoon spent in a mindfulness exercise undertaken to make me more productive feels diminished, and I cannot say exactly why.

The simplest account would be that play and leisure are good only as ends in themselves, and that any external purpose thins them; but I can see at once that such a principle cannot accommodate the contracted novel or the trophy-hunted game, and I am not inclined to give up either verdict cheaply. Is there a subtler principle that captures the pattern – one stated precisely enough to say where the corruption lies and where it does not? Which of my verdicts, if any, does it force me to abandon? And what would it predict about the cases I have not yet settled: a child paid for drawings she would have made anyway, an amateur playwright whose hobby becomes a fringe success, a walker who takes up counting steps, a novelist who accepts a commissioned series under another writer's name? I do not require the principle to be moral in character; I would be content with an aesthetic or psychological account, if that is what the verdicts jointly entail.
