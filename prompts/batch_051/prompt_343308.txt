A friend and I have been arguing for weeks about collective memory and history: when a community's shared memory of an event conflicts with the documentary record—over what happened, how it happened, or what it meant—which should give way? We cannot settle it, and the question has become pointed because our book club has just chosen Tim O'Brien's The Things They Carried for next month. I want you to read the novel through exactly that argument for me.

The novel is an unusually fierce case of the quarrel. Its narrator insists that a true war story is true by the way it feels rather than by its correspondence to what happened, retells the same episodes with different details, and openly complicates whether some of what he presented as literal fact ever happened that way. For the purposes of this reading, treat the platoon and the readership the narrator is trying to make believe as a memory community, and treat the historian's and archivist's version of those events as the record.

As a literary critic who also works in the philosophy of memory and historiography, write a reading that runs in both directions. On one side, show where the novel seems to argue that a group's memory can—and sometimes should—outweigh, correct, or override the record, and identify the scenes that carry that argument. On the other side, show where the novel resists or complicates its own case: where memory looks self-exculpating, mythmaking, coercively passed on, or too convenient, and where the record seems to have a claim that the story cannot dissolve. Do not reduce the book to an illustration of the philosophical debate, and do not distort the debate to fit the book; end by saying what the novel adds to the philosophical debate that argument alone does not. Pay particular attention to the distinction the book draws between story-truth and happening-truth, the young man on the trail whose death the narrator initially presents as his own doing, and the retellings of the death in the flooded field.

Begin by brainstorming the scenes and characters that bear on the question, and state your assumptions before you settle on a reading. Throughout, separate what is textually settled about the novel and factually settled about the historical record from what is genuinely contested among people who work on collective memory and the ethics of history, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
