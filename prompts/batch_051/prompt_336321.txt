I recently came across a metaphysics article aimed at a general audience that summarizes Black's two-sphere argument roughly like this. Imagine a universe whose only contents are two iron spheres, exactly alike in size, temperature, and material, separated by a mile of empty space. Each sphere has precisely the same intrinsic qualities, and the relational facts are all symmetric, so neither sphere has a property the other lacks. Yet there are plainly two spheres, not one. From this the article concludes that the identity of indiscernibles is false; the only ways to save it, it says, are to posit a primitive 'thisness' for each sphere or to count relational properties like being a mile from another sphere, which it dismisses as changing the subject. Something about this feels too clean to me — in particular, I suspect 'property' and 'plainly two' are doing a lot of unexamined work — but I don't know the literature well enough to say where the summary goes wrong, if it does. Is this a fair account of Black's argument and its upshot? Please separate what is accurate, what is a common simplification, what a defender of the identity of indiscernibles would object to, and whether the article's conclusion survives those corrections.
