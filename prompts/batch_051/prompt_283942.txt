I'm taking a philosophy of language course this term, and we've been working through the prescriptivism–descriptivism debate. A classmate built an argument that looks, to me, as if it proves too much, and I can't locate the step to give up. It runs like this. (1) A sentence has bad grammar only if it breaks a rule of grammar. (2) Every real rule of grammar belongs to a particular language or dialect; there is no language-neutral grammar. (3) Languages and dialects are conventional systems, so no variety's rules are more intrinsically correct than another's. (4) Therefore the only grammar against which a speaker's sentence may properly be measured is the grammar of the variety she herself speaks. (5) But no one can break the grammar of her own variety, since her variety is fixed by what she says and accepts; any apparent violation would simply turn out to be part of the pattern. (6) Hence no one ever uses bad grammar, and what look like errors are just features of a different dialect or idiolect. But even the descriptivists in the room find (6) unacceptable: the same classmate still treats a slip of the tongue as a slip, still calls a child's 'I goed to the park' an error, and still expects our professor to mark nonstandard constructions in an essay as failing the stated conventions of standard written English. If (6) is right, those practices seem to be mere prejudice.

I should say where I stand so the diagnosis is useful. I'd most like to keep (2) and (3), which seem to me the core of descriptivism; I'm not asking for a general defense of prescriptivism. What I want is a diagnosis of this particular chain of reasoning: which premise or step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? If the better move is to accept some carefully qualified version of (6), what version exactly could a descriptivist accept, and how would it handle slips, child errors, and the marking of essays against a standard? Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among linguists and philosophers of language here, and flag any point where you are presenting a contested view as the consensus.
