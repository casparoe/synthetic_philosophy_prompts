I've been trying to figure out what I actually think about when counting becomes mathematics, by going on my gut across a bunch of cases. Take the cases as I describe them; I'm not asking for the archaeology to be checked. Here are my verdicts.

1. A shepherd around 7000 BCE cuts one notch on a stick for each sheep that leaves the fold in the morning, and scores across each notch as the animal comes back at night. My verdict: that's counting, and maybe elementary arithmetic if we're being generous, but not mathematics.

2. A temple administrator in Mesopotamia around 3500 BCE keeps accounts with little clay tokens of different shapes for sheep, jars of oil, and measures of grain. A bill of lading gets made by sealing the tokens inside a clay ball and pressing their shapes into the outside. My verdict: not mathematics. It's a clever one-to-one code and bookkeeping, but I don't feel mathematics has started.

3. The Ishango bone, from around 20,000 years ago: a bone with notches arranged in columns, where one column has been read as the primes between 10 and 20 (11, 13, 17, 19), and another looks like some kind of doubling pattern. If those groupings are intentional, my gut says this is mathematics, or at least proto-mathematics. This is uncomfortable, because the bone is much older and socially "simpler" than the Mesopotamian tokens, yet it feels more mathematical to me.

4. A five-year-old can recite number words up to 20, but when told to bring five spoons she just recites the words while grabbing spoons and never pairs word to object, so she ends up with the wrong number. My verdict: not counting, and certainly not mathematics.

5. A toddler with no number words can set a table by matching one fork to one napkin and notices when somebody has no fork. My verdict: that's an ingredient of mathematics, but not yet doing mathematics.

6. An Old Babylonian scribal student around 1800 BCE copies out a standard multiplication table (say one that lists a number times 1 through 20, 30, 40, and 50, written in sexagesimal), and then works through fixed word problems that use the same products. My verdict: I want to say this is mathematics, even though it's rote.

7. Plimpton 322, same period: a clay tablet with four columns and fifteen rows of base-60 numbers. Two columns are usually read as the short side and hypotenuse of whole-number right triangles (Pythagorean triples), another column looks like a squared ratio, the rows are ordered as if generated by a systematic recipe rather than measured from actual fields, and there's even an arithmetic slip. My verdict: unambiguously mathematics.

8. A crow watches someone place items one at a time into a box and notices when the number inside stops matching its expectations, up to about four items. My verdict: numerical competence, not mathematics.

I have a rough candidate principle: mathematics starts where someone states or manipulates relations between numbers, rather than merely pairing a number with a collection. But it's in trouble immediately. The scribal multiplication table is basically a list of facts, and "manipulate" is doing suspicious work; the Ishango bone is just clustered notches with no explicit statement of a relation; and the clay tokens are a one-to-one code that still creates abstract numerical equivalences of a kind. So either the principle is wrong, or some of my verdicts have to go.
