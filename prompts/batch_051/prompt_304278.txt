ok so I've been chewing on this for months and I can't tell if it's a real question or just semantics. People say some patterns in nature are actually explained by math, not just described by it. The two cases I keep coming back to: periodical cicadas that emerge every 13 or 17 years, and the story is that 13 and 17 being prime explains why predators can't sync up with them. And honeycomb: bees build hexagons, and the story is that a hexagon grid wastes the least wax for the storage you get. I can see that the math fits. What I can't see is how you would ever show the math is why the pattern exists, instead of us just fitting the prettiest formula to whatever we already found.

Every test I can dream up seems to only check that the pattern is real and useful, not that the math is doing the explaining. Like, you could look for a non-prime cicada brood and see if it gets wiped out more often, or build a physical wax model and see what shape it settles into. But those just tell me the pattern holds, not wether the number is the reason it holds.

So I want a test, not a definition. How would you test the claim that a mathematical fact actually does explanatory work in nature? Design the strongest empirical test you can — comparative, experimental, simulation, whatever — and tell me which result would count for 'the math explains it' and which for 'the math is just a good summary'. Then be honest about what even the best result leaves open: does it show the bee is doing geometry, or only that the building process happens to land on a minimum? Does a clean result tell us anything about whether the explanation is in the world or just in our heads? And if a test can't reach that last part even in principle, does that make the whole 'math explains nature' claim unfalsifiable, or just mean part of it is a philosophical choice rather than an empirical one?
