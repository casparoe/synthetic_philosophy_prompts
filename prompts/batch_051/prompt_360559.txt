I cover science and ideas for a general-interest outlet, and last week I moderated a festival discussion after a screening of a biographical drama about a photojournalist who died in the 1990s. The film invents two episodes that are unsupported by the documentary record and disputed by people who knew him: that he staged his most celebrated photograph and that he had an affair with a source. During the discussion, two critics on the panel had the following exchange. (1) One said the film wrongs no one, because the man is dead and the dead cannot be harmed; the only obligations that matter are to the living audience, who understand they are watching fiction. (2) The other replied that reputation is an interest that survives a person — we speak without strain of dishonoring the dead — and that the film will overwrite the public memory of a man who can no longer answer back. (3) The first answered that historical drama is a recognized interpretive genre; no reasonable viewer takes an invented scene as documentary fact, and a disclaimer is enough to contain whatever residue remains. (4) The second answered that the convention cannot do that much work: the invention is a specific, damaging claim about an identifiable real person, it will be most viewers' only encounter with him, and if the same assertion were made about a living person in a news article it could be defamatory — so putting it on screen makes the accusation more vivid, not more innocent. I would like you to adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and then say who is right about what overall. Finally, state the principle both critics are missing about what we owe the real dead when we dramatize them.
