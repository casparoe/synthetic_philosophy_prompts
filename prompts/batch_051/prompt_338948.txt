I'm a novelist—mostly literary historical fiction, but deliberately small-bore stuff—and I've been roped into a panel at a writers conference this spring called "the novel after postmodernism." The moderator asked each of us to send in a page of our philosophical priors ahead of time so the conversation doesn't just idle in craft talk. I've never had to lay mine out in once place, and now that I've tried I've got the nagging feeling they don't all fit together. I'm to close to it to see where.

Here's roughly what I sent:

(a) I'm convinced by the Lyotard line that the big legitimating stories—Progress, Reason, emancipation, History with a capital H—have lost their credibility, and I'd add that this is one reason I keep my novels small.
(b) At the same time, I write plots with beginnings, middles, and ends, and I don't take that to be a problem: a novel isn't claiming to be a theory of history, it's just a made thing that gives a shape to life it doesn't have.
(c) I don't buy the idea of a neutral, positionless account of the past. Every history, every biography, every novel that touches real events is told from some spot, and I say so in my endnotes.
(d) But I also don't think all versions of the past are equal. A novel that respects the documentary record and doesn't sand off the awkward parts is truer than one that invents whatever it likes, and I'd hold that line on the hard cases too—Holocaust denial isn't just another spot to tell it from.
(e) The reason I write about one house, one family, one failed factory is that the small scale is more honest than trying to write about the course of nations.
(f) Yet I also think those small stories only earn their keep because the big forces—capital, empire, war, race—show up in them. A local novel that never lets the reader feel the big forces is just quaint.

I'm not looking for reassurance; I want to know where I've papered over a contradiction. Go through the pairs, tell me which conflicts are real and which ones melt away once you get specific, and rank the real ones by severity. Then, for each real tension, tell me the cheapest thing to give up or restate, and what it costs me as a novelist. I'd rather find out before the panel than on it.
