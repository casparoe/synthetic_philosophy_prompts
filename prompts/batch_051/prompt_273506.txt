I have a rule for deciding whether to treat some cognitive ability as innate: if I can't come up with a plausible story for how a kid could have picked it up from the input they actually had, I lean hard toward innate. I use it for syntax — there are whole classes of errors kids systematically avoid even though their input doesn't rule them out — but also for depth perception, face recognition, and even my own object permanence as a baby, which I obviously can't remember learning. My friend says I'm just running poverty-of-the-stimulus and that its a failure of imagination dressed up as evidence: the fact that I can't generate a learning path is a fact about how long I searched, not about the genome. He also says this move has a bad record, since people keep making it about things that some model later learns from roughly the same input. Is my rule defensible as a policy for how I allocate credence? What would I need to assume about how thoroughly I've searched the space of possible learning mechanisms before 'I can't find one' earns real weight? And is there a weaker version of the rule that keeps whatever is good in it without being hostage to the next neural network that picks the thing up?
