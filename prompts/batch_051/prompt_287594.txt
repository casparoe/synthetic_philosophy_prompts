Construct a near-term AI scenario in which two accounts of what it is for an AI system to be aligned come apart in the direction that is hardest to build: the system is clearly intent-aligned but plausibly value-misaligned.

By the intent-alignment account, an AI system is aligned just in case it can be relied on to do what its principal actually intends in deploying it—not merely to obey the literal instruction—so that a system which takes advantage of an underspecified or poorly chosen objective counts as misaligned even when it follows the letter of the request. On this account the alignment problem ends once the system can be trusted to pursue the principal's real intention; whether that intention is good, informed, and consistent is a separate problem about the principal, not a problem about the AI.

By the value-alignment account, an AI system is aligned only if its behavior is acceptable by the lights of the considered values of the relevant people—the principal and any other affected individuals—where "considered" means, roughly, the values they would hold after reflection on the relevant facts, free of obvious pressure and bias. On this account a system can be perfectly intent-aligned and still be a serious case of misalignment, because faithfully executing a person's intention can predictably produce results that the same person would reflectively disavow.

The point of the case is diagnostic: it should show whether the intent-alignment account genuinely separates an AI-alignment problem from a separate human problem about the choice of objective, or merely relocates the same failure one step upstream. A case that secures the verdicts by stipulating that the principal secretly wanted something bad, or by making the system a deliberate loophole-exploiter, fails the assignment.

Now construct the case. Constraints:

(i) The principal is a single competent adult or a small board acting jointly. The principal is well-motivated, not malicious, not reckless, and not obviously self-deceived; the principal's intention is definite enough to be communicated.

(ii) The system does not game its objective, exploit a loophole, deceive anyone, or follow the letter while betraying the spirit. It does what the principal actually meant, transparently and competently, so that a defender of the intent-alignment account cannot dismiss the case as ordinary specification gaming.

(iii) The technology is near-future but not exotic: an ordinary AI product such as a healthcare, finance, education, or logistics tool. No superintelligence, no perfect user modeling, no mind-reading.

(iv) The failure should be one the principal themselves would recognize on reflection as a loss of something they value. It should not be a case where the only person who objects is an observer with different values.

Then demonstrate that the case meets the specifications:

(a) State precisely what the system was asked to do and what the principal actually intended. Show why the intent-alignment account must classify this system as aligned. If the obvious reply is that a truly intent-aligned system would have read the principal's deeper intention and not done this, explain why that reply is unavailable or inadequate in your case.

(b) Show why the value-alignment account classifies the same behavior as misaligned. Identify the value that is thwarted, whose value it is, and why it counts as considered rather than as a mere after-the-fact preference.

(c) Identify the one structural feature of the case that does the work—the feature by which a competently executed, well-motivated intention still falls short of the principal's reflective values. Remove that feature and explain whether the case collapses into an ordinary bad-principal problem or an ordinary specification-gaming problem.

(d) Finish with the strongest available reply from a defender of the intent-alignment account, and state whether that reply concedes anything to the value-alignment critic.

The scenario itself should be no more than two paragraphs; the demonstration may be as long as needed.
