I was arguing with a friend about whether "possible" and "positive probability" are basically the same thing, and I cooked up the following argument to show they can't be, but now I'm not sure it doesn't have a hole in it. Here's what I said:

(1) Suppose you flip a fair coin infinitely many times — or, if that's too weird, imagine drawing a real number uniformly at random from the interval [0,1].
(2) Any particular infinite sequence of heads and tails (or any particular real number) is a possible outcome of this process.
(3) If an outcome is possible, it must have positive probability; somthing with probability zero can't happen.
(4) So every particular infinite sequence (or real number) has positive probability.
(5) But the outcomes are mutually exclusive and exhaustive, so their probabilities have to add up to 1.
(6) Yet there are uncountably many outcomes, and uncountably many positive numbers can't add up to anything finite, let alone 1.
(7) Contradiction: each outcome has positive probability, but their total can't be 1.

So either the setup in (1) describes something impossible, or one of these steps is wrong. My friend insists the problem is (3), and that probability-zero events do happen all the time, but that seems backwards to me — if it has probability zero, how can it happen? Someone else told me the real problem is (5), because probabilities only have to add up over countably many events. I want to know exactly which step fails, and on what interpretation of the word "possible" the argument is actually valid. Also, if (3) is the culprit, give me the best defense of the claim that probability zero does not mean impossible, because I still can't shake the feeling that it does.
