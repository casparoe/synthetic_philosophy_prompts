I'm a PhD student in political theory, and I keep using the Montgomery bus boycott as the test case for the chapter I'm writing on political obligation and civil disobedience, but every time I try to force it into the standard categories it slips out. I already know the history well enough: Rosa Parks's arrest in December 1955, the MIA and King, the carpools and mass meetings, the boycott lasting over a year until the federal courts struck down bus segregation, and the fact that the buses themselves were run by a private company even though segregation was legally required. So I don't need the narrative; I need someone to do careful theory with it.

Take the boycott (1955–56) as a case study. Work through what at least three standard positions on political obligation—consent theory, the fair-play account, and a natural-duty account—would each say about what the boycotters were doing: violating an obligation, temporarily suspending one, claiming an exemption, acting from a higher duty, or something the theory doesn't even have language for. Then layer that with Rawls's account of civil disobedience (roughly: public, nonviolent, conscientious law-breaking, aimed at the majority's sense of justice) and tell me how well the boycott counts. I'm especially stuck on the fact that the central tactic—staying off the buses—wasn't breaking any law at all; what law-breaking there was feels peripheral, and some of it went in the other direction, like the city prosecuting boycott leaders under an anti-boycott law. So please say honestly where the theory has to be stretched to fit this case.

Then run it in reverse: what does the episode do to the theory? Does it show that the standard theory of civil disobedience is missing a category—call it refusal to cooperate or economic withdrawal—or is the mismatch an illusion, just me overreading the word "civil" and treating a boycott as if it had to compete with civil disobedience when its really a different kind of action? I want the worry taken seriously but not flattered: tell me if the theory handles the case fine and I'm the one conflating categories.
