I have been turning over an argument that a friend in my philosophy and decision theory group has been pressing in our discussions of akrasia, and I want to figure out where it breaks, because its conclusion seems to me impossible to accept. The case we keep coming back to is this: on Sunday morning a woman judges, with full information and no unusual pressure, that she should spend Sunday evening writing a long-overdue report rather than watching a series, and she resolves to write. By the evening she has not revised that judgment; she still thinks writing is what she should do, she still wishes she would do it, but she streams the series instead and later describes herself as having given in. That looks like a textbook instance of weakness of will. My friend argues that this description cannot survive the right account of time preference.

Here is her argument. (1) What shifts between morning and evening is the shape of her delay discounting. Her discounting is hyperbolic rather than exponential: the weight she attaches to a future cost or reward falls steeply as it approaches the present, so the immediate reward of the show is barely discounted in the evening, while in the morning it was hours away and heavily discounted relative to the report's future payoffs. (2) Hyperbolic discounting is not a local malfunction but a general feature of how temporally situated agents weigh earlier and later outcomes, and at any one time it gives her a coherent ranking of alternatives from that time's standpoint; it does not violate any basic constraint of consistency in her reasoning. (3) There is no time-neutral standpoint from which the morning ranking is more rational or authoritative than the evening ranking; each ranking is just the same discounting function applied from a different present. The shift from morning to evening is therefore a rationally permitted change in perspective, not an error. (4) If the evening ranking is rationally permitted and no less authoritative than the morning ranking, then in acting on it she is not acting against what she has most reason to do at the time she acts; she is acting in line with the reasons her own present perspective makes salient. (5) But weakness of will requires acting against what one still judges best, or has most reason to do, at the moment of action. Since the analysis in (4) shows there is no such conflict, the evening action is not akratic. (6) The same analysis applies to the standard pattern of temptation and giving in, so the familiar phenomenon of weakness of will is not a distinct form of practical failure; it is, at worst, a rationally permissible sort of preference change over time. (7) Yet weakness of will clearly is a distinct form of practical failure, and any account that dissolves it by redescription is missing something. Where does this argument go wrong, and what exactly do I give up by rejecting whichever premise or transition is to blame?

I should add two constraints. I am inclined to keep (7) and I suspect the damage is somewhere in the movement through (3) to (5), perhaps because it slides from a ranking to a judgment, but I want to know whether that suspicion can be made precise without declaring hyperbolic discounting irrational or privileging one temporal perspective over another. Please diagnose the argument step by step, say which move should be rejected and why it looked plausible, and price the main alternatives for someone who wants to preserve weakness of will as real.
