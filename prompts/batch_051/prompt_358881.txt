I'm a retired organic chemist, so I've spent a lot of time around risk assessment and regulatory thresholds. Lately I've been working through Keynes's A Treatise on Probability, and the part where he distinguishes the probability of a proposition on given evidence from what he calls the weight of the argument has me puzzled. As I understand him, you can have two hypotheses that both come out at probability 0.7 on the available evidence, but one rests on a huge body of varied, independent data and the other on one thin, possibly biased sample. Those are not the same epistemic situation, even though the number is the same. He treats the amount or completeness of the evidence as its own dimension, not something captured by the probability itself.

Now here's the practice that's bugging me. In climate economics and parts of environmental regulation, the same institutions that write page after page about deep uncertainty and tipping points also run integrated assessment models that put precise probability distributions over things like climate sensitivity and damage functions, then compute expected net present values and optimal carbon prices from those distributions. They sometimes admit in the prose that the tails of those distributions are basically guesses, but then the policy recommendation still comes down to a single expected value or a single optimal number.

What view of probability or uncertainty does that practice presuppose? Does it presuppose that Keynes's weight-of-argument point doesn't matter for decision, or that it can be handled by assigning probabilities to the probabilities? Is the practice actually consistent with Keynes's distinction, or does it quietly reject it? And if an agency took the weight-of-argument point seriously, would it have to give up cost-benefit analysis in these deeply uncertain cases, or could it just report something different alongside the expected value and still be coherent?
