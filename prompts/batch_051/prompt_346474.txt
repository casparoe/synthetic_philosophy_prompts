ok so i help run my school's philosophy-and-econ club and next week i'm doing the main talk. my argument is that people overrate markets as truth-detectors. the no-trade theorem says rational people with common priors should basically never trade just on private information, because the fact that the other person wants to trade is itself information they could learn from. so i want to push the line that when we see alot of trade in a market — including our school's dumb little prediction market on whether the principal actually resigns — that doesn't give us evidence that the price is right or that the buyers know something the sellers don't. if anything, the liveliness of a market should make us suspicious of it as a truth-tracker, since a market that was perfectly aggregating information would mostly just sit there quietly. i also want to say that citing a price move as proof that some rumor is true, or saying people are putting money on it, is a real mistake.

the audience: our philosophy teacher is into bayesian stuff and will care about priors and updating, the ap econ teacher is a hayekian who thinks prices aggregate dispersed information and that markets are basically efficient, and two seniors from the investing club actually trade and will say that putting your money where your mouth is is the only real way to show you know something. plus some kids who just think prediction markets are fun.

i need a prep sheet, not a polished written defense. give me the objections in roughly the order they'll actually come at me, phrased the way those specific people would say them, with what i should concede, what i should hold, and the weakest point in my argument that i should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss. and tell me the question i'm hoping nobody asks.
