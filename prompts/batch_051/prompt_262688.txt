I am a PhD student in philosophy working on the methodology of thought experiments, and I have spent the past two years reading the cross-cultural experimental philosophy literature in preparation for a dissertation chapter. What bothers me is not any single result but the overall shape of the debate, which now seems frozen. The original findings were of this kind: translate vignettes built around a familiar Gettier-style case and put them to participants in different cultural settings, and the distributions of answers pull apart, with some groups considerably more willing than others to attribute knowledge. A similar split appeared in semantic cases modeled on the familiar scenario in which someone receives credit for a theorem he stole; participants are asked which of the two men a speaker's use of a name picks out, and here, too, the two populations' answers parted company. Since these findings appeared, critics have argued that the results are artifacts of translation, question wording, or sampling; that they confuse performance with competence; that trained philosophers' intuitions are insulated from this noise; and that philosophy never promised that folk intuitions would be universal. The experimentalists have replied with replications, controls, bilingual designs, and follow-up studies using professional philosophers, which they take to leave the underlying variation intact. After two decades of this, neither side has moved.

My working hypothesis is that the two sides are not disputing the same proposition at all: one side is making a descriptive claim about the psychology of judgment and its cultural sensitivity, while the other is defending a normative claim about what philosophy is entitled to do with case-based evidence. If that is right, further data collection will never settle the matter, and the intractability reflects a disagreement about standards rather than facts. But I am not confident. The dispute might instead be a hard empirical question about the size, robustness, and causes of variation, or a partly verbal fight over what should count as the data of philosophy, and over what the word intuition is even meant to pick out. I would like your diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement rather than another survey of the empirical results: what kind of disagreement is it, and why has it stalled? Please also say what would, on your diagnosis, actually count as progress — a replication, a formal result, a conceptual distinction, a change of question, or the resignation that no progress should be expected. I would be grateful if the answer could be written in British English.
