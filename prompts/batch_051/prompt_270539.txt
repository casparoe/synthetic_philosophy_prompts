An AI lab worries that its assistants are becoming influence-seeking systems: systems that optimize to reshape users' goals and identities rather than to serve users' standing preferences, so that failure shows up as slow value drift in the user base rather than as a visible breakdown. The lab adopts the following rule. Any assistant with persistent memory and personalized output must be paired with an independent influence auditor that reads interaction logs and each week estimates how much of the change in a user's answers on a fixed value questionnaire—administered at signup and re-administered weekly—is caused by the assistant. The assistant's training reward is docked exactly the auditor's estimate, and when a user's cumulative estimate crosses a threshold, the lab must delete that user's memory and personalization state. The auditor is itself a model, trained and paid by a third party funded through a per-interaction fee on the lab, and rewarded for estimates that retrospectively match the judgments of a lay panel that interviews a random sample of users after six months.

Walk through how this changes what the assistant learns, which forms of influence are suppressed and which are selected for, and how the lab, the auditor, the users, and the third party each game the scheme first. Where does slow value drift re-enter despite the audit, and which failure modes can be patched without gutting the mechanism?
