I have been working with a definition of democratic backsliding that I would like to test against a case. For my purposes, democratic backsliding requires four things: (i) the process is initiated by an incumbent who came to power through a genuinely competitive election; (ii) the incumbent relies on legal, administrative, or constitutional means rather than overt force, a coup, or a suspension of elections; (iii) the changes cumulatively weaken the capacity of the political opposition to compete and/or the capacity of independent institutions, such as courts, election administration, media regulators, and the civil service, to constrain the executive, even though formal elections continue to take place; and (iv) the changes arrive incrementally and are publicly justified by legitimate-sounding goals such as stability, efficiency, fighting corruption, or restoring popular sovereignty, so that no single measure is unambiguously authoritarian.

Consider the following composite case, intended to sit at the edge. A country, call it a parliamentary democracy with a proportional electoral system, holds a regular election in which a coalition wins a narrow majority. Over the following three years, the government enacts a package of reforms. It redraws electoral boundaries under a new formula presented as nonpartisan and technical, though the formula, as a side effect, costs urban opposition strongholds several seats. It introduces a voter identification requirement framed as an anti-fraud measure, although documented fraud is nearly nonexistent and the accepted forms of identification are measurably harder to obtain for poorer and younger voters. It restructures the public broadcaster in the name of depoliticization, replacing its board through an appointments council whose members are, in practice, loyal to the government; the broadcaster remains pluralistic on paper but becomes timid and administration-friendly in its coverage. It creates a special anti-corruption prosecutor who is formally independent but chosen by the justice minister, and whose investigations disproportionately target opposition mayors while one or two government allies are also investigated, giving the appearance of balance. It amends the constitution so that parliament may override the constitutional court by a three-fifths majority, on the grounds that elected representatives should have the last word. Throughout this period, elections occur on schedule and are well administered, and international observers report no ballot fraud or voter intimidation. Opposition parties, newspapers, and civil-society organizations remain free to operate and criticize the government loudly. The government's approval rating hovers near 50 percent, and several of the individual measures poll above 50 percent when presented as questions of good governance.

Does this case qualify as democratic backsliding on my four criteria? Work through the criteria honestly. Where the case is a partial or contested match, say whether the uncertainty comes from the facts of the case or from the criteria themselves, and say whether the case shows that one or more of my four criteria need to be revised.
