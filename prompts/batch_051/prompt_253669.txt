I am a retired geophysicist, and for the past few years my reading has been in decision theory and the philosophy of collective action rather than earth science. The argument I keep returning to, and the one I would like you to assess, is the unilateralist's curse. As I understand it, the curse runs as follows. Suppose many well-intentioned actors each have the power, acting alone, to bring about some state of affairs that will affect all of them. Each actor forms a private judgment about whether the action is wise. Any given actor is mostly sensible but none is infallible, so for a bad action each has some small probability of wrongly judging it good. If every actor acts on private judgment alone, then as the number of actors grows, the probability that at least one will err and act approaches one. The usual moral is that unilateral action should be restrained by a principle of conformity: when one knows others may disagree, one should not simply act on one's own assessment.

The development that now bothers me is that the actors I actually worry about are not independent people with independent evidence and independent errors. They are many instances of the same large language model, or of a handful of closely related models, pretrained on overlapping corpora and fine-tuned in similar ways, each given a lever over the world. Their judgments are not independent draws from some error distribution; they are near-repeats of a single computation, so their errors are strongly correlated. In the limiting case, ten thousand copies of one model are just one judge repeated ten thousand times, and the probability that at least one copy endorses a bad action does not tend toward one as the curse suggests. But that is not reassuring either: one confidently wrong judge is its own kind of danger, and correlated failure rules out exactly the diversity of views that might have caught the error.

Does the unilateralist's curse survive this change? I would like to know whether the independence assumption is doing all the work, whether some weaker condition short of independence still generates the curse, or whether the argument should instead be reformulated as a problem about correlated systemic error rather than about the statistical inevitability of a lone bad actor. I am interested in what happens to the argument itself, not in policy recommendations, and in particular in which premise breaks first and whether a repaired version can say something weaker but still true.
