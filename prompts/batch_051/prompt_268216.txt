Over the past decade I have watched one of the few reliable arbiters of public disagreement quietly stop functioning, and I want to know whether the change has been named and mapped. Audio and video recordings used to occupy a distinctive epistemic role: a leaked tape could settle a dispute about what a politician said, and a bystander's phone footage could override an official account. That role was never unlimited, but it was stable enough to function as a shared floor under public testimony — a point at which open-ended disagreement normally stopped. Now that deepfakes and other cheap, convincing fabrications are widely available, the floor itself is eroding in at least three separable ways: newly forged recordings; the dismissal of genuine recordings as suspect because they could have been generated; and retroactive doubt about recordings made before the technology existed. I am not after the general literature on misinformation. I am asking whether this particular role — a shared, normally trusted floor under public testimony — has an established name, whether its erosion is best analysed as one phenomenon or several, and how these pieces relate to neighbouring ideas such as the epistemology of testimony and ordinary loss of trust. If no standard label covers the whole thing, I want one coined, with boundaries that distinguish it from a general distrust of testimony. Please write your answer in British English.
