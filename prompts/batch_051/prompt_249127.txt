I'm teaching an upper-level undergrad course on philosophy of language and logic, and in two weeks we hit the unit on bivalence—the thesis that every meaningful declarative sentence is either true or false, full stop. The class is 16 students (mostly philosophy majors, a few math/computer science people), and I have one 90-minute session to make the problem real. In the past my approach has been to walk through Aristotle's sea battle, vague predicates like tall and bald, and presupposition failures like the present king of France is bald, then give the standard menu: supervaluationism, Kleene/partial logic, and the idea that some sentences are neither true nor false or lack a definite truth value. It doesn't stick. The students can state the positions back to me, but deep down they keep treating truth as binary; bivalence is so baked into their habits that the motivations for gaps feel like a technical curiosity. I really want one runnable classroom activity—a game or structured exercise, not a lecture—where participants collide with the gaps themselves before I label them. The gap should show up in the rules, scoring, or incentives, not in something I say afterwards. I don't want a discussion of definitions. I want them to feel forced to choose between (a) assigning true or false to something that isn't settled and (b) trying to leave it unassigned, and then see that (b) isn't just laziness.

My half-baked seed: a betting or adjudication game where students must post true/false verdicts on a mixed stack of sentences to earn points, where some are future contingents (it will rain exactly 2.3 mm in the quad tomorrow), some are borderline vague (a 5'11'' person described as tall), and some carry failed presuppositions (the present king of France is bald). The scoring should be arranged so that bivalence costs them: they post a verdict, lose points or get entangled in predictions they can't back, and only then can the class see why someone might reject the principle. I'm open to other formats—a prediction market with play money, a card-sorting game, a role-play where a bivalent "court" has to rule on gappy cases and a "defense" argues for gaps. It just has to work with 14–18 students in 90 minutes, no real money, no reading before class, and it has to produce the insight rather than my mini-lecture producing it.

Please give me the rules and materials (ideally stuff I can print or improvise), the session timeline with rough timings, a payoff scheme using points or rank or some other currency students care about, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto the theory: bivalence vs. determinacy, future contingents, vagueness, presupposition failure, and at least one positive account such as supervaluationism or strong Kleene/partial logic. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time—I'm especially worried about one confident student blurting out "well, it's neither!" five minutes in and puncturing the discovery, or the class spendng the whole time arguing about the weather forecast instead of the underlying point—and how to handle it without deflating the exercise. Finally, be straight with me about which feature of the idea a classroom game cannot reproduce and has to be supplied by me afterwards. My hunch is that no activity can make them feel the difference between "not yet true" (a future contingent on one view) and "already gappy" (like a failed presupposition on some views), but you may disagree.
