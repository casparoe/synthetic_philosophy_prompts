I have been working on a problem in the design of low-impact AI systems, and I would welcome a candid assessment of a construct I have been using, which I call the repair-invoice account of side effects.

The problem is this. The usual way to make an agent low-impact is to penalise it for deviating from a baseline: some representation of what the world would have been like if the agent had not acted, or if a muted version of the agent had acted. But choosing that baseline, and choosing how to measure deviation from it, already requires deciding which properties of the world count as problematic differences. I suspect the baseline approach therefore quietly presupposes at least a partial specification of value, which is exactly what the idea of defining side effects without one was meant to avoid.

The repair-invoice account moves the question from before the action to after it. Suppose that, once an agent has completed its assigned task, it presents its principal with an itemised invoice of the changes its behaviour brought about. Each line names a change and states the cost of returning the world to the nearest state in which that particular change had not occurred; the principal then records, for each line, the largest amount they would in principle be willing to pay to have that restoration performed. Call a change a side effect when this amount is positive, and an intended effect when it is zero or negative. The agent's impact is the sum of the positive amounts across all lines, and the agent is low-impact when that sum is small. In practice the invoice need not be a literal document; the agent would be trained to minimise its anticipated impact, where the anticipation is learned from past post hoc elicitations.

The central claim is that this avoids full value specification. We never need a once-and-for-all ranking of possible worlds, a list of protected features, or an advance statement of which side effects matter. We need only local, concrete comparisons — a world with some particular change against the nearest world without it — elicited after the fact, one line at a time. That is closer to asking someone to object to an itemised bill than to asking them to write a constitution.

An example may make the idea clearer. A robot is sent to fetch a rare book from a library. It moves a chair to reach the shelf, leaves the book on a reading table, and scuffs the floor as it turns. Under the account I am proposing, the robot did not need to know in advance which of these are side effects. Afterwards, the librarian prices the lines: they would pay something, however little, to have the chair put back; they would pay more to have the floor repaired; and they would pay nothing to have the book returned to the shelf, because the book being on the table is the task itself — they might even pay to keep it there. Across many tasks, the robot minimises the positive entries without anyone having written down rules such as that chairs are to be put back.

My questions are these. Does the account genuinely differ from the baseline approach, or is the repair price merely the baseline metric in another guise? Is there something recognisably close to this already in the literature, and if so, what is it? And where does the account most obviously break down? I am particularly unsure about two points: whether post hoc willingness-to-pay can be elicited without already knowing the principal's values in a form rich enough to undermine the point; and how to handle irreversible changes, for which the restoration cost is effectively infinite.

Please answer in British English.
