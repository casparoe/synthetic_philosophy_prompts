I am a novelist, with the usual working knowledge of narrative craft but no formal training in philosophy, and for the past year I have been writing a book set in a near-future society that has developed a safe, permanent way to dampen grief, jealousy, and other suffering-heavy affects. I keep returning to Brave New World as the obvious ancestor of that premise, and I want to think more carefully about what Huxley actually does with the idea rather than what people half-remember him as doing.

Read the novel through the lens of the philosophical debate over the value of negative emotions: specifically, the distinction between the view that sadness and grief are merely instrumental goods — a price we pay for love, attachment, and depth, which we should rationally eliminate if we could do so without cost — and the view that at least some negative affect is constitutively bound up with the goods it accompanies, so that grief is not an unfortunate side effect of loving a mortal person but part of what it is to love one, and a life without it would be different in kind rather than simply happier. I would like the reading to center on a tension I keep noticing as a practitioner of fiction: the novel seems to condemn a world from which strong negative emotion has been engineered away, yet the novel's own power as a narrative depends entirely on the reader's capacity to be moved by pity, dread, and grief. Does the form of the book undermine its apparent thesis, or does it enact a stronger version of it?

Which scenes or turns would you point another novelist toward — the Savage's grief, the nursery conditioning, the Controller's remarks about the price of high art — and where does the book resist the philosophical claim that negative emotions are valuable? I am less interested in the familiar verdict that the World State is dystopian than in what the novel specifically contributes to the claim that a life without sadness and grief would be worse, and where a philosopher of emotion would say Huxley gets something right that the philosophical essays miss, or wrong that the essays would correct.
