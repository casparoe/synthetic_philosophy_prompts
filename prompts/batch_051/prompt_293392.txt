Okay so the standard moral argument against habitat destruction is basically an animal-welfare argument. Clearing a forest or draining a marsh is bad because the animals who lived their lose their homes, get killed, or starve — and the more animals whose lives get wrecked, the worse the destruction is. I get that. But then I found this other view (I think from the "wild animal suffering" people) that says most wild animals don't have net-positive lives anyway. Huge clutches, r-selected species, most individuals die young from starvation, disease, or being eaten, so their lives are probably more suffering than happiness. If that's right, then preserving habitat is basically preserving a machine that churns out lots of short, grim lives — and habitat destruction might actually reduce total suffering, as long as the dying isn't drawn out. So here's what I want to know: does the standard animal-welfare case against habitat destruction survive that development, does it need repair, or is it just dead? I'm less interested in whether the net-negative claim is actually true and more in what it would mean for the argument. Which exact premise of the standard argument does the net-negative claim attack, and is there a version of the standard argument that still works even if the net-negative claim is true? Like, could you patch it by saying it only applies to long-lived large animals with decent lives, or do you have to switch to biodiversity or beauty as the reason to save habitats?
