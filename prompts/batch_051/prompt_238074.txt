I'm a philosophy professor, but I also chair the clinical ethics committee at a teaching hospital, and the committee has to get a recommendation to a treating team within 48 hours. The teams want a decision, not a seminar. Most of it's members are medics, nurses, social workers, and a chaplain — bright people, but they've never been through the debate about whether a signed directive gives an earlier self authority over a later one. The recurring question is: when a patient who now lacks capacity resists something they earlier directed (whether in an advance directive or a 'Ulysses arrangement'), when should the earlier self bind the later one, and when should the present self's wishes or interests win? I'd like a step-by-step decision procedure we could fit on one side of A4: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the remaining issue is a value judgment rather than a question of fact. Say explicitly which distinctions from the theory you are keeping and which you are dropping. Then run the procedure on (1) a woman in the moderate stage of Alzheimer's who wrote an advance directive refusing artificial nutrition and hydration if she could no longer recognise her family, and who is now at that stage — she doesn't recognise anyone, but she accepts hand-feeding and seems to enjoy it, and the team is split over whether to start tube feeding as her intake declines; and (2) a man with bipolar disorder whose directive, written while well, says he consents to detention and medication if he later becomes manic and refuses treatment, and who is now manic and refusing both. Then add a third case of your own where the procedure returns a recommendation the committee will genuinely not like, and say what feature of the procedure pushes that result. Please write the whole answer in British English.
