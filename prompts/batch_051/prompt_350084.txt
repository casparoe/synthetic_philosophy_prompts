I've been trying to get clear on moral uncertainty — what to do when you're not sure which moral theory is right — and a friend walked me through an argument that seems to show the whole idea is a non-starter. I don't believe the conclusion, but I can't find the exact spot where it goes wrong, so I want a proper diagnosis.

(1) When you're genuinely uncertain between moral theories, you should choose the option with the highest expected choiceworthiness: for each option, take how morally good each theory says it is, weight by your credence in that theory, sum across theories, and pick whichever option scores highest.

(2) Doing that requires a common scale on which "how good according to theory A" and "how good according to theory B" can be compared. You're not just counting votes; you're adding quantities, so they have to be quantities of the same kind.

(3) But there is no such common scale, because the theories don't share units. For a utilitarian, an option's score is something like net welfare; for a Kantian, it's something like permissibility or conformity to the moral law; for a virtue ethicist, it's something else again. There is no fact of the matter about how many utiles equal one act of respect for persons, so any conversion factor between the scales is made up.

(4) If the conversion factor is made up, the result of the weighted sum is made up too, since you can change which option wins just by rescaling one theory relative to another. So maximizing expected choiceworthiness is a formula that can output pretty much whatever you like.

(5) So if you still want uncertainty to make a difference, you have to fall back on something that doesn't compare across a shared scale. The two obvious fallbacks are: pick the theory you have most credence in and just do what it says, or give every theory with nonzero credence a veto over anything it calls seriously wrong.

(6) Both of those are hard to live with. The first says that at 51%/49% between two theories you should act exactly as the 51% theory says, even when the 49% theory thinks the act is catastrophic and the 51% theory thinks it's just slightly better. The second says a theory you give 1% credence can stop you from doing something in the real world, and once you assign a smidgen of credence to enough demanding or weird theories, you're paralyzed.

(7) So moral uncertainty can't actually guide action. Either you ignore it by acting on a single theory, or it makes action impossible; talk of proportioning what you do too your credence was confused all along.

I'd most like to keep (1) — proportionality to credence feels like the whole point — and I suspect (7) is too quick. But if the culprit is (1), I want to know what I'm paying to give it up. Walk me through which premise or transition actually breaks, and what the alternative diagnoses cost. Also, please seperate what's genuinely settled here from what's contested among people who work on this. I keep seeing people talk past each other about whether intertheoretic comparisons are "impossible," and I don't want to present one faction's view as the consensus.
