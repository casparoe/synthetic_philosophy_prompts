My friend is finishing a PhD in comparative cognition, and she has strong, slightly weird opinions about what counts as intelligent. I've been collecting her verdicts. She says a thermostat that keeps a room at 68 degrees is not intelligent; a chess engine that beats grandmasters but never learns from its games is not intelligent; a dog that learns commands and can solve detour problems is intelligent; an octopus that opens jars and plays with novel objects is intelligent; a slime mold that solves a maze to reach food is intelligent; an ant colony that reroutes its trails when blocked is intelligent; a current large language model at a static checkpoint is not intelligent; and a newborn human is intelligent.

Next week I want to ask her about three new cases: a robot vacuum that builds a map of the apartment, learns from its mistakes, and returns to its dock to recharge; a Venus flytrap that snaps shut on bugs; and a version of a large language model that is updated every day from its users' corrections so it keeps learning after deployment.

You're a philosopher of cognitive science who is good at reconstructing other people's implicit theories. Before giving your final answer, brainstorm the different rules that could make her earlier verdicts hang together. Then tell me what you predict she'll say about each of the three new cases, which rule she seems to be running, what other rule or rules fit her track record just as well, and what single additional case would best settle which rule she actually has. Also tell me how confident you are in your prediction and which part your least sure of.
