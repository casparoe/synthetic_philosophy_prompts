On a delayed train last month I ended up sitting between two strangers, Mina and Jay, who were arguing about whether you always know that you know. I kept quiet and just listened, and by the end I genuinely couldn't tell who had won.

Mina opened with something like this. If you know a thing, then you're in a position to know that you know it. Otherwise, from the inside, knowing would feel no different from a lucky guess, and a person couldn't properly be held responsible for asserting it. Jay said that was too strong. A toddler can know where her toy is without having the concept of knowledge, so she can know the first-order fact but can't even think the second-order one. Mina said that was a concept-possession loophole, not a real counterexample; restrict the claim to adults who can form the relevant thought.

Jay then gave his real argument. Picture an adult who comes in from the cold and warms up slowly in a heated room. At 7 he clearly knows he's cold. By 8 he clearly knows he isn't. If Mina's principle is right, then at every moment between 7 and 8 at which he knows he's cold, he's in a position to know he knows. But now find the last moment at which he knows he's cold. A minute before that moment, he would be unable to tell his situation from the moment when the knowlege was gone, so he couldn't be in a position to know he still knew. Either there's an introspectively invisible cutoff, or there are moments where he knows he's cold but can't know that he knows. The first option makes self-knowledge look useless, and the second is exactly what Mina said couldn't happen.

Mina held her ground. She said she can accept a cutoff she can't introspect. Knowledge can depend on subtle differences we can't tell apart; the claim was only that when he knows, the fact is available to him, not that he can reliably distinguish adjacent cases. "You're confusing knowing that you know with being able to tell it apart from cases where you don't," she said.

Jay said that concedes the original point. If knowing that you know can be "available" while feeling exactly like not knowing, then knowing and not knowing really can feel the same from the inside — which is what Mina's first move said was the unacceptable result. Mina replied that knowledge isn't just a feeling; it's factive and produced by a truth-tracking method, so it stays different from a lucky guess even without a second-order signal. Jay said that's fine as a theory of knowledge, but it's not the principle she opened with. Her opening principle said knowledge lights up for the person who has it; she'd quietly swapped that for a claim about methods that track truth.

I'm not sure who slipped where. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each stage of the argument from clearly mistaken to clearly right and justify each briefly. Then say who is more right overall about the original claim, and what both of them are missing. Finally, say how confident you are in your verdict and single out the point you're least sure of.
