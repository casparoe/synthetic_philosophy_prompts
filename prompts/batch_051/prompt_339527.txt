I have been trying to work out a claim about what deepfakes do to public evidence, and I keep reaching for a concept I call the enclosure of the epistemic backstop. By an epistemic backstop I mean the relatively cheap and widely available kind of evidence—above all photographic, audio, and video recordings—that public life relies on to settle disputes when testimony conflicts. The backstop is not constantly consulted, but its availability shapes what people can credibly assert and deny, and it has functioned, in effect, as a commons: almost anyone could produce a recording and expect others to treat it as defeasible but weighty evidence without first passing through an institutional gatekeeper. My central claim is that synthetic media do not mainly destroy this backstop; they enclose it. Enclosure does not make common land disappear—it replaces open access with gated, managed access—and analogously, deepfakes do not make recordings worthless. They make the persuasive force of a recording increasingly depend on institutional verification: forensic authentication, chain-of-custody infrastructure, provenance systems, platform content moderation, and the authority of newsrooms. The backstop would thus keep working for governments, large media organizations, and well-resourced litigants, while becoming less available as a check that ordinary citizens can wield against those same actors.

I would like an honest assessment of this enclosure concept. Is it coherent, or am I overloading an economic metaphor? Is it distinct from the familiar worry about the liar's dividend, or is it just a restatement of it? Has something like it already been worked out under another name? And most importantly, where does the analogy break—what does the enclosure framing get wrong about how recordings actually acquire or lose evidential force, and what would it predict that is false?
