I'm giving a department colloquium next month defending the knowledge norm of assertion: a speaker is properly warranted in asserting p only if she knows p. My line is that the live fight isn't really between a knowledge norm and a truth norm — it's between knowledge and a norm of being in a position to know, and the truth-norm people are defending a bit of a stalking horse. My argument: ordinary challenges like 'how do you know?' and the weirdness of 'p, but I don't know p' track knowledge, not mere truth or high probability, and the standard counterexamples (true, well-evidenced but unknowing assertions) are either improper in a way the truth norm can't explain or were made by someone in a position to know all along. The audience is mostly epistemologists — two of them quite committed Williamsonians — plus a couple philosophers of language who work on assertion and common ground, and one experimental philosopher who thinks folk judgments about assertibility are too context-sensitive for any univocal norm to survive. Give me the Q&A prep sheet: objections ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way this specific audience would phrase them, what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and the question I'm praying nobody asks because its the soft spot in my argument.
