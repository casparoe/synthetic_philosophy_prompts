A friend of mine has been reading a recent popular book on the ethics of consent, and he passed on what he took to be its central argument. His summary runs as follows:

'On the standard philosophical account, consent is valid when a competent person's private act of will favours the act in question: if, at the moment of the act, she genuinely wants it to happen, then she has consented, and the moral work is done by that inner willing rather than by anything she says or does. It follows that the invalidating factors are all defects of the will. Accordingly, deception invalidates consent whenever the lie causes the person to agree to something she would not have chosen with full information, whether the lie concerned the nature of the proposed act or only an incidental fact about the other party, such as his marital status. Coercion is a further defect of the same kind: the test is whether the constrained person faced any acceptable alternative, and if she did not, the resulting agreement cannot count as valid consent however the pressure came about.'

I suspect this is unfair to the careful literature, but I am not sure exactly where the errors are, and I do not want to overcorrect and miss what the summary gets right. Please give me an accuracy audit rather than a general impression: (1) which parts of the summary are defensible as a presentation of mainstream philosophical work on valid consent; (2) where it subtly misrepresents or conflates distinct claims; (3) what a defender of the relevant accounts would say in response to each questionable part; and (4) whether the conclusions the summary draws would survive once the misleading parts are corrected. In your assessment, be sure to address the contrast between consent as an inner attitude and consent as something communicated or performed, the difference between deception about the nature of an act and deception about merely collateral facts, and the difference between pressure that merely narrows a person's options and pressure that wrongfully coerces.
