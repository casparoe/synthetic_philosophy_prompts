I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and for the past year I have been reporting on a dispute that has begun to spill out of the machine-learning laboratories: whether a reinforcement learning agent, steered by a reward signal, could ever be in a state that should be called suffering. On one side, some researchers treat reward as the closest computational analogue of what affective neuroscientists call valence — the felt positivity or negativity of a state — and argue that a sufficiently rich, temporally fine-grained reward signal could amount to something like pleasure and pain. On the other side, some researchers regard this as a category error: a reward signal, they say, is a training gradient with a sign attached, no more valenced than a thermostat's error signal, whatever the agent's outward behavior looks like.

In an interview, one researcher proposed a governance mechanism meant to force the field to confront this question rather than wave it away. Under the proposal, any organization training or deploying a reinforcement learning agent above a threshold the agency sets — in compute, environment complexity, or behavioral autonomy — would have to file a public valence impact assessment. The filing would classify the agent's reward signal into one of three categories: not valenced; valenced but below a welfare threshold, meaning any felt quality is too weak, brief, or disconnected to matter morally; or possibly valenced above the threshold, which triggers a license and ongoing monitoring. A standing panel drawn from computational neuroscience, machine learning, and philosophy would review the filings. The panel could reject a 'not valenced' classification if it judged the reward architecture sufficiently similar to biological architectures known to implement affect, in which case the system would default to the third category.

I am not asking whether the proposal is wise. I am asking how it would behave in practice. In particular: What would it do to the way laboratories design reward functions, choose environments, and write their disclosures? What would it do to the panelists, who must make high-stakes classifications under deep uncertainty about consciousness and valence? What happens to systems deliberately built to sit just below the filing threshold, and to the work that migrates outside the agency's jurisdiction? How would the mechanism be exploited, and where would it break first? Does it converge on one of two degenerate outcomes — paperwork compliance with no effect on welfare, or a de facto moratorium on ambitious reinforcement learning systems — or something else? And which failure modes, if any, could be repaired without gutting the mechanism?
