I am a novelist, partway through a book set in a sharply stratified society, and I keep stalling on how my narrator ought to treat a character whose defining trait is envy of the wealthy. On reflection, I seem to hold two things that pull against each other.

I am attracted to a strong sceptical view of envy: that it is, strictly speaking, a vice. It is a positional emotion, fixated on the standing of another rather than on the good itself. It cannot be satisfied by any rise in the envier's own condition that does not lower the envied, and it invites self-deception, because it habitually dresses up spite as a judgement of desert. The systematic appeal of this view is that it is unified and unflattering. It gives me a clear principle as a writer: the character's envy is not to be trusted as perception; it is to be examined, and to a large extent resisted.

Yet there is a competing intuition I cannot shake. When the inequality at issue is itself unjust — when the fortune is inherited, or extracted from another's labour, or otherwise undeserved — the character's envy does not feel merely corrosive. It seems to track something true. It keeps her from naturalising the hierarchy, from converting her inferior position into a fact about her worth. Rawls gives this thought a respectable formulation in his discussion of what he calls excusable envy: where inequalities are stark enough to wound the self-respect of the worse off, envy is not simply a moral failing but a response that is in some measure excusable, even to be expected. In the world I am describing, the servant who envies the family she works for is not, I find, making an error about that world. She may be making the only emotionally available protest against it.

Those two positions cannot both be true exactly as I have stated them. I am not asking which side wins; I am asking what would justify each possible resolution. Give me the strongest formulations of the case for treating the intuition as a rationalisation to be debunked, and of the case for treating the theory as too crude to be retained unamended. Then give me your own negotiated verdict. In particular, consider whether there is a reading on which both survive — for example, by distinguishing envy proper from resentment or indignation, or by separating the cognitive content of the emotion from the wish that characteristically attends it — and say what the theory and the intuition would each have to concede on such a reading. Please also say how confident you are in your verdict and single out the point you are least sure of. Write your answer in American English.
