I am a novelist, and I serve on the board of a small foundation that gives emergency grants and first-book fellowships to working-class writers. The board has been offered a bequest from a late financier whose fortune came largely from high-interest consumer loans marketed in the same low-income communities our grantees come from; the loans were legal, but their terms were widely deplored as predatory. The bequest would roughly double our annual grants. Two board members want to decline: the foundation's independence—its reputation for not being bought—is our only real asset, and accepting would make our grantees question whose interests we ultimately serve. Two others want to accept: the man is dead, the money already exists, and refusing it would be moral vanity, a way of keeping our hands clean at the expense of the very people the grants would help. I have been asked, as the member most accustomed to examining how an institution tells its story, to draft the short written opinion to circulate before Friday's vote. It should recommend a concrete decision—accept, decline, or accept with stated conditions—and should give the strongest argument on the losing side its full due.
