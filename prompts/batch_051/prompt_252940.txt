So I've been having this running argument with my roommate, who's in a history PhD program—she keeps telling me "the Renaissance didn't happen" and that all these period labels are just later inventions, and its been bugging me because I teach high school world history and I have to put something on the slides. I've been drafting a short essay for a non-academic magazine, and I'd love comments, especially on accuracy and on whether the idea actually holds up. Here's the gist of it. I want to argue that these three labels are not invented in the same way or to the same degree. The Middle Ages is the most invented: it's basically a leftover category, a name for the stretch between antiquity and its supposed revival, and it only makes sense if you already accept the story of a rebirth. The Renaissance is half-real: it names an actual self-concious cultural program—there really were people who thought of themselves as reviving classical antiquity—but later historians turned that self-image into a claim about the period itself, as if a successful marketing campaign became the name of the era. The Scientific Revolution is the least invented, even though the label was stuck on retrospectively by historians rather than used by the people involved: the thing it picks out, a change in what counted as evidence, method, and intellectual authority in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, has a kind of internal coherence that doesn't depend on anyone at the time having a name for it. So my one-liner is: the Middle Ages is a gap, the Renaissance is a successful self-description, and the Scientific Revolution is a real rupture that just got its name late.

What I most want to know is whether this trichotomy is doing real work or whether I'm just rearranging the usual continuity-versus-change debate. Where would a careful historian of science most object? And is it fair to treat a self-conscious program as only half-real, or does that sneak in the assumption that self-images are less real than structural changes?
