I keep noticing a configuration in practical life and in moral reflection that I do not have a word for, and I am hoping someone can either name it precisely or help me carve it at the joints.

The clearest instance is my own attempt to be as good as possible. When I decide that every choice should be governed by the question "what would be maximally good here?", something in me tightens. I become careful, self-monitoring, a little mechanical. Friends notice that I am listening to them as if they were tasks, and they become less comfortable, less candid, and in the end less helped. My aunt, by contrast, does not orient her life around that question: she gardens, gossips, reads novels, and simply is the most dependable and generous person I know. It looks as though aiming directly at maximal moral goodness makes me worse at producing the very moral goodness I am aiming at, while a person who aims at other goods — beauty, humor, friendship, rest — reaches a goodness that looks higher, not lower.

I have seen the same shape in nearby cases. Aiming at happiness seems to make happiness recede, while immersion in something else lets it arrive. Trying to be spontaneous produces the opposite of spontaneity. Trying to force sleep keeps one awake, and trying hard to seem modest can itself become a kind of vanity. The pattern seems broader than the familiar thought that aiming at happiness can be self-defeating, because the moral case is not exactly about feelings: the goal remains a real standard, and the failure is not merely that the goal eludes you but that the manner of pursuit positively creates the opposite.

Does this configuration have an established name or associated literature? I am not asking for a bare label. What I want to know is which cases truly belong to the pattern, which only resemble it, and why the direct pursuit of the value backfires. Does the moral case share a single structure with the happiness and spontaneity cases, or is it a different phenomenon wearing the same clothes? Where are the boundaries: are there goals that can be pursued directly without loss, and what features mark the difference? If there is no established concept, I would be glad to hear a coined term together with a definition sharp enough to distinguish it from ordinary weakness of will, from the thought that one should sometimes simply not try too hard, and from the thought that some theories ask you not to believe them. Please also say how confident you are in your answer and which part of your account you are least sure about.
