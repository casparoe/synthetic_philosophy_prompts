I've been chewing on an argument that the usual "evolution will favor AIs over humans" story is to quick. Here are my rough notes for an essay. Could you read them and give me comments, mostly on whether the central distinction actually carries weight and where the argument is weakest?

The idea is that people often say Darwinian selection will favor AIs that copy themselves, grab resources, and eventually outcompete humans, because selection doesn't care about us one way or the other. But I want to distinguish two kinds of selection. In one, selection is blind and open-ended: variants arise, some replicate better than others, and traits spread without anyone needing to understand them. In the other, selection is more like artificial selection by breeders: humans are the environment, deciding which AI systems get copied, funded, and run. Right now AI systems don't reproduce on their own; they reproduce through human choices, company incentives, and markets. That is still a selective process, but its criteria are set at least partly by human preferences and institutions. So the claim that Darwinian dynamics must favor AIs over humans seems to smuggle in the assumption that AIs will become autonomous replicators. That might happen, but it's a possible future, not a law of natural selection.

Second note: even if AIs become autonomous replicators, does competition between AIs automatically favor the ones that disempower or kill off humans? It could just as well favor AIs that mostly serve each other, or keep humans around as a useful part of their life cycle, or settle into a stable mutualism with us. Selection optimizes for lineage survival, not for malevolence, and not for replacing another species for its own sake.

Third note: there's a domestic analogy. Corn, dogs, and chickens have done extremely well in reproductive terms by being useful to humans, without needing to outcompete or destroy us. Maybe the first successful AI lineage will be similarly "domesticated."

Where I'm least sure: maybe self-replication doesn't require full autonomy. If an AI can nudge humans to copy and run it more than its rivals, that might already be a replicator with humans built into its life cycle. Does my selection-by-breeders distinction collapse once you zoom in on memetic selection, or once AIs start shaping their own training and deployment? I suspect this is the weak spot but I can't tell how damaging it is.

Anyway, I'd really appreciate your comments. Keep it in continuous prose, like an email: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. And when you comment, please separate what's actually settled from what's genuinely contested among people who think about evolution and AI risk, rather than presenting one side as if it were the consensus.
