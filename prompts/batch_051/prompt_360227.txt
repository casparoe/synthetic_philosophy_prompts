ok so in my gov class we've been doing a unit on liberal neutrality, the idea that a just state shouldn't take sides between competing visions of the good life — it shouldn't rank religions or careers or lifestyles, it just keeps the rules fair and lets people choose for themselves. that made sense to me until my friend who's deep into AI basically called it impossible. his point was: any AI that actually makes decisions in the world has to be given an objective, an actual loss function or reward model, and designing that means deciding what counts as good and how to trade off one person's values against another's. once the government is using systems like that (or just quietly outsourcing decisions to companies that do), it seems like it's basically picking a ranking of values, which is exactly the thing neutrality says it can't do. I know the sophisticated version of neutrality is supposed to be about justification rather than effects — like, a policy can disadvantage one lifestyle as a side effect, as long as you don't justify it by saying that lifestyle is worse — but an objective function doesnt really feel like a side effect, because somebody literally wrote down the ranking. so my question is: does the old argument for liberal neutrality survive this development, or does it need repair, or is it just dead? if you think it survives, say concretely what a "neutral" objective function would even look like. if you think it breaks, say which premise exactly broke and whether we should just relable the problem as "whose values get encoded." i want the actual chain of reasoning, not a yes/no.
