I've agreed to give a lecture at a translation studies program—my partner's colleague chairs it and heard a contrarian version of this paper at a conference, so now I can't get out of it—and I need a Q&A prep sheet to survive what comes after. I'm a philosopher of language by training, but this crowd isn't analytic philosophy and the way I'd defend myself to my home department won't land. The thesis: when people in translation studies call a text or a word untranslatable, they are almost never making a descriptive claim about the absence of semantic equivalents in the target language. They are making a disguised normative claim about what a translation owes the source text or source community—about fidelity, power, hospitality, interpretive authority. So the ethics of translation isn't a consolation prize you turn to once semantic equivalence fails; it's the real content of most untranslatability talk, and treating "untranslatable" as a property a text just has produces the sloppy thinking the field has spent decades trying to get away from. The compressed argument: (1) if untranslatability were a descriptive semantic deficit, it would be local, temporary, and fixable, because languages borrow, coin, and paraphrase for anything a community actually wants to say; (2) the celebrated cases keep getting called untranslatable even after perfectly adequate target-language paraphrases are on offer—saying a term is untranslatable is often a way of refusing the available paraphrases, not a report that none exist; (3) what persists in such cases is a demand that the translation not assimilate the source to the target culture's categories, and that demand is about what should be done, not what can be done.

The audience is the translation studies faculty and doctoral students, three or four people from comparative literature, and one senior semanticist from the linguistics department. The translation studies people are mostly in the politicized, anti-domestication line: they distrust "equivalence" as an alibi, they think smooth fluent translation is a way of erasing difference, and they care a lot about who gets to decide what a text means. The comp lit people like the idea that something in a text resists transfer and that this resistance matters politically. The semanticist thinks untranslatability is a category mistake but also thinks translation studies underestimates how much languages can stretch. I suspect the translation studies people will object that I've filed their political claims under semantics, or that I've made untranslatability toothless by turning it into a matter of posture, or that my view can't explain why the label attaches to particular texts rather than to translation itself—and the objections will be delivered in the moral vocabulary of the field, so I need replies that meet them there.
