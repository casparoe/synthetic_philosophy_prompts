I've got a policy in my own thinking and I want to know whether it's defensible or just a bias dressed up as methodology. Whenever a general principle I hold conflicts with my intuitive verdict about a concrete case, I keep the case and adjust or drop the principle. I almost never let the principle overrule the case. My reasoning is that principles are compressed summaries of case judgments, so a clear case on the other side is like an observation falsifying a curve fit: the principle gives way, not the data point. A while ago I held that wronging someone always requires a victim; then I hit a case of someone desecrating the body of a person they'd never met, and I felt sure it was wrong in a way that wrongs the dead person. So I dropped the victim requirement rather than override the case. But a friend pointed out that the cases-over-principles policy is itself a principle, and I don't hold it tentatively the same way — I treat it as fixed method. And I worry I'm using it to make my views infalsifiable in practice, since I can gerrymander the "best" principle to fit any case I happen to feel strongly about. I also reach for the simpler principle when several fit the cases, and can't say whether that's the same move or an extra bias. So: is there a defensible version of cases-over-principles, when would the reverse policy be right, and are case intuitions really like data or is the analogy doing hidden work? Be blunt if the whole framing is off or the question can't be answered well as posed. Write it continuous prose like an email, no headings, bullets, bold, or tables.
