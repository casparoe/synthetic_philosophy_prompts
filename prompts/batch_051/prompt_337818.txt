In the theology reading group I'm in we've spent months going in circles on predestination and free will — all the arguments are layed out on the table and nobody's moved — and it's starting to look like the disagreement itself is the thing worth diagnosing, not any particular argument. The Calvinist side says: if God is sovereign and infallibly foreknows everything, then what I'll do tomorrow is already fixed, so my "free will" is just me doing the thing I was always going to do, with the illusion of alternatives. The Arminian side says that reading makes God the author of sin and turns us into puppets, so there has to be a genuine power to do otherwise, even if that means God's foreknowledge has to be understood differently or his control gets limited. Augustine shows up on both sides, which should probably tell us something: the Calvinists cite the late anti-Pelagian stuff, the Arminians cite the earlier stuff on the will.

My own hunch is that this is basically the compatibilism/incompatibilism fight wearing a theology costume, and that the two sides mean different things by "free" — one means "not coerced, acting from my own settled character," the other means "able to have done otherwise in the exact same circumstances." Once you split those senses, I suspect a lot of the specifically theological disagreement either dissolves or relocates, maybe to whether foreknowledge by itself, without a decree, is enough to fix the future. But I don't want a verdict on Calvin vs. Arminius. I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is: mostly verbal, a hidden factual/exegetical question, a disagreement about which cases count as data, or two bedrock intuitions about freedom and responsibility that can't be argued for from anything more basic? And given the diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a distinction, a redefined question, a better place to look — or should we just expect the stalemate?
