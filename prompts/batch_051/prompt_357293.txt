i've been toying with a principle for judging people in the past, and I want someone who actually knows historiography and moral philosophy to stress-test it before I keep using it.

I call it the live-option rule. You can hold a historical person morally responsible for failing to meet a standard only if that standard was a live option in their own situation — where "live" means something stronger than "somebody somewhere had written the idea down." I mean it was advocated by people they could have taken seriously without already being the kind of person who happens to agree with us today. So a Roman slaveholder isn't off the hook merely because abolition wasn't "invented" yet (versions of the idea existed), but because there was no abolitionist constituency in any corner of their world they could plausibly have answered to. A Nazi bureaucrat, by contrast, is fully on the hook, because anti-Nazi standards were live around him — his neighbors, his church, maybe his own pre-1933 self.

The reason I like the rule is that it turns the presentism charge into a question about an agent's social environment rather than about dates. "Presentism" becomes the mistake of applying a standard that was live for us but wasn't live for them — not of applying our standards full stop. Which means a lot of the judgments that get dismissed as presentist are actually fine, and the real argument becomes empirical: whose voices were available in that room in 1850, and could this person hear them?

What I want checked: (a) is the principle coherent, or does "live option" collapse once you press on it; (b) does it already exist under some name I don't know, so I should just point at someone instead of coining this; (c) where does it break — what cases does it get obviously wrong or go silent on; (d) is the empirical question it raises even answerable, or did I just move the hard part into the archives?
