In Part I of the *Discourse on Method*, René Descartes writes:

> For as to the reason or sense, inasmuch as it is that alone which constitutes us men, and distinguishes us from the brutes, I am disposed to believe that it is to be found complete in each individual; and on this point to adopt the common opinion of philosophers, who say that the difference of greater and less holds only among the accidents, and not among the forms or natures of individuals of the same species.

Descartes is here defending a binary picture: reason marks a division in kind between humans and nonhuman animals, and within humanity it is present whole in every individual. But suppose we now take seriously the idea that sentience and the capacities relevant to moral status are matters of degree—that an insect is not a thermostat, a dog is not an insect, and a human is not merely a dog scaled up, but there is no single point along this continuum at which “reason or sense” switches on cleanly. Does Descartes’s argument survive this changed assumption? Identify the specific premise under pressure, and say whether a repaired version can still preserve something of his distinction—perhaps between beings that can give reasons and beings that cannot—without the binary. Also say what finding or argument would change your mind, and where in your reasoning a different reading of the passage or a different empirical finding about animal or artificial cognition would most likely overturn your verdict.
