I have been re-reading Plato's proposal, in the Laws, that the city should preserve and regulate the institution of the drinking party, and I find myself unsure whether the argument can survive a modern empirical picture of what alcohol actually does. As I read it, Plato's thought is that wine acts as a kind of solvent on self-command: by loosening the grip of shame and sober inhibition, it exposes what a person is like underneath—and, provided the party is supervised by sober, older directors, this makes the symposium something like a moral testing ground and gymnasium, in which citizens first reveal and then practice regaining control over appetites they normally keep hidden. The proposal seems to rely on two assumptions: that intoxication is a tolerably uniform process of stripping away acquired self-control so as to reveal a stable underlying self, and that this same process can be turned to training ends.

Suppose now, as much recent work on intoxication suggests, that alcohol is not well described as a uniform disinhibitor. Its effects vary with dose, expectation, setting, and personal history; it degrades working memory, foresight, and restraint unevenly; and much of what drinkers say and do is shaped by the social script of the occasion rather than by the unmasking of a pre-existing character. Grant that changed assumption. Does Plato's educational argument survive it, or does it collapse? Which of the two assumptions is really doing the work, and which one does the modern picture put under pressure? Is there a repaired version of the argument that keeps the supervised party as a form of moral education while abandoning the claim that wine reveals the true self—or does the institution lose its rationale once drunkenness can no longer be treated as a reliable diagnostic of character and a safe instrument for practice? I am interested in the fate of the argument itself, not in a historical report on what Plato scholars have said.
