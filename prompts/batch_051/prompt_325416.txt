I recently came out of a long argument with a friend about whether populist appeals to "the will of the people" rest on a category mistake. He argued that the charge is question-begging: a people, he claimed, is an agent in the same ordinary sense that an individual person is — it has interests, a continuing identity through time, and a will that institutions can either express or frustrate. His strongest move was that internal division does not defeat the analogy, because an individual's will is itself an integration of conflicting impulses achieved over time; if a person counts as a single agent despite inner conflict, then a people can too. From this he concluded that democratic politics is the discovery and execution of that will, and that dismissing popular judgment as a muddle is what elites say when they dislike what the people want.

I am skeptical that the analogy can carry that much, but I want it audited rather than just rejected. Please treat it as an analogy in common use — the people as a person writ large — and ask how far it holds when it is put to work to license the conclusion that a single authentic popular will ought to be politically authoritative. I would like:

1. An explicit table of correspondences: what plays the role of the person's will, preferences, beliefs, body, memory, self-deception, and capacity both to be wrong about its own interests and to change its mind.
2. The main structural disanalogies, separating those that are damaging for this populist use from those that are merely superficial.
3. An account of what the analogy quietly imports that the target may lack — a first-person standpoint, genuine unity, an authentic will beneath the plurality of voices, and preferences that are not manufactured — and which of those imports are doing the actual work.
4. A verdict on how much of the populist conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether a corporation, a crowd, or a body politic of overlapping groups would be a better analogy, with a note on what that replacement would change.

Throughout, indicate how confident you are in each substantive claim, and single out the point you are least sure of. End your response with a one-sentence summary of your verdict as the final line.
