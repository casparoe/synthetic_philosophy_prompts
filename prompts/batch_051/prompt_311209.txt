Take Martin Luther King Jr.'s Letter from Birmingham Jail as a case study for public reason — specifically the Rawlsian idea that when we argue in public about constitutional essentials and basic justice, we owe each other reasons that aren't just our religious commitments, because coercion has to be justifiable to people who don't share our comprehensive doctrines. I know the history, so don't recap it. What I want is the mapping.

Here are the facts I want you to treat as given, since the model you'd be might not have them reliable: the letter was written in April 1963 to a group of white clergymen who had published a statement criticizing the Birmingham campaign as unwise and untimely. In it King defends nonviolent direct action against the charge of extremism, distinguishes just from unjust law by appeal to Augustinian and Thomistic natural-law ideas, invokes the imago Dei and the prophets, and at the same time appeals to the American creed, the Declaration, and constitutional rights. It's an open letter, published in newspapers, addressed formally to fellow clergy.

Now the questions I actually want worked through. First, be disciplined about who the relevant public is here. Public reason usually says it governs the public political forum and matters of basic justice — does an open letter from a Baptist minister to eight clergymen about a civil rights campaign count, or is it doing something adjacent but different, like intra-community discourse? If it doesn't cleanly fall in public reason's jurisdiction, say so and do the next-best analysis instead of forcing it.

Second, separate the letter's religious moves from its political moves, and say which are doing the justificatory work for the injustice of segregation and the permissibility of the campaign. Are the natural-law arguments meant as public reasons (shared ground available to nonbelievers), or are they loaded with theology that only works if you already accept a religious picture? Is the religion load-bearing, or pastoral packaging around arguments King could have made in purely political terms? I want a careful mapping, premise by premise, not a vibe.

Third, take the proviso seriously: Rawls allows comprehensive reasons so long as proper political reasons are also offered in due course. Does King satisfy that, fail it, or is he a case where the proviso is doing a suspicious amount of work after the fact? If he satisfies it, what exactly is the political reason he supplies, and when? If he doesn't, is that a problem for him or for the proviso?

The fourth question is the payoff. The standard objection is that public reason is unfair to religious citizens because it tells them to translate their deepest grounds into secular-ish terms before they can speak in public. Does King show that's no real burden, because he seems perfectly able and willing to speak both languages at once? Or does it only look that way because we like his side and are reading the translation back into him? What does this episode do to public reason — confirm it, expose it as a rationalization, or show that the whole framework never anticipated a case like this? And where is public reason silent about what actually moved people in Birmingham, rather than wrong about it?

Keep the answer honest: if the theory can't explain some feature of the case, say so and leave it unexplained instead of bending the case or the theory. I'm less interested in a verdict than in a map of which parts of the letter the theory can digest and which parts it chokes on.
