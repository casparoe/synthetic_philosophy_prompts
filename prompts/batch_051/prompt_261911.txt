A film-philosophy seminar is reading Peter Weir's The Truman Show through Adorno and Horkheimer's concept of the culture industry. Take the concept, for purposes of the prompt, to be the thesis that under administered social conditions culture is produced industrially; that standardization is hidden behind the look of novelty and spontaneity (what the theory calls pseudo-individuality); that cultural works become commodities that merge with advertising; and that entertainment offers not a break from work but an extension of it, an escape that returns the viewer to the same conditions rather than to any real alternative. Using only the film's general story and well-known scenes, and not relying on verbatim dialogue, assess whether Seahaven should be read as a depiction of the culture industry, a product of it, or both. Treat Christof and the show's apparatus as one level of the industry, the film's own status as a Hollywood product as a second, and the audience—on-screen and in the theater—as a third. Say where the Adornian lens genuinely exposes the film's logic, where the film resists the reading, and whether the ending counts as genuine refusal or as the kind of managed transgression the theory predicts will be reabsorbed. End by saying what the film gives the theory that a straight argument does not.
