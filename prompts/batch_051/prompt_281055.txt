At my writing group on Tuesday, two of the other novelists got into it about modal realism — roughly, David Lewis's view that all possible worlds are just as real as this one — and I want to steal the argument for a scene, but I need to know who's actually winning at each turn before I put it in my protagonist's mouth. No citations or reading lists please; I just want the reasoning picked apart.

Elena opened (move 1) by saying the view actualy explains something about fiction she's never been able to articulate: if every complete way a cosmos could be is equally real, then a novel isn't inventing people from nothing; it's more like a report about a world that was already there. That's why "did the count regret the murder years later?" can have a determinate answer even when the author never wrote it down.

Marcus said (move 2) that this is exactly backwards. If every possible version of my novel exists somewhere, then the version where the count kills his brother and the version where he doesn't are both real. My choices don't settle anything; they just point at one pre-existing option. That takes the engine out of writing, because writing runs on the feeling that choosing this resolution over that one matters.

Elena (move 3) said pointing is still an achievement. A map doesn't create the coastline, but making a good map is hard. And a novelist who "finds" the right world is a better author than one who points at the wrong one.

Marcus (move 4) said the map analogy hides a load-bearing difference: maps can be checked against a shared actual coastline, but the text of a novel underdetermines which world it's about. Any number of candidate worlds fit every sentence, so the word "description" is doing no work until someone supplies a rule, and that rule is just the reader's convention.

Elena (move 5) said there's a standard fix. The novel picks out the closest worlds — the ones most similar to the actual world — that make the text true. Some worlds are nearer than others, and "what happens in the novel" is what happens in the closest worlds. So the rule isn't made up; it's part of the furniture.

Marcus (move 6) said "most similar" just re-installs the human practice under a new name. Which similarities count depends on genre, on what the reader cares about, on what our actual literary community treats as intact. So Elena sold the view as parsimonious, but she's smuggling the reader's standards back in.

Elena (move 7) said even so, the view buys you something: no special ontology of half-real fictional characters. A made-up name can refer perfectly well to a real possible person, so you don't need strange entities that neither exist nor don't. That's worth the price.

Marcus (move 8) said the price is higher than she admits. On the view, "actual" is just an indexical, like "here" or "now" — it means "the world I'm in." There's a world where he says this very sentence while drinking tea and another where he says it while drinking coffee, and neither is more real. That flattens the difference between the novel I wrote and the ones I didn't into a matter of cosmic seating. A working novelist needs to believe one ending is genuinely better than another, and his view can't say that.

They asked me to settle it; I couldn't. I know they're both being unfair to the other at a couple of points, but I can't sort out where. Could you do a referee's card, point by point, rather than a flat winner? Don't just say "both make good points" and stop; I need alot more fine-grained than that.

Format:
(1) Summary — restate the dispute in about 100 words.
(2) Move-by-move — a table with columns Move / Who wins that move / Why, one row per move 1–8, total around 350 words.
(3) Each side's strongest point — about 120 words.
(4) What both are missing — about 120 words.
(5) Bottom line — about 100 words, who's more right overall and whether there's a position that keeps the best of both.
