A city council, worried that it repeals old ordinances without knowing why they exist, adopts the following procedure. Any proposal to repeal or substantially amend a municipal ordinance in force for at least twenty-five years must include a public Chesterton report prepared by the repeal's sponsors. The report must set out (a) the best reconstruction of the original problem the ordinance addressed and why lighter remedies were thought insufficient; (b) which identifiable groups currently depend on the ordinance or have adjusted their conduct around it; and (c) an estimate of the time and money required to reinstate a functionally equivalent rule if repeal proves mistaken. The report is posted for ninety days of public comment before any vote. If the repeal passes and a substantially equivalent ordinance is re-enacted within five years, the sponsors are listed in a public hasty-repeal register and barred for two years from sponsoring further repeals.

Defenders describe this as an institutionalized Chesterton fence and a Burkean check on reform; critics say it is a machine for manufacturing status quo bias. Evaluate it as a mechanism rather than as a statement of principle. How would it actually operate? What would it incentivize among council members, city staff, interest groups, and residents? Where would it be gamed first, what second-order effects would it have on the content of newly passed ordinances and on the city's capacity to remove genuinely obsolete rules, and would it tend to preserve the reasons behind old rules or merely preserve the rules themselves? Finally, say whether its most likely failure modes could be corrected without gutting the scheme, and how. Please write your answer in American English.
