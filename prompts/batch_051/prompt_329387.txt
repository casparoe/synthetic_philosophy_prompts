I'm a grad student in a philosophy of computation seminar, and we've been going back and forth about what should count as an effective method. The criteria we were given, roughly: it has to be specified finitely and unambiguously; each step must be mechanical, the kind of thing a clerk or a machine could do without insight; it must halt after finitely many steps for every input; and when it halts it has to give the right answer.

Last week I brought in a little brute-force program I wrote: a satisfiability checker for propositional logic. It builds the whole truth table for whatever formula you give it—for n propositional variables it writes out all 2^n assignments and checks whether any row makes the formula true; if yes it says satisfiable, otherwise unsatisfiable. It's about twenty lines of code. It's finite, unambiguous, totally mechanical, and it always halts, because a formula has only finitely many rows in its truth table; and it's always correct. But for a formula with 300 variables the table has about 2^300 rows, which is more than the number of atoms in the observable universe, so running it is physically impossible even though the procedure is finite and in principle halts.

So I'm stuck on a simple thing: does my program qualify as an effective method by those four criteria, and if it does, are the criteria missing something important? I get that computability theorists tend to say "effective" just means what a Turing machine can do, in which case my program is in. But if an effective method is supposed to be a method an actual reasoner could use, it doesn't feel very effective. Act as a philosopher of computer science and think through this carefully. Check the case against each criterion one by one, say where the criteria underdetermine the verdict, and tell me whether we need a fifth criterion such as feasibility—or whether feasibility is a different question entirely that the four criteria were never meant to answer. If you think the right line is polynomial time, or finishing within the physical limits of the actual universe, defend that line against the obvious objections; my classmates wont stop pointing out that some polynomial algorithms are absurdly slow and some exponential ones are fine at the sizes anyone actually cares about. Don't just give me a yes or no.
