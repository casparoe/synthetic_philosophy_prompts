I got into an argument last night about whether it's ever sensible to say 'I give myself an 80 percent chance of going to the gym tomorrow.' My friend said sure, it's just uncertainty about your own future willpower, no different from the weather. I think there's something categorically weird about it, but I couldn't explain why, so I slept on it and produced this little numbered argument. I'd like you to act as a philosopher working in action theory and decision theory, steelman each step as charitably as you can, and then tell me whether the argument is valid and sound. If it fails, say exactly which step or transition fails and what a corrected version of that step would have to say; if it goes through, tell me what the conclusion actually commits me to. And please write the whole reply as continuous prose, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — just like an email.

(1) To assign a probability to your own future action is to make a prediction about a system — yourself — and the probability is supposed to describe how likely that system is to produce the action.

(2) A prediction about yourself is self-information, and you cannot simply quarantine it while you deliberate. If I genuinely think I'm 80 percent likely to skip the gym, that number carries information about my motivation, my fatigue, how much I hate the workout, and so on, and rationality means conditioning on relevant information you have about yourself.

(3) But information you condition on during deliberation changes the state that produces the action. The state you're in once you've absorbed 'I'm 80 percent likely to skip' is not the state you were in when the probability was being evaluated.

(4) So the probability feeds back into the very process it was supposed to measure. It's no longer a fixed parameter of the choice; it's one of the causes of the choice — more like a forecast that changes the weather than a forecast of the weather.

(5) A probability is only well-defined over a fixed state of the system being predicted. When the act of assigning it shifts the state, there is no fixed state left for the probability to be about, unless the feedback reaches a stable fixed point.

(6) In ordinary deliberation the question is held open, so there's no stable fixed point to settle into. Therefore, while you are genuinely deliberating, you cannot assign any well-defined probability to one of your own choices. Deliberation crowds out prediction.

I'm most suspicious of step (5), because it starts to sound like it's assuming the only probabilities are over fixed, observer-independent states, and maybe there's another kind of probability that lives inside the deliberation instead of outside it. But I can't make that precise. My friend thinks step (3) is obvious nonsense because learning something about yourself doesn't change the thing the belief is about, the way learning about the weather doesn't change the weather. Which of us is closer? And is there a nearby version of this argument that is actually sound, even if this one isn't?
