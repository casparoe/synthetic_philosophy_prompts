Two friends of mine got into it at a party after a third friend, Natalie, had a rare sword deleted from her MMO account by another player using an exploit. Not whether she was upset — she had spend months grinding for it and cried when it vanished — but whether she really lost anything, and whether the whole category of virtual harms is real. I kept going back and forth, and after an hour they turned to me to settle it. I couldn't, so now I'm handing it to you. Here's the back-and-forth as best I remember it.

(1) Priya said: Natalie was genuinely wronged. The sword is property in every way that matters — it's an entry in a database, but so is a bank balance. It took real labor to acquire, it's scarce inside the game's economy, and other players treat it as hers. Saying it's "just pixels" confuses the substrate with the thing itself; a dollar bill is just paper and ink, and stealing one is still stealing.

(2) Tom said: a bank balance is backed by legal institutions that will actually enforce your claim. The game company can delete Natalie's item tomorrow and that's in the terms of service she accepted. She didn't own a thing; she had a revocable license to use some server-side code. If you strip away the real-world effects — money, time, distress — what's left isn't property, it's a shared fiction.

(3) Priya said: the terms-of-service point shows that the law refuses to protect her, not that the sword isn't property. A contract can be exploitative. And the sword could be sold for real money on the grey market, so deleting it made her materially worse off. The fact that its value depends on a community's practices doesn't make it fake — money, tenure, and national borders are all practice-dependent, and they're real enough to ground theft and trespass.

(4) Tom said: "could be sold" is doing a lot of work. The grey market is against the rules, barely liquid, and most players never cash out. But even if I grant the financial loss, that supports my point: the loss matters only where it touches ordinary stuff like money, time, and distress. The virtual sword itself isn't what's harmed. If Natalie sobbed over the death of a character in a novel, we'd call the sobbing real and the death fictional; same story here.

(5) Priya said: a fictional character isn't anyone's property and can't be sold. And you just conceded that Natalie was genuinely harmed in the ordinary sense, which is all I needed — if a real harm occurred, the act was a real wrong. You can't say "she was truly harmed, but nothing was truly taken." That's like saying a widow was truly harmed by her husband's death but the marriage wasn't real.

(6) Tom said: the widow analogy cuts the other way. A marriage is real because there's a whole legal and social institution upholding it. The MMO "property" has only a company's say-so and a black market. Natalie's distress is real, but it's a psychological event in her, not a fact about a virtual sword.
