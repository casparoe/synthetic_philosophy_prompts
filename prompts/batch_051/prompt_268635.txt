I have settled, tentatively, on prioritarianism as the most defensible account of distributive ethics: a benefit of a fixed size matters more, morally, the worse off its recipient is; the weighting function is strictly increasing and strictly concave in well-being; and equality itself has no intrinsic value. That final commitment is why I think prioritarianism, unlike egalitarianism, can press the levelling-down objection rather than having to blunt it: making the better off worse off while benefiting no one is simply a loss, and the view says so outright. I want a map of what would have to be true for this position to be wrong. Which cases, patterns of ordinary moral judgement, structural considerations, or formal results would count against it, and which defeaters should I treat as decisive rather than merely awkward? Please sort them, and say which ones a committed prioritarian should themselves concede as counting against the view rather than dismissing as a change of subject. I would like especially careful treatment of four pressure points: the arbitrariness of the weighting function, and whether that makes prioritarianism unfalsifiably flexible; the ex ante/ex post distinction under risk, which seems to pull the view in two directions; the possibility that at least some of our concern is tracking relative standing rather than absolute level; and the thought that a fully generalised levelling-down objection might reapply to prioritarianism once we allow that undeserved advantage is itself a bad that levelling down could remove. Say as well what prioritarianism commits me to that utilitarianism and egalitarianism need not accept, and whether those commitments give the view genuine exposure rather than merely relocating the debate. I personally suspect the weighting-function objection is the deepest. Tell me whether that is right, and answer in British English.
