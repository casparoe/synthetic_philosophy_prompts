I retired a few years back after four decades in organic chemistry, and I've been sitting in on a graduate epistemology seminar at the local university. The instructor knows I'm comfortable with probability and asked if I'd design one problem for the take-home final. I said yes — Bayesian stuff scratches the same itch as fitting a rate constant to noisy kinetics data.

The course is an upper-level MA/Phd seminar, and the problem should take about 90 minutes. It has to be on Jeffrey conditionalization and uncertain evidence: the rule where you reweight your probabilities across a partition of possible observations without setting any single cell to 1, while leaving the probabilities conditional on each cell fixed. I don't want the usual plug-and-chug question they've all seen. Here's a seed I've been fiddling with: a chemist runs an assay whose readout can be red or blue, but the strip is old and sometimes reads ambiguously, so after looking she's only 0.8 confident it's red rather than certain. She has priors over some hypothesis H the strip is supposed to test, and she updates by Jeffrey's rule. I'd like the problem to make students work out whether two successive uncertain observations like this commute (does the order matter?), and whether Jeffrey updating is standard conditioning in disguise — conditioning on the chemist's visual experience itself, with a suitable chance of misreading. I vaguely suspect the second is yes if you enrich the algebra, but I want the students to argue it rather than just assert it.

Could you draft: (i) the exam question itself, worded so a student can't answer just by reciting the rule; (ii) a model answer of roughly 700 words that covers both the mechanics and the conceptual issue; and (iii) a marking scheme out of 25, with five or six must-hit points, the marks each is worth, and a seperate sentence on the typical mistake each point is catching. Keep the whole thing self-contained — no outside sources or reading lists.
