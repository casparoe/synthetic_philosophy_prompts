You're a philosopher who works on metaethics, especially the relationship between facts and values (the is–ought gap, Moore's open-question argument, all that). I want you to actually take a side, not survey the options, and I want your reasons on the table.

Here's the asymmetry I want you to make sense of. In ordinary moral argument, people are extremely selective about when "that's natural" gets to count as a point in favor of something. If someone says "humans evolved as omnivores, so eating meat is morally fine," everybody reaches for the same move: naturalistic fallacy, can't derive an ought from an is, evolution explains but doesn't justify, etc. But the same people, five minutes later, will say things like "humans naturally form long-term pair bonds, so we shouldn't treat marriage as merely a social construction" or "it's natural for a mother to bond with her child, so that bond is valuable and worth protecting." There, the "natural" is doing real justificatory work and nobody reaches for the fallacy.

Now, you could say both moves are invalid and the second group should be embarrassed too. But I don't think that's what most people mean, and I'm not even sure it's right. So the question: is there a defensible asymmetry here? Is there a principle that lets some appeals to natural facts serve as reasons while others get ruled out as fallacious, or is "appeal to nature" just a rhetorical bludgeon people pick up when they dislike the conclusion?

Make it hard for yourself. Deal with the disease objection (diseases are natural and we treat them as bad, so "natural" clearly isn't doing the work by itself), with the possibility that "natural" is always just a placeholder for something else (health, flourishing, design, typical functioning), and with the difference between deducing an ought from a bare is versus using a natural fact as evidence for a value that's already in play. I also want you to be careful about the fact that the naturalistic fallacy and the is–ought gap aren't quite the same thing—Moore's target was defining 'good' in natural terms, not deriving normative conclusions from descriptive premises—so don't just run them together unless you say why.

Start by brainstorming the strongest versions of both positions: the one that says the asymmetry is defensible and the one that says it's just bias. Then argue for whichever you think is closer to true. Commit. Tell me which side you'd bet on, how confident you are, and what would change your mind.
