Last night I had a long argument with a friend who works in economics. Her claim was that it is a category mistake to object to her models on the grounds that their assumptions are false. She reached for the same analogy all evening: a model is like a map. A subway map leaves out trees, elevation changes, curvature of the tracks, and exact distances, and it is a better map for doing so; no one rejects the subway map because it fails to resemble the territory, because the only question is whether it gets you to your destination. She then applied the analogy across the board. Perfect competition, zero transaction costs, agents who plan with full foresight, and firms that maximize as if they solved an explicit optimization problem are, in her view, deliberate distortions of the same kind as the spacing of stations on the map, and a model is to be judged solely by its predictive success. When I objected that a map is meant to guide someone through an independently mapped territory, whereas her models are supposed to explain why outcomes happen and what would happen under policies that have not been tried, she answered that prediction and navigation are the same activity for these purposes, and added that Friedman had settled the methodological question long ago. The argument ended exactly where it began.

I would like an audit of this analogy rather than an adjudication of the underlying dispute. Please give the correspondence explicitly — what in the analogy plays the role of the territory, the map, the mapmaker's choices, the legend, the omitted features, the traveler, and the destination — and then identify the places where the mapping breaks down. In particular, separate the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion my friend drew from the ones that are merely incidental, and say what the analogy quietly imports about the modeler's knowledge of what has been omitted and about the purpose for which the model is used. Finally, tell me whether her conclusion — that the falsehood of assumptions should not count against an economic model — survives the audit, whether a better analogy would carry the weight she wants the map to carry, and whether the map analogy is licensed to prove what Friedman's F-twist is usually taken to establish. A table of correspondences would be welcome. Please answer in American English.
