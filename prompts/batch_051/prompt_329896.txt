I am preparing a seminar on Plato's treatment of wine in the *Laws*, and I have so far isolated three distinct functions that he assigns to the managed drinking party. First, it is a test of character: wine deprives men of their usual restraint and exposes the dispositions of the soul more cheaply and safely than actual temptations would. Second, it is a training in self-control: by subjecting men to pleasure under shared rules, it builds the capacity to resist shamelessness and excess. Third, it is a remedy for the infirmities of age, as in this passage from Plato's *Laws*, Book 2, 666b–c, translated by R.G. Bury:

> But when a man has reached the age of forty, he may join in the convivial gatherings and invoke Dionysus, above all other gods, inviting his presence at the rite (which is also the recreation) of the elders, which he bestowed on mankind as a medicine potent against the crabbedness of old age, that thereby we men may renew our youth, and that, through forgetfulness of care, the temper of our souls may lose its hardness and become softer and more ductile, even as iron when it has been forged in the fire.

I want another distinct function or justification for the institution of wine in the *Laws*, one that is not on my list and not a restatement, special case, or combination of these three. In particular, do not simply say that it makes people sing; if singing matters, tell me what further end that serves and why it is a fourth rationale rather than part of the medicine for old age. Then tell me honestly whether the fourth function is genuinely present in the text and whether it is any good as an argument.
