I’ve got an epistemic habit I’ve been using for years, and I’m now not sure whether it’s a principle or just a shortcut. I first picked it up from reading Laplace’s Philosophical Essay on Probabilities as an undergraduate, where he defends treating events as equally probable when we have no reason to favour any of them. I’ve since generalised the idea into something more modern: whenever I need to set a prior and I know only a few constraints — say a parameter is bounded and has a known mean, or a set of probabilities must sum to one across n options — I pick the distribution that maximises entropy subject to those constraints. I tell myself this is the most honest thing to do because it doesn’t smuggle in information I don’t have. But my friend says this is a confidence trick: selecting the maxent distribution is itself a strong assumption about the world, and different ways of describing the same constraints can give different results. She gave the example of a quantity constrained to be between 0 and 1 with no other information: maxent says uniform, but if I had instead parameterised it as, say, the odds, the most even-looking distribution is not the same. That unsettled me, but I still keep using the rule in practice, for instance when I’m trying to estimate risks from sparse data at work. Is the maximum entropy policy rationally defensible as a way of setting priors, or am I dressing up a bias as a principle? If it’s defensible at all, can I state a version that keeps the good bits without the bad? Please answer in British English.
