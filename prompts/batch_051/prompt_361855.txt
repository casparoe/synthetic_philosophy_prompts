I'm giving a department colloquium next month defending a reading of Rousseau's Letter to d'Alembert that's meant to be a bit revisionist. My thesis is that the standard reading—that he opposed theater because it corrupts morals, licenses bad passions, and degrades actors—understates the real argument. I think the deeper worry is about civic spectatorship: theater trains people to be an audience, watching and judging from outside, and that stance leaks into how citizens relate to their own political community. On my reading, this explains why Rousseau treats a theater in Paris differently from a theater in Geneva: in a large, already-corrupt capital it's just another distraction, but in a small republic it would displace the participatory practices that keep citizens republican. It also explains how he can grant that a well-made play is well made and still object to it, because the problem isn't primarily the content being shown; it's the relation to the audience that showing creates.

The audience will be mostly political theorists and early-modern historians of philosophy. There are maybe six Rousseau specialists, a couple of people who work on deliberative and epistemic democracy, and the rest are generalists who know the Social Contract much better than the Letter. The deliberative-democracy people are likely to resist the participation/spectatorship opposition, and the Rousseau people are going to think I've underweighted the moral psychology of amour-propre and his actual claims about women, actors, and Geneva. The chair is a Kantian who will think the whole thing is a mess, but she'll probably keep quiet.

Give me a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of my reading. I want the objections I'm actually going to get from this room, phrased roughly the way those people would phrase them, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are. For each, include a reply that could land in two minutes or so, and a note on where that reply is weakest. Also include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.

Assume I've already handled the obvious textual-conservative objection that I'm importing a twentieth-century concern with spectatorship into an eighteenth-century text about mores. I want the harder stuff.
