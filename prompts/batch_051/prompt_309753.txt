William James writes in *The Moral Equivalent of War*: “Our ancestors have bred pugnacity into our bone and marrow, and thousands of years of peace won’t breed it out of us. The popular imagination fairly fattens on the thought of wars.”

I take James seriously that the martial virtues are deeply rooted and will not simply vanish in a peaceful society, and I find myself with a set of verdicts about their peacetime descendants that I cannot yet make cohere. I admire the firefighter who runs into a burning building to save a child, and I count that a real form of courage. I admire the peacetime soldier who endures harsh training and stands ready for a war that never comes, even though I wish the role were unnecessary. I admire the nonviolent protester who stands calmly before an armed police line, but I would hesitate to call that virtue martial. I admire the boxer who trains for years to master controlled aggression, even though I do not want to see anyone badly hurt. I do not admire the corporate “warrior” who speaks of crushing competitors and running a war room, even when the risks are real and the discipline is genuine. And I feel nothing but contempt for the online polemicist who talks endlessly of war, strength, and glory but has never done anything hard or dangerous. Is there a single principle that captures these verdicts, or at least most of them? Please state the principle or candidates precisely, test them against my cases, and tell me which of my verdicts they force me to give up. Also say what the principle predicts about a case I have not listed: an alpine climber who takes mortal risks primarily to test themselves.
