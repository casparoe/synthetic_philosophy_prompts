I have been trying to work out when self-examination is good for a person and when it becomes harmful, and I keep arriving at two positions that each feel right until they are pressed. Call them the examinationist position and the ruminationist position. The examinationist says turning attention onto your own thoughts, motives, and feelings is the main instrument of self-knowledge and a serious life, and that any harm is just the pain of hard honesty. The ruminationist says repetitive, mood-brooding self-focus is itself a large part of what keeps people stuck in depression, anxiety, and self-criticism, and that much of what calls itself self-examination is the very habit that makes people worse. I want to produce an adversarial collaboration between these two positions: a document both sides could sign, recording what they share, where they genuinely differ, and what would change their minds. Draft that document. It should contain (1) a numbered list of claims about self-examination that both positions accept, made as strong as possible without either side conceding what it actually denies; (2) a table of the real disagreements, each phrased so that each position would accept the wording of its own side; and (3) the cruxes—the sub-questions such that a settled answer would force at least one side to move—each paired with a concrete situation or small intervention both sides would agree bears on the crux, and each side's prediction about it recorded in advance. Be committed in the drafting: choose the specific cruxes and formulations that best reconstruct these two positions, and say why those are the right ones to make explicit, rather than offering a menu of every possible disagreement.
