I'm putting together a handout for a philosophy of social science seminar on whether generational categories — Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z, stuff like that — are real kinds or just marketing demographics with fuzzy cutoffs. Could you write a short decision tree or taxonomy of the positions? Build it from the distinctions first rather than from a list of names. Each branch node should be a question a first-year grad student can answer without already knowing the jargon, e.g. (a) does membership require a shared formative historical experience, or is being born inside the usual year-range enough? (b) are the boundaries meant to carve nature at the joints, or are the cutoffs admitted to be arbitrary? (c) do the labels do genuine causal-explanatory work about attitudes and behavior, or do they just redescribe those things? (d) does the fact that people now organize their self-understanding around these labels make the categories real regardless of shared traits? Try to include leaves for: a strong social/historical-kind realism where defining events delimit generations; a moderate statistical-regularity view with fuzzy borders; a looping-effect/constructionist view where labeling makes the group real through self-identification; and an eliminativist view where they're pseudo-kinds serving punditry and advertising. Also include one leaf for a view I'm not sure anyone holds but that occupies a natural cell in the tree (say, exact-border realism: fixed birth-year intervals are real joints even without shared events), and mark it 'unnamed' if nothing fits. Use at least one concrete case involving someone born right around the usual Millennial/Gen Z line and one involving a supposed defining event that happened before the cohort could remember it. Flag which positions your tree can't cleanly seperate and say whether any empty cells are empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just underexplored. Keep it under 700 words, tight enough to read in ten minutes.
