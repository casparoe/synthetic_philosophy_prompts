I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for a long time, and the position I keep coming back to is that rationality is more like a martial art than a body of knowledge. The actual claim is that there's a trainable skill underneath all the explicit rules, built the way a punch or a throw gets built: repetitive, artificial drills—katas—practiced until they're automatic. Calibration training, base-rate drills, precommitment scripts, inner-simulator check-ins where you notice what story you're telling yourself from outside the situation: these are the katas. I expect someone who puts a few hundred hours into them to make systematically better real-world calls than their matched twin who only read the books and did the problems once, in roughly the way a karateka's front kick is there in a real fight even though the fight looks nothing like the dojo. I also expect the transfer to be at least somewhat general: someone who's drilled noticing inside-view reasoning should be harder to snag with sunk costs, and that should show up outside the exact exercises.

So my question is: what would count against this? If I'm wrong, what's the most likely way I'm wrong, and what evidence should actually move me—not just the stuff critics throw at it? Sort it by how decisive it would be. For example, if controlled studies found trained people beat controls on the exact tasks practiced but not on structurally similar novel tasks, is that decisive or just awkward? If the gains wash out after six months, does that kill the martial-art analogy, or does a punch also rust without practice? What would show that what transfers is really just content—you learned the right facts and named the right biases—rather than a general skill? Where's the line between "no far transfer" and "your outcome measure was too noisy or too close to training"? I'd also want to hear what the views own defenders would concede counts against the view if it came out, not just what a skeptic would say. And be honest about whether I've rigged the view so no possible outcome could count against it; if I have, tell me, and whether that's actually a problem for a view like this (I suspect it might be, but I go back and forth).

The single thing that would hit me hardest is a preregistered, long-term trial where a group trained with these drills for months shows no advantage over active controls on pre-agreed outcome measures like forecasting, betting decisions, or avoiding exploitative choices in unfamiliar contexts. But maybe it would be unreasonable of me to demand that; tell me if so.
