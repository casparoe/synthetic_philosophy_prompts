so in my reading group we've been on Rawls for a few weeks and last session someone turned the excusable envy bit into a whole metaphor. his claim was: envy is like a smoke detector. the beeping is unpleasant, sure, and nobody enjoys being around it, but the correct response to a shrieking detector is not to get annoyed at the detector—it's to check the house for fire. by analogy, when people seethe at some inequality, the envy is diagnostic: it tells you something about the social structure, and we should treat it as excusable rather than writing it off as a vice. I found this half-convincing and half-suspicious, and I want someone to actually stress-test it. can you give me the correspondance table first—what maps to the smoke, the sensor, the battery, the false alarm, the wiring, the person who's trying to sleep—and then go through where the analogy breaks? in particular, I'm worried that a smoke detector responds to a physical signal that's more or less causally tied to fire, while envy responds to a comparison someone makes, and that comparison can be badly miscalibrated or self-serving. does envy actually track injustice the way a detector tracks smoke, or does it track perceived relative deprivation plus a story about what's fair? and does the conclusion—envy as signal rather than vice—survive once the disanalogies are laid out, or was the metaphor carrying the whole argument? if it doesn't survive, is there a better analogy (pain, a dashboard warning light, a canary in a coal mine) that does the same work more honestly?

for context, the Rawls line as I understand it (and as the group read it) is that envy becomes excusable when it's a reaction to inequalities that are already unjust and that threaten people's self-respect, not when it's just resentment at anyone doing better than you. the smoke-detector guy was using that to say the feeling should be treated as evidence about the world, not as a character flaw in the person feeling it.
