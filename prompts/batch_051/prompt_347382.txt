I am a retired research scientist—most of my working life was spent in the physical sciences—and I have only recently begun reading philosophy and theology with any seriousness. The doctrine of the Trinity has caught my attention not for devotional reasons but because it looks like a precise problem in the logic of identity and counting. In Thomas Aquinas's Summa Theologiae, First Part, Question 30, Article 1, reply to the first objection, he writes:

The definition of "person" includes "substance," not as meaning the essence, but the "suppositum" which is made clear by the addition of the term "individual." To signify the substance thus understood, the Greeks use the name "hypostasis." So, as we say, "Three persons," they say "Three hypostases." We are not, however, accustomed to say Three substances, lest we be understood to mean three essences or natures, by reason of the equivocal signification of the term.

I have been developing a small theory I call the gauge condition on identity. Its central claim is that assertions of identity and plurality are never well-formed absolutely; they are always relative to a sortal count noun—a "one what? / three what?" that fixes the unit in which things are being counted. Outside such a count noun, a question like "Are these one or three?" is not false but incomplete. On this view, "one God and three persons" is no more contradictory than "one kilogram and one thousand grams": the numerals answer different questions, and sameness under one count noun need not carry over to another.

Aquinas seems to me to be running something like this principle. He does not deny that there are three of something; he examines which count noun is admissible—"persons," or in Greek "hypostases"—and he refuses "three substances" because that count noun, through its equivocation, would set the wrong gauge. The sortal is doing the logical work; the reality counted is neither one nor three except under a description.

My concern is that I may have built an instrument that can dissolve any apparent contradiction whatever merely by changing count nouns. If all identity talk is count-noun-relative, then "one God and three gods" comes out as well-formed as "one God and three persons," provided I accept "god" as a gauge; and nothing in my principle yet says which count nouns are legitimate gauges and which are not. I would welcome an honest assessment. Is the gauge condition coherent? Does it already exist under another name? Does the Aquinas passage genuinely support it, or merely resemble it? And where does the view break?
