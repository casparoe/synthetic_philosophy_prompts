I run the documentation team for a consumer LLM assistant — model cards, the "what this thing is" page, and the internal scripts support agents read from. Every release we have the same fight about mental language. One engineer writes that the model "wants" the user to provide more detail; the comms person says we're anthropomorphizing and it will blow up on us; a support agent told a customer the other day that "it's just autocomplete, it doesn't believe anything." I've read some Dennett and I'm attracted to the idea that taking the intentional stance is just a predictive strategy: you attribute beliefs and desires when doing so lets you predict behavior better than the design stance or the physical stance would. But I have no idea how to turn that into a policy that tells us, line by line, when we are allowed to put a belief- or goal-word in front of a user or a regulator. One of my engineers wants us to just use the three stances literally and allow mental language whenever the intentional stance pays off better than the alternatives; I worry that "pays off" is doing an awful lot of work, and that cost in front of a regulator is not the same thing as predictiveness.

Please write me a reusable decision procedure my team can actually run, in under an hour per case, when someone proposes to use mental terms about the model — "wants," "aims," "believes," "tries," "is trying to," "refuses" — in model cards, user-facing copy, or support scripts. It should be grounded in what the intentional stance actually licenses, not in vibes. I need: the questions to ask in order; what counts as a good enough answer to keep the mental language versus strip it out; where the procedure simply says "no, rephrase" and where it stops and hands the decision to a person because the question has stopped being empirical. It should also be honest about the places the procedure will misfire. Then run it on three cases. (a) A support agent wants to tell a user "the model is trying to stay neutral about politics." (b) A model card draft says that the assistant "aims to be helpful, harmless, and honest." (c) During a long conversation the model keeps saying "I believe I already answered that," and the product team wants to keep the behavior but document it as "the model believes it has answered." I suspect the procedure tells us to cut at least one thing we'd rather keep — tell me which step forces that, and whether the procedure can settle the seperate question of what we should put in the public model card as opposed to the internal support scripts.
