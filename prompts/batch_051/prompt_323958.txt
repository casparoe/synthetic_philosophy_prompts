I teach an upper-division seminar on word meaning, and the recurring obstacle is that students arrive with a tacit lexicographic picture — each word has a finite list of stored senses and context simply selects one — without seeing that this is itself a substantive position in a wider logical space of views about whether words have meanings at all. I would like a sorting instrument, in the spirit of a “which theory of word meaning do you actually hold?” decision tree, that locates students by how they answer concrete questions about lexical flexibility rather than by which label they choose.

Build it around three test items: “book” (the red book on the table; the book I finished writing; the book is out of print), “healthy” (a healthy child; a healthy diet; a healthy complexion; a healthy economy), and “run” (run a race; run a meeting; run a program; a run in a stocking; a run of good luck). The branch nodes should be claims a student can accept or reject without knowing the names of theories — for example, whether a word carries a standing meaning into each context; whether the shift from object-“book” to text-“book” is selection among stored senses or a rule-governed extension; whether the bank by a river and the bank that lends money are two words while the two uses of “book” are one, and what that distinction is really tracking; whether two dictionaries that divide “run” into different numbers of senses can both be right; whether “He ate the whole bowl” requires a contents reading in the lexicon itself or lets context construct it. The leaves should be descriptively named positions in the logical space — something like a standing single-core view, a prototype or radial-category view, a sense-enumeration view, a rule-based generative view, a pragmatic or contextualist view, a family-resemblance use view, an eliminativist view, and any others the space genuinely requires, aiming for between seven and ten leaves, with “unnamed” noted where nobody defends a combination. No node should ask a question that already uses the theoretical label.

Please organize the response as follows, in this order, with approximate lengths relative to the whole: (1) Diagnostic cases and questions (about 20%): the contrasting sentences for each item, what each contrast is meant to test, and why the three items together keep the tree from being decided by a single distinction. (2) The decision tree (about 35%): every node phrased as a question answerable without theoretical vocabulary, the path to each leaf, and for each fork a note on which underlying claim it actually tests. (3) The leaves and their awkward commitments (about 25%): one short paragraph per leaf stating the view in plain terms and naming the commitment its defenders find hardest. (4) Gaps and inseparables (about 20%): combinations of answers the tree permits but no position occupies, with a diagnosis of whether each is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and any pairs of leaves the tree cannot truly separate flagged rather than papered over. I will add the reading list myself, so please do not cite sources or treat the leaf names as references to a literature.
