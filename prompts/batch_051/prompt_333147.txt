I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and several times a week I must decide how to render a probability claim about a single event — a hurricane landfall, a candidate's odds in a prediction market, a consumer genetic risk score, a climate tipping point. Readers act on the wording, whether that means flood preparation, medical follow-up, or a vote, and an assigning editor signs off on the final phrasing. I want a reusable editorial procedure — a checklist or decision flow my desk can run in under an hour without formal statistical training — for deciding when to write "there is an x% chance" (probability stated as a fact about the world) and when to write "analysts put the chance at x%" or "the model estimates x%" (probability attributed to a judgment). The anchor is the principle, associated with David Lewis, that a rational agent's degree of belief ought to defer to the objective chance of an event once the agent has conditioned on admissible evidence, but deference is not owed to mere subjective confidence or to a model output unless it tracks such a chance. The editorial translation should be: where a rational reader should defer to a chance, the magazine may state the probability in its own voice; where deference would be misplaced, it must attribute the number. Translate this into a sequence of questions, with an account of what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where each branch terminates — report as objective chance, report as attributed judgment, or escalate to a human because no rule settles it — and flag the points where judgment cannot be eliminated. Then run the procedure on three cases: a National Hurricane Center forecast giving a 30% chance of landfall at a named city; a direct-to-consumer genetic report of an 18% lifetime risk of a disease for one individual; and a prediction-market price implying a 2% chance that a long-shot candidate wins. At least one application should yield wording a journalist would resist, and you should say why the procedure requires it anyway. End by stating what the procedure cannot do — for instance, it cannot certify that a model is calibrated or establish that a source's quoted chance is not a disguised opinion.
