I'm a PhD student working on the demos/boundary problem—who ought to count as the people who make binding decisions—and my officemate has a stack of confident verdits but I can't reverse-engineer the principle. Her running example is France. She thinks a woman who moved from Dakar to Lyon thirty years ago, works as a nurse, pays French taxes, and has kids in French schools should obviously get the vote even though she never naturalized. A Norwegian tourist on a three-week holiday obviously shouldn't. She thinks a French citizen who's lived in Barcelona for twenty years with no property in France, no French tax obligations, and no intention of moving back should eventually stop voting in French elections. And she thinks a woman who lives in Italy but works in France every day should vote in the local elections where she works, even if not national ones.

The case I actually want to test her on is sort of the mirror image of that frontier worker. A Senegalese farmer has never set foot in France and isn't subject to French law in any direct way, but her income depends almost entirely on whether France keeps its market open to Senegalese produce; French trade and subsidy decisions can wipe her out in a single season. Would my officemate say the farmer should get some kind of formal say in those decisions—consultation rights, standing to challenge a tariff, maybe a vote in some trade-related body? I'm less interested in the bare yes/no than in the rule she seems to be running, the other rules that fit her earlier verdicts as well, which of those rules gives which answer about the farmer, and what further case would most cleanly separate the remaining candidates. I also can't tell which of her earlier verdicts is doing the most work in her own head.
