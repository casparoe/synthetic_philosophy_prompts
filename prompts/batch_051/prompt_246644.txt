Kavka's toxin puzzle is normally a one-shot: a billionaire pays you a million dollars tonight if you genuinely intend to drink a nauseating but harmless toxin tomorrow; the money is yours regardless of whether you actually drink, so tomorrow you'll have no reason to drink and—so the standard line goes—you cant form the intention, so you never get paid. Now suppose the same offer comes every single day for 30 days, and the length of the series is common knowledge: each evening you're offered a fresh million for intending to drink the next morning. A policy-level view says you could adopt a standing policy on day 1—intend every evening and drink every morning—and the early drinks are an investment in your own resoluteness; maybe you even drink on days when it doesn't pay just to keep the policy credible to yourself. But an act-level backward-induction view says: on the last evening, you know you won't drink the next morning, so you can't intend, so the last round is worthless; then on the second-to-last evening, drinking the next morning can't buy you anything, so... and the whole 30 rounds unravel. Work through which analysis wins and where exactly the unraveling bites. Then say whether the one-shot impossibility survives the repetition, and whether it matters if instead you know only that the series is finite but not when it ends.
