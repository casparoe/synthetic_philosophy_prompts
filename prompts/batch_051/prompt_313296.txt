I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy or linguistics. I work on the language-processing layer of a reading-support tool, including the grapheme-to-phoneme pipeline that turns text into speech. Before I encode my working assumptions into the architecture, I would like an audit of whether they hang together as a set. The tool is mostly English-first for now, with other alphabetic scripts to follow later.

The assumptions I currently hold are these:

1. An alphabet is, above all, a notation for speech. It is doing its job well to the extent that it maps letters onto the phonemes of the spoken language, and the ideal case is one letter per phoneme and one phoneme per letter.

2. Departures from that one-to-one mapping are defects. For any alphabetic language, the closer a script gets to maximally phonemic spelling, the better the script.

3. English spelling is mostly a pile of historical accidents, silent letters, and inconsistent analogies, and by the standard in 1 and 2 it is very far from ideal. Yet it is not pure noise: it often keeps a morpheme's spelling constant across related words (sign keeps its g because of signal; electric keeps its c because of electricity), and readers make use of those constant forms.

4. Since an alphabetic script records sound, the right way to build English grapheme-to-phoneme conversion is mainly deterministic letter-to-sound rules together with a small dictionary of genuinely irregular words. A full morphological analyzer or a large word-level pronunciation dictionary should not be necessary.

5. What an alphabetic spelling records is still phonology, not meaning and not simply the surface sounds: it records an underlying phonological form, and regular rules then map that underlying form to the pronunciation a speaker actually produces.

6. Where English spelling already distinguishes homophones, as in pair, pare, and pear, the system should preserve the distinction rather than normalizing it away, because collapsing the spellings would discard information readers rely on.

I would like the audit to do four things: identify every pair or larger subset of the assumptions that cannot all be held as stated and say plainly what the conflict is; rank those conflicts by severity, where severity reflects both how directly they contradict each other and how much of the system above depends on them; for each conflict, state the least costly revision and what it would force me to give up or make more complicated; and flag any places where two assumptions appear to conflict but, on a more careful interpretation, do not.
