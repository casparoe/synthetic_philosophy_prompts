Task: You are advising a research program on the moral psychology of resentment. The program has collected seven vignettes of envy and resentment, reproduced below. Rate each vignette on a single dimension: the degree to which the feeling it depicts is better understood as a signal of something unjust in the background arrangement than as a personal vice.

Use a criterion in the spirit of Rawls's idea that some envy is excusable rather than merely base—for instance, whether the resentment tracks an advantage that is unearned, procedurally illegitimate, or hard to justify to the person who ends up worse off—but state your criterion in your own words and amend it as you judge best. Then sort the seven cases into three tiers:

Tier 1 — substantially more signal of injustice than vice
Tier 2 — genuinely mixed
Tier 3 — substantially more vice than signal

Within each tier, order the cases from strongest to weakest claim to being counted as injustice-signals. After each case, give exactly one sentence of reasoning for its placement. If two cases seem effectively tied, place them adjacently and say in a sentence why you did not separate them.

The cases:

(a) A hospital porter earning a near-minimum wage envies a surgeon's income; his resentment is directed less at the surgeon than at a pay scale that treats some life-sustaining work as nearly worthless.

(b) A project manager envies a same-rank colleague whose office is slightly larger, and finds herself hoping the colleague stumbles in the next performance review.

(c) A tenant envies a landlord who inherited three apartment buildings and lives entirely on rents; she resents that her rent keeps rising while the landlord does no productive work, and that some units sit empty during a housing shortage because leaving them vacant is more profitable.

(d) A student from a well-off family envies a classmate who received a need-based scholarship, believing the award rewarded hardship instead of merit and should have gone to the strongest candidate regardless of financial background.

(e) A citizen envies a contractor who won a public works contract through a relative in the procurement office; the contractor's firm is now delivering the project late and over budget, and the citizen resents being taxed for it.

(f) A retiree envies a neighbor who won a large lottery jackpot, even though the two had similar opportunities to buy tickets and the retiree chose not to play.

(g) A junior researcher envies a senior colleague's effortless self-assurance—not the colleague's grants or publications, but the trait itself, which she wishes belonged to her.
