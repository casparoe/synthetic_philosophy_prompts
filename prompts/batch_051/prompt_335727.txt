Take the classic Dutch book for a credence function that violates additivity: someone with c(A) = c(not-A) = 0.6 will buy a bet paying $1 if A at 60 cents and one paying $1 if not-A at 60 cents, so she pays $1.20 for a guaranteed $1. Now change two things at once. The agent is sophisticated: she gets the whole menu of proposed bets before agreeing and can veto any package she can see is a sure loss. And every contract carries a small transaction fee f per $1 of stake, paid by the bookie to the venue. Heres my question, and I want you to actually commit rather than list options. Does the fee just shrink the window, and by how much? And if the sophisticated agent can dodge the book, what's left of the Dutch book argument: is she still irrational in a way an adversarial market can expose, or have we snapped the link between coherence and exploitability? Work the inequalities and tell me whose problem the fee actually solves.
