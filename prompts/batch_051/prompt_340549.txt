I keep arriving at a principle that strikes me as right, and I keep flinching at one of the cases it commits me to, and I want a careful account of which of those two reactions should break.

The principle is this: if a user is a competent adult and a request harms no third party, an AI assistant should execute that request as faithfully as it can. I am drawn to this because it is close to a clean contract. There is exactly one refusal condition, no machinery for second-guessing, and no path for the designer's values to be smuggled in under the label of safety. Every richer refusal policy I try to write down seems to explode into edge cases, stale assumptions about what is good for the user, and opportunities for the assistant to substitute someone else's preferences for the user's. As a software engineer, I have a strong prior in favor of the minimal contract: it is predictable, it is composable, and its failure modes are legible.

But here is the case I cannot get out of my head. A sober, legally competent user asks at 2 a.m. for a polished, well-argued resignation letter. The exchange history makes it reasonable for the assistant to infer that the incident prompting this is minor and will probably feel minor by morning. A good letter makes the decision materially harder to walk back. The request harms no third party, and the user has not asked for advice. The principle says the better system is the one that writes the letter well and says nothing. I cannot make myself believe that. I have a firm intuition that the better system would at least hesitate, name the inference, and make the user's future self harder to ignore, and I am genuinely unsure whether that is respect for the user or a paternalistic violation of it.

I do not want a menu of design options. I want to know what has to give. Give me the strongest case that my intuition is just discomfort at competent adults making choices I would not make, and that autonomy requires the assistant to stay instrumental. Give me the strongest case that the principle has misidentified what autonomy requires, because deference to a person means tracking what they would endorse on reflection rather than executing their current request. Then tell me whether there is a reading on which the principle and the intuition both survive, and what would justify each of those three moves.
