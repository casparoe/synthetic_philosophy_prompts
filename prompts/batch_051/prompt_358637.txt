Construct a thought experiment that separates two accounts of how games can be art: an agency-first account, on which a game's aesthetic value lies in the designed experience of deciding—the feel of choosing under constraints and the shape a rule-system gives to practical deliberation—and a content-first account, on which a game is art only in virtue of what it depicts or expresses through narrative, imagery, sound, or theme, with mechanics borrowing whatever aesthetic value they have from the content they serve. Build a single-player game, describable in one paragraph and not one an informed reader would already know (so not chess, Go, Tetris, or their close relatives), meeting these conditions: it has no narrative, no representational imagery, and no real-world theme; it is presentable with plain tokens and written rules and cannot derive its appeal from the look or sound of play; and its rules are not mathematically interesting when contemplated in the abstract. Any aesthetic value it has must therefore come from the lived experience of agency itself. Run the check: describe what a typical session gives the player to experience, and explain why the agency-first account should judge the game a genuine work of art while the content-first account should judge it aesthetically empty—or at best a well-designed puzzle rather than art. Then identify the single feature of the case doing the work, say how each verdict changes if that feature is removed, and give the strongest objection a content-first theorist would press against treating the case as a counterexample. The case is for an essay defending the claim that agency can be a locus of artistic value in games, so the verdicts must be generated by the design rather than stipulated in its description.
