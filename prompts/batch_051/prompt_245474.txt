I'm giving a presentation next week to a city council's budget committee. My proposal is a three-year pilot: the city runs internal prediction markets on whether specific capital projects will finish on time and within their cost estimates, and project managers have to treat the market price as one formal input when deciding whether to keep funding, pause, or kill a project. It is not full futarchy, nobody is bound by the market, and I'm not even using that word in the room. The bigger claim I'm defending is that for this class of decisions — where the outcome is publicly measurable after the fact — a conditional prediction market aggregates dispersed information better than the existing mix of staff reports, contractors' estimates, and public hearings. The committee is mostly career public administrators and municipal finance people, with two procurement lawyers and one political theorist who will see this as outsourcing democratic judgment to traders. Draft me a Q&A prep sheet: what will they actually ask, in their own vocabulary and likely order, how damaging each question is, what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and where my reply is weakest. Give me a two-minute reply for the objection I'm probably underestimating, and tell me the question I should be hoping nobody asks.
