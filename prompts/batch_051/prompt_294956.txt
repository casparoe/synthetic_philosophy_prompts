In section VI of The Future of an Illusion, Sigmund Freud defines an illusion in a way that explicitly severs the question of a belief's truth from the question of its psychological origin: an illusion, in his sense, need not be false, and may even turn out true. What matters for calling something an illusion is that its motivational support comes from a wish, above all from the child's early helplessness and longing for a protective father, rather than from an impartial survey of evidence. Freud insists that his account of the origin and function of religious doctrines is not itself a refutation of those doctrines. Take the central conceptual claim to be this: religious belief is, in at least a significant class of cases, psychologically sustained by wish-fulfillment rather than by evidence-responsive belief formation. This looks like a claim about hidden psychological processes, but also like a conceptual claim about how to classify a belief as an illusion. Design the best empirical test you can of one operationalizable part of this thesis. Specify the population and comparison, the manipulation or measure you would use, and the concrete outcome that would count as supporting or undermining the claim. Then say which parts of the claim your test cannot touch, even in principle. In particular, address whether a positive result would license Freud's broader genetic story about religion, or whether it would show only that some religious commitments function in wish-fulfilling ways in some people. Finally, say what residue would remain for a purely conceptual disagreement even if all empirical results came back as Freud would have predicted.
