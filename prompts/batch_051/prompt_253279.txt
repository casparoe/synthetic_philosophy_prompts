Lewis's triviality results are usually read as showing that indicative conditionals cannot both express ordinary propositions and have probabilities that track conditional probabilities. The core argument runs as follows. Suppose a probability function P is defined over a Boolean algebra of propositions, and suppose that for every pair of propositions A and C, the indicative conditional with antecedent A and consequent C expresses some proposition A→C in that same algebra, and that P(A→C) = P(C|A) for every P in a class of probability functions closed under conditioning. Fix A and C such that 0 < P(C) < 1 and all the conditional probabilities below are defined. Applying the equation to P(·|C) gives P(A→C | C) = P(C | A∧C) = 1, and applying it to P(·|¬C) gives P(A→C | ¬C) = P(C | A∧¬C) = 0. The law of total probability then yields P(A→C) = P(C), hence P(C|A) = P(C). So A and C would be independent under every probability function in the class, which is absurd. The standard moral is that at least one premise must go.

Now consider a specific later development: a family of dynamic and expressivist treatments on which a conditional does not denote a proposition with truth conditions at a world, but encodes an update rule or test on a state of information. Roughly, to evaluate the conditional, one first adds the antecedent to the information state and then checks whether the consequent holds in the resulting state. On such views there is no proposition A→C in the original algebra, so the law of total probability cannot be applied to the conditional itself. What these views do try to explain is a graded notion of acceptance, and the natural proposal is that the degree to which an agent should accept the conditional is P(C|A).

Does Lewis's triviality result survive this development? In particular, is the dynamic view a genuine way out, or does a parallel triviality result reappear once we ask what it is for an agent to hold a graded attitude toward an update rule rather than a proposition?
