I'm taking a seminar on Williams and contemporary moral psychology, and I've been trying to turn the integrity objection to utilitarianism into a step-by-step argument. I can't decide whether my reconstruction is valid or where exactly it goes wrong. Here is what I have:

(1) An account of right action is acceptable only if an agent guided by it need not, in acting rightly, become alienated from the motives that constitute her deepest personal relationships.
(2) If act-utilitarianism is the correct account of right action, then when a man can save either his wife or a stranger, his saving his wife is right only if he first determines that this is what impartial welfare-maximization requires.
(3) To determine that, he must step back from the direct motive of love and treat the rescue as one candidate among others, ranked by aggregate expected welfare.
(4) A person who steps back in that way no longer acts from the direct motive of love, and someone who would pause at such a moment to run that calculation has already let the wrong kind of thought into the act.
(5) So act-utilitarianism, if accepted, directs the man to perform the rescue under a condition that undermines the very personal value the rescue is supposed to express.
(6) An account of right action that directs agents to undermine the value at stake in close relationships is unacceptable.
(7) Therefore act-utilitarianism is unacceptable as an account of right action.

Is this argument valid as stated? If not, which step fails, and under what reading of that step? If it is valid, which premise should a sophisticated utilitarian deny, and why?
