I'm prepping a class on Aristotle's four causes and keep getting stuck on that section in Book II chapter 3 of the *Physics* where he takes a bronze statue and breaks its causes into the bronze itself, the sculptor, the shape or form, and the end or purpose. What bugs me is that modern researchers officially treat final causes as either quaint or unscientific, but when they write grant proposals they seem to be doing exactly what Aristotle described—just with euphemisms like significance, impact, and translation.

So here's what I'd like: a deadpan parody of a university research impact statement, around 700 words, for a project in, say, developmental biology or materials science, where the template explicitly forbids teleological language—no "in order to," no "designed to," no "purpose." The humor should come from the writer failing, over and over, to avoid final causes while still justifying the research. Use invented lab names and a footnote from a research integrity office about the misuse of final causes. The standard moves in the debate about whether teleology reduces to efficient causes or selection pressures should show up, but only through the bureaucratic prose. A philosopher of biology should find it fair, and a grant administrator should wince a little. It should read like something a grants office might actually send out.
