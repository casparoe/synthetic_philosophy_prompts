A reading group has spent the term arguing about the epistemology of testimony, and two members have settled into entrenched positions. One is a reductionist about testimonial justification: a hearer is justified in accepting a report only if that justification ultimately rests on perception, memory, and inference, so the hearer needs positive reasons for treating the speaker as competent and sincere. The other is an anti-reductionist: testimony is a basic source of justification, so, absent defeaters, a hearer is default-entitled or prima facie justified in accepting what a speaker says without first assembling positive reasons. The group has agreed that they owe one another an adversarial-collaboration document instead of another round of argument.

Draft that document. It should include:

(1) A numbered list of claims about testimony and its epistemic role that both can sign—about how much of what we know depends on testimony, about what happens when trust is misplaced, about the difference between testimonial justification and testimonial knowledge, and about uncontroversial cases where testimony is clearly enough or clearly not enough. These claims should be substantive rather than vacuous, and each should be worded so that neither side would reject it as stated.

(2) A statement of the precise residual disagreement. Give each side's formulation of its own view and of the other's, in wording the respective side would accept, and identify the minimum claim the two sides are actually fighting over.

(3) A list of cruxes—sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one side. I expect two cruxes to be whether there can be a non-circular empirical argument that testimony is generally reliable, given that any such argument seems to depend on testimony itself, and whether children's early, unchecked reliance on caregivers' reports is justified or merely adaptive; include any further cruxes the collaboration would converge on.

(4) For each crux, the case or observation both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction about that case recorded in advance.

Where the disagreement turns out to be partly verbal, say which part and reformulate it so the real dispute can be discussed directly. Format the result as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table.
