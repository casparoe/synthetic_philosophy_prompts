I am a high school student working on a project about whether information about a person should belong to that person. I have been trying to move beyond the general principle by thinking through one concrete version of it, and I would like an analysis of how it would behave in practice rather than a judgment about whether it is a good idea in the abstract.

Suppose a country enacted the following rule. Every resident receives a personal data account. Whenever a company uses information that is linked or reasonably linkable to a particular resident—for targeted advertising, credit scoring, insurance pricing, hiring decisions, or model training—it must log that use and pay a small royalty into the resident's account. The royalty is capped per person per month. Only substantial events generate royalties: purchases, location pings, social media posts, and search queries count; single clicks and passive page views do not. A company can avoid the royalty only by using data aggregated so that no individual can be re-identified, or by obtaining the person's genuinely free and informed consent to use the data without payment. A government agency audits company logs and fines underreporting.

What incentives does this create for companies in how they collect, store, and aggregate data? What incentives does it create for individuals in how they generate and share qualifying events? How would the system be gamed, and by whom? What second-order effects would likely follow for ad-supported services, for privacy itself, and for the auditing agency? Finally, does the royalty system actually make the information belong to the person, or does it function more like a tax on the processing of personal data with a rebate attached?
