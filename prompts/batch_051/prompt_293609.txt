I keep noticing an asymmetry in my own moral reactions and I want to know whether its defensible or just comfortable. After a hurricane, a shop that jacks up bottled water to twelve dollars is treated like a predator. I feel that too, even though everyone who buys the water is still better off buying it than going without — there's no other water, so the deal is mutually beneficial, just on greedy terms. But the next day I'll order a shirt from a factory that pays its workers maybe three dollars a day, far less than I'd call a fair wage, and I don't feel like I'm buying from a predator. If I squint, the structure looks awfully similar: in both cases someone with a good option profits from someone else's terrible outside options, and in both cases the person with the terrible options is better off taking the deal than refusing it. If a campaign tried to close the factory, I'd worry about the workers losing the best option they have. So is there a real principle that condemns the hurricane gouger but lets the factory owner off, or is the difference just psychological — one is visible and dramatic, the other is a supply chain I don't have to look at? Give me the strongest principled distinction you can build, then stress-test it against nearby cases: a landlord who raises rents after a fire, a payday lender in a poor neighborhood, a company pricing an essential drug just below what desperate patients can scrape together, a snow-shovel seller after a blizzard. Then tell me straight whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up, and what that would commit me to.
