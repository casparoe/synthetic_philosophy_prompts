im a third-year undergrad taking a philosophy of science class where we've been reading Malthus's An Essay on the Principle of Population next to Popper on falsification, and its collided weirdly with my part-time job. I work in my university's planning office, the group that maintains the ten-year campus master plan, and maybe once a month someone brings in a long-run projection — enrollment growth, campus energy demand, regional housing, the groundwater levels the campus depends on — and when a projection misses badly the same fight breaks out. One side says the model is falsified and we should stop using it; the other side says it's "right but early," the underlying mechanism is sound and the timing was just off. Right now these fights get settled by whoever argues loudest, and the director, who has to sign off on every projection we keep, wants a decision procedure we can run in an afternoon, not a seminar.

The Malthus passage we keep coming back to in class is from the first chapter of the Essay:

"It has been said that the great question is now at issue, whether man shall henceforth start forwards with accelerated velocity towards illimitable, and hitherto unconceived improvement, or be condemned to a perpetual oscillation between happiness and misery, and after every effort remain still at an immeasurable distance from the wished-for goal."

So here's what I want. First, in a sentence or two, say what that passage commits Malthus to about the shape of long-run predictions, and make that the principle. Then turn it into a reusable procedure for deciding when a long-run prediction that has missed should be treated as "right but early" and kept (maybe revised) versus treated as falsified and dropped. The people running it haven't read Malthus and won't read him, so the checklist has to carry the conceptual weight. Give me the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with "drop it," and where it explicitly hands the decision to a person because it's a values call rather than a factual one. Then run the procedure on two cases: (1) a 2015 projection that campus enrollment would keep growing 3% a year so we'd need a new residence hall by 2025 — enrollment flatlined after 2020 and the hall was never built, but the modeler says the mechanism is intact and the projection is just early; (2) a 2010 projection that the regional aquifer would be effectively depleted by 2025, which didn't happen, but the sustainability staff want to keep citing it because it motivates conservation. Make sure at least one of the worked cases comes out in a way the office won't like, and say plainly what the procedure can't settle. As the very last line, give a one-sentence summary of the core rule the procedure implements.
