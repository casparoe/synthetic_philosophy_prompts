I teach an honors seminar called *The Expanding Circle* — 20 undergraduates, one 90-minute session per week. I’m trying to build a classroom activity that makes participants feel the transition, described in the moral-sense portion of Darwin’s *The Descent of Man*, from sympathy that stops at familiar faces to sympathy that reaches strangers. In the passage I have in mind, Darwin argues that once a person has learned to extend his social instincts and sympathies to all members of his own nation, even those he does not personally know, the only remaining thing preventing those sympathies from reaching all nations and races is an artificial barrier, not a natural limit of the affections.

I want the students to discover, through the game’s incentives and rules rather than through a lecture, that the boundary they initially treat as natural can be re-described as a barrier they are themselves maintaining — and, critically, that removing the barrier is not the same as having a workable shared rule for acting on the wider sympathy. The activity should therefore not be a simple empathy-priming exercise; it should put participants in a position where their own practical decisions create, enforce, and then destabilize a moral boundary.

Please design the runnable activity for this setting: group size around 20, 90 minutes, no prior reading required. Include roles or rules, materials, a payoff scheme that does not require real money, a timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto Darwin’s distinction between naturally limited sympathy and an artificial barrier. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which feature of Darwin’s claim the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator afterward.

Act as an expert designer of philosophy-classroom simulations. Before settling on a proposal, briefly brainstorm three candidate mechanics and explain why you chose the one you did.
