Yesterday two friends of mine got into an argument about decision theory and I made a hash of refereeing it. Mara has gotten really into risk-weighted expected utility: the idea that a rational choice doesn't have to maximize ∑pᵢuᵢ, because you're allowed a "risk function" r that transforms the probabilities before they weight the outcomes, with r(p) ≤ p for a risk-averse person. Her pitch was the Allais pattern: lots of reasonable people take a sure $1m over a gamble with 89% $1m, 10% $5m, 1% $0, and then prefer a 10% shot at $5m to an 11% shot at $1m. Expected utility can't produce that pair from any utility function, she said, but REU handles it once you stop weighting small probabilities linearly.

Leo said that's not a theory, it's curve-fitting: if r is allowed to be arbitrary, any pattern of choices has a post hoc "explanation," and worse, r(p) ≤ p means you're treating a 10% chance of a good outcome as if it mattered less than its probability, so you're acting on credences you don't actually hold. Mara said r isn't a belief; it's a third kind of attitude, a bit like caring about how goods are distributed across states of the world rather than only about the total. Leo answered that across persons there are separate people who can be treated unfairly, but across states only one state will obtain, so caring about the "spread" across states that can't all happen is just personifying possible worlds. Mara said expected utility theory does that too—it weights each outcome by its probability, as if each state had a claim. Leo said probability isn't a claim; it just records how strongly each state should figure in your estimate of what will happen.

Then it got concrete. Mara said a person who is strongly risk-averse in this sense—say their r makes a 98% chance of the good outcome count for much less than .98—might rationally turn down a one-off gamble with positive expected value and a 2% chance of catastrophic loss, even after diminishing marginal utility of money is fully accounted for, because the bad outcome can matter more than its probability suggests. Leo called that double counting: put the catastrophe's badness into the utility function once, and if the gamble still has positive expected utility, turning it down is refusing to act on your own values; adding r punishes the bad outcome twice. Mara said utility and r are answering different questions, but she didn't get to finish.

I said something about it being "context-dependent," and neither was impressed. You're a decision theorist: please adjudicate the whole thing. Go through their exchange point by point, say who's right about which specific claim, flag the moves that don't land, and then tell me who is more right overall. Be precise about the formal bits—what r actually does, whether REU can really recover the Allais pattern, and whether Leo's double-counting charge sticks—but don't just do the math; say where each argument is sound or off.
