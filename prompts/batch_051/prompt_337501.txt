hey, i help run a lunchtime philosophy club at my high school and our next topic is generic sentences, the ones like "ducks lay eggs" and "mosquitoes carry malaria" where the sentence sounds true even though it's only female ducks that lay eggs and only a tiny fraction of mosquitoes actually carry malaria. we want a handout that works like a personality quiz: people answer questions about concrete examples, and then a scoring key tells them which theory of generics they actually hold, not which theory they say they like.

can you build that for us? here's what i have in mind.

make it 9 or 10 questions. each question should just be one generic sentence, with a one-line setup of the relevant facts, like "ducks lay eggs (only adult females do)" or "books are paperbacks (most books are paperbacks)". the answer options should be TRUE, FALSE, or IT DEPENDS. for the IT DEPENDS ones, add a short follow-up asking whether it depends on the context or on how you read the sentence, but keep it simple.

then write a scoring key that maps patterns of TRUE/FALSE/IT DEPENDS onto different positions. i know the space of views is something like this, but feel free to add a leaf if you think there's a real view i'm missing:

- statistical/majority view: a generic is true when most of the kind has the property. this view has a hard time because it makes "mosquitoes carry malaria" false and "books are paperbacks" true, which both feel wrong.

- normality or characteristic-property view: the property has to be normal or characteristic for the kind, even if only some members do it. so "ducks lay eggs" is true because egg-laying is part of how ducks work as a kind, but "books are paperbacks" is false because paperback-ness isn't characteristic of books.

- striking-property view: the property just has to show up in enough members and be striking or important, especially if it's dangerous. so "mosquitoes carry malaria" and "sharks attack swimmers" come out true because the property matters, not because most members have it.

- prototype/stereotype view: the property has to be part of the mental picture of the kind. so the question is whether the generic sentence matches the stereotype, not the real-world numbers.

the questions should actually seperate these views, so no question should ask something like "do you think generics are about normality?" — every question has to be a concrete case where the views give different answers. also tell me which two views your question set can't fully tell apart, and say what extra example would split them.

one more thing: flag the answer combinations nobody actually holds. for example, is there anyone who's a strict universalist about generics, so "ducks lay eggs" is just false because most ducks are male? if that's a real leaf, give it a name and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. if it's empty, say so, and say whether it's empty because it's incoherent, just unattractive, or just unexplored.

keep it readable for high schoolers but don't hide the logic. you don't need to bother with citations or reading lists. oh and leave a little note at the top explaining what a generic is in one or two sentences.
