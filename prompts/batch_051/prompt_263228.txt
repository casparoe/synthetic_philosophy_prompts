I spent most of yesterday evening arguing with a friend about whether consciousness has a place where it all comes together. He kept insisting that, whatever the neuroscience eventually shows, there must be some point or stage at which the separate streams of perception and thought get assembled into the unified experience I am having right now. I pushed back with the multiple drafts model: there is no final, canonical draft, no privileged moment at which experience is displayed to an inner observer in a Cartesian theater, only parallel and continuously revised content-streams competing for influence over what gets said, done, and remembered. By the end I realized how hard the view is to grasp if it is presented only as a negative claim; it has to be felt through one's own reports of an experience before the framework is named.

I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of mind, sixteen students, one 90-minute session. I want to run an activity or small demonstration in which the students' own reports of what they consciously experienced turn out to be retrospective constructions rather than read-offs from a finished presentation in consciousness, and in which that discovery happens before the Cartesian theater or the multiple drafts model is mentioned. The materials should be modest: a laptop and a projector with a timed display are fine. You are welcome to build the activity around a timing illusion such as the color phi phenomenon, in which two differently colored spots flashed in quick succession at two positions are reported as a single moving spot that changes color partway along its path, or around a task that makes participants reconstruct a sequence of events after brief or ambiguous stimuli. Please include rules or procedure, a timeline, what participants should come away seeing, and a debrief that maps the moments of the activity onto the parts of the view. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time and which feature of the multiple drafts model a classroom activity cannot reproduce and the facilitator must supply.

Please format your answer as exactly five numbered items, each no more than two sentences, with no introductory or concluding text. The items should cover, in order: setup and materials; procedure and rules; the moment the phenomenon is expected to surface; the debrief and mapping; and the predictable failure modes plus the limits of the demonstration.
