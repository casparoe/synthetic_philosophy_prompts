In Nicomachean Ethics II.4, Aristotle writes: "acts done in conformity with the virtues are not done justly or temperately if they themselves are of a certain sort, but only if the agent also is in a certain state of mind when he does them: first he must act with knowledge; secondly he must deliberately choose the act, and choose it for its own sake; and thirdly the act must spring from a fixed and permanent disposition of character."

I have been trying to use this passage to state a precise argument against the situationist critique of virtue ethics, but I cannot tell whether it is valid or where the load-bearing claim is. The argument runs:

(1) On Aristotle's account, an action is virtuous only if it springs from a fixed and permanent disposition of character; call this the stability condition.

(2) A fixed and permanent disposition of character entails a high degree of cross-situational behavioral consistency: someone genuinely compassionate will respond compassionately across a wide range of circumstances, not only in favorable ones.

(3) Situationist findings in moral psychology show that ordinary people are not highly consistent in apparently compassion-relevant, honesty-relevant, or helpfulness-relevant behavior; comparatively minor situational factors—time pressure, the presence or absence of bystanders, ambient cues—produce large differences in response.

(4) Therefore ordinary people do not satisfy the stability condition, and so do not possess the virtues in Aristotle's sense.

(5) Since virtue is normally presented as a widely attainable ideal rather than a condition reserved for a few moral sages, this constitutes a serious internal problem for an Aristotelian virtue ethics.

Is this argument valid? If not, which numbered step fails, and under what interpretation of that step? I suspect the weak point is (2), because a stable disposition might be situation-sensitive in a reasoned way, but I worry that this reply makes the disposition unfalsifiable. Is there a better place to attack the argument, or does Aristotle have the resources to accept the conclusion without giving up the central role of virtue?
