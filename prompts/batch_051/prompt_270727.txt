The meta-problem of consciousness is the problem of explaining why we are so strongly disposed to judge that consciousness has a subjective, non-functional "feel" and therefore that there is a hard problem. I keep coming back to a debunking argument about the meta-problem, and I distrust it because it seems to prove too much. Here it is with the steps numbered.

(1) Whenever a person sincerely reports a conscious state or an intuition about consciousness — for example, a report to the effect that experience has a subjective feel that no physical description captures — that report is the product of some neurocognitive process.

(2) For the particular report that there is a hard problem, call the producing process P. P's output is driven by an introspective model of the person's own experience, and that model represents experience as ineffable, private, and non-functional.

(3) P would continue to produce the same report even if the modeled property — the ineffable, private, non-functional feel — did not exist, because what drives the report is the model, not the property the model represents.

(4) A person's only evidence for the existence of this non-functional feel is that the introspective model produces the report or intuition that there is one.

(5) If a person's only evidence for a claim is produced by a mechanism that would produce that evidence whether or not the claim were true, then the person is not justified in believing the claim.

(6) Therefore, no one is justified in believing that there is a hard problem or that consciousness has a non-functional aspect.

(7) But premise (5) is fully general. Applied to introspective reports of pain, hunger, or one's own intentions, it would equally show that we are never justified in believing in any of our conscious states; and applied to the introspective reports used to construct the debunking explanation itself, it undermines the very evidence on which premises (1)–(3) rest.

I might be able to swallow (6) as a form of illusionism, but I cannot accept (7): I am not prepared to say that I am unjustified in believing I am in pain while having the pain. Where does this argument go wrong, if it does? If I had to choose, I would rather give up (3) or (4) than (5), but I want to know what each move costs.
