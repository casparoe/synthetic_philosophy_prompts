I've agreed to give a work-in-progress talk at a joint philosophy of religion / philosophy of mind colloquium, defending a line I've been kicking around in rationalist-adjacent circles: 'ineffable knowledge' is a motte-and-bailey. My thesis is that on the weak reading — some experiences are just hard to verbalize — the claim is true but boring, while on the strong reading — a subject is in a cognitive state whose content could not possibly be put into words — the claim is either false or it stops being knowledge, since a state with no expressible content can't play the role in inference and action that makes a state count as knowledge in the first place. So the mystic's 'I know something I can't say' is either a complaint about vocabulary or a confusion about what knowledge requires. The audience is a mixed bunch: a Wittgensteinian who'll press the saying/showing distinction, a scholar of the apophatic tradition (Pseudo-Dionysius, the via negativa) who thinks I've missed the whole point of negative theology, and a philosopher of mind who works on nonconceptual content and will think my 'no content, no knowledge' move is way to fast.

I want a prep sheet, not a defense. Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll actually come, phrased the way those three would phrase them — especially the apophatic person, since I don't speak that idiolect natively and I keep flattening negative theology into just another positive claim. For each objection: how likely and how damaging it is, what I should concede up front, what I should hold, a short reply that fits in Q&A time, and where that reply is weakest. Then flag the objection I'm hoping nobody brings up — I suspect it's from the acquaintance/non-representational side: that some contents can be known without being representational, so my inference-and-action test only covers propositional knowledge and the interesting cases slip past it. Tell me whether to pre-empt that one in the talk or leave it buried. And commit: don't give me a menu — tell me which objection is most likely to actually sink the talk, whether I should narrow the thesis to propositional knowledge before the first slide, and what the one reply is that has to land for the paper to survive.
