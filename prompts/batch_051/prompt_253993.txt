I keep seeing Newton and Leibniz trotted out as proof that discoveries are inevitable, that if one guy hadn't come up with calculus the other would have and so individual genius barely matters. But I don't think I've ever seen anyone actually run the episode through that lens carefully. For the calculus case, the rough facts as I understand them: Newton developed fluxions earlier and sat on much of it, Leibniz published first with cleaner notation, both arrived at the ideas independantly, and then there was the ugly priority fight. Concretely, what was independently discovered and what was not (notation, foundations, the fundamental theorem, the inverse relation between tangents and areas)? What does near-simultaneous independent discovery actually establish about inevitability, and does it only support the weak claim that some calculus-like toolkit would have shown up eventually, which is almost empty? What would the inevitability thesis have predicted at each stage, and where does it have to stretch or ignore things? Finally, what does the priority dispute itself show about whether it matters who gets there first, and what does the whole episode show back about the inevitability thesis — is it a confirmation, an anomaly, or just a single data point that can't carry the weight people put on it?
