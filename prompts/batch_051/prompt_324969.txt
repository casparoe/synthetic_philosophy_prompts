I've been turning over a Dennett-style argument about the two-bitser, and I can't tell whether it's a deep point or one of those arguments that lives and dies on a single word. Numbered:

(1) A vending machine's quarter-tester is about quarters, if at all, only because its designers had intentions about quarters and built the device to serve them. (2) So the machine's aboutness is derived, not original. (3) But the brain's neural object-detectors were built by natural selection, and in the relevant respect the brain is no more self-constructing than the vending machine. (4) So if being designed for a purpose makes the machine's intentionality derived, being selected for a function makes the brain's intentionality derived to, unless we insert a 'meaning glow' that does no explaining either. (5) Therefore the original/derived intentionality distinction collapses: either nothing has original intentionality or 'original' marks a difference we haven't actually specified.

Where does this break, if it does? I especially feel like the move from (3) to (4) switches the meaning of 'derived', but I can't state the two readings cleanly. So: is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and what exact version of that step would make the conclusion actually follow?
