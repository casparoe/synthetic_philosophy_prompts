I've been reading some LessWrong-adjacent discussions about the problem of justifying deduction, and a blog post I came across summarized Lewis Carroll's "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" (*Mind*, 1895) like this. Carroll, the post says, showed that deductive reasoning cannot be justified. The Tortoise accepts A and B but refuses to accept Z; when Achilles tries to force him by adding the conditional "if A and B then Z," the Tortoise simply asks why he must accept that too. This starts an infinite regress: each new conditional becomes just another premise that needs to be accepted, and no amount of additional premises can ever force the conclusion. Therefore, the post concludes, deductive inference can never be justified, because any justification would require an infinite chain of rules. The only way out, it says, is a non-rational leap of faith or a primitive faculty of "seeing" that the conclusion follows, which makes Carroll an early skeptic about the normative force of logic.

The post then quotes this passage from Carroll to support its reading:

> "Then I must ask you to accept C."
> "I'll do so," said the Tortoise, "as soon as you've entered it in that note-book of yours. What else have you got in it?"
> "Only a few memoranda," said Achilles, nervously fluttering the leaves: "a few memoranda of—of the battles in which I have distinguished myself!"
> "Plenty of blank leaves, I see!" the Tortoise cheerily remarked. "We shall need them all!" (Achilles shuddered.) "Now write as I dictate:—
> (A) Things that are equal to the same are equal to each other.
> (B) The two sides of this Triangle are things that are equal to the same.
> (C) If A and B are true, Z must be true.
> (Z) The two sides of this Triangle are equal to each other."
> "You should call it D, not Z," said Achilles. "It comes next to the other three. If you accept A and B and C, you must accept Z."
> "And why must I?"
> "Because it follows logically from them. If A and B and C are true, Z must be true. You don't dispute that, I imagine?"
> "If A and B and C are true, Z must be true," the Tortoise thoughtfully repeated. "That's another Hypothetical, isn't it? And, if I failed to see its truth, I might accept A and B and C, and still not accept Z, mightn't I?"

My vague sense is that the blog post is conflating something—perhaps treating modus ponens as a premise rather than a rule of inference, or confusing psychological acceptance with logical validity—but I don't trust my ability to unpack it.
