I am drafting a novel whose protagonist, after a head injury, reports that a wound hurts—she can locate it, describe its quality, and rate its intensity—yet she seems entirely untroubled by it: she does not dislike it, does not flinch, and feels no need to withdraw from the pain. In researching this, I came across pain asymbolia, a condition in which patients say they are feeling pain but lack precisely that dimension of dislike and motivation; the sensory character remains while the suffering and the urge to stop it disappear. This strikes me as a serious problem for the strong philosophical view that the badness of pain is intrinsic to the way pain feels—that there is no gap between feeling pain and finding it bad. Give me a rigorous assessment. State the strong view in its most defensible form, identify which premise clinical reports of pain asymbolia put pressure on, and tell me whether the view survives, needs repair, or collapses. If it can be repaired, state the weaker claim about the badness of pain that remains defensible; if you think the phenomenon has been overread, say precisely what it does and does not show.
