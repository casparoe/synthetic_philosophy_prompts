I have been trying to make the therapy/enhancement distinction precise, and I keep running into the problem of the baseline against which a capacity is measured. Let me state a criterion I am working with. An intervention is therapeutic when it restores, preserves, or prevents a deficit in a capacity relative to some reference level of functioning; it is enhancing when it raises the capacity above that reference level. The criterion itself does not yet say which reference level to use, and I can identify at least four plausible candidates: (1) the individual's own previous level before decline; (2) the statistically normal level for the person's age and sex; (3) the statistically normal level for young, healthy adults of the species, held fixed across the lifespan; and (4) a normative ideal of full health, even if that ideal is not statistically typical for any age. Now consider the following borderline case. A seventy-four-year-old with no diagnosable disease has episodic memory function that is exactly typical for their age: on a standard neuropsychological memory battery, their raw score falls at the 50th percentile for 74-year-olds and at the 5th percentile for 40-year-olds. Their own test results from age 40 are available and show a score at the 50th percentile for 40-year-olds. They begin a long-term drug regimen that does not push memory beyond anything observed in healthy 40-year-olds, and after several months their score has returned to precisely their own age-40 level. Work through the case under each of the four reference levels and state whether the regimen comes out as therapy or enhancement. Then address the harder question: is there a principled reason to privilege one of these reference levels for the therapy/enhancement distinction, or should we conclude that the distinction is not a single distinction but a family of distinct distinctions that need not agree with one another? If you think it needs revising, give the revised version and show what it says about this case.
