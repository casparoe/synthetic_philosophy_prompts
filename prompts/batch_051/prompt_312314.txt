For a one-off seminar on federalism and subsidiarity with advanced undergraduates — about 18 students and an 80-minute session — design a game or structured activity that makes participants feel the force of the real allocation problem behind subsidiarity: that the injunction to handle matters as close to those affected as is workable is not a mechanical default, because local information and preference fit pull some decisions downward while spillovers, scale economies, and the demand for coordination pull others upward. I want participants to discover, through what actually happens during the activity, that the right level for a decision depends on features of the decision itself — who holds the relevant information, who bears the costs and benefits, and whether units can act independently or must coordinate — rather than on a general presumption in favor of the smallest unit. In particular, design the rules so that at some point each group faces a visible trade-off between keeping a decision to itself, which fits its own situation better, and handing it upward to a joint body, where the joint choice is worse for it locally but better for everyone taken together. Neither the word subsidiarity nor the theoretical framing should appear until the debrief.

Please specify the rules and materials — no real money — plus a payoff scheme that gives every participant or every unit a score they have reason to care about, a timeline for the 80 minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the relevant features: private or local information, externalities and spillovers, economies of scale, and exit and voice options (leaving a unit or contesting its decisions). Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how I should handle it, and be explicit about at least one feature of subsidiarity a classroom game cannot reproduce — something that appears only in real constitutional orders — and what I should supply verbally in the debrief to compensate for that gap.

Hard length limit: 1,000 words, maximum. No citations or references to the literature are needed.
