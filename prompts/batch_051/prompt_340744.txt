As a PhD student working on causation in the law, I keep returning to a tension in how Anglo-American criminal and tort doctrine handles omissions. The black-letter rule is that merely failing to act is not a cause of harm and attracts no liability; a bystander who watches someone drown is said not to have caused the death. Yet once a legal duty attaches — a lifeguard's contractual role, a parent's special relationship, a statutory obligation — an omission of the very same kind becomes the cause of the harm: the lifeguard's failure to throw the rope caused the drowning, the parent's failure to feed the child caused the starvation. The causation finding seems to turn entirely on the duty finding. I want to know what theory of causation by omission this pattern presupposes. Reconstruct the implicit view and be precise about whether the practice strictly requires a normative or contrastive account of causation by omission — on which an absence is causal only relative to some expected baseline of conduct — or whether it is equally compatible with the view that omissions are genuine absences in a real causal structure, with the duty rule doing independent normative work. Also assess whether the two halves of the doctrine presuppose consistent things, and say what in the doctrine would have to be reformulated if the presupposition were reversed: for instance, would we need to stop saying the omission caused the death, or would the duty requirement have to be relabeled as something other than a precondition for causation?
