I am a high school student who helps run a small volunteer project preserving old games and fan-made software, and I keep running into the same question: when is a work genuinely abandoned, in the sense that reviving, copying, or reusing it no longer wrongs or interferes with anyone who still has a claim to it? I would like a working definition of "abandoned" for creative works and software, with a few non-software cases included to test its limits.

Here are the cases:

1. A novel published in 1947. The author died in 1979 leaving no spouse, children, or other heirs; the publisher dissolved in 1995; and the holding company that bought the rights has never responded to inquiries.
2. A photograph found loose in a thrift-store box, with no name, date, or identifying marks, and no record of who donated it.
3. An open-source library posted publicly whose sole maintainer last changed the code eight years ago and does not reply to messages. The code remains available under a permissive license, so anyone may legally use it, but no one maintains it.
4. A multiplayer game whose servers the studio shut down ten years ago. The studio still exists and owns the code, and says it has no plans to re-release the game; fans want to run their own servers.
5. A house that has stood empty for twelve years. Its owner is known and alive and continues to pay the property taxes but never visits.
6. A domain name registered in 2003 for a fan site. The owner let the registration lapse in 2010, and the name is now held by a parking company that pays to keep it registered but never built a real site.
7. A living, reachable composer who says about an early piece that he no longer has any interest in it and does not care what anyone does with it.

I am confident that cases 1 and 2 are abandoned in the sense I care about, and that cases 5 and 6 are not; I cannot decide about cases 3, 4, and 7. Please propose a definition, run it through all seven cases, say where it gives a verdict you would not want to defend, and tell me which of your final verdicts you find least secure.

Structure your response in these sections, in this order:

- Preliminary definition: your first explicit definition, before applying it to the cases (about 100 words).
- The seven cases: apply your definition to each case in order and state the verdict it yields (about 350 words).
- Where the definition struggles: identify the cases that come out wrong, counterintuitive, or with no clear answer, and explain why (about 150 words).
- Revised definition and verdicts: give your revised or defended definition, your final verdict on each case, and the verdict of which you are least confident (about 200 words).
