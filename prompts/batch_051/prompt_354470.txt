I'm a PhD student in historical theology, and I keep coming back to the Galileo affair because its usually told as the Church reading the Bible literally (Joshua's sun standing still, the earth being fixed, that cluster of passages) while science said otherwise, but the more Origen and Augustine I read, the stranger that story gets. Origen's way of handling the Genesis creation days, as I understand it, treats a purely literal-physical reading as close to a category mistake; Augustine says, in effect, that scripture is not an astronomy textbook and that Christians shouldn't make themselves ridiculous by insisting on a physical reading when good reason suggests otherwise; and the medieval tradition had a whole repertoire of non-literal senses ready for exactly these passages. So I want to use the affair as a case study for the literal-vs-allegorical axis, taken all the way from Origen's multiple senses up through early modern Catholic interpretation and later fundamentalist literalism.

What I'd like from you is a careful case analysis that runs in both directions, not a history paper. Use the framework to say what interpretive options the Church and Galileo actually faced, and what each should have done by the lights of, say, Origen, Augustine, Aquinas, and then a modern fundamentalist literalist. Then tell me which features of the episode the framework explains well, where it goes silent or misdescribes things (patronage, Counter-Reformation politics, the state of the astronomical evidence, the fact that it was also a fight about who gets to interpret), and what the episode in turn shows about the framework: is it evidence against literalism, evidence that interpretive method is downstream of interpretive authority, or something messier? A good answer should be disciplined about who counts as the "player" and what counts as the hermeneutic choice, and should say where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak.
