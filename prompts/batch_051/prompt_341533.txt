Six months ago I was put in charge of a reorganization at my workplace that failed, and in the postmortem a colleague kept coming back to one phrase: we had never really jumped out of the system. The phrase has followed me since. A friend used it again while explaining the old MU-puzzle, in which you start from the string MI and try to derive MU under four string-rewriting rules; the standard move, he said, is to leave the rules behind and count the I's modulo three. By now, at least in my circle, jumping out of the system works as a slogan, and I think it is doing more than one job at once. I can distinguish at least five candidate claims it might be making. (1) A formal system cannot establish, using only its own resources, certain facts about itself that become provable from a stronger or differently encoded standpoint. (2) A person operating entirely within an institution's concepts and procedures cannot reliably detect the blind spots those concepts create. (3) A problem that resists every attempted solution inside a fixed framework is often best addressed by changing the framework or adding a new primitive, rather than by making further moves within the old one. (4) A system can correct local failures by its own rules, but it cannot from the inside reliably revise the very rules that keep producing those failures. (5) The phrase is a misleading spatial metaphor, because one never steps outside all systems at once but only moves from one system to another. I want these laid out as a menu rather than collapsed into a single thesis: for each, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or a claim that is false or nearly false, and then say which candidate each of my two encounters was actually leaning on. My suspicion is that the postmortem needed the fourth reading while the puzzle needed the first, and that the slogan gets its force from letting people borrow the rigor of the one while meaning the vaguer other; I would rather have that suspicion tested than echoed.
