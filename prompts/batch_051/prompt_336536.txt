Standard English dictionaries — and the dictionary apps and lookup tools built on them — draw a boundary they almost never state. Look up "go" and you will not be given a separate entry for "went": "went" and "gone" are presented as forms of the one word "go." The same is true for "child" and "children," and for "good," "better," and "best." Yet "runner" and "childhood" are not treated as forms of "run" or "child": they get entries of their own, even though they are built from the same stems and their meanings follow transparently from those stems.

This boundary does not track any single obvious property. It cannot be purely a matter of shared letters or shared sound, since "went" shares none with "go" and is folded in, while "runner" shares almost its entire form with "run" and is split off. It cannot be purely a matter of closeness of meaning, since a single headword for "run" holds senses as distant as racing, managing an organization, and an unbroken series of performances, while "runner" — a person or thing that runs — is separated. And the practice wavers exactly where a clean theory would need to be decisive: dictionaries differ over whether "broken," "interesting," and "building" are inflected forms of a verb or independent adjectives and nouns, and they treat "harder" as a form of "hard" while treating "hardness," which is just as plainly made from it, as a new word.
