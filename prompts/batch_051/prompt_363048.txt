Karl Popper's argument in The Poverty of Historicism is usually read as claiming that large-scale historical prediction is impossible in principle, or at least that there are no laws of history of the sort that would let you forecast the future trajectory of a society the way you can forecast a planetary orbit. The sharpest version of his case is the growth-of-knowledge argument: the future course of history depends heavily on future scientific and technological knowledge, and you cannot predict now what you will only know later, because predicting it would already be knowing it. Relatedly, he says that observed regularities or trends in history are singular statements about a unique sequence of events, not universal laws, and there is no guarantee they persist.

Now take the development I'm interested in: over the last two decades we've gotten enormous historical and real-time social datasets, cheap computation, and forecasting systems (prediction markets, machine-learning models, and LLM-based forecasters trained on huge amounts of text) that are sometimes strikingly good at assigning probabilities to medium-run social outcomes, like conflict onset, economic crises, migration flows, election results, and the spread of technologies. Those systems usually don't extract anything that looks like a law of history; they find lower-level regularities and features and update probabilities as conditions change. But they do seem to be doing exactly the kind of long-range social prediction Popper tought was shaky, and they do it by exploiting patterns no human theorist articulated.

Does Popper's argument survive these systems? More specifically: is their success just conditional, local, trend-following prediction of the sort Popper could have accepted, or does it put real pressure on either the trends-are-not-laws premise or the growth-of-knowledge argument? And does the fact that forecasts feed back into the social process (people read the forecasts and act on them) make his case stronger, because it's his old point that social predictions alter the thing predicted, or does it just change which predictions are stable? I want a committed ruling here, not a survey: say whether Popper's core argument still holds, needs repair, or should be narrowed, and tell me exactly which premise your answer makes you reject or retain.
