Hey — I cover science and ideas for a general-interest outlet, and I'm working on a piece about how much moral blame falls on an individual soldier when the war they're fighting is unjust. At a panel last week a just-war guy kept saying the moral equality of combatants was basically settled, and a human rights lawyer kept answering that a soldier on the unjust side who shoots an enemy soldier is doing something wrong even if his own side has spent years propagandizing him. The two were clearly using different theories, and I dont want to just define them for readers — I want a real verdict on which ones hold up.

Here are the five positions I've managed to shake out of the debate:

A. Strong equality: soldiers on both sides have the same moral permissions; the justice of the overall war doesn't change whether an individual soldier may kill an enemy combatant.
B. Wrong-but-excused: unjust soldiers act wrongly when they kill, but most of them are excused by conscription, duress, or reasonable ignorance about their cause.
C. Role ethics: once someone is a lawful combatant in uniform, they step into a role whose internal rules permit what would otherwise be murder; the moral questions belong at the level of the institution or the law of armed conflict, not the individual soldier.
D. Objective asymmetry: whether a soldier may kill depends on the justice of their cause, not on what they believe; a combatant fighting for an unjust cause acts impermissibly even if he's sincere and well-intentioned.
E. Pragmatic convention: the equality thesis is probably false, but we should keep treating soldiers as if it were true because any alternative would wreck reciprocity, prisoner-of-war protections, and the ability to end wars.

Rank these from most defensible to least defensible. Give me exactly five numbered items — one per position, in your rank order, each a sentence or two — with no introduction and no conclusion. In each item say what lands it there, and if it helps say where you'd draw the line between a conscript and a volunteer.
