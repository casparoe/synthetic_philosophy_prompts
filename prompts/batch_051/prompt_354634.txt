I am giving a paper next month to a small interdisciplinary working group advising a city on the wording of a new interpretive marker at the site of a nineteenth-century massacre whose local commemoration long preceded any professional historical attention. The group includes two academic social historians who regard community memory as unverified tradition with no evidentiary standing; a heritage officer who wants the text to reflect consultation and inclusion; a representative of the descendant community who can recite the community's version in detail and treats the archive as a record made by the killers and their officials; and a city attorney who cares mainly about what is defensible if the text is challenged or later used in schools. My thesis is a burden-of-proof rule for commemoration: where a community's transmitted memory is specific, stable over generations, and load-bearing for its self-understanding, the marker may present it as the community's memory even when the documentary record is silent, and the silence of a partial or hostile record does not by itself refute it; but where the record positively settles a factual claim against the memory, the marker must acknowledge the discrepancy on site rather than quietly omitting it for the sake of cohesion.

I am not asking for a restatement or a defense of the argument. Give me a preparation sheet for the question period: the objections in approximately the order they will come and the damage each can do, phrased the way each faction would actually phrase them, with what I should concede, what I should hold, and the one reply that must land for the thesis to survive. Include the objection I should be most afraid of — I suspect it concerns who counts as the community when the descendants themselves divide over the memory — and identify the weakest part of my position, which I should prepare to defend rather than hope nobody notices. Throughout, separate what is settled about the relation between collective memory and the historical record from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
