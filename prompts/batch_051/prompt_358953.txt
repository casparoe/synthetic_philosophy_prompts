I'm pretty sold on prioritarianism: the view that we should maximize total well-being, but with extra weight attached to benefits that go to people who are worse off. What I like about it is that it captures the sense that a given gain matters more down the distribution, without treating equality itself as a good. That's the feature I keep coming back to, because it means the view dodges the leveling-down objection in a clean way. Suppose the best off person is at 100 and the worst off is at 10. Leveling the best off down to 20 while leaving the worst off at 10 doesn't improve anything on this view, because nobody is better off and total weighted well-being drops. As an all-things-considered verdict, that seems obviously right to me.

But I have this stubborn intuition I cant fully shake. Compare those two worlds side by side. World A: best off at 100, worst off at 10. World B: best off at 20, worst off at 10. I find myself wanting to say that B is better in at least one respect, or that something bad has been removed, namely the size of the gap between them. And I don't just mean the worse off person would feel less envy, or the society would be more stable, or any downstream effect like that. I mean that the inequality itself is a bad feature of A. If that intuition is right, prioritarianism misses a dimension of value that egalitarianism catches.

So here's what I'm stuck on. I don't want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would actually take for the intuition to be the thing that goes: what would a genuinely successful debunking explanation look like, and how would I tell it apart from simply refusing to listen to the intuition. And I want to know what it would take for prioritarianism to go instead, or to need amendment: what exactly would the view have to be modified to capture, and what would it lose by adding it. Is there a reading of "better in one respect" on which both survive, or is that just a hedge with no real content? Walk me through the costs of each move and what would justify it.
