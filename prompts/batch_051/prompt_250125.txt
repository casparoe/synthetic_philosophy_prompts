I’m a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. I recently worked through the third chapter of Nelson Goodman’s Fact, Fiction, and Forecast, where he introduces the predicate ‘grue’ with, roughly, this definition in paraphrase: an object is grue if it is examined before some future time t and is green, or examined after t and is blue. His argument is that all our observations before t support “all emeralds are grue” just as strongly as “all emeralds are green,” even though we find one of those generalizations absurd. Goodman then in effect asks what licenses the distinction. His answer, as I understand it, is that predicates like “green” are entrenched in our past inductive practice, while “grue” is not.

That passage makes me think of a failure mode I have seen in production machine learning systems. Please write a deadpan internal postmortem, around 600 words and in American English, for a fictional ML forecasting service whose model has learned a ‘grue’-like feature: it fits all training and validation data through timestamp T, and then flips its predictions after T exactly as the feature defines. The form should be an engineering incident report with sections such as Summary, Root Cause, What Went Well, and Action Items. The satire should stay fair to Goodman’s actual point: use the incident to dramatize why a model that optimizes only for fit to observed data cannot automatically distinguish projectible from non-projectible predicates, and why the engineers’ proposed fixes keep presupposing the distinction they claim to be deriving from data. Do not state that moral outright; let the report reveal it.
