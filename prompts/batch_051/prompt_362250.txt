I'm a graduate student in epidemiology, and I recently found myself in a long argument with my advisor about whether our causal-inference machinery earns the right to use the word "cause." That led me back to Bertrand Russell's 1912 essay On the Notion of Cause. I had remembered it as a historical curiosity, but rereading it made me realize how directly it bears on what I do every day. The part I keep thinking about is the passage in which Russell observes that the law of gravitation does not contain the word "cause," and that in the advanced physical sciences causal terms get replaced by functional relations among variables. He goes on to suggest that the notion of cause is a relic of a pre-scientific, anthropomorphic view of nature, kept alive mostly by habit.

I want to know whether that argument survives what has happened since. My advisor, who works with structural causal models and do-calculus, says Russell's worry has been overtaken: causal concepts have now been given mathematically precise content through directed acyclic graphs, interventions, and counterfactuals, and they are indispensable in epidemiology, economics, and the social sciences. But I am not sure this addresses Russell's point rather than merely relocating it. Does the rise of interventionist causal modeling refute Russell's argument, repair it, or leave its core intact? Which premise of his argument is actually under pressure—the claim that mature science can do without causal vocabulary, the claim that causal notions are inherently anthropomorphic, or something else? If his conclusion is too strong, is there a weaker Russellian position that a philosophically careful user of causal models ought to accept?
