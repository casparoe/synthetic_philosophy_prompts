I have spent years moving between the Netherlands and Japan, and I have watched people I deeply respect handle the same small situation in opposite ways. A candor-first friend — often, in my experience, Dutch — will tell someone plainly that the meal was bland or the haircut unfortunate, because being given the truth straight is treated as a form of respect. A consideration-first friend — often, in my experience, Japanese — will find something sincerely positive to say, or answer around the question, because protecting the other person's face until they can notice the problem themselves is treated as a form of respect. I am not a philosopher; I am just someone who has had to live inside both norms, and I want a document that a thoughtful advocate of each of these two positions could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on the question: when, if ever, is a white lie told to protect another person's feelings morally wrong?

Please write that document in two parts. First, a numbered list of agreed claims: what both positions actually hold about honesty, care, respect, and harm; what each side is not claiming; and what each side admits the other gets right, stated in wording the other side would accept. Second, a disagreements table. For each remaining disagreement, give the precise statement of the difference, with each side's own preferred wording written down, and then identify the crux under that disagreement — the sub-question whose settlement would move at least one party. For at least two of the cruxes, propose a concrete, realistic social situation that both sides would accept as a fair test of the crux, and record in advance what each side predicts about how it should be handled, along with what outcome or observation each side agrees would count against its own position.

Conclude by saying which parts of their disagreement are substantive and which are mostly verbal or produced by different uses of words like respect and honesty, and then state the narrowest residual disagreement the two parties could not dissolve.
