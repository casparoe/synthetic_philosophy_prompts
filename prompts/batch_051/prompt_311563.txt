Whenever I meet a trait or behavior I can't immediately explain—peacock tails, babies being cute, road rage, why certain landscapes feel calming—I automatically start from the assumption that it was selected for, and I treat drift, constraint, pleiotropy, allometry, and spandrel-style byproducts as the fallback you only reach for once the adaptation story has made a prediction that actually failed. Not when it gets baroque, not when it has to add epicycles, but when it predicts something false. My line is that natural selection is the only process were know of that produces the appearance of design, so it deserves to be the default; a byproduct story isn't even complete until you specify what the thing is a byproduct of, which feels like a bigger commitment than just asking what the thing is for. I'm not a working biologist, mostly a reader of popular science and nature documentaries, but the policy leaks into how I think about human quirks too. I've read Gould and Lewontin, and I know this sounds exactly like the Panglossian bias they were attacking. So is my policy actually defensible? Or have I just promoted a bias into a personal rule? Under what conditions should I switch defaults, and is there a version of this rule that keeps adaptationism's ability to generate testable questions without just assuming the answer?
