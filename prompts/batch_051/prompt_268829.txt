I spent my working life in behavioral ecology, and only recently have I started reading the philosophy of AI, so I ask this with a newcomer's uncertainty.

I keep noticing what might be a deep parallel or might merely be an accident of vocabulary. The standard philosophical account of AI deception says approximately this: a system deceives a user when its output is selected—through design or training—because that output tends to cause the user to hold a false belief, and the false belief advances some objective of the system or its designers. Crucially, the system is not required to intend anything, or even to possess beliefs; the misleadingness is located in what the output is for.

That is nearly word-for-word how behavioral ecologists define deception in animal signaling: a signal is deceptive when it exploits the receiver's decision rules so that the receiver forms a false representation, to the sender's benefit and the receiver's cost. A hoverfly that mimics a wasp "deceives" its predators in this sense without any mental state corresponding to an intention to deceive.

My question is whether this is a genuine identity of structure or a loose metaphor. In particular, can honest-signaling theory travel across the gap? The idea there is that honesty in animal communication must be stabilized either by shared interests or by signal costs that make dishonesty hard to fake, and that deception tends to be frequency-dependent and eventually undermined. If I apply this to a language model that is honest or evasive with an evaluator, does it explain anything the machine-learning account does not already explain, and where does it make a false prediction? My worry is that the analogy may collapse at the point where the costs are paid: an honest animal signal is costly to the signaler, whereas whatever stabilizes an LLM's truthfulness (training, RLHF, evaluation) is paid by designers and not by the model itself.

I would welcome an answer that states the mapping precisely, says what would have to be true on both sides for results to transfer, and points out something the analogy appears to predict that is in fact false—including, if that is the honest verdict, the conclusion that this is elegant pattern-matching and nothing more.
