I have been defending a position in moral philosophy and want it attacked properly rather than politely. Please play the role of a moral philosopher with no prior commitment to utilitarianism but with a working command of debates about aggregation, lexical priority, and the badness of suffering. Think through the problem step by step, and begin with some unstructured brainstorming before you produce your organized answer.

The position I hold is a lexical threshold negative utilitarianism with a commensurability caveat. Its commitments are: (1) suffering is the only thing of intrinsic negative moral value, and happiness counts only derivatively—roughly, insofar as its absence or loss is itself a source of suffering; (2) there is a threshold in the severity of suffering, drawn by the subject's relation to it: unendurable suffering is the kind the person undergoing it would at the time do anything to end, while below-threshold suffering is something one can bear without that structure; (3) below the threshold, pleasure and pain are commensurable in the ordinary additive way, so a large enough benefit can justify a mild harm and a large enough heap of mild harms can outweigh one mild harm; (4) above the threshold, the badness is lexical: no amount of below-threshold suffering and no amount of pleasure can outweigh a single episode of unendurable suffering, no matter how the numbers scale. The intuition doing the work for me is the pinprick argument: I find it impossible to accept that enough pinpricks could add up to something as bad as one person's unendurable agony. On this view I am committed to saying that preventing one person's unendurable agony is worth causing any finite number of pinpricks to others, while the reverse trade is never worth making, and no pile of pleasure ever purchases a single such agony.

What would count against it? I want an honest map of the position's exposure, sorted from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to say for each proposed defeater whether a committed defender of the position would concede that it counts or would dismiss it and why. Address at least the following, though add anything else you think cuts deeper: whether the threshold can be drawn non-arbitrarily given that a pain can be made to cross it by continuous increases in intensity; whether the view collapses under pressure into either ordinary aggregation or an all-pains-lexical view; whether it yields judgments about trade-offs, rights, or population ethics that even its defenders should find hard to accept, including the familiar world-annihilating implications of negative utilitarianism and whether the threshold makes those implications better or worse; which of these escape routes are live and which are merely logical possibilities; and what the view predicts about borderline cases that its rivals do not. Conclude with an honest verdict on whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability, and, if so, whether that insulation is a defect or an acceptable feature of a view of this kind.
