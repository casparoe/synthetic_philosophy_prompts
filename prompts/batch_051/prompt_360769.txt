I am setting the final essay question for an upper-level philosophy of language seminar. The exam is 90 minutes and closed-book. The question should be built around the following passage from Bertrand Russell, "On Denoting" (1905):

"The subject of denoting is of very great importance, not only in logic and mathematics, but also in the theory of knowledge. For example, we know that the center of mass of the solar system at a definite instant is some definite point, and we can affirm a number of propositions about it; but we have no immediate acquaintance with this point, which is only known to us by description. The distinction between acquaintance and knowledge about is the distinction between the things we have presentations of, and the things we only reach by means of denoting phrases."

Write an essay question that makes students do two things. First, explain why Russell thinks the center of mass example shows that thought and knowledge can outrun acquaintance, and how his analysis of definite descriptions is meant to underwrite that claim. Second, evaluate whether the account can explain how a speaker's assertion or thought is about the center of mass itself, rather than about whatever object happens to satisfy the description. The question should force students to develop the description/object relation rather than reciting Russell's analysis or the sense/reference distinction.

Then provide a model answer of about 600 words and a marking scheme that separates an A from a B by naming the specific interpretive and critical moves each must contain. The model answer should be in American English.
