I have been thinking about Douglas Hofstadter's strange-loop account of the self, and I noticed a structural parallel that I cannot decide is deep or superficial. On Hofstadter's view, a human self is not a discrete thing but a pattern of symbol-processing; lower-resolution versions of that pattern are continually copied into the brains of other people, especially those who know and love the person. When the original brain dies, the self does not go out of existence all at once: it persists, dimly and partially, in those other brains.

Here is the parallel. In distributed-systems engineering, a service is kept available by replicating its state across multiple nodes. If the primary node fails, the replicas keep serving, and the system as a whole survives the loss of any one node. Replication brings a well-known set of tradeoffs: replicas must be kept consistent, they can drift apart while partitioned from one another, and one often accepts eventual consistency in exchange for availability.

That seems to me to map almost point-for-point onto Hofstadter's picture. Each person who knows me holds a replica of my self; death is the failure of the primary node; the surviving replicas are lower-fidelity and mutually inconsistent; and the self that remains is, as it were, eventually consistent.

I would like to know whether this is a real identity of structure, a loose analogy, or a confusion. If it is real, I would like it made precise: what exactly maps to what, and what would have to be true on both sides for results to transfer? For instance, does the consistency-availability tradeoff transfer to persons—that is, must a posthumously distributed self trade coherence among its versions for continuance in any one of them? And what does the analogy predict that turns out false? I suspect it breaks around first-personal awareness, or around the fact that Hofstadter's low-resolution copies are not themselves subjects, but I want a rigorous account of where exactly the mapping fails and whether the analogy earns its keep or only borrows vocabulary.
