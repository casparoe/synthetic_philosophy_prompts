In a course on the ethics of belief, I was taught a working set of criteria for when a person has a genuine moral duty to subject one of their own beliefs to critical scrutiny — a "duty to doubt." The criteria were these: (1) the belief matters because it guides decisions that would plausibly harm others if false; (2) the person has the time, skill, and resources to investigate without bearing costs wildly disproportionate to what is at stake; and (3) there is some concrete, specific indication that the belief may be mistaken, over and above the bare abstract possibility that any belief could be false.

Here is the case that puzzles me. Mireya has for years been a volunteer translator and intake coordinator for a small refugee-aid organization run by a founder she admires. Recently she received an anonymous letter alleging that the founder diverts a fraction of donations to personal expenses, and she noticed two minor discrepancies between the organization's public annual report and the receipts and bank slips she handles. Each discrepancy has an innocent explanation offered by the founder, but neither explanation can be confirmed from the documents she is currently permitted to see. Mireya has the accounting background to audit the records herself, but doing so would require copying confidential files and taking them to the organization's board without the founder's knowledge. If her concerns turn out to be groundless, the accusation alone could destroy an organization on which several dozen refugee families — including her brother's household — depend. Does Mireya now have a duty to doubt, according to the criteria? Please evaluate each criterion in turn, identify where the criteria underdetermine the answer, and then say whether the criteria themselves need to be revised in light of this case. Lead with your bottom-line verdict in the first sentence, and then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
