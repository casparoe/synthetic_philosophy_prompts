You are a philosopher working at the intersection of virtue ethics and moral psychology, with a particular interest in distinctions that collapse too easily in ordinary talk — here, the distinctions among temperance, self-control or willpower, prudence, and repression. Please think through the cases one by one, first sketching two or three candidate principles that might organize my verdicts, noticing where each candidate strains, and only then giving your best proposal. Be explicit about which of my verdicts cannot be saved by the principle you recommend, and say why you would overturn that verdict rather than complicate the principle to keep it.

I have been collecting my own reactions to cases in this area, and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to. My rough working hunch is this: temperance is present when the desire itself is well ordered; self-control is present when a recalcitrant desire is well governed; prudence is present when the governing consideration is merely instrumental; and repression is present when the desire is disowned or attacked rather than integrated. But I can already see the hunch struggling with several of the cases below, so treat it as a revisable first pass.

Here are my verdicts.

1. A man who loves chocolate, eats a small square each evening, never binges, and reports no struggle or temptation to eat more. I call this temperance, though I am uneasy because there is no recalcitrant desire for him to master.

2. A woman who quit smoking ten years ago and no longer wants cigarettes at all; refraining costs her nothing. I call this temperance, but it began as a health decision, so I worry it is only prudence whose origins are no longer felt.

3. A dieter who fights strong junk-food cravings all day, locks the kitchen with a timed app, succeeds, but remains preoccupied with food and unhappy. I call this self-control, perhaps admirable, but not temperance.

4. A person who deals with any urge to drink by telling himself that the urge is not really his — a foreign intruder — and refuses to acknowledge it. He stays sober but cannot give a sincere account of what he wants. I call this repression, not self-control.

5. A woman who gives up drinking after a health scare, still wants a drink each evening, and manages it with seltzer and a ritual. I call this prudence rather than temperance, because the desire itself has not changed; but I cannot say why this is not as good as the ex-smoker in case 2.

6. A monk who fasts as a spiritual discipline, and who, rather than banishing hunger, pays close attention to it and lets it teach him something. I call this a kind of temperance or askesis, not repression, even though he deliberately courts a desire he will not satisfy.

7. A person with a strong attraction to a married coworker, who keeps interactions brief, refuses to dwell on the attraction, and fully admits to himself that the attraction exists. I call this self-control in service of decent boundaries, not repression.

8. A child who does not take cookies before dinner only because she will be punished and would take one the moment she is unsupervised. I call this neither temperance nor self-control — mere compliance under threat. But I cannot articulate why being governed by threat is different from self-control when both override a desire.

9. A marathoner who keeps a strict sleep schedule purely as a means to a race time. I call this prudence, not temperance, and not repression, though he does override the desire to stay up late.

10. An adult raised to regard sexual desire as shameful, who now monitors every passing thought, feels guilt at any attraction, and treats his own desire as an enemy to be exterminated. I call this repression, and likely destructive; yet I worry that if the monk's deliberate refusal counts as legitimate discipline, this might too, and I do not want to say that.

My questions are these. Is there one principle, or a small family of candidate principles, that captures all or most of these verdicts? What role does the recalcitrance of desire play in distinguishing temperance from self-control? Does the origin of the restraint — fear of punishment, health advice, moral conviction, social expectation, spiritual discipline — matter for whether the resulting state is virtue, prudence, or repression? Does the monk's case really differ from the shame-raised adult's case, and if so, what precisely does the difference consist in? What does the account you propose predict for cases I have not listed, such as a person who meditates with the aim of extinguishing craving altogether, or a teenager who refrains from drinking mainly to fit in with a sober peer group? And which of my verdicts should I give up?
