I'm a retired chemist, and for the last year or so I've been reading philosophy of mind, which is how I ended up going round and round on qualia with my friend Theo. Theo did a chemistry degree before switching to philosophy, and he's become something of a Dennettian: he thinks the notion of qualia is an incoherent posit and that the famous cases don't actually force it on us. I've kept a list of his verdicts on the standard cases. On Chase and Sanborn, the two coffee tasters who both enjoy the same coffee less — Chase says the coffee now tastes different to him, Sanborn says it tastes the same but his standards have gone up — Theo says the reports can be cashed out entirely as changed dispositions to classify, prefer, and judge, so positing intrinsic private properties does no work. On blindsight, where a patient denies seeing but guesses shapes above chance, he says there's a real difference from normal sight, but it's fully captured by whether the information is available for report and voluntary action, so no extra quale is needed. On a wine taster who after training says the same wine now tastes different, he says training changed her discriminations and attention, nothing more. When I pushed him with a first-year who had just heard of the inverted spectrum and announced his colours might be inverted, Theo said the boy couldn't keep the idea stable or say what would settle it, so the report wasn't even a coherent datum. And on a subject who insisted red and green "feel different" beyond any disposition but couldn't sort, name, or react differently and whose report wandered from day to day, Theo said it was just noise.

Now here's the case I can't get him to commit on, or rather I haven't asked yet because I want to know what he should say if he's being consistent. A patient in a drug trial — a former painter, articulate, not suggestible — reports, weeks after the drug, that colours look different to her. She doesn't sort them differently, name them differently, like different ones, or show any change in matching, memory, emotion, or physiology. Every test we can devise comes out identical to her baseline, and the only thing that has changed is that she steadily and consistently reports the colours themselves look altered. If Theo wants to be consistent with his earlier verdicts, what should he say about this one? Which of his earlier cases is the closest analog, and if his precedents underdetermine it, what principle would he be adopting with each choice? And what further detail or case would cleanly seperate the candidate rules he might be using?
