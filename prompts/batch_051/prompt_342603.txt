After a long argument last night, I want to write a short essay defending my side, and I would like comments on the notes below. My friend, who works in AI policy, kept insisting that aligning AI with human values is fundamentally an engineering problem: elicit people's preferences, aggregate them, and put the result in the objective function. I think that move hides a philosophical problem, and I want to know whether the argument as sketched actually works.

Working title: Human values are not a data source.

The core argument: The phrase "align AI with human values" faces a dilemma. Read individually, a person's values are not a stable object waiting to be elicited; revealed preferences are incomplete, context-sensitive, and often constructed in the choice itself, so alignment lacks a fixed target. Read collectively, aggregation is not a neutral technical step. Majority preferences can cycle; Pareto-respecting social choice can conflict with individual liberty; and Harsanyi-style derivations of utilitarianism reach their conclusion only because their assumptions already encode impartial, risk-neutral extended preferences. Any aggregation rule therefore embodies a substantive normative choice, and liberal neutrality makes this explicit: a genuinely neutral procedure cannot choose among rival conceptions of the good, yet refusing to choose is itself a substantive stance and leaves the system without determinate values to optimize. The slogan is thus either empty or a disguised commitment to a moral theory.

Please comment on whether this dilemma is genuine or whether one horn collapses, and whether I have overstated what the impossibility results show. Do not survey possible replies. Take a definite position on whether the argument succeeds, and if you think it fails, identify the single premise you would reject first and defend that choice.
