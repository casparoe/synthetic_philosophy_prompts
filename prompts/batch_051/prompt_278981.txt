My company's anti-corruption training walks us through a compliance manual that draws a bunch of lines I can't quite reconstruct the logic of. I can accept a bottle of wine from a supplier if I disclose it within 48 hours, but not a case of wine; a fruit basket from a client's office is fine, but a box of chocolates from the same client during a tender is a red flag. The US law our parent company follows has an exception for small "facilitation payments" to a low-level official just to do their job — stamp the permit they're already supposed to stamp — while the UK law our London office follows doesnt allow those at all. Meanwhile my colleagues in our Bangkok office say refusing a gift from a longstanding partner is an insult and would damage the relationship more than any "corruption" the gift could cause. What theory of corruption does this whole apparatus presuppose? In particular, does it presuppose that corruption is basically a priced quid pro quo, so the problem is size and explicitness rather than the relationship itself? Does it presuppose a picture of officials as impersonal role-holders who shouldn't form personal obligations — a picture clientelist systems would reject? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly needs from what it merely sits well with, say where it's philosophically underdetermined (several theories could license it), and tell me what a compliance regime built on a relational or culturally pluralist view of gifts would have to change. Also please flag what's settled from what's genuinely contested here, rather than presenting one camp as the consensus.
