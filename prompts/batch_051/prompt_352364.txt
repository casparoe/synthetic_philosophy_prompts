I recently had to choose between staying in a comfortable, predictable position and taking one that paid less and preserved none of my old routines, and I found that neither the hedonistic question (which life will feel better overall?) nor the eudaimonic question (which life is more meaningful?) captured what I was actually weighing. What I was weighing was the fear of a boring life. I have tried to unpack that fear, and it has led me to a small theory that I would like assessed.

I call it the falsification account of novelty. Its central claim is that novelty is not an ingredient of well-being, alongside pleasure or meaning, but a maintenance condition for having a self at all: the set of futures you can imagine for yourself — call it your horizon — must be periodically contradicted by experience if it is to remain a horizon rather than a schedule. When outcomes keep arriving as predicted, the horizon quietly hardens into a timetable, and the future stops pulling on you because you already hold it. The distinctive harm of a boring life, on this view, is not the absence of enjoyment but this horizon-hardening. The analogy I keep reaching for is that novelty stands to the self roughly as REM sleep stands to memory: not itself a nutrient, but a process whose absence degrades the machinery that makes other goods available.

To keep the theory from being merely atmospheric, I distinguish three things. Variety is new content inside an unchanged frame: a new restaurant, a new route to work, a new topic of conversation. Adventure is deliberate exposure to outcome-uncertainty about matters that touch your self-understanding: moving somewhere you know no one, taking work whose demands you cannot yet predict, entering a friendship that might alter you. Novelty, strictly speaking, is the falsification of some prediction you had been treating as background; it can be small, as when someone reformulates a problem so that it briefly makes no sense to you, or large, as when a loss or success reorders what you thought came next. The thesis is that variety alone is palliative, adventure is the usual delivery mechanism, and novelty is the actual nutrient.

I am aware that this may already exist under other names. It seems adjacent to predictive processing, hedonic adaptation, the self-expansion model, sensation seeking, and narrative identity, but I cannot tell whether the specific construct — novelty as falsification, and the harm of boredom as horizon-hardening — has already been worked out, or whether it merely borrows authority from those frameworks while adding nothing. I also want to know whether it is true enough to guide the real decision I am facing rather than just to redescribe it.

So I would like a candid assessment. First, is the theory coherent enough to evaluate, and if not, which part is doing no work: the falsification idea, the horizon-hardening idea, or the analogy? Second, what is the nearest existing idea, and does my version say anything that idea does not — what, if anything, is worth keeping? Third, where does the theory break or misclassify? Give me the strongest counterexample you can think of: a boring life that is not a hardened horizon, or a novelty-rich life that is not thereby more well. And tell me plainly whether this is a discovery or a set of metaphors with false precision.
