So I'm a novelist, and the historical fiction writers around me have this line they lean on constantly: we're just doing what Herodotus did — collecting variants, keeping the one that fits the feel of the period, flagging the rest — and the twentieth century just gave that method a fancy name, source criticism. From there the move is: a well-researched novel is therefore as reliable a picture of a place and period as any history book, just delivered through invented scenes instead of footnotes. I've started to suspect the analogy is carrying to much weight and I want it audited. Give me the correspondence table: what in the novelist's practice is supposed to map onto Herodotus's 'this is what I heard, this is what I saw' moves, onto Thucydides' claim to have been there and his dismissal of the poets and logographers, onto internal criticism (filling the gaps in a coherent way) and external criticism (authenticating documents and checking provenance)? Then the places it breaks: where the novelist's need to make the past feel continuous and meaningful forces choices a source critic would mark as unfounded, where the economic pressures of fiction select for the clean story over the messy record, where invented dialogue does something no footnote can do. Which of those breaks matter for the conclusion that the novel is reliable as history, and which are just genre differences? Does the conclusion survive, or was the analogy doing all the work? And if the strong version dies, what narrower claim could someone still honestly make about what a historically serious novelist is doing?
