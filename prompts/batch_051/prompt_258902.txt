Right, this one's for a series I'm doing on old thought experiments with one knob turned. I want you to take Robert Nozick's experience machine, vary it, and then work through how the analysis changes.

Here's the modified setup (not the original, so don't just rehearse the standard discussion). A machine is built that does not feed you pleasant simulations. It puts you into a permanent state of complete absence of craving and suffering: no pain, no wants, no boredom, no regrets, no hopes, no curiosity, no felt need to leave or to find out whether anything is real, and likewise no positive pleasure, no achievements, no relationships, no awareness of the outside world. The state isn't blissful; from the inside nothing matters, including whether the state continues. Before deciding, you get a ten-minute trial. During the trial you are fully in that state; afterwards you snap back to your ordinary values, and you remember that while it was happening nothing seemed to matter either way. After the trial you must chose once: enter permanently or never. Entry is irreversible, and the machine ensures you will never want to leave.

Call the relevant view 'tranquilism': only the absence of craving and suffering matters; pleasure, achievement, knowledge, love, and so on have no intrinsic value except instrumentally, insofar as they reduce craving or suffering. I don't need citations or a survey of Nozick scholarship, just the arguments.

The question. Nozick's original case against the experience machine gives roughly three reasons: we want to actually do things rather than merely have the experiences of doing them; we want to be a certain kind of person; and we want contact with a deeper reality. Work through each of those in turn for this modified machine. Does tranquilism give a different answer to each than ordinary hedonism does, and does desire-satisfaction theory end up somewhere else altogether? In particular: is the tranquillist's reply to the 'contact with reality' point just that the desire for reality is one more craving to be extinguished, and if so does that make the view self-sealing or suspiciously convenient? And where is the real trouble for tranquilism in this case — the fact that you have to decide while you still have cravings, or the fact that entering would extinguish the very desires that approved the entry in the first place? End with a worked-through verdict: on this modified machine, is tranquilism more or less plausible than in the standard case, and why?

You are a professional philosopher working on well-being and practical rationality.
