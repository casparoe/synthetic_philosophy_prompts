My wife read one of those "your brain decides before you do" books last year and now every dinner turns into the Libet argument, so I finally talked my reading group into a proper adversarial collaboration. We have two camps: the Libetians, who think Libet's readiness potential experiments show that the conscious will is at best a latecomer and that free will as people normally understand it is in trouble, and the compatibilists, who think the experiments show something real but mostly about motor preparation, not about the decisions that matter for moral responsibility. I know the names are terrible but they stuck.

Write the joint statement these two groups could actually both sign after a good-faith adversarial collabortion. I want it in this format:

1. A numbered "agreed claims" section. It should cover what both camps accept about the experimental setup and the findings — the Bereitschaftspotential, the clock method, the order of the readiness potential and the reported conscious intention, what the experiments did and didn't measure. The claims should be neither vacuous nor slanted, and each side should actually be able to sign them.

2. A disagreements table. For each disagreement, give the precise statement each side would accept as its own view — so the Libetians would say "yes, that is exactly what I think" and the compatibilists would say the same about theirs. Don't paraphrase either position into something they'd reject.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one side. I suspect the real cruxes include (a) whether the readiness potential is a signature of the specific action decision or just non-specific motor preparation, (b) whether the reported time of the conscious intention can be trusted given the clock method Libet used, and (c) whether the "veto" is a real voluntary power or itself has an unconscious precursor. Add any others you think are doing the actual work.

4. For each crux, describe one concrete experiment or case both camps would agree bears on it, and write down in advance what each side predicts. For example, for (a), something like a two-button free-choice version where one side predicts the early readiness potential will already be lateralized toward the eventual choice and the other predicts it stays bilateral until after the reported choice — but you should tighten the design so both sides would actually accept it as a fair test.

Finally, if you think part of the dispute is verbal or comes from the two sides using "decision" or "free will" differently, say which part and why. And if the collaboration would actually change either side's stated position, say what it would change to. I mostly want something I can bring home to my wife, but I want it to be the honest version, not the marriage-saving version.
