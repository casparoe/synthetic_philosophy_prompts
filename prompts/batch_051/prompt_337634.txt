I would like an audit of a set of commitments I hold about how scarce health resources should be allocated. I suspect that the set may not be fully consistent, but I cannot locate the strain, and I do not want to prejudge the question by saying which commitment I would be most willing to revise.

My commitments are these. First, in acute scarcity—such as the allocation of ventilators or intensive care beds during a pandemic—I believe it is legitimate, and probably right, to use a triage protocol that prioritizes expected survival and expected life-years saved, with the result that younger patients and those without serious comorbidities often receive priority; I also believe ability to pay should play no role in such decisions. Second, in ordinary cost-benefit analysis for regulation and safety policy, I believe all lives should count equally: agencies should use a single value of a statistical life for everyone, without adjusting it upward for the wealthy or downward for the old, and without valuing a year of life more highly merely because it belongs to a younger person. Third, I believe the quality-adjusted life year is the best currency we have for comparing health interventions, and that cost per quality-adjusted life year should guide decisions about which treatments and programs receive public funding. Fourth, I am committed to the view that health systems must not discriminate against disabled people, and I am suspicious of any measure that assigns a lower quality weight to years lived with a disability, since such a measure seems to make treating or saving disabled people look less valuable than treating or saving nondisabled people. Fifth, I accept that the rule of rescue can make it right to spend extraordinary resources to save an identifiable person in immediate danger—a child trapped underground, a patient with a very rare disease for which a costly treatment exists—even when the same resources would produce more quality-adjusted life years if spent on prevention or population health.

Do these five commitments hang together? I would like the response to be an audit rather than a lecture. Please order whatever tensions you find by severity; distinguish the tensions that are genuine from those that are only apparent, and say why the apparent ones dissolve; and for each genuine tension, say what would have to give, and what the cheapest repair would be and what it would cost. Please be specific about which commitment, or which underlying principle, cannot remain as I have stated it.

Finally, please tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict about whether the set is consistent, and where in your own reasoning you think you are most likely to be wrong. Write the entire response as continuous prose, as in a letter or an email, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
