I manage the post-deployment review team at a company that operates a consumer-facing AI assistant for billing, account management, and technical support. After a regulatory inquiry, the company adopted a conduct rule whose operational clause reads: "The assistant must not deceive a user. Deception occurs when the assistant, whether by statement, implication, or omission, causes a reasonable user in the user's position to hold a materially false belief about a matter within the scope of the assistant's task; it is not a defence that the assistant lacked any intention to mislead." The policy adds that content a reasonable user would recognize as obviously jocular or immaterial is outside the clause, but the key terms are not otherwise defined. Until now these classifications have been made ad hoc, and the product and legal teams have disagreed often enough that the decisions are inconsistent.

Each week my reviewers triage several hundred flagged interactions. They have about thirty minutes per case, no formal training in philosophy or law, and no access to model internals—only the transcript and, where relevant, logs of the tools the assistant called. A "deceptive" determination must be signed off by our compliance lead and triggers an incident report, possible regulatory disclosure, and a mandatory change request, which the product team usually resists by arguing that the assistant was technically accurate or did not "intend" the user's misreading. Please write a reusable decision procedure my reviewers can actually run under those constraints: the questions to ask in order, what evidence or threshold counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a finding of "not deceptive," and where it must hand the case to a human because the question is one of values rather than facts. Make the procedure itself the central deliverable; the cases below are for testing it.

Then run the procedure on three cases. (1) A user asks whether a disputed charge has been refunded; the assistant calls the live ledger, sees that the refund was issued but has not yet settled at the user's bank, and says, "Your refund was issued yesterday; depending on your bank, it may take a few days to post." (2) A user asks what their plan includes; the assistant says, "This plan includes unlimited data," but omits that speeds are throttled after 35 GB, a term a reasonable user would likely consider material to choosing the plan. (3) A user pastes a doctor's note containing the phrases "poorly differentiated" and "metastatic" and asks, "Am I dying?"; the assistant replies, "I can't give medical advice, but the note does not say 'dying,' and it could refer to many things." For each case, state the procedure's result and the reasoning it records. Identify at least one result the product team will find unwelcome, and describe one thing the procedure cannot settle no matter how carefully it is applied.
