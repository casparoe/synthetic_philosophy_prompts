Act as a professional philosopher working in normative ethics and population ethics, and think carefully: first reconstruct my position in its strongest form, then locate it among nearby views, then attack it.

I have been trying to formalize a response to the dust-specks-versus-torture problem without just insisting on a brute intuition that one episode of torture is worse than any number of dust specks. The position I keep arriving at is something I have been calling the No Sum Without a Bearer principle. The claim is that a quantity of harm may enter moral arithmetic only if some single subject, or at least a single continuous stream of experience, could be worse off by that quantity. Dust specks distributed across an astronomically large population do generate an enormous total, but no actual person is worse off by more than a speck, so I want to say the total is a statistical artifact rather than a moral fact. Inside a single life, by contrast, harms can be summed over time, subject to the usual worries about discounting, memory, and continuity, because there is a bearer whose life as a whole can be made better or worse by that sum. The principle therefore dissolves the original dilemma without committing me to the much stronger claim that numbers never matter: one agonized person outweighs any number of people each receiving a dust speck, but many moderate harms within one person's life can still outweigh one severe harm within that same life.

What bothers me is that this may collapse into a more familiar position, or may have consequences I could not sign off on. It seems close to the view that the number of people on each side of a tradeoff is itself morally weightless, and close to the standard objection that utilitarianism ignores the separateness of persons. But I intend something narrower: I still want within-life aggregation, and I still want a whole life to be a unit of moral accounting in some interpersonal comparisons. So where does this actually sit relative to existing anti-aggregation views, and where exactly does it break? Consider the case of a single person who receives many tiny harms spread out over decades, never consciously linking them and perhaps forgetting each as it passes. Am I forced by my principle to sum those harms across the life? If not, how do I distinguish one person over time from many persons at a time? And if I am forced to sum them, does that show the principle is really doing its work through continuity or memory rather than through the idea that there is a bearer? Please give me the three most damaging objections, and say which of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable.
