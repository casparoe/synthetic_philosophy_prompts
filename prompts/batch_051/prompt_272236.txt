In my formal epistemology seminar we went through van Fraassen's Reflection principle, and I've ended up with an argument that seems to prove too much: every step looks fine in isolation, but the conclusion is absurd, and I can't see where it comes apart. Here's the principle as I understand it. Let P be my current credence and P_t my credence at some future time t. Reflection says P(H | P_t(H) = r) = r: conditional on the supposition that my future self will have credence r in H, my current credence in H should be r. In other words, I'm supposed to treat my future self as an expert about H relative to my current information. The standard defense is that violating Reflection exposes you to a diachronic Dutch book, and that your future credences are, after all, the result of conditionalizing on more evidence than you now have.

Now the argument a classmate and I built:

(1) If Reflection is a requirement of rationality, then whenever I know (or am nearly certain) that my future credence in H will be r, my current credence in H should be r, or at least within a hair of r.

(2) I can know about a future credence even when that future credence won't track any evidence. For instance, I've just learned I'll be given a drug tonight that reliably makes subjects extremely confident that they can fly. I currently have overwhelming evidence that I can't fly, and I know the drug produces this confidence regardless of the evidence. I am confident that by midnight my credence that I can fly will be, say, .95.

(3) From (1) and (2), my current credence that I can fly should be within a hair of .95.

(4) But that's plainly irrational: it means adopting a credence against my current evidence, purely because a future, chemically scrambled version of me will hold it.

(5) So Reflection, taken as a coherence norm, demands something irrational. Therefore Reflection, at least unresticted, is false.

(6) In the seminar, the only principled grounds anyone floated for Reflection were the diachronic Dutch book argument and the future-self-as-expert idea. If Reflection goes unrestricted, then either those arguments are unsound or they support only some restricted principle.

(7) And here's the real trouble: every restriction I've tried — defer only to future selves that will conditionalize, only to future selves at least as informed, only to future selves I now judge rational — looks circular or ad hoc. "Will conditionalize" amounts to "will update in a way I now endorse," and "rational future self" is the very notion Reflection was supposde to underwrite.

So the argument seems to force a choice between deferring to a stoned future self as an expert and giving up a principle that should, intuitively, be close to a theorem of Bayesianism. Both are too much. Which step should I reject, what do I pay, and is there a principled restriction of Reflection that survives the case? Also: I know this area is genuinely live, so please flag what's actually settled versus what's contested among people who work on this, and don't present a contested reading as the consensus.
