I've come around to a "Malthus was right but early" view, though not in the cartoon overpopulation sense. The constraint he identified never went away, it just moved. Each time we escaped a particular scarcity—cheap fossil energy, deep aquifers, topsoil, the Haber-Bosch nitrogen fix, the atmosphere's capacity to absorb carbon—we did it by drawing down some finite buffer or degrading a sink, so diminishing returns didn't vanish, they got externalised and delayed. On this view Malthus picked the wrong constraint and the wrong timescale, but the structure was right: exponential demands eventually meet an inelastic limit.

What would count against this view? Not the standard "technology saved us" line, which I think my view absorbs, but the strongest defteaters, sorted from fatal to merely awkward. I especially want to know how much the Simon-Ehrlich result—real prices of a basket of metals falling over the 1980s while population grew—and the demographic transition actually hurt the view, versus just postponing the reckoning. What would the world have to look like for you to conclude not "early" but "wrong"—that substitution can outrun depletion indefinitely? And be honest about whether the view has been padded until it can't fail, and whether that's a problem for a claim with this long a time horizon. Which of the defeaters would someone committed to the view themselves concede counts against it, and which would they dismiss?
