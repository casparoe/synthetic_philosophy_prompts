I am a novelist, currently writing a book in which a condensed-matter physicist and a philosopher argue about emergence, and I have been trying to make my own views precise by recording verdicts on standard examples. I would like you to serve as a philosopher of science with expertise on reduction and emergence. Please think carefully: begin by brainstorming several candidate principles and testing them against my cases one by one, before settling on your final account. My eventual interest is in which of my gut verdicts survive.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. The temperature of a gas. Verdict: reducible. Temperature just is, approximately, mean molecular kinetic energy.
2. The liquidity of water. Verdict: reducible. Liquidity is nothing over and above the arrangement and motion of water molecules; no separate liquid ingredient exists.
3. Ferromagnetism. Verdict: reducible. The alignment of many spins gives you a magnet, but the macroscopic magnetization is a summary of what the spins are doing; it is not a further thing with causal power of its own.
4. Starling murmuration. Verdict: reducible, though only just. I know it can be generated by a few local rules, so I cannot call it emergent; yet the shape of the whole seems to steer the individual birds. This case nags at me.
5. A traffic jam that forms without any accident and creeps backward through the traffic. Verdict: emergent. The jam is a distinct moving object with its own history and velocity; it acts on the cars, and it is not identical to any car or group of cars.
6. Superconductivity. Verdict: emergent. It is a collective effect with no single-electron counterpart, and it gives the material new causal powers, such as zero resistance and persistent currents, even though the standard microscopic theory explains its mechanism through electron pairing.
7. An ant colony's shortest-path foraging. Verdict: reducible. Individual ants follow a pheromone rule, and the path just emerges from that, but I feel no need to posit a colony-level entity with causal oomph.
8. A stock market crash driven by panic. Verdict: emergent. The crash is a self-reinforcing pattern that feeds back on the beliefs and actions of the traders; it seems to have causal power over and above any trader's behavior.
9. The painfulness of pain. Verdict: strongly emergent and not reducible. Nothing about the underlying neurons, or about the functional role of pain, seems to capture the first-person feel.

Now my difficulty. I had been working with a rough principle: a phenomenon is emergent to the extent that the pattern produced by the parts constrains or alters the parts that produce it. That rule makes murmurations and traffic jams equally emergent, so one of those verdicts must go. It also makes ferromagnetism and superconductivity equally causal, so one of those must go too. A different idea I keep circling is that reducibility is an explanatory or epistemic matter, namely whether you could derive the macro behavior without simulating the micro behavior, while emergence is a causal or ontological matter; but I cannot make that distinction behave.

Could you supply a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that best captures my verdicts? Please state each candidate precisely, say which verdicts it accommodates and which it forces me to abandon, and apply the best account to one case I have not listed: the hexagonal cells of a honeycomb, or the fact that a trained neural network generalizes to inputs it never saw, or the rigidity of a solid table. Choose whichever you think is most revealing. I would rather keep a simple principle and drop a gut verdict than keep every verdict with an ad hoc clause.

Please end with a single line beginning exactly "Summary:" followed by a one-sentence statement of the principle you finally endorse.
