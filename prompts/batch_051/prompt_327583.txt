I keep tripping over the same structure in local housing fights, and I cant tell whether it has a name or whether I'm mashing together two different things.

First: a city debates a 40-unit apartment building on a vacant lot. Everyone at the hearing already lives there, and the argument that lands hardest is that 'we' didn't ask for the traffic, parking, or shade. The people who would rent those units dont live there yet, so they aren't in the room and can't vote. The pro-housing side speaks for them anyway: 'future residents' are the real community, they say. Second: a town votes on allowing townhouses precisely so younger families and service workers can move in. Only current residents may vote — but if the measure passes, the electorate in ten years will partly consist of the very people the vote is about.

That looks like a loop: the policy decides who the community is, while the community is supposed to decide the policy. Neither NIMBYism nor 'locals vs. outsiders' captures it, since both sides claim to speak for a local community, just at different dates. And it isn't the standard affected-interests problem either, because whether the affected people ever become residents depends on the decision itself.

Does this configuration have an established name in political philosophy or urban theory? I care less about the label than the carving: what exactly is the predicament, what are the nearest concepts, how do they differ from what I've described, and which fits best. If there's no name, propose one and give its edge cases. Also tell me if you think the loop in my examples dissolves on closer inspection.
