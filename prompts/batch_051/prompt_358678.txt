Last week two people in my decision theory reading group spent way too long on the absent-minded driver, and I'm still not sure who was right. The setup they used went like this. A driver is on a highway with two identical exits, one after the other. He can't tell them apart, and he's absent-minded: each time he reaches an exit, he can't remeber whether it's the first or the second. If he exits at the first exit, he gets 0. If he continues past the first and exits at the second, he gets 4. If he continues past both, he gets 1. He knows all this in advance, and at each exit his only options are continue or exit.

Here's the back-and-forth as best I can reconstruct it. (1) Lena argued that the driver should first settle on a policy: flip a biased coin at each exit, continuing with probability p, with each flip independent. Then expected payoff is 0 times (1−p) for exiting first, plus 4 times p(1−p) for continuing then exiting, plus 1 times p² for continuing twice. That's 4p − 3p², which is maximized at p = 2/3, giving 4/3. So the rational driver leaves home planning to continue with probability 2/3. (2) Sam replied that this is a plan, not a decision. When the driver actually finds himself at an exit, he knows he's at one of the two exits but not which. If he assumes he'll follow the policy p, the odds it's the first versus the second are 1 : p, since the first exit is always reached but the second only if he continued at the first. So exiting right now has expected value 4p/(1+p), while continuing now—assuming later him also uses p—has expected value (4−2p)/(1+p). If p is less than 2/3 he should continue; if p is more than 2/3 he should exit; only at p = 2/3 is he indifferent. So p = 2/3 is the unique policy the in-the-moment self wouldn't want to revise. But Sam immediately called that result suspect: to even form the beliefs "first with probability 1/(1+p), second with probability p/(1+p)," the driver has to already know which policy he's following, so the in-the-moment justification just recovers whatever ex ante plan was assumed. (3) Lena said that's exactly what makes it a policy rather than a one-shot guess. The plan "continue at the first exit, exit at the second" is worth 4 but is unavailable to this driver, because it requires knowing which exit he's at—the very thing absent-mindedness destroys. Given the constraint of implementing one rule on both indistinguishable occasions, p = 2/3 is the best policy that can actually be carried through, and the fact that the driver would endorse it both before leaving and upon waking is the right test of rationality, not evidence of circularity. (4) Sam said the "endorsement upon waking" isn't doing independent work—it was built in from the start. The probability of reaching the second exit isn't a fact about the world; it's the driver's own future choice. That makes ex ante expected value and in-the-moment beliefs two parts of one fixed-point equation, and fixed-point equations don't by themselves say which perspective—the planner or the amnesiac self—has authority. If the later self can't be bound, then ex ante optimality and in-the-moment optimality are genuinely different problems, and no convergence at 2/3 settles which one should govern.

I'd like a point-by-point adjudication: rate each of the four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a brief justification for each, say who is right about which specific claim, and say what important thing both of them are missing. Please don't just lay out the options; I want you to actually commit at each step and give an overall verdict about what the rational driver should do, both when planning and when sitting at an exit, and whether this is a real paradox or only a confusion.
