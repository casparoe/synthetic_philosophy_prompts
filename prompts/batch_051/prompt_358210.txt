I am a PhD student working on twelfth-century moral psychology, and I have to respond to a draft chapter for our reading group. The chapter advances a summary of Peter Abelard's account of sin that strikes me as plausible in outline but probably misleading in detail. As the chapter tells it, Abelard held that the moral character of an action is settled entirely by the agent's intention: a deed is a sin just in case the agent willed it with an evil intention, and the outward performance adds nothing, so an attempted murder and a completed murder are guilty to exactly the same degree. The chapter illustrates the view with Abelard's example of a monk who is bound and sexually violated and who remains innocent because, on the chapter's account, his will remained opposed to the act. The chapter then argues that this interiorization of ethics has a disturbing consequence: once the deed drops out of the analysis, no distinction remains between a culpable inner movement and a merely undergone one, so the first stirrings of temptation, concupiscence, and carnal pleasure would already count as sins on Abelard's principles. It concludes that Abelard's position turns ordinary felt temptation into sin and thereby proves too much.

I suspect this summary misses the very distinction that does the real work in Abelard. My own reading has been that he wants to separate consent from everything that precedes or merely accompanies it—especially desire, temptation, and involuntary pleasure—and that he insists the deed contributes nothing to the sin, though the summary seems to run these categories together. Could you audit the summary in detail? For each major claim, say what Abelard would accept, what is a conflation or overstatement (I am especially unsure about the relations among intention, will, desire, and consent), and whether the chapter's concluding objection lands against Abelard or only against the summary's version of him.
