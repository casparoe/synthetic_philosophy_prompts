Novelist here, so I spend my days writing interior monologue and treating my characters' self-reports as if they're at least partly authoritative. But I've caught myself in a wierd double standard, and it bothers me. When I explain why I ended a friendship, why I hate my last chapter, or why I said that cruel thing at dinner, I treat my explanation as direct access: I was there, I inspected the motive, I'm reporting from the scene. When someone I've known for twenty years explains the same kind of thing—why she left her husband, why she really gave up on the manuscript—with the same confidence and a track record of predicting herself about as well as I predict myself, I still hear it as rationalization, a flattering story she's telling herself after the fact. I don't think I'm unusual in this. We all treat our own introspections as data and everyone else's as confabulation until proven otherwise.

Is there any principle that justifies this? Maybe it's just that first-person access is special and third-person access isn't, but I can't see what exactly makes it special once I've matched the other person's honesty, articulacy, and predictive record to my own. What's the strongest defense of the asymmetry—not a debunking of introspection in general, but a real argument that my own introspective reports deserve more credence than yours? And where does it break down? I want an honest verdict on whether I should keep trusting the asymmetry, reverse it, or give it up.
