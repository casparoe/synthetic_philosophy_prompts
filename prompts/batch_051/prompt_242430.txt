I spent my career working on deterministic nonlinear systems, and I never quite lost the habit of thinking in terms of exponential divergence, attractors, and basins of attraction. Since turning to history I have repeatedly met the for-want-of-a-nail view of contingency: a trivial antecedent — a lost nail, a delayed message, a fit of temper — ramifies through a chain of consequences until it overturns a kingdom. That view is often presented as if it were simply the humanistic cousin of sensitive dependence on initial conditions, as though chaos theory had supplied the mathematics for an old proverb. But I suspect the invocation is too quick. The theory usually summoned in support of the proverb does not say merely that small differences grow; it also says that after local divergence a trajectory typically settles onto an attractor whose statistical properties can be remarkably robust to small perturbations of the starting point, and that basin boundaries rather than individual initial conditions organize which outcomes are possible. I would like a careful assessment of whether the for-want-of-a-nail argument for contingency in history survives contact with that fuller picture. Which premise of the argument does the structure of chaotic dynamics actually put under pressure, and can the argument be repaired into a qualified thesis about where and how small causes make a decisive difference, or does it reduce to the weaker claim that the nail decides details while the gross outcome was overdetermined?
