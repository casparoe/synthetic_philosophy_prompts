In preparing a lecture on Leibniz's theodicy, I have been turning over the following objection to the claim that the actual world is the best of all possible worlds. I cannot satisfy myself that it is sound, and I cannot localize where it slips.

(1) If God exists and is omnipotent and perfectly good, then, if he creates a world at all, the world he creates must be at least as good as every other possible world.
(2) There is no possible world at least as good as every other possible world. For suppose W is a candidate best world. Let W+ be a world just like W except that it contains one further moment of innocent, unalloyed pleasure and no additional evil. W+ is possible; and because W+ contains every good contained in W plus an additional good, W+ is better than W. Since W was arbitrary, no possible world is best.
(3) The actual world exists, and its existence is explained by God's creative act.
(4) By (1) and (3), the actual world must be the best possible world; but by (2), there is no best possible world. Contradiction.
(5) Therefore the Leibnizian position—that God creates a world and that the created world is the best of all possible worlds—cannot be maintained as stated.

I half-suspect the construction of W+ in (2) smuggles in an additive, atomistic conception of the value of a world, but I want the failure located precisely. Is the argument valid? If it is invalid, which step fails? If it is valid but unsound, state which premise a Leibnizian should reject and give the strongest Leibnizian conception of world-value or possibility under which that premise fails.
