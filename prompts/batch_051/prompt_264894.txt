In 1890 William James published The Principles of Psychology, in which consciousness appears as something continuous and self-revealing: on his account there is no gap between having a thought and being conscious of it, and our passing mental states are known from the inside without any further act of introspective observation. He also spends considerable energy diagnosing what he called the psychologist's fallacy, the habit of substituting the psychologist's after-the-fact description of a mental state for the state as it was originally lived. In the late twentieth century, philosophers such as David Rosenthal developed the higher-order thought theory of consciousness, a near-mirror-image view: a mental state is conscious only when it is the object of a distinct higher-order thought to the effect that one is in that state, so consciousness is always a relation between two separate states rather than an intrinsic feature of either. James never read this literature. Reconstruct, from the Principles, the position he would most plausibly have taken on it. Lay out the main candidates: rejection of the higher-order state as leading to a regress, reduction of it to something James had already described in his own terms, or acceptance of it as a novel empirical hypothesis about mental architecture. Then defend a verdict about which candidate, or which combination of candidates, best fits the whole of James's psychology rather than any single slogan. In giving your verdict, say specifically whether James would have seen the higher-order thought theory as a sophisticated instance of the psychologist's fallacy, as a genuine correction to an ambiguity in his own view, or as something in between.
