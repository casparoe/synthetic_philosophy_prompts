I've been going down a rabbit hole about South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission, the setup after apartheid in the mid-nineties where people who had done political violence could ask for amnesty. The deal, as I understand it, was that you had to come forward, tell the whole truth in public, and show the act was political rather than just for private gain; if you did, you walked, no trial. A lot of people write this off as impunity with better lighting, like the new government was too weak to prosecute so it dressed a deal up as reconciliation.

But I keep coming back to a different idea about what punishment is for. Maybe the point of a trial isn't mainly to hurt the offender or scare the next one; maybe it's that the state stands up in public and says, in so many words, this was wrong and the victim mattered. If punishment is really about expression and acknowledgment rather than pain, then a public confession in front of victims and the whole country might do more of the work of punishment than people think, even when nobody goes to jail. It names the person, strips the excuses, and forces an account into the record.

I want you to analyze the TRC through that lens. Map it like a case study: who the parties were and what they actually had to lose (victims, the outgoing regime, the armed wings, the new state), what a full set of trials could and couldn't of delivered in that moment, and what the amnesty-for-disclosure swap was doing if punishment is expression rather than pain. Then push back on the lens: where does it fit the real commission — the public hearings, the naming of names, the conditions on amnesty — and where does it break down, for example with families who got an acknowledgment but never got anyone held, the reparations that dragged out or never came, or the offenders who gave a performance instead of a confession? Finish by saying what the TRC shows about this expressive view of punishment: does it make the commission look better than the retributivist view does, or does it reveal that a state says whatever it needs to when its too weak to prosecute.
