I'm a PhD student doing mostly Kant and post-Kantian stuff, and I've picked up a habit when I read transcendental arguments that I can't tell whether to trust. You know the form: start from something supposedly undeniable—that we have self-conscious experience, that language can mean anything at all, that there are norms—and infer that some condition must already be in place for that thing to be possible. My rule is this: I rewrite the claim as an ordinary conditional ("if there is experience at all, then X must already be in place") and then ask whether that conditional is being offered as a conceptual truth or as a broadly psychological/empirical claim. If it looks psychological, I stop giving it any special transcendental force and demand the same evidence I'd demand of a cognitive psychologist; if the author can't supply that but still pushes the claim as a priori necessary, I mostly write the argument off.

My supervisor says this is rigging the game: any interesting necessity can be relabeled as hidden psychology and then made to fail, so my rule all but guarantees I'll never be convinced by one of these arguments. That stung, because I genuinely want to be convinced by the good ones, and I'm not sure how I'd tell from the inside whether I'm filtering or just refusing to engage. Is my policy defensible as a screening rule? If it is, what's the version of it that keeps the filtering benefit without ruling out the sound transcendental arguments; if it isn't, what should I replace it with? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning—no preamble, no restating the question.
