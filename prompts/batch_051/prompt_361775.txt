I've been in a reading group on political philosophy for the past two years, and the debate about the ethics of voting has stalled in a way that puzzles me. One side holds that eligible citizens have a pro tanto duty to turn out and vote, because voting is a contribution to self-government. Another side holds that voting is best understood as an expressive act, so what matters is what a ballot says about a voter's values, not what it causally does. A third side holds that, in elections where every viable option is morally compromised, the central question is whether one may vote for the lesser evil and thereby become complicit in wrongdoing. The three sides agree on the relevant facts and on the badness of injustice, yet no one has moved. My working theory is that this is not one dispute about a single action called 'voting' but three disputes about three different actions: participating at all, expressing one's values, and choosing among bad options. Diagnose this debate for me rather than adjudicating it. Is my diagnosis correct, or is the impasse better explained by different starting intuitions about complicity, by a hidden factual question about the marginal effect of one vote, or by the sides answering different questions under a single heading? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, an empirical finding, a change of question, or an argument that no progress should be expected?
