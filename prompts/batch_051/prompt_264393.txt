I'm a novelist, and I keep tripping over the question of what we owe real dead people when we put them in fiction. My gut gives pretty clear verdicts case by case, but I can't see the principle behind them, if there is one.

- A novel that gives George Eliot an invented love affair and makes it the emotional backdrop to her writing Middlemarch: fine with me. She's long dead and no one's reading it as biography.
- A film that shows a named real man betraying Anne Frank's family for money, when the evidence points away from him and no one was ever proven to have done it: not fine. That's pinning an atrocity on someone.
- A biopic that merges two real Victorian surgeons into a single composite: fine, it's a genre convention.
- A novel that puts invented speeches in Lincoln's mouth, kept consistent with what he actually said and wrote: fine.
- A farce in which a real recently-deceased prime minister is revealed to have caused a child's death and covered it up: not fine, even as comedy.
- A novel that gives a real abolitionist a secret second life as a slave-catcher, with no historical basis: not fine.
- A novel that gives a real composer a gambling habit and money trouble, when the letters are silent either way: I honestly dont mind it, even though it's an invented vice.

So what I want is this: is there a principle — or a small set of principles — that gets all of these, or nearly all, and that says what counts as beyond the pale? What does it predict about a case I keep dithering on: a novel that shows a real long-dead queen ordering a murder that historians now think she probably didn't order, but that was widely rumoured at the time? And tell me straight which of my verdicts, if any, a clean principle forces me to abandon.
