My hospital has a clinical algorithm review committee and somehow I've ended up chairing it. Right now we're reviewing a proposed deterioration risk score for inpatients. It's a Bayesian logistic regression, and the developers want to set the prior using the maximum entropy principle for rare subgroups: where there are very few adverse events in the training data, the prior should be the flattest distribution that still reproduces the aggregate constraints we actually know, like the overall event rate, and should otherwise avoid imposing any stronger assumptions. Their pitch is that this makes the prior objective and defensible rather than letting some clinician's hunches quietly drive the predictions.

Half the committee thinks this is the only honest way to represent uncertainty when data are thin. The other half says the neutrality is fake: entropy is parameterization-dependent, so the so-called least informative prior in one set of variables can be highly informative in another, and you've just shifted the arbitrary choice to which constraints and which variables you count. Our policy says any model used for real-time clinical decisions needs an explicit rationale for how uncertainty is handled under sparse data, so I have to write a short decision memo before Thursday's meeting.

The memo should recommend approve, reject, or approve with conditions (for example, require the developers to also fit the model with a few informative priors and show the score is insensitive, or restrict the max-entropy default to specific uses). I want it to actually work through the philosophical issue, not just list pros and cons, and to include the strongest consideration on the losing side. If there are conditions, say what the developers would need to do and by when, concretely. What should our bottom line be?
