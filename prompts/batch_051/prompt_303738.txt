You are a trained moral and political philosopher who has spent years inside the internal strategy debates of the effective-altruism movement, and you now advise donors and funds on how to allocate marginal resources. I want a defended prioritization, not a survey and not a reading list. Before you argue for a ranking, take a preliminary pass in which you construct, in your own words, the strongest available version of each critique below and the strongest reply an EA institutionalist could give against it. Then let the ranking follow from that, not from whichever critique is easiest to state vividly.

Setting. A mid-sized EA-aligned fund — call it $40 million a year in grants, with a staff small enough that a single marginal hire or staff-week is a real decision. Its grants span global-health interventions and catastrophic-risk reduction, and its implicit theory of change is canonical EA: maximize expected welfare, discount for neglectedness and tractability, compare everything to the counterfactual baseline, and treat "more measurable" as, all else equal, more decisionworthy. Over the next five years it must choose how to allocate marginal effort among four broad uses: (a) funding additional randomized impact evaluations and other measurement infrastructure; (b) funding movement-building — recruitment, community health, mentorship, events; (c) funding policy work with diffuse, difficult-to-attribute effects; and (d) expanding the fund's own evaluation staff. The question is which critique should most change how the fund divides those resources.

Three critiques press on different parts of that theory of change:

1. The institutional critique. The movement concentrates money and agenda-setting power in a small, culturally homogeneous elite network; deference norms and a shared style of quantitative reasoning crowd out outsiders and heterodox views, so the fund's own priorities are likely to be the output of an insular consensus rather than an open search.

2. The earning-to-give critique. The standard talent model encourages some of the most capable people to take high-paying jobs and donate the surplus, leaving the underlying systems — corporations, governments, labor markets — unreformed. The fund inherits a talent pool and a donor base shaped by that choice, and it may be quietly reinforcing a division of moral labor that would not survive systematic scrutiny.

3. The measurability critique. The fund's methods select for outcomes that can be counted, modeled, and attributed to specific grants; work whose value is real but illegible — political shift, institutional trust, intellectual ferment, preventing invisible disasters — gets systematically underpriced at the margin.

The task. Rank these three by how much they should change the fund's marginal allocation over the next five years, assuming each is at least partly true. For each critique, explicitly separate two questions: whether the critique is true enough for practice, and whether, even if true, it actually should change what this fund funds or measures. A critique can be substantially correct and still be decision-irrelevant for a $40-million fund facing these tradeoffs; that gap is part of the work. Defend the ordering with explicit criteria — at minimum importance, tractability of the corrective, how neglected the critique currently is within the movement's own self-scrutiny, and how many other decisions depend on the point — and say which criterion is doing most of the work in your ranking.

Finally, tell me what would change your mind. Identify the single most plausible empirical finding or argument that would flip your top-ranked critique to the bottom or the bottom to the top, and name the premise or criterion in your reasoning you would least want to defend in front of a skeptical committee. Argue the positions directly; do not gesture at literatures or named sources.
