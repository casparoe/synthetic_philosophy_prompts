I keep running into a particular argument among the engineers and decision-theory-adjacent people I work with, and I want it stress-tested. Acausal trade is the idea that two agents with no causal channel between them — they cannot send signals, exchange resources, punish, or even observe each other's actual choices — can nevertheless cooperate, because each agent predicts that the other will follow a certain cooperative decision procedure, and each is better off if both follow it. The standard analogy in my circles, used to make this look tractable, is an open-source protocol spec. Two separate engineering teams can write completely different codebases, never exchange a single message, and still build systems that interoperate, provided they both conform to the same public protocol. Each team implements the spec because it expects the other to, and once both do, each collects the benefit of interoperability; no real-time negotiation was ever needed. The argument then says acausal agents can do the same thing in the abstract: conform to a shared cooperative decision procedure as if it were a public spec, predict each other's conformance, and thereby capture the gains from trade. The practical conclusion people draw is that building an AI or other future agent that can profit from acausal trade is mostly a software problem: make it conform to a widely shared, spec-like cooperative decision procedure, and the rest follows.

I want an audit of this analogy. First, give the correspondence table: what plays the role of the protocol spec, the two codebases, the implementers, the act of conforming, the absent communication channel, the integration point, the interoperability benefit, and the shared ecosystem. Second, go through the places where the mapping breaks. In particular, look at what the open-source story quietly imports that the acausal case may lack: the spec was written somewhere, by someone, and both teams are causally downstream of the same document; reference implementations, test suites, and visible integration failures allow mismatches to be found and repaired; the spec persists and gets revised by an ecosystem; and both teams share an infrastructure, a market, and sometimes a community. Third, separate the disanalogies that are load-bearing for the practical conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic. Finally, settle whether the conclusion survives: is acausal cooperation really analogous to conforming to a public spec, or is the analogy smuggling in common causes, feedback loops, and shared infrastructure that acausal agents by definition lack? If the analogy cannot carry the conclusion, say what would be a better analogy, and spell out what that better analogy would actually require of any agent we build.
