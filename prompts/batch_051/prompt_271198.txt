Nearly a decade before Bell published the theorem now used to test hidden-variable theories, Einstein was dead. He also never saw the first Bell-inequality experiments now usually described as loophole-free: the measurements published in 2015 that closed the fair-sampling and communication loopholes simultaneously. Imagine an exercise in rational reconstruction rather than biography. Einstein is brought up to speed on Bell's derivation and on those 2015 results, and he grants that the experimental outcomes are sound. His mature writings give us a fairly stable set of commitments: he wanted spatially separated systems to possess their own real states, he rejected the idea that measuring one system could act on a distant one, and he regarded the quantum state as an incomplete description of individual systems. Working from those commitments and not from any claim about his temperament, determine which premise of Bell's theorem he would have been forced to surrender, and which of the surviving positions he would most plausibly have judged least bad. The candidates differ by which premise they give up: locality, realism (or counterfactual definiteness), measurement independence, single definite outcomes, or the demand for a deeper account of the correlations at all. In your answer, distinguish no-signalling from locality (the Bell correlations do not permit superluminal signalling, but they still violate local realism); then assess each candidate — Bohmian nonlocality, superdeterminism, many-worlds, and operationalism — against Einstein's stated desiderata. End with a considered verdict on which option he should have preferred by his own lights, and whether those lights push toward a coherent position or toward an unstable compromise.
