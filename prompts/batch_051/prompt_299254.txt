I teach a 90-minute undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of mind with about 16 students, and we are working through the question raised by Douglas Hofstadter's Aunt Hillary and the Ant Fugue: whether an ant colony, whose individual members follow local rules and seem to know almost nothing about the colony's overall condition, can nonetheless be said to have a mind, intentions, or beliefs of its own. Design a runnable classroom simulation for this session that makes the students feel the force of that question before it gets named in discussion.

The simulation should put each student in the role of a single ant, constrained by a small set of local rules: they may look only at adjacent cells of a large floor grid, may communicate only through marker tokens or other mechanical signals you specify, and may not speak, gesture, or plan from a bird's-eye view. The group should nevertheless produce an intelligent-looking colony-level pattern—a food trail, sorted piles, a raid column, or something equivalent—that emerges from the rules rather than from any participant's intention. Include some environmental roles, such as pheromone evaporation or an obstacle, so the system has structure beyond the ants themselves.

Provide the full rules, simple enough to teach in under ten minutes; a materials list using cheap, easily assembled items; a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes; and a debrief sequence that maps specific moments in the simulation onto the philosophical ideas, including the contrast between colony-level and ant-level descriptions, the distinction between signals as mere triggers and signals as symbols, and the question of what would have to be added for the colony to have beliefs, intentions, or a self. Also include a note on what will predictably go wrong the first time it runs—for example, students smuggling in global communication, marker saturation, or mistaking an accidental regularity for an organized pattern—and how to correct it midstream, and an honest note on which features of a candidate colony mind the exercise cannot reproduce and must instead be supplied by the facilitator in discussion. The activity should stand on its own, without citations or outside reading.
