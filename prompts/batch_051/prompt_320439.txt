I recently joined a small fellowship committee, and we are considering a two-stage voting procedure for awards that require both excellent research and excellent teaching. The proposed rule runs as follows. In the first stage, each member records a yes/no judgment on two separate premises—that the applicant's research is excellent, and that the applicant's teaching is excellent—and also records a direct yes/no vote on whether to award the fellowship. The committee then computes a premise-based verdict: award the fellowship if and only if a majority judges each premise to be true. If that premise-based verdict disagrees with the majority's direct vote, the chair opens a second stage in which members may revise their premise judgments, but only by stating publicly which consideration changed their mind. The final decision is the premise-based verdict computed from the revised judgments; the direct vote is used only to trigger and frame the second stage. Analyze how this mechanism would actually behave if it became a permanent rule. In particular, discuss what it incentivizes in first-stage voting, how the second stage is likely to unfold, how members might game the rule or vote strategically, what the rule reveals or obscures about the committee's actual reasoning, and whether the explanation requirement repairs the underlying inconsistency or produces new failure modes. Please answer in American English.
