Here's a jury instruction I'm working with:

"The prosecution must prove the defendant's guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. A reasonable doubt may arise from the evidence, or from the lack of evidence. It is not enough that the evidence shows the defendant probably committed the offence, even if that probability is very high; you must instead be firmly convinced that he is guilty."

Now consider a trial on a charge of entering a ticketed event without paying, made a strict-liability infraction. The entire case is this: the event was on private land with one entrance; exactly 1,000 people entered; the gate ledger shows 100 paid and 900 entered without paying, but it does not record who paid. The defendant is shown by other attendance records to have been one of the 1,000 people inside. No witness can say whether he was among the hundred payers or the nine hundred non-payers, and there is no other evidence. Does this instruction permit a conviction? Or is "probably committed the offence, even if that probability is very high" a phrase aimed at something different from a known base rate like this? Walk me through what the plain words, the purpose of the reasonable-doubt standard, and the drafters' likely intent each yeild, and say which approach should control here.
