I'm a PhD student working on inverse reinforcement learning and I keep running into the same ambiguity in the literature: what exactly are we assuming when we say an agent's behavior reveals its preferences? I'd like a sorting quiz for a seminar handout, the kind where people answer questions about concrete cases and the scoring tells them which view of revealed preference they're actually committed to. Aim for eight questions. Each should be a short vignette — someone ordering the salad but saying they wanted the burger, an addict who says she wants to stop, choices that flip under framing, a demonstrator whose behavior is noisy or suboptimal in a robot-learning setup, someone whose preferences shift after a long delay. The answer options should be intuitive judgments about the case, not theory labels. Then give a scoring key with the real logical positions: strict revealed preference, a representationalist view where we infer a latent reward but treat noise and inconsistency as misspecification, a constructed-preferences view, and maybe a deflationary view that theres nothing over and above the choice patterns. Derive these from the claims that actually split people (e.g., is an inconsistent choice evidence against a stable preference, or evidence that the elicitation is broken?), not from names of theories, and say explicitly if two positions turn out not to be separable by the questions. If you think the framing rests on something confused — like revealed preference and reward inference are really different projects — say so up front instead of forcing the quiz. American English, please.
