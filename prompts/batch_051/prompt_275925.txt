I have spent most of my life in the physical sciences, where one learns to trust an analogy only after identifying where it breaks, and since retiring I have been reading through aesthetics with the particular obsession of whether games can be artworks. The position I keep defending against my more literary-minded friends rests on a musical analogy. A game, I want to say, is like a score: the designer is the composer, the rules and underlying code are the written notes, the player is the performer, and an individual playthrough is a performance. Just as it would be strange to deny that a symphony is an artwork because it consists of instructions that must be realized in sound by musicians, it would be strange to deny that a game is an artwork because its concrete form depends on choices made by players. On this analogy, the player's agency is not an impurity that undermines authorship but rather the game's counterpart of interpretive freedom: a pianist's rubato, dynamic shadings, and pedaling do not make the sonata stop being the composer's work, and likewise the player's chosen route, pacing, and style of play do not make the game stop being the designer's. My friends insist the comparison is strained, because the pianist is trying to realize the composer's intentions, whereas the player is usually trying to win, optimize, break the system, or simply amuse herself.

I would like a rigorous audit of how far this score-and-performance analogy actually holds. Please begin by laying out a formal correspondence table: what is being mapped to the composer, the score, the performer, the performance, and perhaps to rehearsal or interpretation, and what exactly plays each role in the case of a video game. Then identify the principal points where the mapping strains or collapses. I am especially interested in distinguishing the disanalogies that genuinely matter for the conclusion the analogy is being used to support — that player agency is aesthetically akin to performer interpretation, and therefore that games can by art in the same way music can — from the disanalogies that are merely incidental and leave the argument intact. If the analogy cannot carry that weight, tell me plainly whether a weakened version of the conclusion survives, and whether there is a better comparison for what a player's agency contributes to the artistic standing of a game.
