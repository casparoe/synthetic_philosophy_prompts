You are an expert in the philosophy of art and the metaphysics of works of art. I would like you to think carefully and not jump straight to a conclusion. Begin by brainstorming the distinct reasons someone might value an original over an indistinguishable copy; then reconstruct my verdicts in their strongest form; only after that, try to state a principle that captures them, and test that principle against cases I have not listed.

I am a novelist, and I have been trying to work out what I actually think about originals, copies, and forgeries, because my gut keeps saying different things in apparently similar cases. Here are my verdicts.

First, a friend painted a small landscape that hung in my study for years. After the original was badly damaged, I had a copy made that is, to my eye, indistinguishable. I keep the copy in a drawer. It does not do what the original did, and I would not hang it in the original's place.

Second, I once bought what was described as an early draft of a famous novel. The text on the page interested me. When I learned it was a modern forgery, I lost all interest in it, even though the words on the page had not changed and the paper still looked plausibly old. The same object became worthless to me.

Third, I feel almost nothing about first editions of novels. A mass-market paperback containing the same text is, for me, fully the novel. I do not think the story lives in the author's typescript or in the first print run.

Fourth, I do care about the actual typescript when it carries the author's crossings-out and marginal notes. But what I want is to study the handwriting and the sequence of revisions; a perfect facsimile would give me nearly everything the original gives me. I would not pay much simply for the piece of paper itself.

Fifth, for a photograph, I unexpectedly care which print it is. A print made by the photographer matters to me in a way that an indistinguishable later print made from the same negative does not, even though I cannot see any difference between them.

Sixth, for recorded music, I do not care at all about a first pressing of the vinyl. A lossless digital file of the same master is, for listening purposes, just as good, and I do not envy anyone who owns the original disc.

Seventh, I once watched a live performance that was also recorded. The recording, made the same night, does not substitute for having been in the room. What I valued was being present while the thing happened, not merely the sound and the sight, which the recording preserves.

My working principle has been something like this: the original matters only when the work is a particular object or event with a causal connection to the maker; when the work is a text, score, or recording—a sort of pattern that copies can carry in full—the original is just one token, and nothing is lost in reproducing it. But my third and fifth cases, or maybe my fifth and sixth, seem to pull against each other, and my principle seems too crude to explain why the photographer's own print matters while the first pressing does not.

What principle, if any, captures all of this, or nearly all of it? State it precisely, say which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon or revise, and tell me what it predicts about a case I keep going back and forth on: a print made by the photographer after the original negative has been lost, working from a high-resolution scan that someone else made years earlier. Is that print an original in the way the photographer's own print is, or is it something closer to a forgery, and why? Please treat me as a thoughtful but untrained interlocutor: no citations or reading lists, just careful reasoning.
