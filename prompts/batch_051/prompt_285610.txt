Until recently I belonged to a religious community in which conformity was treated as a moral achievement and departure from the prescribed pattern of life as harmful both to the person who left and to those who remained. I have since left, and I am trying to work out what was wrong with that picture. In doing so I have been reading Mill's On Liberty, especially his case for treating divergent ways of living as experiments from which others can learn, his distinction between self-regarding and other-regarding conduct, and his warnings about the tyranny of custom.

I work as a reinforcement learning engineer, and the vocabulary in which I actually think is exploration and exploitation, policies, reward functions, constraints, regret, novelty, population diversity, and social learning. I am fully at home in these terms, so the technical side does not need simplifying. I keep reaching for that vocabulary to make sense of Mill, but I want to know whether the translation is faithful or merely comfortable. My working instinct is that Mill is fundamentally defending a diversity-preserving exploration strategy at the level of a whole society, one that avoids premature convergence to a local optimum under conformity pressure, and that much of the rest of the essay is elaboration. I suspect that is not the whole story, but I cannot say what is missing without falling back on Mill's own language.

Please restate Mill's position—the value of experiments in living, the claim that coercion is justified only to prevent harm to others, and the critique of conformity—in that vocabulary as faithfully as you can, without quietly importing the assumptions of RL and presenting them as the translation.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of Mill's argument map cleanly onto exploration, exploitation, regret, constraints, or social learning? Which parts did you have to distort to make them fit? Which parts have no counterpart in the RL vocabulary at all, and why? In particular, tell me whether the harm principle and the utilitarian ground of Mill's defence of liberty survive the translation, and whether an RL treatment can represent the value of individual experiments as anything other than instrumental value to the collective. I would also like your honest view on what the failures reveal: are they defects in the RL framework—things it simply cannot represent—or do some of Mill's claims turn out to be less precise than they look once one is forced to specify states, rewards, values, and agents?

Please write the answer in British English.
