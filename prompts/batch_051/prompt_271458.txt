At a party last weekend two friends got into an argument about comedy that I can't stop thinking about. One of them, Ramiro, was insisting that all laughter is basically a policing mechanism: at bottom you're laughing because somebody is being taken down a peg, or at least you're relieved it's them and not you. The other, June, said that can't be right, because the funniest things she knows — a cat baffled by a cucumber, a perfectly timed pun, a magic trick that upends your physics — have no victim at all; it's about your expectation getting yanked. Ramiro shot back that suprise alone isn't funny: a snapped brake cable on the highway is a surprise nobody laughs at. June said that's a threat, not a harmless twist — laughter only fires once your brain tags the surprise as safe. Ramiro said she was moving the goalposts, and that banana-peel humor is funny precisely when the victim is a self-important businessman. June said no, it's funny when the guy springs up unhurt and confused; it's the near-miss, not the humiliation. They went in circles. I want an adjudication, point by point: which of their claims actually hold up, where each one is right about the other's blind spot, and whether there's a way to split the difference without just ending at "it's a bit of everything."
