A friend and I spent last night arguing about a provision in my employer's code of conduct, and I would like you to work through the interpretation rather than simply take one of our sides. The code provides: "No employee shall, without authorization from Legal, disclose any confidential Company or client information to any outside party or use such information in any manner adverse to the Company's interests or inconsistent with the employee's duty of loyalty to the Company."

My situation is this. I am a data analyst at a consulting firm whose client, a municipal authority, uses a risk-scoring model we built to allocate emergency rental assistance. I have found what looks like a systematic error: the model scores several heavily immigrant neighborhoods as much lower-risk than comparable areas, and the authority is about to deploy a new version that makes the pattern worse. I raised the issue with my manager and with our internal model-validation team twice; both times I was told the pattern was an artifact and not to pursue it further.

Frustrated, and genuinely unsure whether I was being unreasonable, I did not go to the press or to the authority. Instead, I posted a de-identified but non-public extract of the input data and the model's predictions as a bug report on the public issue tracker of the open-source fairness library our team uses, asking the maintainers and other users whether they recognized the failure mode. I did not seek authorization from Legal before posting. My manager now says the posting was a breach of the clause above.

My friend thinks this is not a close case: "any outside party" plainly includes whoever can read a public repository, and raising an internal concern with strangers because I disbelieved two internal reviews is exactly the disloyalty the clause exists to prevent. I think the clause is aimed at employees selling client data to a competitor or leaking a client's trade secrets, and that the people who drafted it never imagined an employee using a public bug forum as a way of getting an independent second opinion from colleagues.

Please set out what the clause requires in this case if it is read (i) according to the ordinary meaning of its words, (ii) in light of the purpose of the provision, (iii) as its drafters would have expected it to apply had they been asked, and (iv) as a reasonable reader in my position would understand it. Where these approaches diverge, say which should control and why. I care less about the employment-law answer than about what the phrases "any outside party" and "duty of loyalty" can actually bear, and whether what I did is best categorized as whistleblowing, betrayal, or neither.
