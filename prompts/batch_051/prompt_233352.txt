Hannah Arendt's essay 'Lying in Politics' opens by conceding that deception in government is nothing new: "Truthfulness has never been counted among the political virtues, and lies have always been regarded as justifiable tools in political dealings." She develops this into a strong claim: the capacity to lie and the capacity to act are intertwined, because both require the mental freedom to deny what is actually the case and to imagine the world otherwise. Yet the essay's verdict on the Pentagon Papers does not read as business-as-usual. Later in the essay she treats the Vietnam-era deceptions as a qualitatively new phenomenon—a collective, partly self-deceived enterprise of image-building and problem-solving in which facts had to fit theory and decision-makers lost the shared factual world that ordinary political lying presupposes and exploits.

These two lines seem to pull in opposite directions. If lying is as old as politics and rooted in the faculty that makes action possible, what exactly is new and specially destructive about the lying the Pentagon Papers reveal? Are the two positions consistent, and if so, what does the distinction turn on: the kind of lie, its scale, the self-awareness of the liars, its relation to factual truth, or something else? Set out the most faithful reconciliation and then the strongest reading on which Arendt is simply contradicting herself; close by saying which is more honest, and what it would take to overturn it.
