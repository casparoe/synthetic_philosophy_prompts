I teach a graduate epistemology seminar of twelve students, and I can have the room for eighty minutes. Design a runnable in-class activity that makes students feel the problem of old evidence before it is named. No lecture up front, and the phrase "old evidence" should not be spoken until the debrief. Assume the students already know Bayes' rule and how to update by conditionalization; do not re-teach those.

Stage a made-up episode of inquiry around a simple empirical regularity the students can actually observe or verify in the room — not a real historical case. Split the class into two groups. Group A first records its prior credence in the regularity, then observes it and records its post-observation credence; only later is Group A given a theory that accounts for the regularity. Group B first adopts the same theory, records its credences, and then sees the regularity arrive as a prediction. Both groups should use the same betting-slip or scoring convention, and all credences should be recorded at fixed, comparable time points. The rules must be arranged so that when the groups work out their own numbers, Group B gets a clean Bayesian boost, while Group A discovers that the post-observation credence of 1 it recorded before the theory existed leaves no room for any boost — even though Group A now says the theory seems strongly supported by the regularity.

Then write a debrief that maps each moment of the activity onto the formal problem, separates the old-evidence problem from the separate fact that a theory was built to fit data already in hand, and shows what counterfactual prior on the regularity would have to be in place for Group A's feeling of confirmation to go through. Include exact rules, materials, a timeline, the facilitator's prompts, the debrief questions, a note on what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and an honest note on which feature of the old-evidence problem the activity cannot reproduce. Everything in the activity should be invented, so nothing needs to be looked up. End with a single sentence beginning "Summary:" that states in one sentence the main thing the activity should leave students seeing.
