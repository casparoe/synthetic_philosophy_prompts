You are a professional philosopher of science, specialising in formal epistemology and anthropic reasoning. I want a careful diagnosis of an argument that has been worrying me, so please start by mapping the steps at which one could resist it and what each kind of resistance commits one to, then give your preferred judgement rather than a mere survey.

First, the background. In anthropic reasoning there are two standard principles. The Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA) says, roughly, that I should reason as if I were randomly selected from the set of all actual observers. The Self-Indication Assumption (SIA) says, roughly, that my own existence is itself evidence: other things equal, a hypothesis on which more observers exist is better confirmed by the fact that I exist, roughly in proportion to the number of observers it posits. I am drawn to SIA because it seems to explain why I should not be surprised to find that I exist rather than never having existed. But the following argument, which I take to be a version of the 'presumptuous philosopher' objection, seems to show that SIA proves too much.

Here is the argument as I have reconstructed it.

(1) According to SIA, the observation that I exist favours a hypothesis H_many over a hypothesis H_few by a factor of about N when H_many posits N times as many observers as H_few and the two hypotheses are otherwise on a par.
(2) Let H_few be the hypothesis that the universe contains exactly one observer. Let H_many be exactly like H_few except that it says the universe contains exactly N observers, for an N of our choosing, where the hypotheses make identical predictions about everything we could directly observe, and every observer in them is epistemically indistinguishable from me.
(3) By (1), my existence confirms H_many over H_few by a factor of about N.
(4) But H_many was produced from H_few simply by writing a larger number into the observer count. If a rule of reasoning lets an arbitrary stipulation generate arbitrary confirmation, then we could manufacture near-certainty for any claim we liked, with no new data, merely by adding to it the assertion that the universe contains enormously many observers.
(5) A principle that lets us manufacture near-certainty from the armchair, with no new observational evidence, should be rejected.
(6) Therefore SIA should be rejected.

I cannot tell whether this is a genuine reductio of SIA or whether something has gone wrong in applying SIA to a stipulated case. I would most like to keep SIA, since I find the idea that my own existence is evidence compelling, but I also do not want to give up step (5). Which step is the right one to reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the right move is to reject SIA, say what principle of anthropic reasoning, if any, should take its place and whether it can do the explanatory work that made SIA attractive. If the right move is to reject one of the later steps, say exactly how the stipulated case fails to be an 'other things equal' case, and whether that defence commits me to treating the number of observers as a physical magnitude confirmable by indexical evidence.

Throughout, please distinguish clearly between what is settled and what is genuinely contested among people who work on this topic. In particular, do not present a contested interpretation of SSA, SIA, or the force of the presumptuous-philosopher objection as if it were the consensus. Where your diagnosis rests on one contested reading rather than another, flag it and say which camp would resist it.
