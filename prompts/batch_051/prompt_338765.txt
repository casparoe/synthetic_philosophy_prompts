Last week in my ethics reading group, two members got into a standoff over G. E. Moore's account of the highest goods, and neither would give ground. We had been working through the part of Principia Ethica in which Moore discusses the ideal, and the quarrel was over whether his claim that the most valuable things are states of consciousness — roughly, the enjoyment of human companionship and of beautiful objects — can be reconciled with his insistence that the value of those states depends on whether the objects involved really have the qualities the subject admires. Elena opened by saying that Moore is constantly misread as a hedonist. For Moore, she said, the value of loving someone does not consist solely in the pleasure the lover feels: if you love a person for their honesty and they turn out not to be honest, the episode of loving has less value even if it feels exactly the same from the inside. So pleasantness cannot be the whole story.

Marcus answered that he granted her reading of the text but thought it only exposed a contradiction. If the value of a state of consciousness depends on facts outside the state, he said, then the thing that has the value is not a state of consciousness at all; it is a state plus the fact that the world happens to cooperate. Elena replied that this is too quick, because on Moore's picture these conscious states are intentional — they are about the qualities of people and artworks — so whether one's love or aesthetic delight latches onto something real is built into the content of the state, not an extra ingredient added from outside. Marcus came back that two people could have states with the same intentional content while one is loving a genuinely honest friend and the other a very convincing fraud; the content is identical, but Moore's truth-requirement makes the first state more valuable. That difference has to track something outside the mind, so the attempt to keep the value inside consciousness fails. Elena then shifted ground: perhaps Moore should be read as locating the value in the organic whole, the conscious state together with its object, and the sentence about states of consciousness is a rough summary rather than a strict claim about where value resides. Marcus answered that this reading creates its own problem, because Moore clearly thinks an unappreciated beautiful world has only negligible value while an appreciated one has enormous value — which suggests the appreciation, not the object, is what carries the value. The view, he said, is pulled in both directions at once.

I was asked to referee and could not do it credibly. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: identify what each of them is right and wrong about at each step, say how the apparent tension in Moore's view is best handled, and give an overall verdict about who is more right.
