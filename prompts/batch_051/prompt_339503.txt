Okay, so I've spent to long reading old LessWrong threads and arguing on the forum about this, and I'd rather commit to a position and have it attacked properly than keep flip-flopping. Let me just say it: I basically hold that evolution is a stupid optimizer. What I mean is that natural selection looks clever mostly because it's been running for billions of years with absurd population sizes and a trivially simple reproduction filter, not because it has anything like gradient information or a decent inductive bias. And where people claim to see a deep analogy with gradient descent, I read the analogy as mostly bad news for natural selection, not as evidence that evolution quietly solved hard optimization problems: both get stuck in local optima, both are myopic in practice, neither does proper credit assignment, and both leave frozen accidents lying around because undoing them would cost fitness along the way. I'm also suspicious of loose claims that evolution "discovered" modularity, meta-learning, or nice priors, when to me it just brute-forced a giant search space with a compute budget no ML lab will ever have.

What I want to know is what would count against this. I don't mean the usual "eyes evolved independently so evolution is smart" line — I've got a reply to that one. I want the map of where my view is actually exposed: which observations, formal results, simulation results, or conceptual arguments should genuinely lower my confidence, ranked from decisive to merely awkward. For each one, tell me whether a defender of the stupid-optimizer view would themselves concede it as counting against them, or would dismiss it as missing the point. In particular: are there results about population-based search with recombination that provably or empirically do something gradient descent can't on a natural class of landscapes? Does something like the Baldwin effect or niche construction cut one way or the other here? What does my view predict that the "evolution is actually a competent learning algorithm" camp doesn't, such that a clean experiment could embarrass one side more than the other? And am I set up so that any apparent cleverness gets automatically written off as "more search, more parallelism" — in which case, just tell me I've insulated the view into a position that can't lose, and whether that's a problem for a claim about a historical process rather than a scientific theory.

So, as an expert on both evolutionary theory and the theory of optimization, someone who could pass an Ideological Turing Test for both camps: steelman my view first so I know you've got it, then brainstorm a bunch of candidate defeaters before filtering, and then give me the ranked list.
