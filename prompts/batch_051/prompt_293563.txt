I was arguing with a friend last night about Pascal's mugging. He kept insisting the fanatical conclusion only goes through because the victim gets to invent some tiny nonzero probability for an arbitrarily huge harm, and that once the probability is a fixed known number the pressure to pay disappears. That made me think of a version we couldn't settle.

Suppose the mugger can demonstrate a machine that, on any given day, independently tortures 10^30 simulated minds for a year with probability exactly 10^-30, and otherwise does nothing. For a single day the expected harm is one mind-year, so presumably you shouldn't pay much to stop it. But the mugger then says he will run the machine every day for 10^28 days unless you pay him now to switch it of for good. Over the whole run the expected harm is 10^28 mind-years, even though the chance the machine ever fires is still only about 1 per cent.

Does standard expected-value reasoning now say you should pay, and has the fanatic just rebuilt Pascal's mugging out of pieces the anti-fanatic was happy to accept? Or does the anti-fanatic have a principled reason to resist the sum? Take a definite position on whether the fanatical argument survives this version, and give your reasons. Write the answer in British English.
