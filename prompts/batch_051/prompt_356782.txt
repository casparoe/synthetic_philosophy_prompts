I'm writing a paper for a political philosophy course on what it actually means for a state to "join" a customs union, and I've gotten tangled in a distinction I can't stop poking at. Two views keep coming up in my notes. One, call it the title view, says a state has transferred sovereignty over a matter just when the treaty moves that matter from the state's list of competences to the union's list — the paper assignment is the transfer. The other, call it the control view, says sovereignty is transferred only when the state can now be bound on that matter without its current consent; if the state keeps a veto over every decision on the matter, it hasnt really given the thing up.

I'd like a case where the two views come apart in the hard direction: the title view says sovereignty has clearly been transferred, but the control view says it has not. Not the familiar story where the treaty says one thing and a court's interpretive creep quietly takes the power anyway — I want the reverse, where the paper transfer is unmistakable but effective control stays with the member state. Build me a realistic customs union with, say, three members and a plausible treaty. The treaty should put a specific, recognizable matter (external tariff setting seems good, but use whatever works) squarely on the union side, while the decision rule leaves each member a genuine veto over that matter. Then walk through the checks: state exactly what the treaty assigns and to whom, what the decision rule is, and why each view gives the verdict it does. Finish with which single feature of the case is doing the work, how removing it makes the two views converge, and how a defender of the title view would attack the control view's verdict. The case is for a philosophy-of-law argument, so it has to earn the verdicts from the two definitions rather than stipulating them.

Lay it out as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the scenario; (2) what the title view says and why; (3) what the control view says and why; (4) the feature doing the work and what changes if you remove it; (5) how the title-view defender would attack the control view's verdict.
