I run a small book club and next month we're doing Fitzgerald's *The Great Gatsby*. I want to use it to get at something I've been chewing on: idolatry and misplaced worship, in the broad sense of what makes valuing something the wrong way rather than just valuing it a lot. The obvious move is that Gatsby idolizes Daisy — the green light, the parties thrown in the hope she'll show up, the way he has built his whole idea of himself around winning her back. But I don't want the lazy reading where you just slap the word "idolatry" on his love and feel done. I want the reading to actually separate romantic love from idolatry, or ambition from worship. Does the novel suggest the problem is that Daisy is an unworthy object, or that Gatsby relates to her the wrong way, or that he's trying to get something from her that a finite person can't plausibly give — some kind of salvation or permission to remake himself? I'd also want you to reckon with the eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg looking over the valley of ashes, and with Nick's strange mix of admiration and disgust for Gatsby; I have a hunch those are doing something about false gods, but I can't quite articlate it.

So: give me a sustained reading of the novel through this lens, as if for a discussion group that has read it recently and doesn't want a summary. Point to the scenes you'd anchor it in, say where the novel complicates or resists the neat versions of "idolatry is just valuing the wrong object" or "idolatry is just too much devotion," and finish with what the novel shows us about misplaced worship that a clean philosophical definition might miss.
