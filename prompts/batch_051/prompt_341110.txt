Here is an argument, from the theory of higher-order evidence, which concludes that epistemic akrasia — believing P while also believing that one's total evidence does not support P — is not merely irrational but impossible.

(1) To believe P is, from the first-person point of view, to take P to be true.

(2) If you take P to be true, you are thereby rationally committed to taking your total evidence to support P; otherwise you would be regarding yourself as believing beyond your evidence.

(3) Epistemic akrasia is exactly the state of believing P while believing that your total evidence does not support P.

(4) No coherent subject can be in a state that commits them to taking their evidence to support P while they also take it not to support P.

(5) Therefore no one is ever in the akratic state; apparent cases must be described as vacillation, half-belief, or changing one's mind.

Is the argument valid? If not, say exactly which step fails, and give the strongest reading of the argument on which some weaker version of the conclusion survives. I half-suspect "committed" in step (2) is doing two jobs — one normative, one descriptive — but I cannot make the diagnosis precise. Answer in exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. If the question is badly posed, or the argument depends on a nonstandard definition of epistemic akrasia, say so plainly instead of grading each step as though every notion were in order.
