I keep running into a tension in what I read about social choice theory, and I would like it resolved. The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem is often glossed as saying that once there are at least three alternatives, every strategy-proof social choice function is dictatorial. Yet the same literature describes various non-dictatorial procedures as strategy-proof: second-price (Vickrey) auctions, for example, and median-voter rules when preferences are single-peaked. If the theorem really forces dictatorship the moment strategy-proofness is imposed, how can those procedures be strategy-proof? I want to know whether I am misstating the theorem, whether those examples are only strategy-proof in some looser sense, or whether they evade the theorem by failing one of its less-advertised conditions. Spell out exactly what the theorem rules out, which of its assumptions each of those examples relaxes, and what a mechanism designer should actually take away from the result.
