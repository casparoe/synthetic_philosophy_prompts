Okay here's a familiar move against exclusivism about salvation. It starts from the observation that which religion a person ends up with is mostly a matter of where and to whom they were born — call this the geography-of-belief argument. From that it concludes that a good God can't make salvation turn on such an arbitrary lottery. So exclusivism — only one tradition has saving truth — looks morally indefensible. Inclusivism (one tradition is uniquely true but some people outside it can still be saved) tries to dodge the problem, but a lot of people run the geography argument as a route all the way to pluralism, where several traditions are genuinely saving paths. The argument quietly rests on a picture of religious belonging as inherited and sticky, like a mid-century village where everybody is born into the local church and stays there. Now suppose that picture is dissolving: conversion, deconversion, and switching between traditions are way up, a lot of people now describe themselves as spiritual-but-not-religious and mix practices from several traditions at once, and plenty of people first seriously encounter a religion through a feed or a creator rather than through their parents or neighbours. Does the geography-of-belief argument survive this? Which premise exactly does the new situation put pressure on, and is there a repaired version that still bothers exclusivism but for a different reason — maybe the new arbitrariness is your recommendation algorithm rather than your birthplace? And does the argument end up supporting full pluralism, or only the weaker inclusivism? I'm interested in the logic of the argument, not in a survey of who believes what now.
