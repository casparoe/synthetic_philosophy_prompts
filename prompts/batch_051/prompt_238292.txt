I've been trying to get straight what the faithfulness assumption actually earns you in causal discovery, and I'm starting to think it's earning less than its usually credited for. The background as I understand it: the causal Markov condition only gives you one direction—d-separation in the true causal graph entails independence in the observed distribution—but if you want to infer graph structure from independence facts, you need something like the converse, where an independence that shows up in the data lets you conclude the corresponding nodes are d-separated. Faithfulness is exactly the assumption that the distribution contains no independence relations that aren't entailed by the graph. The standard defense I keep seeing is that unfaithful distributions live on a measure-zero set of parameter values, because they require an exact canceling of two causal paths. But measure zero in parameter space doesn't obviously mean "can be ignored in practice," since all sorts of states we should take seriously have measure zero in the right continuous space; a coin balancing on its edge is measure zero among orientations, but that's not an argument that a betting procedure can assume it never happens. And even if exact unfaithfulness is rare, near-unfaithfulness—where the dependencies are highly sensitive to small parameter changes—might not be.

Please argue that faithfulness is not a reasonable default assumption in causal discovery, and that most of the inferential work people want it for can be done by the weaker minimality condition instead. Address the measure-zero argument directly, and be explicit about what minimality leaves open that faithfulness would have closed off, and whether we should regard that as a flaw in minimality or a flaw in the demand for a stronger guarantee.
