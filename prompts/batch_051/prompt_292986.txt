For a seminar handout on the definition of art, write a decision tree that takes a reader from their initial reaction to a concrete imagined case to a named position on what makes something art. Build the tree around the passage in Leo Tolstoy’s What Is Art? in which he proposes that art consists in one person’s consciously transmitting an experienced feeling to another through certain external signs, such that the other is infected by that feeling and experiences it. Open the handout by paraphrasing that passage in one or two sentences and explaining why it is a useful diagnostic starting point, since it fuses several separable claims: that art is a human activity, that it proceeds from an experience, that it operates through a public vehicle, and that the criterion of success is the audience’s being made to share the feeling.

The tree should have at least eight branch nodes and at least seven named leaves: Tolstoy’s infection theory; a Croce–Collingwood-style expressionism that drops the audience-infection requirement; a Bell-style significant form view; a Beardsley-style aesthetic-function view; a Dickie-style institutional theory; a Weitz-style family-resemblance anti-essentialism; and a cluster-concept view that treats art as a stable but non-exhaustive aggregate of criteria. If some positions cannot be cleanly separated by your questions, flag that explicitly.

Each branch node should be a question a reader can answer without already knowing the labels, and should isolate one axis on which these positions genuinely come apart. Useful axes include: whether the felt state must be the artist’s own; whether actual audience infection is necessary or merely the intention to transmit; whether the vehicle itself must exhibit a special internal structure or expressive quality; whether something outside the work—such as a curator’s decision, a historical tradition, or a community’s practice—determines art status; whether the object must be intended as art; and whether the concept of art is better understood through family resemblance than through necessary and sufficient conditions.

Use at least eight concrete cases, including a forged Vermeer that deeply moves viewers, an unexhibited poem locked in a drawer, a whale song that humans find profoundly moving, a Duchampian urinal, an AI-generated painting with no human feeling behind it, a child’s drawing admired only by its parents, a ritual mask from a culture with no word for art, and a natural rock formation placed in a gallery that genuinely moves visitors. Phrase every answer option as a practical judgment about whether and why the object is or is not art, never as an endorsement of a named theory.

Provide a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto the leaves. Where an answer pattern leads to a position with no historical defender, mark it as unoccupied and add one sentence diagnosing whether it is unoccupied because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Conclude with one short paragraph stating which pair of leaves your tree has the hardest time separating and why.
