Lately I have been working through some decision theory on my own, and I keep coming back to a result that seems more consequential than most informal discussions of it let on. As I understand it, Harsanyi showed that if each person's preferences over lotteries obey the standard expected-utility axioms, and a social ranking of those same lotteries also obeys those axioms, and the social ranking respects unanimous individual attitudes — in particular, it is indifferent between lotteries that every individual is indifferent between — then the social ranking has to be representable as a weighted sum of the individuals' utility functions. People sometimes call this an aggregation theorem and gesture at it as a way of saying that utilitarianism falls out of rationality plus a minimal respect for unanimity.

If the theorem really says that, then I do not see what is left to argue about. It would mean that utilitarianism is not one ethical view among several, but something the mathematics forces on anyone who accepts expected-utility theory and refuses to override matters on which everyone agrees. The only remaining disagreement would be about how much weight each person should get, and even raising that question seems to concede the main point, namely that the good of the whole just is some weighted sum of individual utilities.

Could you sort out what this theorem actually proves and what it does not? In particular, I want to know whether taking the social ranking to be representable as a weighted sum and then concluding that one ought to maximize total weighted welfare is as clean as the informal descriptions imply, or whether one of the premises already contains more moral content than it appears to. I am also unsure whether the weights are genuinely up for grabs or whether choosing them would require interpersonal comparisons that the theorem itself does not supply. Please say how confident you are in your account, and single out the point you are least sure of.
