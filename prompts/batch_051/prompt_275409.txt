Draft the opening joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two positions on what makes a statement racist. The first position, the intentionalist view, holds that a statement is racist in virtue of the speaker's attitudes and communicative intentions: roughly, it is racist when the speaker intends to express or to activate racial contempt, hostility, or a hierarchical ordering of racial groups, including through coded speech aimed at only part of the audience. The second position, the effect view, holds that a statement is racist in virtue of its characteristic or reasonably foreseeable effects: its tendency to reproduce racial hierarchy, to demean or subordinate its targets, or to encourage further hostile treatment of them, whatever the speaker's private intentions. Both positions treat dog whistles as central rather than marginal, but they locate what makes a dog whistle racist in different places: the intentionalist in the speaker's concealed address to an in-group, and the effect theorist in the split uptake itself and in its downstream social consequences.

The statement should open with a numbered list of claims both positions can sign without equivocation. These should cover the paradigm cases on which they agree, why dog whistles matter for any adequate account, the distinction between a statement's being racist and a speaker's being blameworthy, and the dialectical ground rules each side accepts for settling borderline cases without stipulation. Then include a disagreements table with, for each residual disagreement, the intentionalist's wording, the effect theorist's wording, and a note on whether the two wordings differ substantively or merely verbally. After the table, identify the three or four genuine cruxes — the sub-questions whose settled answers would move at least one side — and, for each crux, describe a test or vignette that both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

Anchor the whole document in one running example, which both positions must classify at each stage. Let the example be this invented case: a candidate for a council seat, in a borough where a particular phrase has for decades functioned as a coded dismissal of a racialised neighbourhood and its residents, says in a speech that the council must restore pride and order to the streets around the old estate. The candidate has no traceable history of racial hostility, and private papers discovered later show no awareness of the phrase's coded local use; nonetheless, one audience immediately hears the remark as a racial dog whistle, whilst another hears it as a neutral call for civic upkeep. Do not resolve the case; use it to force each position to state precisely where it diverges from the other and what evidence would change either mind.
