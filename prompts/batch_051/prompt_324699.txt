You are a professional linguist and philosopher of language who is skilled at turning technical debates into material non-specialists can engage with. I run a small blog for readers who have taken maybe one linguistics or philosophy course but do not identify with any school. I would like you to write a "Which theory of grammatical rules do you actually hold?" sorting quiz.

The quiz should have eight to ten questions. Every question should present a concrete, self-contained mini-case and offer two or three answer options that express a small, first-order judgment about that case. No question may ask the reader to choose a theory by name, slogan, or self-description. The quiz should sort readers by what they say about cases, not by which label they like.

The instrument must be able to separate at least these four positions: (a) a dual-mechanism "words and rules" view, on which a productive rule engine generates novel and default forms while irregular forms such as go → went or goose → geese are stored in a lexicon; (b) an exemplar- or analogy-based view, on which speakers generalize over stored forms, so-called rules are only concise summaries, and there is no separate rule machinery; (c) a social-convention view, on which a grammatical rule is a community-level norm, so individual correctness and error are defined by reference to the community rather than to private mental machinery; and (d) a deflationary or model-theoretic view, on which talk of rules is just compact description of the data and warrants no claims about what is represented in any speaker's mind.

Build the quiz from the logical space rather than from that list of names. First identify the diagnostic axes on which these positions come apart and state the matrix of combinations they allow. The axes should include at least: (i) whether irregular forms are stored separately from regular ones or produced by the same underlying mechanism; (ii) whether rules are psychologically real or descriptive idealizations; (iii) whether an exception falsifies a rule or merely routes around it; (iv) whether correctness is fixed by an individual grammar or by community convention; and (v) whether a sound change can be exceptionless or necessarily advances word by word. Then locate the four positions within this matrix, note which combinations of answers no position occupies, and briefly diagnose each empty cell as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. If two positions cannot be separated by any case, say so plainly rather than papering over it.

The cases must include the following, each introduced with enough context for an intelligent novice: (1) suppletion, such as go/went or be/is/was, and why suppletive forms look different from ordinary irregulars; (2) a wug-style novel form, for example what someone should produce for a made-up noun or verb; (3) children's overregularization such as goed or foots; (4) variability within a speech community, such as dived/dove or sneaked/snuck; and (5) a miniature historical corpus in which a sound change has applied uniformly in most eligible words but only sporadically in a few, with the options representing the contrast between exceptionless sound laws and word-by-word lexical diffusion without naming either as the "right" position.

The scoring key is the heart of the instrument. It should treat patterns of answers, not individual answers, as diagnostic; it should show how a reader is sorted into one of the four positions or into a named "mixed" outcome, and it should explain what a mixed pattern suggests. Include at least one pattern that sorts to each of the four positions, and at least one pattern that falls between them.

Think carefully before producing the final version. Start with a short preamble that lays out the axes and the matrix of combinations; then give the questions; then give the scoring key. The prose should be polished and suitable for a bright non-specialist audience.
