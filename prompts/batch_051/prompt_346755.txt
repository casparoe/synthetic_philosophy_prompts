I've been going back and forth with a friend about whether a donation platform that personalizes pity would end up vindicating Schopenhauer or Nietzsche, and I can't settle it. The setup: a large disaster-relief app runs continual experiments on its users. From each visitor's past giving behavior and real-time mood signals, it learns which images of suffering, which word choices, and which donation amounts produce the strongest empathic response. It then shows different people different "suffering content": some users see gentle, hopeful photos and small asks; others, the ones who respond strongly, get repeated, graphic imagery and follow-up messages reminding them of the people they didn't help. The company's line is that this is just Schopenhauer with better instrumentation — compassion is the real moral force, so they're maximizing it. My friend says it's exactly what Nietzsche was warning about: pity as something that multiplies suffering, makes the giver feel noble at a distance, and ends up corroding the very people it's optimized to exploit.

I'm writing a short position piece for a philosophy club, and I want your best reconstruction rather than a textbook summary. What would Schopenhauer and Nietzsche each say about this platform — both the practice itself and the pathologies it would likely produce over time, like compassion fatigue, performative giving, or resentment toward the people it claims to help? Where would they agree, where would they part ways, and which of them does the case actually favor? Obviously neither of them could have anticipated machine learning, so this is an exercise in extending thier commitments. And please push back if I've set the contrast up too crudely or if the scenario already smuggles in a verdict. Since the whole thing is speculative, signal clearly which parts of your answer are firmly anchored in what they actually wrote and which parts are your own extrapolation.
