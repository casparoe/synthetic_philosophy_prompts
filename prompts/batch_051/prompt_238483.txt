I have been trying to work out why some broken promises feel so much weightier than their material consequences seem to justify, and I have arrived at a view I call the scaffolding account of promissory obligation. The idea is this: when I promise something, I invite the promisee to treat my future action as a fixed premise in their practical reasoning, as scaffolding on which they may, with my permission, build plans. Breaking the promise is therefore not fundamentally a matter of causing a loss or disappointing a hope; it is a matter of leaving someone's practical reasoning supported by a premise that I knowingly made false. On this account, the wrongness of a broken promise is measured not by how much the promisee relied on it in the cost sense, but by how deeply the promise penetrated their deliberation — what I have been calling premise-penetration.

Here is the case that made me take the distinction seriously. If I promise a colleague to teach a course in the spring, and she spends the winter redesigning her research agenda, arranging childcare, and turning down editorial work around that expectation, and then I withdraw shortly before term for a better offer, most people think I have wronged her quite seriously, even if the department fills the course easily and she is materially no worse off than before I promised. Conversely, if I promise to water a friend's plants and forget, but the friend would not have planned anything different either way, the failure reads as minor even though the plants die and the material damage is larger. I think the difference just is premise-penetration: in the first case my future action had become load-bearing in the structure of her plans, while in the second it never did.

Before I present this to a reading group, I want an honest assessment. Is this a genuine third account, or is it ultimately the expectation-based account of promising with planning-theory vocabulary? Is premise-penetration actually doing any normative work, or is it merely tracking the promisee's trust or the depth of my assurance? And where does the account fail cleanly — either a promise that is clearly binding despite near-zero premise-penetration, or a promise that is clearly trivial despite very deep penetration? I am especially worried that the view overweights the promisee's private epistemic state: it would make the same broken promise seriously wrong when made to a meticulous planner and only trivially wrong when made to a relaxed friend, and I do not know whether to own or repair that consequence.
