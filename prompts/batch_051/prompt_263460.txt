In the literature on probabilistic coherence, the debate over what Dutch book arguments actually establish seems to me to have settled into a stable impasse. The core theorem itself is not in dispute: if an agent's degrees of belief violate the probability axioms, then there is a package of bets, each fair by the agent's own lights, whose combined payoff is negative no matter how the world turns out. One camp takes this to show that incoherent credences are rationally defective without qualification. A second camp grants the theorem but denies the normative bridge: sure loss shows only that an incoherent agent can be exploited, and exploitability is a practical vulnerability, not an epistemic failing — a thinker who refuses to bet, or who sees the package and declines it, exhibits no doxastic defect. A third camp argues the argument is circular, because moving from the theorem to a requirement of rationality presupposes that a rational agent must accept any package of bets each of which it considers fair, and that money has linear value; those assumptions amount to an expected-utility conception of practical rationality that already implies probabilistic coherence. A parallel impasse attaches to the diachronic extension, in which agents who depart from conditionalization are shown to be exploitable — but only, the critics say, because the argument presupposes that the agent has fixed in advance a determinate policy for how it would update on later evidence, which is exactly the kind of constraint the argument was supposed to earn.

I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, not an adjudication of it. Is this a substantive dispute, or is it verbal — a slide between epistemic and practical senses of 'rational'? Are the camps answering distinct questions under one heading, such as one about the structure of an agent's commitments and another about the norms of inquiry? Is there a hidden technical question doing the work, for instance whether the result can be reproduced with weaker assumptions about the acceptability of fair bets and the linearity of value, or whether the betting apparatus can be removed altogether? I suspect — as a hypothesis, not a fixed view — that the debate is half verbal and half substantive: 'rational' is ambiguous in a way that lets each side keep its conclusion, but beneath that sits a real question about whether any consistency argument can ground an epistemic norm in the absence of a prior account of what belief is for. Is that diagnosis right, or is there a better one? And on the diagnosis you defend, what exactly would count as progress: a theorem, a distinction, a reframing of the question, or the concession that the question was ill-posed?
