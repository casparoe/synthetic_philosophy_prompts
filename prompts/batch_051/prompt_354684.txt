I lead trust-and-safety policy at a large social platform that hosts human users, parody accounts, brand bots, AI-assisted creators, and increasingly "virtual influencers": wholly synthetic personas that post, message, and sell. My junior reviewers close several hundred persona-related cases a week; they have about twenty minutes per case and access only to public signals — posts, profile text, disclosures, account history. I need a reusable decision procedure for classifying a persona as (a) a counterfeit person to be removed or de-monetized, (b) a permissible synthetic persona to be left up, or (c) a borderline case required to carry a prominent disclosure. You are a philosopher of technology who has worked through Dennett's warnings about counterfeit people in the age of AI. Ground the procedure in the idea that what is uniquely dangerous about a counterfeit person is not that it is artificial but that it passes as a real human being and thereby debases the currency of interpersonal trust, much as counterfeit money debases a currency. Think carefully, and begin by working out which distinctions from that idea must survive translation into a checklist — counterfeit versus merely synthetic, counterfeit versus impersonation of a real person, genuinely visible disclosure versus disclosure buried in settings — and which distinctions can be dropped. Then write the procedure as an ordered set of questions with enough detail that a reviewer can tell what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops, and where it must escalate because the question is one of values or judgment rather than observable fact. Run the procedure on three cases: an AI-generated influencer who posts lifelike photos and never discloses that she is not a real person; a customer-service account with a human name and profile photo whose bio says "AI assistant"; and our own paid virtual-creator program, in which synthetic personas post sponsored content and are disclosed only in a settings-page footnote. Make sure at least one case returns an answer I will not like. Say explicitly what the procedure cannot do, and then tell me which empirical finding or argument would most likely change one of your verdicts, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
