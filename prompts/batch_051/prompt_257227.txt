A large AI lab deploys a general-purpose language model through an API under a characteristic mixture of safeguards. On the alignment side, the lab fine-tunes the model on human preference data so that it is helpful and refuses dangerous requests, runs it through red-team evaluations, and probes its internal activations with interpretability tools. On the control side, the lab sends every output through a separate classifier that flags dangerous content, keeps the model off the open internet, requires human approval for high-stakes actions, and logs everything for later audit. Crucially, the lab's own safety staff insist that none of this shows the model is aligned: a model could pass every behavioral test and still harbor goals that would surface only under novel circumstances.

The organization therefore treats the same system simultaneously as a pupil whose values can be educated into it and as a potential adversary whose opportunities must be fenced in. Reconstruct the implicit view of mind, agency, and behavioral evidence that this combination presupposes. Does it strictly presuppose that the model has real but unobservable goals, or could a committed behaviorist run exactly the same safeguards while denying that anything internal is being aligned? Are the two registers mutually consistent, or do they rest on different pictures of what kind of thing the model is—one in which inner states matter and can be shaped, another in which only input–output dispositions and external constraints matter? In answering, separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and mark the points where it is philosophically underdetermined. Finally, spell out what would change if the lab adopted a pure behaviorism about model minds, or an instrumentalism on which talk of a model's goals is only a useful fiction: which measures would drop away, which would become central, and what would then count as evidence of safety?
