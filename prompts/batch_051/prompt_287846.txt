I teach a graduate seminar in formal epistemology, and I keep coming back to the old Dutch book arguments for expected utility theory—the line that any agent whose preferences or degrees of belief violate the usual axioms can be pumped for money, so coherence forces you to be a precise expected utility maximizer. The thing is, a lot of recent work has made room for rational agents with imprecise credences or incomplete preferences: agents who, on some proposition, literally don't have a number, and will refuse to take either side of a bet at a wide range of odds. I want to know whether the coherence argument survives that development, or whether it quietly depended on the completeness assumption.

Suppose I grant that an agent's state of mind is best represented by a set of probability functions (or a set of acceptable gambles), and that the agent is coherent in refusing any bet that isn't forced by every function in the set. Does the Dutch book argument still show anything? Which exact premise gives way—the assumption that you can offer any bet and the agent must accept or reject it at some price, or something deeper about what coherence means? Is there a repaired version of the argument that still rules out something important, like non-convex sets of probabilities or updating rules that aren't suitably generalized Bayesian, and is it as compelling as the original? Or should I just teach my students that the coherence argument for expected utility is dead, and that the rise of imprecise probabilism quietly undercuts it?

I'm asking specifically about the fate of the argument, not about which theory of rational choice is ultimately correct or about practical implications. Please write your answer in American English.
