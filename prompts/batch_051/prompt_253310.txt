Lately I have been covering the intersection of artificial intelligence and moral philosophy for a general-audience magazine, and I keep noticing the same short story surface in interviews and essays: Ursula K. Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas." Most writers reach for it as a ready-made illustration of utilitarianism — the serene and prosperous city whose entire flourishing rests on the abject misery of one child in a locked room. I want to do something different with it. I am working on a piece about how a society's values can drift into a configuration that its own members would reject if they saw it from the outside, and about how such a configuration then stabilizes and passes itself on, so that a bargain that might once have been chosen hardens into a fact of life that people learn to describe as right.

I would like a reading of the story through that lens. Where, if anywhere, do we see drift taking place, and where do we see a decision being made and then rationalized? What work do the explanations given to the children do in transmitting the value system and keeping it in place? Is the citizens' acceptance of the bargain best understood as a changed moral judgment, as self-deception, as a re-description of values, or as something else? And what exactly do the ones who walk away reject — the practice, the pleasure it funds, the underlying value system, or only their own complicity? Just as important, I want the reading to notice where the story resists this framework: it may turn out that the text does not depict drift at all, that it shows us only a stable equilibrium after the values have already been locked in, and that this silence is itself part of what makes the story useful for thinking about the far-future risk of inheriting values we would not endorse. I am not looking for a moralistic verdict, but for criticism that is faithful to both the story and the philosophical problem.
