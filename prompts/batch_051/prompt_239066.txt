i've been toying with something i call 'catalytic hope,' and i'd like an honest assessment of whether it's a real idea or just a metaphor with a name. The core claim: debates about whether hope is rational usually ask how likely the hoped-for thing is. I think that's mostly the wrong question. The right question is whether the hope itself raises the probability of the outcome. Call it the feedback condition: a hope is warranted just in case your holding it makes the hoped-for outcome more likely than it would be if you held a sober, deflated attitude instead. So a union organizer can rationally hope to reverse a dying campaign even when the polls look hopeless, because her hope leaks into her voice and keeps people showing up, and that genuinely shifts the probabilities. But a spectator with identical evidence and no leverage is just marinading in wishful thinking, even if he hopes for the same outcome. It cuts the other way too: a father hoping his daughter gets into a prestigious college may be unwarranted if the hope turns into pressure that makes her perform worse, in which case his hoping makes the thing he wants less likely.

I also think this explains the feeling that too much hope can be morally wrong, not just epistemically sloppy. Unfed hope is borrowed motivation: it runs a movement on credit, and when the crash comes, everyone pays, not just the hopeful person. Too much hope in a group is closer to printing money than to seeing the world rosily.

The case I keep worrying about is the terminally ill patient hoping for a miracle. My condition seems to call her hope unwarranted, and I can't tell whether that's a feature (the view is honest) or a bug (the view is cruel and has smuggled in a bad picture of what hope is for).

So: is the feedback condition distinct from self-fulfilling prophecies and consequentialist thinking about belief, or does it collapse into one of them? What does it get right, and where does it break? Give me the three strongest objections and say whether any is fatal rather than just uncomfortable. And please tell me what would change your mind overall: which argument would lead you to abandon the view, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
