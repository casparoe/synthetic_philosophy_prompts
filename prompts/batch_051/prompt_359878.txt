I've been reading Darwin on the species question, and one bit in the Origin keeps needling me. In the last chapter he more or less says that species aren't a different category of thing from well-marked varieties — the only distinction is whether the intermediate forms are still walking around today or only inferred from the fossil record, and he admits the term species is applied pretty much as a convenience to any close set of similar individuals. That's a philosophical position, not just a field practice, and I realize I held it for fourty years without naming it.

I'd like you to build me a which-species-concept-do-you-actually-hold quiz for a discussion night at my natural history society. The members are mostly retired working scientists like me, not philosophers, so the instrument has to work without jargon — no asking people what they think of Mayr's species concept. Eight to ten questions, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, and a scoring key that maps the answer patterns onto the main positions in the species problem. Each question should be phrased so someone can answer without knowing the labels, so the instrument sorts people by what they say about cases, not by which label they like.

The questions should include at least these cases: a ring species like the greenish warbler complex around the Tibetan Plateau; a pair of cryptic species that look identical but won't mate; a fossil lineage where every bone is intermediate and paleontologists can't draw a clean line; a clonal or asexual dandelion; and a case where horizontal gene transfer scrambles the family tree. The scoring should sort people along a space whose first split is whether species are real, mind-independent units or pragmatic conveniences, and whose later splits separate, if they are real, whether they're best thought of as individuals or as kinds, and then whether the operative criterion is interbreeding, lineage cohesion through time, shared derived characters, or ecological role. The leaves that have to appear are: a reproductive-interbreeding view, an evolutionary-lineage view, a phylogenetic/character view, an ecological-niche view, and a pragmatic or pluralist view along the lines of that Darwin passage. The instrument should be built so that, at the end, each person can see how close or far their answer pattern puts them from the position I just paraphrased from Darwin's last chapter, without being told beforehand which branch that is.

Please flag any combinations of answers that no live position occupies and say briefly whether they look incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. Also, don't paper over positions the tree can't separate — if two branches merge, say so. And at the very end, give me a one-sentence summary as the last line.
