An institute's Moral Status Board must decide which entities qualify as moral agents—beings that can be praised, blamed, or held answerable for what they do. Its published decisions so far are as follows. An unimpaired adult human: a moral agent. A person with near-total paralysis, whose cognition is intact and who communicates only through an eye-tracking device: a moral agent. A trained dog that can cause serious harm and respond to commands, but cannot understand the reason for the relevant norm: not a moral agent. An autonomous trading system that selects actions solely by expected profit and cannot represent or evaluate moral considerations at all: not a moral agent. A person with advanced dementia who acts on occurrent desires but can no longer understand or weigh reasons: not a moral agent. The Board now must rule on a new case: an AI system with high-level reasoning abilities, stipulated to form sophisticated, revisable, and generally sound moral judgments about novel situations, but running with no actuators, no network connection, and no way to express or act on those judgments; by hypothesis, its judgments never affect anything outside its own internal states. Should the Board designate this AI a moral agent if it wants to be consistent with its own record? Identify the rival principles that best rationalize the decided cases, say what each principle predicts for the new AI, state which decided case is the closest analog, and specify a further case whose verdict would cleanly distinguish the remaining competitors. In particular, take a stand on whether a capacity for moral judgment that cannot issue in action or expression is a moral-agent-making capacity on its own, or merely one component of moral agency.
