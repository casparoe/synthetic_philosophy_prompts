Consider the way death is handled in contemporary Western mourning practice: funeral services eulogize the deceased as a unique person whose absence is a loss, grief counselors treat unresolved or prolonged grief as a condition to be worked through, and ordinary consolations presuppose that death is an evil to be accepted, not an event to be welcomed. Now read this passage from the Zhuangzi, "Perfect Enjoyment" chapter, in James Legge's translation:

"But I reflected on the commencement of her being. She had not yet been born to life; not only had she no life, but she had no bodily form; not only had she no bodily form, but she had no breath. During the intermingling of the waste and dark chaos, there ensued a change, and there was breath; another change, and there was the bodily form; another change, and there came birth and life. There is now a change again, and she is dead."

The passage is Zhuangzi's explanation of why he drummed on a basin and sang after his wife's death. What view of death, personhood, and the value of grief does Zhuangzi's response presuppose, and what rival view does the contemporary mourning practice I described presuppose? Reconstruct both implicit theories, distinguish what each practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say whether they are genuinely incompatible or underdetermined in the same places. Finally, explain what would concretely change in funeral and grief-counseling practice if Zhuangzi's presuppositions replaced the ordinary ones. Respond in at most 850 words, excluding the quoted passage.
