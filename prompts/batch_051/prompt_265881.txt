Two friends of mine got into it at lunch about a local proposal to give a randomly selected citizens' panel, rather than the elected council, final say over the zoning code. One of them quoted Aristotle's Politics (Book IV, 1294b): "I mean, for example, that it is thought to be democratic for the offices to be assigned by lot, for them to be elected oligarchic, and democratic for them not to have a property-qualification, oligarchic to have one." He said Aristotle was already making the point that sortition is the democratic method and election is the oligarchic one, so the burden should be on people who treat elections as the only legitimate way to pick lawmakers. The other said an ancient authority isn't an argument, and in a modern mass democracy elections are the only thing that makes rulers answer to everyone, whereas a random panel answers to no one and can be quietly captured by whoever writes the agenda. The first replied that accountability to voters is exactly the problem, because it makes politicians answer to donors and the most motivated partisans rather than the whole public, and Aristotle's underlying point is structural: election selects for the rich, famous, and well-connected, which is just as true now. The second said, fine, but then the real test is whether random panels actually deliberate better or decide more democratically, not what Aristotle called "democratic," and the proposal's supporters haven't shown that. The first said the real test has to be comparative, and the actual comparison is a council that was elected on 29% turnout and is mostly property developers. Adjudicate the exchange move by move: rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right, say who has the better of each step, and say what both of them are missing. Lead with your overall verdict in the first sentence, then walk through the moves, no preamble.
