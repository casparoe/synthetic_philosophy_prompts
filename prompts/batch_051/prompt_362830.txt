I'm on a county working group redesigning how eviction-diversion assistance is triaged. The county's tool gives each applicant a 0–100 risk score, interpreted as the probability of eviction absent assistance, and applicants above a cutoff are prioritized for payment. A proposed procurement rule would bind the vendor to a contract requiring that, for two specified demographic groups, the scoring-plus-cutoff system satisfy all three of the following at the same time: (1) calibration — at every score band, the proportion of applicants who would in fact be evicted absent assistance is the same across the two groups; (2) equal false positives — among applicants who would not in fact be evicted absent assistance, the proportion scored above the cutoff is the same across groups; (3) equal false negatives — among applicants who would in fact be evicted absent assistance, the proportion scored at or below the cutoff is the same across groups. The vendor would face penalties that escalate for each tenth of a percentage point by which any single measure diverges between the groups, and the contract explicitly lets the vendor adjust the scores, the cutoff, the intake questionnaire, and the population it trains on in order to comply.

I am not asking whether this rule is a good idea in the abstract. I want to know how it would actually play out. Under that contract, what is the vendor's first move: squeezing the cutoff, recalibrating the model, quietly narrowing the population that gets scored, or something else? Where do the three requirements jointly begin to bite — is there a point at which they cannot all be met, and what does a rational vendor do at that point, given that penalties attach to group divergence rather than overall accuracy? How do intake workers, applicants, and sister agencies respond once they notice that the score has stopped meaning what it used to mean? Does the penalty structure push toward a degenerate equilibrium — assist nearly everyone, assist nearly no one, or concentrate everyone at a single score band?

Take a definite position rather than laying out the possibilities: say which of the three conditions is most likely to be the one that quietly fails, who wins and who loses when it does, and whether you would salvage the procurement rule by changing it to something like a cap on divergence rather than exact equality, or abandon the rule and replace the pressure on the vendor with something else. Defend those choices.
