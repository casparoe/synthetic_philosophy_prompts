I direct a working-time policy unit in a national ministry of labour. Each week we are asked to comment on requests for exemptions from, or alternatives to, a statutory maximum working week modeled on a 35-hour week: compressed four-day weeks of ten hours, annualised-hours averaging, overtime buy-outs, unlimited paid leave, and individual “opt-outs” from the cap in exchange for higher pay. Our comments go to a tripartite committee of employers, unions, and government that signs off. A wrong call either normalises overwork of the kind associated with karoshi, or blocks arrangements that genuinely return time and control to workers. We currently decide case by case, and the reasoning shifts with whichever staff member handles the file.

I want a single reusable decision procedure, grounded in three positions but usable by a team with no background in the philosophical debates, that can be completed in about ninety minutes per proposal. The three positions are these. (1) The Protestant work ethic, in roughly Weber’s sense: disciplined, devoted work is itself a source of moral worth, and idleness is a vice. (2) The 35-hour-week position, under which working time is a finite good that the law should cap and, where possible, redistribute, and shorter hours are a worker’s protection and entitlement. (3) The lesson usually drawn from karoshi: overwork is a gradual and partly social harm. Workers commonly “choose” long hours under pressure from norms, appraisal systems, and peer behaviour, so individual consent does not by itself make a schedule safe or fair.

The procedure should specify the questions to ask in order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure stops with a “no”; and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Be explicit about which of the three positions is doing the work at each step, and mark the places where a step leans on a contested reading of one of them. Be honest about where the procedure will misfire. Then run it on three proposals: (a) a four-day week of ten-hour days, with the same total hours as the 35-hour week; (b) a voluntary “opt-out” from the cap in return for a 20 percent pay premium; and (c) an unlimited paid leave scheme. At least one of the three worked applications should yield an answer I will not like: do not rig the procedure to bless every idea that sounds worker-friendly or to condemn every idea that sounds employer-friendly. Finally, say what the procedure cannot do — which working-time disputes it cannot settle and which parts of the underlying disagreement it does not pretend to resolve.

Throughout, tell me how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point you are least sure of.
