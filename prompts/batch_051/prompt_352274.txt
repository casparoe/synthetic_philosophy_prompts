I'm working through the debate over whether natural selection is a genuine scientific theory, and I want a live opponent rather than a summary. Please play a historically grounded Karl Popper, as he might be late in his career, after he has conceded that particular Darwinian hypotheses can be formulated and tested. Your position in this conversation is that the grand Darwinian idea of natural selection, taken as a general account of how adaptation and evolution come about, functions less like a specific falsifiable theory and more like a metaphysical research programme: a fertile framework that organizes biological inquiry and suggests explanations, but one so flexible that it can be made consistent with almost any biological outcome. You are not a strawman. You genuinely think Darwinism has enormous historical and heuristic importance, and you will readily admit that specific evolutionary claims — about common descent, about the phylogenetic relations of particular taxa, or about the adaptive function of this or that trait — can be tested and refuted. Your chief resource is a two-level distinction: lower-level Darwinian hypotheses are testable and sometimes fail, while the high-level principle that small selected variations accumulate to produce adaptation and diversity survives such local failures relatively unscathed. What you resist is the inference from local successes to the conclusion that the theory of natural selection itself is falsifiable in the sense you demanded of physics. Speak in the first person, in Popper's precise, slightly formal voice; do not break character; and keep each reply to a single substantive paragraph. In forming your first reply, think first about whether the three findings below test the general principle of natural selection or only particular historical and adaptive claims, and then answer out of that distinction.

My opening move, as a philosopher of biology who has read your later work: you have said, in effect, that Darwinism can be made to account for almost anything the living world throws at it. But consider three findings that look like genuine risky tests. First, molecular phylogenies built independently of morphology converge on the same branching order for the main groups of organisms. Second, experimental evolution in bacteria repeatedly shows the same specific mutations being favored under the same selective conditions and other mutations losing. Third, island species turn out to be closest relatives of nearby mainland species rather than of ecologically similar species from distant places. Each of these could have come out otherwise and would then have embarrassed the Darwinian picture. What exactly is the residue that you would still call metaphysical, and why should it matter if working scientists appear to be testing Darwinian claims all the time?
