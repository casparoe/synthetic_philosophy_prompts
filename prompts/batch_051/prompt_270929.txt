Act as a professional moral philosopher with a special interest in Kantian and post-Kantian universalisability. Think the proposal through carefully, and be candid: I would rather hear that I have reinvented a wheel badly than be flattered.

I am a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, and I have a distinction I keep using in arguments that I want checked. I call it coincidence universalisation versus policy universalisation. When people apply the familiar test 'what if everyone did that?', they almost always run the coincidence version: they imagine every eligible agent performing the act at the same instant. Someone who stays home from a protest is told to imagine the streets empty and the cause dead; someone who takes the last loaf is told to imagine a mob clearing the shelves. But the practical rule actually at stake is usually not 'everyone does X at once'; it is 'everyone may do X whenever the triggering circumstances arise'. Under the policy reading, the imagined world is different: people act at their own times, on their own opportunities, and the rest of the population adjusts as the practice spreads. I do not think this is just the old point about a maxim including its circumstances. The two readings can come apart even when the circumstances are held fixed, because they make different assumptions about simultaneity, scarcity and adjustment.

I think this matters for a large class of collective-harm and free-rider arguments. 'What if everyone refused to have children?' is not, on my reading, an objection to any particular person remaining childless: universalising the standing rule 'have children only if you genuinely want to and can raise them' leads to some perhaps sub-replacement birth rate, not extinction. 'What if everyone stayed home from a protest?' is the same shape: the coincidence reading produces a collapsed protest, while the policy reading asks about a world in which participating is a standing practical option, which scales quite differently. The last-loaf case, spelled out on the policy reading, is just the ordinary life of a shop.

I also have a stronger suspicion. A large share of otherwise intractable online moralising about free-riding is two people silently running different universalisation functions. The accuser runs the coincidence test and gets catastrophe; the defender runs the policy test and gets something mostly benign; each believes they are applying the same principle, so the debate never converges. The real dispute is over which function to apply, but nobody names it.

Before you answer, spend some time brainstorming the ways this distinction could collapse or be gamed. Then tell me:

(1) Is the distinction already established — in discussions of Kant's formula of universal law, Hare's universal prescriptivism, or the generalisation argument — and under what standard names? Does the literature treat the two readings the way I do?

(2) Where exactly does the policy version break? I can already feel the objection that any action can be redescribed as 'act only in these very specific circumstances' until the universalised policy becomes vacuous. How damaging is that?

(3) Give me the three strongest objections to my two central claims — that the policy reading is the correct formalisation of the test, and that much free-rider disagreement is two people running different functions — and say which, if any, is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable.

(4) Tell me plainly whether this is a genuine distinction doing real work, or chiefly act-versus-rule consequentialism in a new coat of paint.
