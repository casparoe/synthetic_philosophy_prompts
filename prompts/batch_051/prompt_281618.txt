A thing I keep coming back to is the pragmatic encroachment versus contextualism debate in epistemology, and it seems to me it has been stuck for close to two decades — the same moves get re-run, nobody moves. The shared data is something like this: in a low-stakes case, someone with good but not conclusive evidence says they know; in the high-stakes case, the same person with the same evidence says they don't (or acts as if they shouldn't claim to). The contextualist reads that as the word 'knows' shifting with the conversational context, so its extension is stakes-relative even if the underlying state isn't. The encroachment person reads it as the knowledge state itself depending on stakes, as if there were a practical condition on knowing. My working theory is that the reason it never moves is beause it isn't actually one debate. It's a semanticist and a metaphysician of knowledge talking about different things — one about what 'knows' means in context, the other about the conditions on the underlying state — and they only look like rivals because both sides insist their view is the right account of the bank cases. If I'm right, what would actually pull the two apart? I keep half-thinking third-person versus first-person data, or metalinguistic probes, or facts about how children pick up 'knows', would do it, but I'm not confident. If I'm wrong and this is a substantive disagreement after all, tell me what exactly they're disagreeing about, whether anything empirical is hiding inside it, and what a test or argument would have to look like to end it rather than just reframe it.
