Hey — I'm a PhD student in public policy working on risk regulation, and I've been going around in circles on a statutory-interpretation problem. I'd rather get a structured walkthrough than another essay.

The state statute I work on reads:

"Section 12(b). Before adopting any rule under this chapter, an agency shall determine that the aggregate benefits of the rule justify its aggregate costs. An agency may adopt a rule whose estimated costs exceed its estimated benefits only upon a finding, supported by substantial evidence, that the rule is necessary to avert an imminent threat to the public health or safety."

It was drafted in 1994. The legislative history is all about chemical fires, contaminated water, a release that's already happening or hours away — the idea being you don't pause for careful monetization when the tank is already leaking.

Now the case the drafters didn't foresee. A state agency wants to require developers of the largest AI models (above a compute threshold that didn't exist in 1994) to spend 2% of each training run's budget on security audits, red-teaming, and emergency shutoff procedures. The agency puts compliance at about $50 million a year and conventionally quantifiable benefits at $10 million, so the ordinary cost-benefit test fails. The real justification is tail risk: the agency assigns about a 1% chance over the next twenty years to a catastrophic event — an engineered pandemic or a mass-casualty loss of control — and says the expected value swamps the costs. But their own cost-benefit guidance says they can't use a number that speculative in the routine balancing, so they're relying on the safety-valve. And the catastrophe plainly isn't imminant: it's diffuse, continuous, and most of the probability mass sits in future systems nobody's built yet.

So does the safety-valve reach it? Please walk me through four approaches — ordinary meaning of the words, purpose and structure of the statute, what the 1994 drafters would most plausibly have said if this exact case had been put to them, and what a reasonable agency lawyer reading it today would conclude. Then tell me which approach should control and why, or whether "which approach should control" is the wrong question here. One thing I can't settle: is "imminent" doing temporal work or epistemic work — is it about the harm being right around the corner, or about the evidence being solid enough that you shouldn't wait for better monetization? Does that distinction actually change any of the four readings?
