I spent my career in a field where, if you wanted to claim something, you eventually had to run the thing — build the apparatus, control for confounds, get the instrument readings. So the biggest shock in my recent philosophy reading has been how much work gets done by small made-up scenarios: a trolley, a sleeping violinist, a stopped clock, and then a conclusion that's meant to be obvious from the armchair. I've arrived a view about this, and I'd like it pressure-tested.

The view is that in at least a large share of philosophical cases, thought experiments function mostly as intuition pumps. They persuade through vivid framing, selective detail, and emotional salience, so the conclusion is carried by the narrative rather than by anything the scenario actually shows about possibility, necessity, or meaning. I'm not saying all thought experiments are useless. In my own science the ones that stuck around usually either had a path to being run for real or were arguments in disguise. But the distinctively philosophical ones — the ones that couldn't be run even in principle, but still get treated as evidence — look to me like cases where we accept persuasive work that we'd never accept from an actual experiment, and that we'd flag as a framing effect if it came from a psychology vignette.

So: what would count against this? I don't want a tour of famous successes. I want a map of my exposure. Which cases would a defender of the evidential value of thought experiments say I have to explain away, and which of those would a thoughtful defender concede are merely heuristic or occasionally misleading? What actual results — in cognitive psychology, experimental philosophy, physics, mathematics — would be the strongest evidence that the armchair is tracking something real rather than pumping intuitions? Which of these would be fatal to the view, and which would just force me to narrow its scope? And be blunt about the risk that I've insulated myself: if my position ends up being "thought experiments are sometimes useful and sometimes misleading," how much is left of it, and is that vagueness a problem for a view like this or just an honest modesty?

Personally, I'd be most moved by an example where a distinctively philosophical thought experiment later became runnable — or was cashed out in a way that let reality decide — and the armchair verdict survived.
