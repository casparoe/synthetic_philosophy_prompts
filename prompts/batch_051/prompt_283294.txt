I keep returning to a passage in Dostoevsky's 1880 novel The Brothers Karamazov, in the chapter The Grand Inquisitor, and it's disturbing me more than I'd like. The Inquisitor, addressing the returned Christ, argues — I'm paraphrasing because I don't trust my memory of the translation — that Christ was wrong to refuse the three temptations in the wilderness. The offers of bread, miracle, and authority were not temptations to be spurned but practical answers to what human beings are actually like. Christ, says the Inquisitor, overestimated us: he treated us as if we could bear the weight of freedom and choose the good from love alone, but most people find freedom a torment. Give them material security, a visible sign, and a fixed outward order, and they will be content; leave them free, and they end up anxious, divided, and resentful, eventually handing the burden to whoever will take it from them. The Inquisitor says the Church has corrected Christ's work out of love for the weak.

I can see a genuinely strong case for this as a psychological and social theory, and it maps onto things I already half-believe: people in tightly scripted communities often report less existential anxiety, the demand for autonomy can look like a demand most people never made, and much of what we call freedom gets exercised as deference to whatever the default happens to be. But I also hold, firmly, that human dignity requires allowing a person to make the wrong choice about the most important things — that if the Inquisitor's bargain were available and people accepted it, they would become something less than human, even if they were content.

Act as a professional philosopher who works on autonomy, well-being, and social choice. Think carefully before you settle on a verdict, and start by brainstorming the strongest versions of each side rather than the straw versions. Then give me a negotiated adjudication, not a Dostoevsky lecture. Present the strongest version of the Inquisitor's argument as a genuine theory of human flourishing, not as a villain's speech; present the strongest version of the autonomy intuition, including exactly what it claims is lost under the Inquisitor's bargain; then tell me which should yield. I can imagine three clean exits: the theory is empirically right about anxiety but wrong about happiness, so its optimization target is mistaken; the intuition is a product of a particular cultural history and should be dropped as a universal claim; or there is a reading on which both survive, perhaps by distinguishing external arrangements of authority from the deeper exercise of the will. If one of those is right, argue for it explicitly; if not, say what a fourth position would look like. I don't want a "both sides make good points" answer. I want the cost of each move priced in.
