Act as a philosopher who works on luck, desert, and distributive justice. Start by brainstorming both sides honestly, then give your actual view.

I keep noticing an asymmetry in how people—especially, though not only, Americans—react to inequality, and I want to know if it can be defended. Two women end up with the same forty million dollars and neither of them did anything illegal. Andrea founded a software company and sold it at the right moment; everyone calls her self-made and treats the money as a reward for merit. Bianca inherited the same amount and has spent a decade running the family foundation competently; people call it unearned privilege and act like there's something embarassing about it. Both pay the taxes they owe. We seem to think wealth you "made" is legitimate in a way wealth you "received" is not.

Tell me whether anything actually justifies that asymmetry. First, build the best version of the principle behind it—something about desert, choice, effort, contribution, or risk—and say exactly which cases it's supposed to cover. Then stress-test it hard. Does it survive a version of Andrea whose success was mostly luck (she was in the right city, her first three products failed, and the one that succeeded only worked because a big client happened to see a demo)? Does it treat Bianca fairly if she did genuine, skilled work inside the family firm rather than just clipping coupons? And run it through the awkward middle cases: lottery winners, capital gains on stocks you just held, an athlete paid for a body he mostly inherited, a homeowner who sells for a fortune because her neighborhood gentrified around her while she slept. Finally, take a side: if the asymmetry survives, does it make the American attitude toward inequality more defensible than the European one, or does the European attitude get it right precisely by distrusting the earned/unearned story as a self-serving myth? If you conclude the asymmetry can't be salvaged, say what we should do with it instead.
