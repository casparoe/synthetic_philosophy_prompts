I work as a software engineer, and I have no formal philosophy background. My team is building a system that ingests a large, messy stream of production logs and incident reports, and the model is supposed to answer questions like which component is actually degrading the service and what we should do about it. We can't check most of its answers directly — if a human could verify them, we wouldn't need the model. Our current approach is therefore to train the model to answer the way a carefully briefed human engineer would answer after reviewing the same logs, and to penalize anything a human reviewer finds implausible even when we can't prove it wrong. The stated rationale is that anything worth calling knowledge should be something a competent human could in principle recognize and vouch for, so tying the model to that standard keeps it honest. I suspect this design quietly commits us to a particular philosophical view about knowledge, but I don't know how to articulate it. Please reconstruct the implicit theory of knowledge behind this practice. Specifically: does the practice strictly presuppose that there is no knowledge beyond what qualified human judges would endorse, or only that human judgment is a useful proxy that usually tracks the truth? Which rival views of knowledge does it quietly rule out, and which does it merely fail to exploit? Is the setup internally coherent, given that we are using human endorsement to train a system whose entire purpose is to tell us things we could not already verify? And what would we have to change in the training setup — rather than only in how the model explains its answers — if we instead allowed that the model could know things no human reviewer would accept at first glance?
