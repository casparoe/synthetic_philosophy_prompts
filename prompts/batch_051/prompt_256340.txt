I am a novelist, and I have reached a scene that has to do the philosophical work of the whole book. The novel is set in a mountain village cut off by a landslide; the only remaining route to a pharmacy runs down a narrow canyon track, and the villagers must decide by morning whether to send a small party down that track to fetch medicine for an outbreak among the children. An older woman who has built and repaired that road all her life insists the track is safe enough. A young civil engineer, herself from the village, says the ground is still moving and the party would be buried.

I need a sustained, scene-length dialogue between the two of them in which the real disagreement is not about hydrology but about whether doubt is a duty—about what one owes, and is permitted, to believe when other people's lives hang on the belief. The engineer holds that when lives are at stake, it is a moral failure to believe without adequate evidence; that confidence outrunning evidence is a form of arrogance, and that the older woman's certainty is precisely the sort of belief no one has the right to hold. The older woman holds that there is an opposing duty: sometimes one owes it to other people to trust, to commit, and to act on knowledge that cannot be fully articulated or verified in time; that the engineer's doubt is not caution but disloyalty to the shared life of the village, and that withholding belief from a neighbor can be its own kind of cruelty. I want each side to come out genuinely attractive, with neither woman a strawman and each answering the other's actual arguments rather than talking past them. The dialogue should stay inside the particulars of this one night, this road, the ill children, and the decision that must be made by dawn, and it should not resolve the underlying philosophical question neatly.
