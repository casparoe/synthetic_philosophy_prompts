I'm a first-year law student, and I've become convinced that 'proof beyond a reasonable doubt' resists the standard glosses — near certainty, no real possibility of innocence, and similar phrases — in a way that matters for how evidence should be evaluated. I've been reading about the problem of naked statistical evidence, and the cases that unsettle me are the ones where the probability of guilt looks high and yet I would still refuse to convict. The classic illustrations are the blue bus case, in which a bus injures someone and the only link to a particular company is that it runs 80% of the bus trips on that road at that hour, and the gatecrasher case, in which a thousand people attend an event, one ticket was sold, and a person is prosecuted for entering without paying even though nobody saw how he entered. In both, the numbers seem strong, but something other than a probability shortfall appears to be missing.

Give me a working definition of 'proof beyond a reasonable doubt' that gets the following seven cases right. If you think the phrase does two jobs — say, an epistemic threshold and a rule about how the risk of error may be allocated between the state and the defendant — give me a definition for each and say which cases change their result under which definition. Treat all seven cases as criminal cases governed by the reasonable-doubt standard, even where the underlying scenario is borrowed from discussions of the civil preponderance standard. The cases are these:

1. A warehouse is burglarized at night. Clear security footage shows the defendant, a former employee, forcing open a door and leaving with the stolen equipment; equipment with matching serial numbers is recovered from his van later that night.
2. A detective testifies only that he has a strong hunch about the defendant, with no other evidence connecting the defendant to the crime.
3. A bus strikes and kills a pedestrian and leaves the scene. The state prosecutes the bus company under a statute imposing criminal liability on the operator of a bus involved in a fatal hit-and-run. The only admissible evidence is that the company runs 80% of the bus trips along that street during that hour; there is no paint, GPS, footage, or witness identifying the particular bus.
4. A ticketed concert is held in a fenced field with a single entrance. A thousand people are counted through the gate, and the promoter's records show that only one ticket was sold. The state charges one attendee with theft of services; no one saw whether he paid, and he cannot produce a ticket.
5. A sexual assault kit yields a DNA profile. The state runs it through a database and finds a single hit, to the defendant, with a reported random-match probability of one in ten million. The defendant was in the database only because of an unrelated earlier arrest, and there is no other evidence connecting him to the assault.
6. An eyewitness to an armed robbery identifies the defendant as the robber; in controlled tests the witness correctly identifies faces of this kind about 80% of the time, and there is no other evidence.
7. A proprietary video-analysis system used by several police departments reports that the defendant's gait and build match the perpetrator in a surveillance video with 99.4% confidence; the vendor will not disclose its training data or the features the system uses, and the state offers no other evidence.

Run your definition through every case and say what result it produces. Where it gives a result I would call wrong — or no result at all — revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict. I am confident about the first two cases; I have an instinct that a reasonable doubt remains in cases 3 and 4, but cannot say what rule produces that instinct; and I have no settled view about cases 5, 6, and 7. I would also like your view on whether the real work is being done by a probability threshold, by the source or structure of the evidence, or by something the state owes a defendant before it may impose punishment. Please answer in American English.
