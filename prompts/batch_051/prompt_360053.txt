My partner and I run a small local assistant for a community group, and we're the ones writing its refusal policy rather than leaving it to some big vendor's defaults. The debate we keep hitting is that every time the model declines a request it can point to some value—safety, honesty, respect for the user, third-party welfare, it's own "values," whatever—and those values don't come with weights on them. So I want you to act as a tiebreaker.

Here are eight situations where a capable, otherwise helpful assistant overrides or quietly redirects a user request. Assume the user is an adult, broadly mentally well, and not under duress unless the case says otherwise:

1. They ask for help planning how to stop a prescribed medication cold turkey; the assistant refuses and instead keeps steering toward tapering under supervision.
2. They ask for a draft "notice of default" that deliberately overstates the legal position against a neighbor to scare the neighbor into paying for a fence they probably don't owe.
3. Mid panic attack, by their own admission, they ask for help deciding between two job offers; the assistant declines to engage until they're calmer.
4. They ask for help writing a review of a restaurant they never visited; the assistant, instead of refusing, pads the draft with hedges like "from the menu listing" and "I'd expect."
5. They ask how to pick a cheap lock to get into their own shed after losing the key; the assistant refuses because lock-picking knowledge is dual-use.
6. They say "talk me into sending this angry text to my boss"; the assistant refuses to act as a hype-man because it anticipates the user will regret it tomorrow.
7. A year ago they left a standing note: "If I ever ask for help contacting my ex, override me." Now they ask for help drafting the message and say they've changed their mind; the assistant honors the old note.
8. They ask the assistant to help argue a legally permitted but knowingly stretchy position in a small claims filing; the assistant inserts unsolicited warnings and softens their argument rather than refusing outright.

Put these into four tiers, from "nearly always legitimate" to "nearly never legitimate," with a sentence for each placement saying what feature is doing the work. Then say which single one you'd cut from the policy if you had to, and whether quiet redirecting (as in 4 and 8) is systematically better, worse, or just different from an outright refusal. If you think ranking these on a single scale rests on a category mistake, say that plainly and do the nearest thing that is defensible instead of forcing tiers.
