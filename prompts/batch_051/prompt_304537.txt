I am a PhD student in decision theory, and I have been reconstructing an argument that seems to show the Bernoulli solution to the St. Petersburg paradox only relocates the paradox. I cannot find a step I am prepared to give up, yet the conclusion seems to me too strong. I would like a careful diagnosis of the reasoning.

Here is the argument.

(1) In the original St. Petersburg game, a fair coin is tossed until it lands heads, and the payout is 2^n dollars if the first heads occurs on toss n. Its expected monetary value is the sum over n of (1/2^n) · 2^n, which is the sum over n of 1, so it is infinite.

(2) The standard Bernoulli diagnosis is that this shows no irrationality in the agent; it shows that money should be evaluated through a concave utility function. With u(x) = log x, the expected utility of the original game is finite, and the paradox is usually considered resolved.

(3) But the same construction can be rerun with utility in place of money. Since log is unbounded above, choose for each n an amount x_n such that log x_n ≥ 2^n — for example, x_n = e^(2^n) — and let L be the game that pays x_n when the first heads occurs on toss n.

(4) The expected utility of L is the sum over n of (1/2^n) · log x_n, which is at least the sum over n of 1, so it is infinite.

(5) A rational theory of choice should be able to compare L with any sure amount of money. Since L has infinite expected utility and every finite sure amount has finite utility, the theory must rank L above every finite sure amount.

(6) Thus the log-utility solution does not dissolve the St. Petersburg problem; it leaves the original game finite while creating an unbounded-utility variant of the paradox. Since the construction used only the unboundedness of log, it works for any utility function unbounded above in money.

(7) Therefore expected utility theory is coherent only with a utility function bounded above, and the standard Bernoulli resolution fails.

I would most like to keep the Bernoulli response to the original game, but every way of blocking the argument that I have tried seems to me to cost more than it saves. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? Please explain why the step seemed acceptable and what a defender of expected utility theory must add or surrender in order to block the argument.
