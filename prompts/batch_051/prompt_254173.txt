While working on a piece about the psychology and philosophy of blame, I assembled an argument that now strikes me as proving too much, and I would like a diagnosis. (1) Moral blame should track only what an agent controls: if two agents differ solely in factors outside their control, they cannot differ in blameworthiness. (2) Two drivers are each reading a text message as they drive through a residential street at the same speed and with the same inattention; a child steps into the road in front of the first and is killed, while no child appears before the second. By (1), the two drivers are equally blameworthy. (3) The point does not stop at outcomes. Temperament, habitual dispositions, the circumstances that tempt or restrain us, and even the intentions that form in us are all shaped by constitution and upbringing, which we did not choose. So the reasoning behind (2) seems to extend: differences in character and choice are no better grounded in control than the child's running into the road. (4) If differences in outcome, circumstance, constitution, and choice all fail to justify differences in blame, then no one is any more blameworthy than anyone else, and the practice of blame and punishment rests on a systematic mistake. (5) But that conclusion is unacceptable: the person who tortures for amusement and the person who struggles against an ungenerous temperament are not morally on a par. Where exactly does this argument go wrong, if it does? Which step should be rejected or revised, and what does that revision cost? I would most like to preserve (2) and (5), but I want to know what that combination costs.
