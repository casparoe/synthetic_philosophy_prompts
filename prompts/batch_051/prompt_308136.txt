Write a pantoum of four quatrains from the point of view of the traveler in the Death in Damascus case. The premise you are working with is this: a man in Damascus sees Death in the crowd, and Death appears startled to find him there; that night the man slips away toward Aleppo, but when he arrives Death is waiting, and the surprise in Damascus turns out to have been surprise that they had not yet kept their appointment in Aleppo. On the decision-theoretic reading, the traveler can choose to stay or to flee, but Death reliably anticipates the choice, so conditional on his staying, Death will be in Damascus, and conditional on his fleeing, Death will be in Aleppo; the act is free, and yet whichever branch he takes, he keeps the appointment.

Let the pantoum's mechanism carry this problem rather than merely describe it. Open the poem with the two rival plans as the first and third lines — one line resolving, in substance, to remain in Damascus tomorrow, the other to flee to Aleppo tomorrow. As the form passes each quatrain's second and fourth lines forward to become the next quatrain's first and third, make the carried lines into the traveler's successive attempts to out-reason the appointment, so that the case that began as a reason to stay becomes, by the time the form has moved it, a reason to flee, and back again. Then, in the final quatrain, return to the exact first and third lines with which the poem opened, so the traveler ends his deliberation still standing between the same two plans, the form itself having closed the loop that the decision was supposed to escape. Do not explain the paradox or import the technical vocabulary of decision theory into the poem; a reader who knows the Death in Damascus case should recognize it in the refrains' entanglement, and a reader who does not should still be left with a poem whose closing repetition feels like a door opening onto the same room.
