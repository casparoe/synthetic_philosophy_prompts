Consider the blue bus case, tried civilly under a preponderance-of-the-evidence standard. A pedestrian is hit by a bus and sues Blue Bus, Inc.; the only evidence is that Blue Bus operates 80% of the buses in town and no one saw the crash. Many think the plaintiff should lose despite the 0.8 probability, because the evidence is "nakedly statistical": it places the defendant in a reference class but does not connect it to the particular accident. Now vary the case. The 0.8 figure comes not from a long-run market share but from a traffic camera that recorded every bus passing this exact intersection during the ten minutes surrounding the crash, except that a passing truck briefly blocked the camera at the moment of impact; the recorded distribution of buses was 80% Blue Bus. Assume this gives the same 0.8 probability that the bus was a Blue Bus, and there is still no eyewitness. Does the standard objection to naked statistical evidence survive when the statistic is tied this closely to the time and place of the event? Work through how each side of the original debate should analyze the variant: the side that treats proof as posterior probability, and the side that demands individualized evidence. Is the line between them best drawn by the specificity of the reference class, by a causal link to the event, or by something else? End by saying how confident you are in your analysis and which step you are least sure of.
