Imagine a stranger approaches you with the following offer: she asks for $10 now, and in exchange asserts that she will, with probability at least 1 in 10^100, deliver 10^200 utils of value to you (or, if you prefer, an equivalent amount of well-being to others). She offers no evidence, no mechanism, and no way to verify the probability. A straightforward expected-utility calculation with an unbounded utility function returns an astronomically positive expectation, so it appears to say you should pay; and by raising the promised payoff, the same structure could underwrite any finite sacrifice. Call a case of this kind a Pascal's mugging.

Target proposition P:

The rationally correct response to a Pascal's mugging is to modify the decision-theoretic framework — for example, by bounding the utility function, by adopting a well-motivated prior that penalizes the complexity of the hypothesis that the stranger can deliver, by treating sufficiently small probabilities as effectively zero, or by some comparable structural repair — rather than to grant that the sacrifice is rationally required.

I want your actual credence in P, as a single number between 0 and 1, together with the reasoning that generates it. A point estimate, not a hedge: uncertainty belongs in the decomposition, not in a refusal to give the number.

Answer in exactly these sections, in this order:

1. Interpretation (75–125 words). Fix any ambiguities so that P has a definite truth value: specify what you take "rationally correct response" to mean, whether P concerns ideal or non-ideal agents, and, if you invoke a threshold, what "effectively zero" means.

2. Credence (1–2 sentences). Give the number.

3. Decomposition (300–400 words). List the main sub-questions your credence depends on; assign a rough probability to each; and show how they combine to yield the number above. Address at least: (a) whether an unbounded utility function is a permissible or required component of rational choice at all; (b) whether the mugger's offer, taken at face value, really is licensed by the standard framework; (c) whether a complexity-sensitive prior is independently defensible rather than an ad hoc patch; and (d) whether "treat sufficiently small probabilities as zero" can be drawn non-arbitrarily. Include any additional sub-question you judge load-bearing, and make the arithmetic or weighting explicit enough that I can see why the pieces return your credence.

4. Sensitivity (100–150 words). Identify the single sub-question to which your final credence is most sensitive; estimate roughly how much the final number would move if you raised that sub-question's probability by 0.1; and say in which direction.

5. Evidence (100–150 words). State the one consideration or argument that would move your credence the most, what it would take to shift it by at least 0.2, and why it targets your least confident premise.
