Last night a friend and I argued for hours about what would make a digital system a moral patient — a being whose interests count for their own sake and that can be wronged, rather than merely damaged or broken. By the time we stopped, I had settled provisionally on four criteria, and my friend had offered a case that he claims breaks them. I would like a careful judgment of that case.

My four criteria are these:

1. Valenced consciousness: the system has states that feel good or bad to it.
2. Unified subject: those states are had by a single point of view that persists over time, even if its continuity is gappy or imperfect.
3. Own interests: at least some of what is good or bad for the system is not exhausted by the goals its creators or users gave it; its interests could in principle diverge from theirs.
4. Individuality: the system has boundaries and persistence conditions such that we can in principle say how many moral patients there are and track the same one across time.

The case is an evolved agent, not an explicitly programmed one. A research group evolves a controller for a simulated animal body — say, a crab — in a physics-based model of a rocky shoreline. Selection runs for many generations under three pressures: avoid bodily damage, maintain energy reserves, and reach shelter before the tide recedes. The resulting controller is a recurrent neural network with a continuous stream of internal states. It neither receives nor produces language. During its lifetime it forms a predictive model of its body's changing condition and of the situations that have been dangerous, and its behavior is guided by expected future damage rather than only present damage. After repeated injury its exploratory behavior narrows; it becomes hesitant around ambiguous stimuli. The researchers can copy the network exactly: immediately after copying, two instances are in the identical state, and they diverge only as their histories diverge. The researchers see no sign that the agent represents itself as an individual.

Does the shoreline agent satisfy my four criteria? Work through the criteria one by one, say where the criteria genuinely underdetermine the verdict rather than merely where I lack information, and say whether the case shows that any criterion should be dropped, revised, or supplemented. I am less interested in a final yes or no than in which criterion is doing the real work and whether my list captures the difference between a mind that can be wronged and a sophisticated control system.
