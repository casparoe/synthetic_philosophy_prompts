I'm putting together a handout on when "it's just a preference" stops being a get-out-of-jail-free card in moral criticism, and I'm trying to inventory every serious proposal for where the line between a mere preference and a bias lies. So far I've got:

1. The belief test — biases are (usually shoddy) beliefs about what a group is like, so they're answerable to evidence; preferences are non-cognitive attitudes that don't aim at the world, so the question of evidence doesn't arise.
2. The target test — a preference is about my own experience ("I like jazz"); a bias is an external preference about how others are or how they should be, and that's the part that's morally up for grabs.
3. The subordination test — a preference becomes a bias when it keys on a trait that carries a history of status hierarchy, so the supposedly innocent taste can't be pulled apart from the social meaning of the trait.
4. The stakes test — preferences get moral scrutiny only when they control access to important goods (jobs, housing, respect), so the same taste can be harmless at home and a bias at the hiring desk.

Give me one more criterion of a genuinely different kind — not a rewording, a special case, or a mash-up of these four. Then tell me honestly whether it does any better than they do on the standard hard case: someone whose dating preferences exclude people of a certain race, where the preference feels private, sincere, and like it involves no factual claim about anyone.
