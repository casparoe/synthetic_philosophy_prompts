I've been kicking around a principle I call 'symmetric default', and I want someone to pressure-test it. The claim is this: when the laws are symmetric and the setup of a problem is symmetric, a unique outcome has to be symmetric too — not as a separate piece of physics but because there's nowhere for the asymmetry to come from. I call the mechanism 'leakage': asymmetry can't leak into the answer unless it was already in the question. Noether's theorem is the famous case, but I think it's just one instance: a differentiable symmetry of the Lagrangian gives you a conserved quantity, and my principle is meant to be the more general explanatory story underneath it. Example: if you drop a perfectly round pebble into a perfectly still pond, the ripple pattern is circular. The laws don't care about direction, and the initial setup doesn't single out a direction, so a unique answer can't have a bulge; that would mean the theory broke the tie without a tiebreaker.

What I want to know is whether this really explains anything or just re-states a uniqueness proof. Where does it brake? A vertical stick balanced on its tip has a symmetric setup but falls one way — I think that's because the symmetric solution is unstable and not unique, but then maybe the uniqueness clause is doing all the work and the symmetry is just scenery. And spontaneous symmetry breaking seems like a direct counterexample; is that fatal, or does it just force me to say my principle only applies to stable unique solutions, at which point it's nearly empty? Also, have I just reinvented Curie's principle or something else in the invariance-of-laws literature under another name? And am I wrong to treat Noether's theorem as an instance of this at all, since Noether is about conserved charges rather than about whether a particular event is symmetric? I'd like a candid assessment: the two or three hardest objections, and whether any of them kills the idea rather than narrowing it. Answer in British English please.
