I recently joined a philosophy-of-religion reading group after years away from the church I was raised in, mostly to work out whether its central doctrines can be stated without obvious contradiction. Last week we got onto the Trinity, and someone ended the discussion by reciting the formula "three persons, one God" as if that settled the matter. The immediate question had been about identity: if the Father is God, the Son is God, and the Holy Spirit is God, then the transitivity and symmetry of identity seem to force the conclusion that the Father is the Son is the Holy Spirit — which no one in the room was willing to accept. The formula was offered as the way out, but nobody could say what it actually asserts.

Before the next session I want a menu of the distinct precise claims that "three persons, one God" could be making. For instance, it might mean that "is God" is a predication rather than a strict identity claim; that identity here is relative to a sortal, so the three are the same God but not the same person; that the persons are three centers of consciousness sharing one divine nature; or something else entirely. For each reading, I want to know whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or a claim with unacceptable consequences — and in particular whether it escapes both modalism on one side and tritheism on the other. Most importantly, say which readings would actually block the transitivity argument the formula was being used to answer, and which would not. I am not asking for the history of the formula or a patristic bibliography; I need the conceptual options. Please also separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and do not present one contested account as the consensus.
