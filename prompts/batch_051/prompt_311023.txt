The frame problem was originally a complaint about old-school symbolic AI: if a robot knows a bunch of facts about a kitchen and then paints the wall blue, how does it know the wall changed color but the fridge didn't, the eggs are still eggs, gravity still works, and so on? The standard story says a logic-based system would need a frame axiom for every action-fact pair and, worse, some principled way to decide which facts are relevant in the first place. Now, the systems people actually build are LLMs trained on a huge pile of human text, and they seem to just absorb what stays the same when something changes from the patterns in the data. Does the frame problem survive that shift, or was it an artifact of representing the world as explicit propositions? I want you to be precise about which part is under pressure — the bookkeeping about what changes, or the deeper question about how anything get's flagged as relevant — and then say what, if anything, of the frame problem is still left for LLM-style systems. Don't just tell me they make mistakes sometimes; tell me whether the mistakes are the old frame problem wearing a new coat, or a different problem that we should stop calling the frame problem.
