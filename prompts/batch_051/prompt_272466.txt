Medical practice in many countries treats irreversible cessation of all brain functions and irreversible cessation of circulation as two alternative tests for the same underlying fact of death. Under the first, a patient may be declared dead while a ventilator keeps the heart beating, and organs may then be taken for transplant. Under the second, death is declared after a brief interval without circulation, and organs may be removed shortly afterward, even though stopped hearts are sometimes restarted in other circumstances. The same legal practice insists that death is a single biological state and that organs may be removed only from the dead. What conception of death does this two-criteria practice presuppose, if any? Reconstruct the implicit theory of death; distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with; explain how the irreversibility required by circulatory death can be compatible with the possibility of restarting a heart; and say what would have to change in transplant practice if the governing definition were settled by whole-brain criteria alone, by higher-brain criteria, or by circulatory criteria alone.
