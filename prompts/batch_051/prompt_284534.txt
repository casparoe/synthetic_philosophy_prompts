I've been translating a short story from French into English for a small bilingual journal, and I keep reaching for the word "untranslatable" while noticing that I mean something different each time. Sometimes I mean there is no dictionary equivalent for a single word; sometimes that no arrangement of English words would preserve what the sentence does in French; sometimes that doing justice to it would require apparatus—footnotes, a glossary, an italicized foreign word—which the format will not allow. I would like a working definition of "untranslatable" that lets me say which of these situations is really untranslatability and which is something more ordinary, like paraphrase, loss, or a constraint of the project. I also suspect that the word sometimes does ethical work: it can function as an excuse for giving up, or as a judgment that a source culture should not be casually domesticated, and I want the definition to make that visible.

Here are the cases I want the definition to handle. (1) A joke that turns on two ordinary English phrases sounding identical; a French translator can invent a different pun that gets the laugh but changes the words. (2) A novel scene in which a character switches from vous to tu, where the switch itself is the dramatic event; English's single "you" cannot mark it without adding a sentence of commentary. (3) A semantically plain quatrain whose force depends almost entirely on meter and rhyme; turned into English free verse it says the same thing and is no longer a poem. (4) The Russian contrast between goluboy and sinii, which English renders easily as "light blue" and "dark blue." (5) A legal term naming a doctrine that exists in the source legal system but not in the target one, so the translator must gloss it in a footnote. (6) The word Schadenfreude used in an English sentence as an English word; nothing is translated, but the German is carried over unchanged. (7) A sentence whose dictionary translation is straightforward but whose force depends on an allusion the target audience does not share.

My loose intuitions: (1) and (3) are genuinely untranslatable, (4) is clearly not, and (2), (5), (6), and (7) I can argue either way. I suspect the term secretly tracks two different things—a fact about the two languages and a decision about how much apparatus the translator is willing or permitted to spend—and I want the definition to say which cases belong to which.

Propose the definition, run it through all seven cases, say where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and then revise it or defend the surprising verdicts. Then tell me which of your verdicts is least secure and what argument would most likely flip it. Start with the definition itself as your first sentence—no preamble, no restating of the question—and proceed from there.
