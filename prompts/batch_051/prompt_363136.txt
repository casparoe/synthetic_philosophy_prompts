I'm a third-year PhD student in political economy, working on comparative development, and I've been deep in the institutions-vs-geography-vs-culture debate about why some countries are rich and others aren't. The thing is, everyone around me thinks in the language of causal inference—potential outcomes, DAGs, identification strategies, SUTVA (stable unit treatment value assumption), natural experiments—and I'm fluent in that stuff, but the debate as its actually written is almost never posed in those terms.

So here's what I want. Take each of the three main positions—institutions as the deep cause, geography as the deep cause, culture as the deep cause—and restate it as a causal claim in that vocabulary, as faithfully and charitably as you can. For each one, spell out what the treatment is, what the outcome is, what variation would identify the effect, what assumptions you'd need (exogeneity, controls, instruments, SUTVA, whatever), and where in a graph the other two factors would live (confounder, mediator, collider, descendant). Then give me the residue report. I've got a hunch that a lot of the disagreement evaporates once it's forced into this vocabulary—the claim that geography shaped history and the claim that institutions are endogenous seem to be doing very different kinds of work—but I also suspect something real gets lost, like path dependence, the non-exchangeability of countries, and the possibility that the question why some nations are rich isn't even well-posed causally because it never specifies a treatment. Which parts translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which have no expression in the causal-inference vocabulary at all? And then stick your neck out: is the residue mostly a sign that the original debate is less well-posed than it looks, or that the causal framework is to thin to say what the debaters are actually fighting about?
