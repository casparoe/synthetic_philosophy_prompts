I'm a novelist, and I've gotten myself into one of those situations where I have to defend, out loud, something I put in a book mostly on instinct. In my latest novel the entire plot turns on a coincidence: two strangers keep crossing paths in different cities, and the crossings turn out to matter. The book got reviewed as implausible, so I wrote an essay defending coincidence in fiction, and now I've been invited to present the thing at a festival full of data journalists and statistically-minded skeptics—the kind of people who will have the birthday paradox and the law of truly large numbers loaded and ready before I finish my first slide, and then there's a long Q&A.

The thesis I actually want to defend is roughly this. The standard debunking move—"given enough people and enough opportunities, some wild coincidences are bound to happen, so your story is meaningless"—overcorrects. Sure, the birthday paradox shows our raw surprise is miscalibrated, but it doesn't by itself show that a particular coincidence carries no evidential weight. To know whether a coincidence is evidence of something, you need to know how constrained the "hit" was in advance—how specific the prediction was before it landed. And fiction, weirdly, is one of the few places where the audience can watch the hit get specified in advance, because the reader is there while the pattern gets built. So maybe narrative isn't the enemy of coincidence-reasoning; maybe it's one of the few places we can practice doing it right.

I'm not a statistician and I'll be outgunned. Could you write my Q&A prep sheet? Give me the eight or so objections this audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage they'd do to my position, phrased the way a data journalist or a skeptic would actually put them, each with a reply that would fit in about two minutes, and a note on where my reply is weakest. And please include the question I should be praying nobody ask.
