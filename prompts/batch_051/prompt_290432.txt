I've been going back and forth on something in defamation law and I'd like help thinking it through conceptually, not a doctrinal summary. The classic story, as I understand it: defamation only covers false statements of fact, so "opinions" are protected. A reviewer saying a meal was terrible or an analyst saying "I think this company will go under" isn't defaming anyone, because those utterances don't assert facts about the world; they're subjective evaluations or predictions, not the sort of thing that can be verified true or false. The whole justification for the opinion privilege is something like: where there's no fact to check, there's nothing for a jury to decide and no claim to call false.

But now consider the stuff that actually functions as "opinions about you" online. Tenant-screening algorithms emit numbers like "likelihood of default: 71%." Reputation apps compute a "safety score" for specific named people. Fraud models flag a particular merchant as "high risk." Those outputs look like ratings or forecasts, so the old argument would seem to say they can't be defamatory. Yet the argument's key premise—that an opinion is a subjective viewpoint that can't be proven false—seems strained for these. A model's risk score isn't a subjective take; it's presented as a measurement, and a probabilistic forecast can be checked against base rates: if the system puts someone in a 71% default bucket and people in that bucket default 12% of the time, the number was arguably false in a perfectly verifiable sense. On the other hand, a forecast still isn't a claim that the person did some particular thing, so it doesn't fit the "statement of fact" mold either.

So my question is about the fate of the underlying argument, not the caselaw: does the old justification for protecting opinion-type speech survive this shift, does it need repair (and what would the repaired version say?), or does it just become inapplicable to machine-generated ratings and forecasts? I'd like you to identify exactly which premise of the argument the algorithmic-score case puts pressure on, and say whether the argument can be restored in a weaker but still meaningful form.
