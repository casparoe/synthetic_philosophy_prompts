For a course unit on forecasting and decision-making, I want a thought experiment where the outside view ends up recommending the riskier action and the inside view recommends caution — the reverse of the usual setup, where the inside view is the overconfident one pushing action and the outside view is the restraining voice. In this exercise, take the inside view to mean forecasting by building a story from the specifics of the case, and the outside view to mean forecasting from the base rate of a reference class of genuinely similar cases. Constraints: ordinary life (a job change, a medical decision, a move, that kind of thing), no science fiction, no perfect predictors, and the outside view has to use a defensible reference class — not one gerrymandered to crank out a high base rate. Both views have access to the same facts about the person and the choice; the disagreement is about how to weight the facts, not about one side knowing something the other doesn't. The risky action should be exactly the kind of thing the inside view is normally accused of pushing people into (quitting a job, moving cities, starting a business), except here the inside view is the one saying don't. After you give the scenario, show the reasoning seperately for (a) the inside view, (b) the outside view, and (c) epistemic modesty, understood as anchoring on the outside view and treating the inside story as a bias to discount rather than a reason to override; say what action each picks. Then tell me which single structural feature makes modesty come out bolder than usual, and whether removing that feature collapses the case back into the standard situation where the inside view overreaches and the outside view reels it in. Finish with the strongest objection a person who thinks modesty can only ever make you more cautious could make, and say whether it works. Keep the whole thing under 800 words.
