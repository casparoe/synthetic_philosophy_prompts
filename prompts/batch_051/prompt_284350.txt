I have been rereading Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan's "Inherent Trade-Offs in the Fair Determination of Risk Scores," and I constructed an argument from it that seems to prove too much. I can't tell which step to reject. The paper says:

"We formalize three fairness conditions that lie at the heart of these debates, and we prove that except in highly constrained special cases, there is no method that can satisfy these three conditions simultaneously. Moreover, even satisfying all three conditions approximately requires that the data lie in an approximate version of one of the constrained special cases identified by our theorem."

Now consider the following chain of reasoning.

(1) The paper's result concerns three conditions on a risk score applied across two groups: calibration, balance for the positive class, and balance for the negative class. Calibration requires that, at any given score, the fraction of people who are positive instances is the same in both groups. Balance for the positive class requires that the average score among actual positives is the same in both groups, and balance for the negative class requires the same among actual negatives.

(2) Each of these conditions is individually a plausible necessary condition for a risk score to be fair. Violating calibration means the score means something different depending on group membership. Violating balance for the positive class means the score is systematically less favorable to one group's actual positives; violating balance for the negative class is the mirror-image error. So a reasonable fairness standard should include all three.

(3) The theorem shows that, except in highly constrained special cases, no method can satisfy all three conditions simultaneously. In particular, if base rates differ across groups and the score is not perfect, at least one of the three conditions must fail. Moreover, this is not a limitation of current engineering: even approximate satisfaction of all three requires the data to be near one of those special cases.

(4) In almost every real application—bail, hiring, lending, medical screening—base rates differ across groups and the predictors are imperfect. Therefore no real algorithmic risk assessment can be fair in the sense of (2).

(5) But that conclusion is too strong. If the theorem showed only that our current methods are unfair, that would be manageable; we would look for better methods. What it seems to show, on this reading, is that the demand itself is unsatisfiable. Anyone who accepts (2) must either conclude that almost all real-world risk assessment is necessarily unfair or admit that their fairness standard imposes inconsistent requirements.

(6) Worse, by parity of reasoning the same conclusion applies to human judges, doctors, loan officers, and parole boards. Whatever implicit risk score a human uses is subject to the same calibration/balance trade-off, so if the argument is sound, human risk assessment is also necessarily unfair whenever base rates differ and prediction is imperfect. That seems like a reductio of treating the three conditions as necessary conditions—unless one is prepared to bite the conclusion that almost all high-stakes decisions under uncertainty are unfair.

Where does this argument go wrong? I want to keep something like (2), or at least keep the idea that each of the three conditions tracks a real fairness intuition, but I worry that (3) and (4) force me to give it up. We can accept the theorem; the question is what it should do to our confidence in the individual conditions. Is the right move to say that each condition is a desideratum rather than a necessary condition, and why would that distinction help? Or is the flaw earlier—for example, in the move from "no score can satisfy all three" in (3) to "no score can be fair" in (4)?
