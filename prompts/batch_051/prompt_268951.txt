I am preparing a session for my advanced undergraduate seminar on Rousseau's critique of the arts, and I would like your help planning the explanation rather than delivering it. The session is on the *Letter to M. d'Alembert on the Theatre*, which the students have not yet read in full, but they have read the *First Discourse* and have a rough sense of Rousseau's claim that the arts corrupt virtue. My worry is that they will come in with a flattened picture of Rousseau as a simple primitivist who opposes theater because it is fictional or because actors are dissolute. I want to dislodge that picture early by centering the session on what I take to be the deeper structure of his argument: that the theater does not merely fail to improve morals but necessarily reinforces whatever moral dispositions the audience already brings, because the playwright must please the audience to succeed, and that this makes theatrical mimesis especially dangerous in a republic that depends on the integrity of citizens rather than on the amusement of subjects.

My plan is to anchor the discussion in the section where Rousseau reads Molière's *Misanthrope* through the character of Alceste. As I recall the passage, Rousseau's point is that Molière makes the virtuous man — the one who speaks plainly and refuses social dissimulation — appear ridiculous, while the accommodating and polished friend appears reasonable. This, Rousseau argues, teaches spectators to laugh at honesty and to identify virtue itself with a kind of social maladjustment. I want the students to see that this is not an objection to an isolated play but an illustration of a general mechanism: the theater's moral effect is governed by the audience's taste, and the audience's taste is not a reliable guide to virtue.

Please give me a plan for a seventy-five-minute class that builds to this reading. I would like the following: the sequence in which the main ideas must be introduced, with some indication of what depends on what; the one concrete example I should carry through the whole session, and what it must have and lack if it is to do this work; the misconceptions about Rousseau's view that students in this situation are most likely to form or bring in, and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off; and a closing question I can ask to check whether they have grasped the difference between "theater is bad because it tells lies" and the subtler claim about audience taste, rather than merely feeling scandalized by Rousseau. Please also indicate what I should openly refuse to explain at this level. In your answer, separate what is settled in the scholarly treatment of this material from what is genuinely contested, and be careful not to present a contested reading as the consensus.
