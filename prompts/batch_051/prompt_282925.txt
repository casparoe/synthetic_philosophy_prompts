I'm running a small reading group on Buddhist epistemology and we're ending with Dharmakīrti's argument that every real thing is momentary. I wanted to close with a poem and thought it would be easy, but my attempts keep turning into either a lecture with line breaks or a vague meditation on impermanence. So I'd like you to write it. The idea the poem should carry is this: perception, for Dharmakīrti, only ever touches a bare unique particular right now; the enduring things we live among -- a pot, a mountain, a person -- are put together by the conceptual mind, because a genuinely permanent thing couldn't cause anything. If it caused its effect at one time and not another, something in it would have to change to explain the difference, and that change would already be the end of its permanence. So the real pot is a rapid sequence of momentary causal particulars, and the stable pot is a useful fiction the mind projects over them.

I'd like a sonnet where the octave gives the naive picture: a clay pot sitting unchanged on a shelf through a hot summer, dust gathering, no one watching. Then the volta should be the inference -- around the turn, the poem should realize that under the apparently stable surface there is only a string of momentary particulars, each one doing its tiny bit of causal work and perishing. The closing couplet should say plainly what has to be given up: the enduring substance behind the appearances. Don't just namedrop Dharmakīrti; someone who works on his epistemology should be able to recognize the distinction between perception of a unique particular and the conceptual universal 'pot', and the argument that permanence is incompatible with causal efficacy. But it should also hold up as a poem for a general reader who has never heard of him.
