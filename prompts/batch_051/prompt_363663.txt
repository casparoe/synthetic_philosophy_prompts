I'm a PhD student in political science working on democratic theory, and I've been arguing with a colleague about how far Arrow's impossibility theorem should govern the design of modern participatory institutions. The theorem, as we both learned it, is stated for ordinal inputs: voters submit weak orders over alternatives, and no social welfare function can satisfy unrestricted domain, Pareto, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship all at once. But the institutions I actually study—participatory budgeting, quadratic voting, continuous approval panels—elicit far richer information than ordinal rankings. Voters allocate scarce budgets, state intensities, and reveal tradeoffs that look cardinal and sometimes interpersonally comparable. My colleague's line is that Arrow's theorem therefore doesn't apply to these mechanisms at all. He claims the impossibility was an artifact of impoverished ordinal input, and that once you let people express "how much" rather than just "which," you can satisfy the spirit of all the Arrow conditions simultaneously.

I'm suspicious of this. It seems to me he may be sliding between showing that the theorem's formalism no longer applies literally and showing that the underlying impossibility has actually been dissolved; and some of what he says may only push the problem into the model of utility that the mechanism is implicitly assuming. I want to know whether Arrow's impossibility result survives the shift from ordinal rankings to richer elicitation mechanisms. If it does survive, which premise—or natural analogue of which premise—still does the real work, and what does a repaired impossibility theorem for cardinal or interpersonally comparable inputs look like? If it doesn't survive, tell me exactly which Arrow condition must be relaxed or reinterpreted, and what weaker but still important claim remains true about aggregating richer preference data.
