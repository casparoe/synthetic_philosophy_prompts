I am compiling structurally distinct objections to the handicap principle—the idea, usually credited to Zahavi, that a signal can be made reliable by its cost, because only a genuinely high-quality individual can afford the waste and the cost therefore becomes evidence of quality (the peacock's tail being the standard illustration). So far I have these four:

1. The Fisherian-runaway objection. A costly ornament need not encode information about quality at all; trait and preference can coevolve through positive feedback until survival costs brake the process, leaving a display that is the expensive by-product of an arbitrary fashion rather than an honest advertisement.

2. The index objection. Reliability can be secured by a physical or developmental constraint linking signal form to the signaled property—a deep call tied to a large body—rather than by strategic cost. Where such a tie exists, the signal is an index and cost does no explanatory work.

3. The cost-accounting objection. The apparent waste may be a bookkeeping artifact. Separate the efficacy costs of making a signal perceivable from the strategic costs added only to prove quality, and many displays stop looking like handicaps and start looking like efficient investments in being seen or heard.

4. The lek-paradox objection. If an ornament honestly advertises heritable quality, sustained choice of the best signalers over evolutionary time should drain the additive genetic variance in quality, shrinking the very thing the ornament purports to reveal; the good-genes version of the handicap principle thereby undermines its own informational basis.

I suspect I am missing an objection or alternative explanation whose structure differs from all four. Give me one that is not on this list and not a rewording, special case, or combination of the ones above, and say what it adds that these four do not. Then tell me honestly whether it is a good objection, novelty aside.
