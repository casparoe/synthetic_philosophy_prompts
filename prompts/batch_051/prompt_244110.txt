I'm in a social science methods course and got into an argument with a friend over whether you can explain things like the 2008 crash or persistent discrimination by appealing to "structures" or "systems" rather than to individual people. My friend, who's an econ major, summed up the methodological individualist position for me like this:

"At bottom, social facts are just aggregates of individual actions. When someone says an institution or a structre caused something, that's either loose shorthand for many individuals making choices given their beliefs and incentives, or it's spooky nonsense. There are no social forces above or between people that do any causing. So a macro-level explanation is only a genuine explanation if you can translate it into microfoundations — a story about which individuals did what and why. If you can't perform that translation, you haven't explained anything; you've just relabled it."

I said that sounded right, but later a professor told me it was a "loaded" version of the view and refused to say more. I genuinly can't tell which parts are the view and which parts are the econ-major version of it. Could you go through it claim by claim and tell me what a careful methodological individualist would accept, what they'd distance themselves from, and what a holist could grant without giving up on structural explanation? In particular: does the microfoundations requirement actually follow from the no-spooky-forces claim, and is "aggregates" doing more work than the individualist needs it to?
