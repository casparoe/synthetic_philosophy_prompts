I'm a philosophy student taking a seminar on meaning and communication, and we've spent the last couple weeks on Grice: the cooperative principle, the maxims, and especially his argument that what a speaker means by an utterance is constituted by a specific kind of communicative intention — roughly, the speaker intends to produce a certain effect in the hearer, and intends that the hearer recognize that very intention. The whole framework is built for people talking to people. But I keep noticing that I, and basically everyone I know, now routinely read implicatures off LLM outputs. When an assistant gives a conspicuously evasive answer to a question, I don't just register the literal content — I take it to implicate that the system wont go there. If it opens by saying "That's an interesting question," I read that as doing a lot of conversational work beyond reporting that my question is interesting to it. And yet there is, presumably, no speaker intention behind any of this in the Gricean sense. So here's what I want to understand: does Grice's argument about speaker meaning survive large language models, or does the LLM case kill it, or does it just need repair?

I'm not asking whether LLMs are conscious, have beliefs, or should count as speakers in some folk-psychological sense; I want to know what happens to the Gricean argument itself. Which premise is actually under pressure, and is there a repaired version that says something weaker but still true — for instance by locating the relevant intentions in the user, in the system's designers, or in the training process, or by relaxing the intention requirement while keeping the implicature machinery?
