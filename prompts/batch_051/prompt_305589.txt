In causal discovery you look at patterns of conditional independence and try to pick a directed acyclic graph whose structure explains them. The standard move is to demand the graph be minimal: no extra arrows beyond what's needed to satisfy the Markov condition (each variable being independent of its non-descendants given its parents). Then you add faithfulness: you assume the only independencies in the data are the ones the graph itself implies, meaning no causal pathways exactly or near-exactly cancel, no fine-tuning. Under faithfulness the minimal Markov graph is unique up to Markov equivalence, and people then treat it as tracking the real causal structure.

What I want: your actual credence that faithfulness is approximately true, in the way would be needed for that inference to work, for the messy social systems a city-scale model deals with. By 'approximately true' I mean outright violations are rare enough, and near-violations (paths that almost cancel but not quite, so tests miss them) are gentle enough, that a minimal graph fit to finite data would still get the arrows right more often than not.

Decompose it. At least these pieces, with a rough probability for each and a one-line justification:

(a) The chance that real social systems routinely involve confounding plus offsetting mechanisms, so that fine-tuning or path-canceling is the norm rather than the exception.

(b) Whether the usual defense, that unfaithful parameter values are measure-zero in some natural parameter space, actually transfers to the parametric choices people trust in social modeling, or whether measure-zero just means rare in theory and common in practice.

(c) How much of the apparent success of these methods in practice is really carried by faithfulness, versus by temporal ordering, known interventions, or the analyst quietly forcing the graph to be sparse.

(d) Whether minimality is doing real causal work or is basically a penalty for overfitting dressed up as metaphysics.
