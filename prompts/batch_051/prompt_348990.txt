I keep coming back to Bell's theorem because, as a software engineer, the way I think about it is basically refactoring: Bell takes a few assumptions that look obviously true about how particles could work locally, and shows that if you keep all of them, you can't pass the tests that quantum mechanics actually passes. So I'm trying to get clearer on exactly which assumption breaks when you look at the math.

In Bell's 1964 paper *On the Einstein Podolsky Rosen Paradox*, the part I want to zoom in on is where he lays out what locality requires. From the PDF I have, he writes:

"The result of a measurement on one system be unaffected by operations on a distant system with which it has interacted in the past."

I don't want to ask about the whole theorem. What I want to understand is narrower: this sentence is doing a lot of work, because it's not obviously the same thing as "no signal travels faster than light." Bell seems to be saying something more specifically about measurement *results*, not about whether Alice can send Bob a message. And the phrase "with which it has interacted in the past" seems to be doing even more work, because it's picking out exactly the case where the two systems are correlated from the start.

Here's the thing I can't quite get straight. Suppose I write a simulation with two particles whose hidden variables are assigned at creation. Alice's detector chooses a setting, and Bob's detector chooses a setting. For Bell's inequality to be violated, something has to give. The usual story is that the violation means either the hidden variables don't exist, or Bob's outcome depends on Alice's setting (or vice versa), or the statistics are somehow not governed by a single joint probability distribution over all four possible measurement outcomes. That last option feels to me like a loophole in how I'm thinking about simulation, not a real physical option. If I can't write down a joint distribution over all four outcomes for a given pair of settings, then my simulation doesn't have a well-defined probability space. But maybe that's exactly the point, and quantum mechanics just doesn't give you that joint distribution.

So my question is: what does Bell's sentence above actually commit you to, formally? If I want to violate the inequality while keeping the idea that measurement on one system is unaffected by operations on the distant system, what exactly do I have to give up instead? Is it a well-defined joint probability distribution over the four possible outcomes? Is it something about the order in which the hidden variables and the measurement settings combine? I'm especially interested in whether Bell's word "result" is doing something different from "statistics of results," because I can imagine a view where Bob's individual results don't depend on Alice's setting, but the *correlations* between their results can't be explained without some kind of global feature of the pair. I don't know if that's coherent.

Please answer at a level that doesn't assume I already know the philosophical literature, but don't oversimplify the formal structure. And please be explicit about how confident you are in the claims you make, and flag the one point you're least sure about.
