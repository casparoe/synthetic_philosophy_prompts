I have been leading a reading group on the modern Stoic revival, and for the last two sessions we have been stuck on the distinction Epictetus draws near the beginning of the Enchiridion between what is up to us and what is not — the so-called dichotomy of control. The group has become somewhat impatient with the therapeutic version of the doctrine, which says simply that you should stop worrying about what you cannot change. Instead we have been collecting objections to the doctrine as a piece of philosophy, that is, to the claim that there is a sharp, normatively important line between an inner sphere of judgment, desire, impulse, and aversion, and an outer sphere of body, property, reputation, and office. So far I have five objections, and I think they are the obvious ones:

First, the boundary problem: the Stoics count judgments, desires, and impulses as up to us, but much of modern psychology suggests these arise largely outside voluntary control, so the inner sphere may be empty or much smaller than advertised. Second, the quietism objection: if external outcomes are strictly indifferent, then resisting injustice, protecting health, and building relationships lose their normative grip, and the doctrine seems to recommend withdrawal from the very things a virtuous life should engage. Third, the preferred-indifferents tension: the doctrine is usually combined with talk of preferred and dispreferred indifferents and of selecting among them in accordance with nature, but this reintroduces a ground of caring about externals that the strict dichotomy had excluded, so either the dichotomy does no work or it stands in tension with the discipline of action. Fourth, the post-hoc rationalization objection: practitioners can use the distinction to assign any unwelcome outcome to the not-up-to-us side after the fact, thereby insulating themselves from accountability. Fifth, the self-undermining objection: the doctrine itself is a judgment about where value lies, so if it is true, then coming to accept it must be an act of assent that is up to us, but the doctrine also treats most belief fixation as caused by impressions rather than voluntary control, leaving it unclear how one adopts the doctrine as an act of reason.

I want an objection that is not in this list and not a variant of anything on it — so not a restatement of the boundary worry, not a version of the quietism worry, and so on. It should cut against the dichotomy in a different way, whether from psychology, ethics, politics, epistemology, or somewhere else. Then tell me honestly whether it is any good. In particular I want to know whether the new objection damages the ancient Stoic doctrine itself, or only the popular modern adaptation that treats the dichotomy as a tool for reducing anxiety.
