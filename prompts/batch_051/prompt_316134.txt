I'm putting together a unit on collective decision-making and want to spend one class on Sidney Lumet's 12 Angry Men (1957) as a way into the theory of information cascades and rational herding. The idea I have in mind is this: when people decide in sequence and can observe the choices of those who went before them, each later decision-maker may rationally treat earlier choices as evidence about what earlier individuals privately observed, and can therefore rationally follow the crowd even when their own private information points the other way. The result is a cascade that can be both self-reinforcing and fragile.

I would like a reading of the jury's deliberations through that lens, not a plot summary. Where does the film genuinely dramatize an information cascade, and where does it instead show conformity, social pressure, intimidation, or ordinary persuasion by argument? How should the early mechanics be read—the initial public vote that isolates a single holdout, the later secret ballot, and the subsequent one-by-one shifts—in terms of private signals, public observation, and the unanimity requirement? Is the holdout best understood as a cascade-breaker with a strong private signal, as someone who simply refuses to let the group's apparent information swamp his own, or as something the formal models have no good category for? And does the ending, in which the entire jury reverses itself, show that cascades are fragile and that private information eventually gets out, or does the film stack the deck in a way a formal model would flag as misleading?

I want the reading to be faithful in both directions: do not reduce the film to an illustration of the theory, and do not stretch the cascade idea to cover scenes better explained by group psychology, status effects, or the personalities in the room. Where the film complicates or resists the cascade reading, say so. It would help to end with one thing the film shows about real deliberation that the abstract models miss.
