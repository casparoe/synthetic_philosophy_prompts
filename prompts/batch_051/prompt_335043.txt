I am a retired natural scientist who has recently begun reading philosophy, and I have been working through transcendental arguments. I would be grateful for a careful judgement on the following reconstruction, which I keep producing but find difficult to assess.

(1) Any experience I can reflect on is already organised by some concepts or other; there is no experience of a wholly unconceptualised given.
(2) The concepts employed in an experience form a scheme, because a concept has its content only through its relations of contrast and inference to other concepts.
(3) Therefore, for every possible experience, some conceptual scheme or other organises it.
(4) A feature possessed by every possible experience is a transcendental condition of experience.
(5) By (3) and (4), there is a single conceptual scheme that organises every possible experience, and possession of that scheme is a transcendental condition of experience.
(6) Hence any being capable of experience must share, in broad outline, one and the same conceptual scheme; the idea of a radically alternative scheme is incoherent rather than merely unfamiliar.

Each step seems individually plausible, yet the conclusion appears stronger than the premises warrant. Is the argument valid? If so, say why; if not, identify exactly which step fails, explain the error, and state how the argument would need to be revised to be structurally correct. Please answer in British English.
