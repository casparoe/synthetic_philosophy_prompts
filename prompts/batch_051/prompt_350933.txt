A prominent behavioral approach to treating depression instructs therapists to keep improved mood from becoming the patient's direct target. A patient who says she just wants to feel happy is redirected toward concrete activities—walking, volunteering, reconnecting with friends—chosen for their own sake or their meaning rather than for the mood they are expected to produce; mood is treated as a byproduct of a life aimed at other things. Yet the same treatment plans measure success chiefly by before-and-after scores on mood questionnaires, and patients are typically discharged when those scores rise. What theory of happiness, and of the relation between aiming at happiness and attaining it, does this practice presuppose? Does it strictly presuppose something like the paradox of hedonism—that felt well-being can be secured only by aiming at something else—or does it merely fit that view while remaining compatible with the claim that happiness can be pursued directly? And is the instruction not to target mood consistent with the use of mood scales as the primary outcome, or do these two elements presuppose different theories of happiness?
