Quick setup: i'm an ML engineer and the vocabulary I actually think in is model evaluation stuff — precision, recall, F1, confusion matrices, calibration, proper scoring rules like Brier and log loss, generalization error, train/test distribution shift, all that. Last week I got into a bar argument with a friend who did philosophy of science in grad school, and he claimed my evaluation toolkit already contains the solution to a problem philosophers got stuck on. I want someone to run that translation honestly and tell me what survives.

As I understand it, the problem is verisimilitude, or truthlikeness. Popper noticed that our best scientific theories are almost all strictly false somewhere, but we still want to say Newtonian mechanics is closer to the truth than Aristotelian physics, or that general relativity is closer than a bare geocentric model, rather than resigning ourselves to the idea that every false theory is false full stop. So he tried to define closeness to the truth out of truth content and falsity content — roughly, a theory is closer to truth to the extent that it entails more true claims and fewer false claims. That blew up: in the 70s people showed that on definitions of that shape no false theory ever comes out strictly closer to the truth than another false theory, and there is a nastier result that how similar two theories look depends on which predicates you treat as primitive, like a distance metric that changes when you relabel your coordinate axes. Later people tried to fix it by treating truthlikeness as graded similarity between worlds or structures in logical space instead of a set-inclusion thing.

Restate this whole program — the motivation, the content-based attempt, the impossibility results, and the similarity-based fixes — in my vocabulary. So: treat a theory as a classifier or probabilistic model over a hypothesis space, its assertions as predictions, the actual world as the ground-truth labels, and verisimilitude as some scoring rule or ranking over models that are all wrong but wrong to different degrees. Map it systematically and honestly: is precision a version of truth content, is recall coverage of the truth, are proper scoring rules just graded truthlikeness, and so on. Then give me the residue report. What translated cleanly, what you had to fudge, and what has no counterpart in ML evaluation at all. And I want the blunt diagnosis, because my friend's smugness is on the line: when something doesn't survive the translation, is that because ML evaluation vocabulary is missing something real, or because the philosophers' problem is partly an artifact of thinking of theories as sets of sentences instead of graded predictive machinery?
