The exam question was built around a passage from Paul Crutzen and Eugene Stoermer’s 2000 article in the IGBP Newsletter, which is often credited with introducing the term Anthropocene. The passage, as I paraphrased it for the student, states that atmospheric concentrations of carbon dioxide and methane begin a pronounced rise within ice-core records in the second half of the eighteenth century, and proposes the late eighteenth century as a defensible start for a distinct human-dominated geological epoch.

The question I set was:

Explain what Crutzen and Stoermer are doing when they propose the late eighteenth century as the start of the Anthropocene, and then evaluate whether their proposed start date should be preferred over other start dates that have been put forward since. In your answer, keep clear the difference between the Anthropocene as an informal historical period and the Anthropocene as a formal geological unit.

The student's answer was:

Crutzen and Stoermer are arguing that we have already left the Holocene and entered a new human-dominated epoch. They pick the late eighteenth century because ice-core records show carbon dioxide and methane rising around then, which they tie to the Industrial Revolution. That seems right because the Industrial Revolution is the obvious economic and technological break. Since then, the Anthropocene Working Group has confirmed Crutzen and Stoermer's date and has selected the Industrial Revolution as the golden spike, so the central issue is settled. The only question left is whether the Anthropocene should be called an epoch or a stage within the Holocene, but that is mostly a matter of taxonomic preference rather than a substantive disagreement about the history.
