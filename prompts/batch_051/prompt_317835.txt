I am a high school student, and I am preparing a submission for my school's science journal. I would be grateful if you could write me a short satirical piece of around 650 words, in the form of the Discussion section of a student laboratory report for a fictional experiment on rats in a maze. In the fictional experiment, a rat is carried through a maze on several consecutive days without any reward; on the final day it is placed at the maze entrance with food at the exit for the first time, and it immediately takes a novel shortcut, including a segment of the maze it had never walked. The Discussion should be written by an invented student author who treats Morgan's canon — the rule that an animal's behavior should not be attributed to a higher psychological process when a lower one is available — as an absolute method from which he never deviates. This author allows himself only reflexes, instincts, stimulus–response associations, reinforcement history, and similar lower mechanisms, and forbids goals, expectations, representations, choices, knowledge, and anything like a cognitive map. The satire should arise from what this commitment does to the explanation: each time one lower-level account fails to handle a detail of the result (the novel shortcut, the absence of prior reward during the days of being carried, the rat's pause at a choice point), the author patches it, and the patches grow more and more elaborate, until the supposedly parsimonious explanation is plainly more complicated than the explanation the canon forbade. Occasional slips in his own wording — writing that the rat chose a turn and then hastily correcting himself — are welcome as long as they remain in character. Please keep the author's voice straight-faced and scientific throughout, and do not have him state the criticism directly. The target is the practice of treating Morgan's canon as a demand to prefer any sub-cognitive story over a simpler cognitive one, so that a principle meant to protect parsimony ends up requiring baroque contortions. No references or citations are needed.
