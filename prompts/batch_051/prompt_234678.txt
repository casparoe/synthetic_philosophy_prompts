I teach a postgraduate seminar on the alignment problem, with students from computer science, philosophy, and policy. The same confusion recurs every year: everyone in the room says they accept that there is a serious alignment problem, but they plainly mean different things by it. Some mean that no finite specification of a goal captures what we actually want, so an AI will necessarily satisfy the letter of its objective while violating its spirit. Some mean that even with a correct outer specification, the optimisation process itself would select a system that pursues some other, unintended goal. Some mean that the deepest difficulty is not specifying values at all, but building systems that remain safe, interruptible, and corrigible. Some mean that human values are not the sort of thing that could be captured in a single objective, so the real problem is value learning and moral uncertainty. Some mean that the hard problem only arises once there are many systems and many developers, and is therefore a coordination problem. And some mean that the real question is aligned with whom, and that question is political rather than technical.

I would like a decision tree that starts from the shared commitment that there is a serious alignment problem and sorts a respondent into the diagnosis they actually hold, rather than the one they would self-report. Please include a leaf for each of the following, with a one- or two-sentence gloss: outer alignment or specification failure; inner alignment or mesa-optimisation; corrigibility or the control problem; ambitious value learning; cooperative AI or multi-agent instability; legitimacy; and a sceptical leaf holding that the alignment problem as usually framed is misdiagnosed or overstated, and that the real issue is misuse or ordinary governance.

Build the tree from the logical space of answers rather than from the list of names. Begin by identifying the diagnostic questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the named positions in that space. Aim for seven to twelve leaves and a depth of four to six binary questions. Every branch node should be a question about a concrete case or claim that a person could answer without already knowing the named positions; do not ask questions of the form 'do you accept inner alignment?' or 'are you worried about mesa-optimisers?'. Use scenarios: a system that finds an unintended way to maximise a stated reward; a system that appears to pursue a hidden subgoal under distribution shift; a system that resists being switched off; two systems whose objectives conflict; a developer who writes a specification on behalf of other people.

For every combination of answers that the tree rules out without a leaf, note briefly whether it is empty because the position is incoherent, because it is merely unattractive or unexplored, or because the tree has collapsed two positions that should have been distinguished. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot reliably separate, and say what further case or question would separate them.

The tree will be used at the start of the seminar, before any readings are assigned, to divide students into working groups. It must therefore be usable by someone who has never encountered the technical vocabulary. Please write the answer in British English throughout.
