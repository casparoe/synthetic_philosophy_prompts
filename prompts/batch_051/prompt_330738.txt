I serve on a standing scientific advisory committee that decides whether simulation models may be used in environmental and economic policy work. We keep written decisions, and we are now asked to rule on a seventh model. Our committee cares about consistency with its own record, so any decision should be justified against our earlier rulings.

Our precedents are these.

1. Fisheries stock assessment. A model assumed that fish are uniformly distributed across the management area and that schools do not shift in response to fishing pressure, both claims known to be false. Its predictions of the next year's landed catch were within four percent of the observed catch in nine of ten years. We approved it.

2. Land-use change. A model assumed full-information, profit-maximizing landowners. It retrodicted historical land conversion accurately but predicted conversion patterns poorly in the two years after construction. We rejected it, and the decision stated that the ground was failed out-of-sample prediction rather than the realism of the assumption.

3. Pharmacokinetics. A model represented the body as a single well-stirred compartment in which a drug distributes instantly and uniformly, an idealization known to be false. It predicted measured plasma concentrations across three drugs and two species with error under ten percent. We approved it as a predictive instrument.

4. Crop yield. A model used measured, field-scale parameters and contained no idealization our reviewers flagged. Its forecasts were no better than predicting the historical mean yield. We rejected it, noting that an unvalidated realistic model is not more useful than a validated idealization.

5. Traffic flow. A model assumed identical drivers with complete knowledge of network conditions. It reproduced the benchmark dataset on which it was calibrated but had no out-of-sample testing. We sent it back and required out-of-sample validation before any approval.

6. Macro-finance. A model with a simplified banking sector predicted credit aggregates well within its calibration period and in the following two years. We approved it, and our decision described the sector as if credit were frictionlessly intermediated, but we did not examine whether the model's results depended on that simplification.

The new case is a microsimulation model of household retirement saving. It assumes that households re-optimize their portfolios every quarter with full knowledge of future tax rules and longevity risk, an assumption the applicants concede is contradicted by survey evidence about how households actually form expectations. The model predicts aggregate saving rates and take-up of tax-deferred accounts out-of-sample across three countries and two policy regimes better than all competing models. But the applicants' own companion paper shows that when the full-information assumption is replaced with the survey-elicited expectation rules, the model's headline result — that tax-deferred accounts raise total private saving rather than reshuffling it — disappears and reverses sign. The central policy conclusion is therefore carried entirely by the idealization.

Should we approve the model, reject it, or approve it with restrictions, if our aim is to be consistent with our own record? Identify the closest precedent and defend that analogy. Then state what principle about unrealistic assumptions we would be adopting if we approve, and what principle we would be adopting if we reject or restrict. If you judge that our precedents underdetermine the choice, say what further case or audit would settle which principle we actually hold.
