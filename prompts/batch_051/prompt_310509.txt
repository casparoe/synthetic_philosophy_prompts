A friend and I recently spent a long evening arguing about whether a putatively modular cognitive capacity—we kept moving between face perception and syntactic parsing—really is modular in Fodor's sense, or whether 'module' is just a flattering name for a process that is fast and specialized but otherwise governed by ordinary central cognition. I left the argument unsure how much of the dispute is empirical and how much is a standoff about what would even count as evidence, and I want to work through that. Take one reasonably well-studied candidate capacity, state the modularity claim for it precisely, and distinguish the core Fodorian marks—domain specificity, informational encapsulation, mandatory or automatic operation, speed, fixed neural architecture, and characteristic breakdown patterns—from the weaker claim that the capacity is merely specialized or dissociable. Then propose a concrete battery of tests (behavioral, psychophysical, lesion or double-dissociation, developmental, or imaging) with at least one aimed directly at informational encapsulation. For each test, specify the outcome that would favor a robustly modular reading and the outcome that would favor the deflationary reading. Finally, and this is where my friend and I deadlocked: say exactly what a positive result would and would not show. If a system passes your encapsulation test, does that show it cannot access central information, or only that it does not do so under the conditions you tested? Could a fast, domain-specific but entirely non-encapsulated process pass the same battery? Is the residue that no operationalization can reach a temporary limit of method, or a reason to suspect that 'module' is more a theoretical convenience than a discoverable natural kind? End with your own assessment of which reading the available evidence, as you understand it, best supports.
