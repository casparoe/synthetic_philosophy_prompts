I retired out of statistical mechanics a few years back and only recently started reading decision theory properly, so forgive me if I've got the language wrong. What's been eating at me is a clash between two things I both find compelling.

One is expected utility theory. Its the kind of theory I used to admire in physics: a few axioms, a complete ordering over gambles, a rule that always tells you what to do—weight outcomes by their probabilities and utilities, pick the biggest. No hand-waving, nothing arbitrary.

The other is a physical intuition I can't shake. The ergodicity-economics people like a coin toss where heads multiplies your wealth by 1.5 and tails by 0.6. The expected multiplier is 1.05, so the ensemble—think of a thousand parallel copies of you all flipping independently—gets richer by 5% a toss. But a single one of those copies almost surely gets poorer over time: the time-average growth rate is exp(0.5 ln 1.5 + 0.5 ln 0.6) ≈ 0.949, a decay of about 5% a toss. Both numbers are correct averages; they're just taken over different measures. And I'd regard it as irrational, not merely risk-averse, to keep betting a fixed fraction of my actual retirement money on that coin, toss after toss, just because each toss has positive expectation. I don't live a thousand parallel lives; I move along one trajectory. I know the textbook comeback is that a concave utility function would make me reject the bet, but that feels like it just relocates the puzzle: expected utility is still an average over parallel lives, and the log curvature that happens to fix this example looks an awful lot like the time-average growth rate sneaking in through the back door.

So I don't want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes—for me to conclude it's a salience illusion or a confusion of one-shot bets with repeated betting—and what it would take for the theory to be the thing that goes, for expected utility to be the wrong tool for an agent embedded in a single history. And is there a reading on which both survive: they're answering different questions, or expected utility should be rebuilt over time-average growth rates rather than ensemble means? If so, which reading is honest and which is just terminological padding? Give me the machinery for telling those apart, not just a verdict.
