Say a rich country with a generous welfare state sets up this rule: anyone can enter and work legally from day one and pays the same taxes as citizens, but means-tested benefits—income support, housing assistance, non-emergency healthcare—only become available once you've paid payroll tax in five of the last seven years. Instead of a cliff, each year of contributions vests one fifth of a 'welfare credit,' so three years buys 60% of a citizen's entitlement; children born in the country are covered from birth and acute emergency care is free for everyone. Run it as machinery, not as a moral view of who deserves what. Who actually shows up, and who leaves when work dries up? What do employers and landlords do with the gap between being legal to work and being welfare-eligible? Does the rule spare the welfare state's budget, or does it just move the cost into wages, overcrowding, and emergency rooms? Where does it leak, and does the political coalition for it survive it's first recession? I want the failure modes and whether they're fixable, not a verdict on open borders.
