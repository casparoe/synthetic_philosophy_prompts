In the course I'm taking on Victorian literature and the epistemology of the supernatural, we've been reading Henry James's The Turn of the Screw alongside Henry Sidgwick's approach to psychical research. As I understand Sidgwick, he argued that reports of apparitions, hauntings, and messages from the dead deserve serious investigation but must be tested against the standing alternatives of fraud, delusion, coincidence, and malobservation, and that the evidence available in his lifetime still fell short of establishing survival after death. I'd like to read the novella through that lens. If a Sidgwickian investigator treated the governess's manuscript as a case report, how would he assess it, and which details would count for or against her reliability? What does the story show about the gap between first-person conviction and what a third party can justifiably believe on testimony? And where does the novella resist the Sidgwickian framework: does its ambiguity ultimately vindicate the demand for independent corroboration, or does it suggest that such experiences have a character that evidential scrutiny cannot capture without distortion? I want a reading that is faithful to both the novel's deliberate ambiguity and Sidgwick's epistemic standards, including a frank account of where the two pull apart.
