I'm preparing a session for a philosophy-of-science reading group on whether Stephen Wolfram's A New Kind of Science deserves to be called science, and I keep coming back to the Principle of Computational Equivalence. As I understand it, PCE claims that once a system crosses a very low threshold of complexity, essentially everything is computationally equivalent: nearly any nontrivial simple program is capable of universal computation, and the apparent differences between a cellular automaton, a weather system, and a mind are differences in encoding and run-time rather than in underlying computational power. A physicist friend insists this is unfalsifiable — whatever we find, Wolfram can reply that the system wasn't simple enough or wasn't run long enough — and that it is therefore closer to a worldview than a hypothesis. I want to know how much pushback is actually available.

Suppose we treated PCE as a genuinely testable claim about the distribution of computational power in the space of simple programs. Please design a concrete test: what space of systems would you sample (elementary cellular automata, a broader class of simple programs, something else), what property would you measure or prove for each sampled system, and what pattern of results would count as supporting PCE versus counting against it? The interesting case seems to be the messy middle: PCE predicts there should be no robust class of systems that generate complex-looking behavior while being provably less than universal. What would it take to find such a class, and what would finding one settle? Then spell out what a positive result would and would not establish — in particular, whether it would license any conclusions about natural systems like brains or weather, or only about the corner of rule space we happened to survey. Finally, tell me which part of the principle, if any, no computational survey could ever touch, and whether that residue is best described as a scientific hypothesis, a methodological bet, or something else.
