I've been stuck on generics lately—the kind of sentence like "ducks lay eggs" or "mosquitoes carry malaria" that's true even though it's false of plenty of members of the kind. What's been bugging me is that the exceptions seem to do completely different jobs in different cases. In "ducks lay eggs," the exceptions (males, ducklings, old females) are just noise; the sentence works by pointing at what the kind is sort of built to do, and you're allowed to quietly bracket all the non-layers. In "mosquitoes carry malaria," the exceptions are the whole point: its true and useful precisely because only a tiny sliver of mosquitoes transmit it, and if every mosquito did, it would stop being informative as a warning. So I've started calling these load-bearing exceptions and inert exceptions. A load-bearing exception is one where the generic's utility comes from the fact that the property is rare-but-important; an inert exception is one the generic has to explain away or exclude to stay true.

My half-baked claim is that the two kinds of exception track two different licensing conditions. Inert-exception generics require the property to be characteristic of the kind—tied to what makes the kind the kind, like the reproductive setup for ducks. Load-bearing-exception generics require the property to be striking or high-stakes, and then prevalence barely matters. I can't tell if this is a real distinction or just me rederiving something obvious, and I also can't tell where it breaks: are there generics where the same exception is both load-bearing and inert, or where you can gerrymander what counts as "characteristic of the kind" until any exception looks inert? Give me the three strongest objections to using this distinction as an account of what makes generics true, say whether any of them is fatal, and whether my two licensing conditions are doing real work or just restating the data. I'd rather you be candid than nice.
