I am writing a position statement for a reading group on a question I keep coming back to: how can a piece of music itself be sad? Before circulating it I want a consistency check on the five claims below.

1. A piece of music can literally be sad: the sadness is an audible property of the music itself. To say that a slow movement is sad is neither to report that it makes me sad nor to report that the composer was sad; it is to attribute a character to the sound.

2. The least mysterious explanation of this property is resemblance. Musical features such as slow tempo, low register, drooping or descending contour, soft dynamics, and legato articulation resemble the way sadness shows up in the human body and voice; in the last analysis, the music's being sad consists in this heard resemblance to human expressive behavior.

3. Attributions of sadness to music can be correct or incorrect, and correctness is fixed by the music's perceptible features together with our shared perceptual competencies — not by any particular listener's felt response. A listener can fail to hear the sadness, or can be moved in an idiosyncratic way, without that changing what the piece expresses.

4. But I also believe the resemblance story stops short of what it is like to hear the music as sad. Someone could notice every structural parallel between the music and sad human behavior and still not hear the music as sad. Hearing the sadness seems to require acquaintance with what sadness is like, and that felt acquaintance does essential work that a resemblance-plus-structure account leaves out.

5. The sadness expressed by a piece is not the composer's or performer's actual emotional state. A piece can be sad even if no one involved in making it was sad at the time, and a person in a completely different frame of mind can produce music that is convincingly sad. The expressiveness belongs to the sound, not to any biography behind the sound.
