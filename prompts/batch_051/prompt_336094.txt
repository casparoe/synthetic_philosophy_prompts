I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and a pattern in public life keeps nagging at me. A government will issue a formal apology for an injustice carried out by its own institutions generations ago — colonial expropriation, forced removal of children, mass internment, legalized discrimination. The officials issuing the apology did none of these things, and in many cases both the original perpetrators and the direct victims are long dead. Yet the state speaks in the first person plural, as if we did it and we are sorry. Meanwhile, the very legal order through which the state acts is built, where individual persons are concerned, on the principle that you are criminally answerable only for what you yourself did.

What does this practice presuppose? It seems to treat the state as a single entity that persists across centuries and can own its earlier conduct. But I cannot tell whether the practice strictly requires that picture, or whether it could be justified some other way — for instance, that people alive today inherited benefits from the wrong, or that the apology is a forward-looking political act aimed at present trust rather than a backward-looking confession of guilt.

Could you reconstruct the implicit view of responsibility, and of what makes an institution the same thing over time, that official state apologies carry with them? I would especially like to know: which features of the practice presuppose a unified state-agent that survives the replacement of all its members, and which features are compatible with rival views; where the practice is underdetermined, such that several coherent theories would all license the same apology; and, if we insisted instead that only individual persons can be answerable for their own deeds, what exactly about the practice would have to change or be redescribed.
