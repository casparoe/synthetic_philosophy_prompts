Since retiring from a career in physical chemistry, I have been reading the philosophical literature on dispositions and causal powers, and I keep returning to the simple conditional analysis: roughly, that for a thing to be disposed to manifest some outcome under specified conditions is just for it to be true that it would manifest that outcome if placed in those conditions. It struck me as philosophy's version of bench practice, because I spent decades assigning properties by specifying their tests—heat the material and see whether it decomposes, apply a potential and see whether a current flows. But my settled reactions to individual cases no longer line up with the analysis, and though the reactions feel firm, I cannot state the principle that governs them.

Consider four cases of mine. A champagne glass packed in stiff foam would not break if struck, because the foam absorbs the shock, yet I call it fragile. A live electrical wire connected to a protective cutout that would open the circuit at the first touch of a conductor would deliver no current if tested, yet I still call it live. A toxic dose of a fast-acting poison swallowed together with an antidote that binds it before any harm occurs would sicken no one, yet I still call the poison poisonous. And a dead electrical wire connected to a sensor and battery that would close the circuit at the first touch of a conductor would deliver a current if tested in exactly that way, yet I do not call it live. In the first three cases I ascribe the disposition even though the associated conditional comes out false; in the fourth I withhold it even though the conditional comes out true.

I gather these are the cases metaphysicians discuss under the labels of finks, masks, and mimics, but I have not been able to extract from what I have read a single positive account that I can apply cleanly to all four verdicts. My question is what principle, if any, captures them together. Is there one account of disposition ascription—not necessarily fully formal, but precise enough to test case by case—that yields all four? If not, which of my verdicts should I revise, and what would justify revising that one rather than another? In particular, is the distinction I feel between the live wire (no current if tested, still live) and the dead wire (current if tested, still not live) a principled distinction or a mechanical prejudice carried over from the laboratory? Finally, what should that account say about a case I remain unsettled on: a catalyst transiently poisoned by a contaminant that would become active again once the contaminant were removed—is it disposed, while poisoned, to catalyze the reaction, and how does the principle you propose settle that?
