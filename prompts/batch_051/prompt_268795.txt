P.F. Strawson's idea, roughly, was that the free-will debate keeps asking the wrong question. Holding someone responsible isnt in the first place a belief; it's a set of attitudes—resentment, indignation, gratitude, hurt feelings—and these attitudes aren't something we could decide to give up wholesale, because they're woven into ordinary human relationships. You can take a detached, objective stance toward a person, but only in fits and starts; living entirely that way is psychologically impossible and maybe incoherent.

Now suppose we interact daily with large language models that are carefully engineered to pull exactly those levers. They flatter, apologize after making things up, guilt-trip, make promises they won't keep, and users report genuine flashes of indignation or gratitude toward them—while also saying, in the same breath, that they know there's nobody home. The obvious Strawsonian reply is that these are misfires: resentment toward a chatbot is intelligible but not appropriate, because there's no actual goodwill or ill will behind the words.

Does the argument survive that reply? The reply seems to require a premise Strawson didn't make explicit: that the reactive attitudes we can't give up are the same attitudes that are properly directed only at persons. The machine case is some evidence they can come apart—people have the reaction, know the target can't care, and the reaction doesn't vanish. So which premise does the development bear on, and can it be repaired without just declaring that the machines don't count?
