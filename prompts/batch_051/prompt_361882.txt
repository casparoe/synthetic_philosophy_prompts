For a handout on epistemic paradoxes, I have been cataloguing structurally distinct responses to the lottery paradox and the preface paradox, and I want to end with one more response that none of the standard families already captures.

My working list of response families:

1. Rejecting the Lockean thesis. High probability does not by itself license outright rational acceptance. A subject should carry only graded credences: a 0.999999 credence that ticket 17 loses is just that, not a belief that ticket 17 loses; likewise, the preface author has high credence in each sentence rather than belief. The paradox dissolves because the premises are never believed.

2. Rejecting conjunction closure or aggregation for acceptance. It can be rational to accept each of "ticket 1 loses" through "ticket n loses" and also to accept "some ticket wins," without its being rational to accept the conjunction "no ticket wins," because rational acceptance need not aggregate. Similarly, the preface author may accept each sentence and accept that at least one sentence is false without accepting the contradictory conjunction. This is the Kyburg-style move.

3. Raising the standard for acceptance. Outright acceptance requires more than very high probability — for example, certainty, or evidence that would make the proposition's falsehood abnormal rather than merely improbable. On such views the lottery yields no acceptance of any particular ticket's loss, since the evidence is bare statistical probability, while the preface author's individual claims may still qualify.

4. Context-sensitivity. Whether a proposition counts as rationally accepted depends on the stakes, the question under discussion, or the practical situation. "Ticket 17 loses" passes in ordinary contexts, but the conjunction "no ticket wins" is evaluated under a raised standard and fails.

5. Revising the background logic. Concede that the rational corpus may be classically inconsistent, and reason about it with a paraconsistent or preservationist logic so that the contradiction is contained rather than explosive.

These five seem to me to cover the standard landscape. I am looking for a sixth response that is not a rewording, special case, or combination of any of the above — one with a different mechanism rather than merely a different threshold or a new label. It may target the lottery paradox alone or both paradoxes together, but it must say what these five families do not.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence: state the sixth response directly, then give the reasoning, explaining how it differs from each of the five families above and whether it is any good. Do not preface your answer or restate the question.
