I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I recently moderated a panel in which a literary critic dismissed a biographer's appeal to a novelist's letters about what the novel was meant to show by saying, "the author is dead." I have heard the phrase used for everything from the claim that what a writer intended is often hard to recover, to the claim that a text means whatever a reader makes it mean, and I would like to know exactly what it can and cannot do in an argument. Please answer as a professional philosopher of literature with expertise in theories of interpretation. Think carefully and begin by brainstorming the distinct precise theses that "the author is dead" could be used to assert. Then present them as a menu of readings, each stated in one or two sentences, and for each give a verdict: uninteresting platitude, substantive and defensible, or false or badly overstated. In separating the readings, pay attention to the difference between what causes a text to be produced, what fixes its literal meaning, what determines the best interpretation of the whole work, and what determines its value or significance; also distinguish actual from hypothetical intention, and the claim that a critic may ignore authorial intention from the claim that a critic must. Finally, identify which reading or readings would actually support the critic's move of setting the letters aside, and which readings are too strong to be credible.
