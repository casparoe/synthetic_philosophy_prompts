Article 3 of the charter of the municipal archives where I work reads: "The Archives shall collect, preserve, and make available records of enduring value that document the history and communities of this city. The Archives shall not create records of its own." The clause was drafted in 1954, when the archive was a room of ledgers and council minutes, and everyone then understood the second sentence to mean only that archivists must not write their own accounts and file them among the official papers.

Next year the city will mark the sixtieth anniversary of the demolition of the Oakbank district, a working-class neighborhood cleared for a highway. Hardly any records of Oakbank's internal life survive: its businesses ran on cash, its meeting hall burned down, and the demolition crews discarded most of the district's paper. A community association has asked the archive to conduct an oral history project: our staff would interview the surviving former residents, record the conversations, and preserve the recordings in the city's holdings, alongside the association's reconstructed list of addresses, names, and photographs. The association argues that if we do nothing, the official record will go on showing Oakbank only as a clearance site, and the district will have left no trace.

My difficulty is the second sentence. No one disputes that the proposed interviews, if made, would be records of enduring value documenting a community of the city; the first sentence plainly welcomes them. But an interview we conduct and record is, in the plainest sense, a record we have created. The sentence's obvious point was to stop archivists from inserting their own voice into the record, not to stop the archive from documenting a community the official record never recorded. Does Article 3 permit the oral history project, forbid it, or permit it only under some redescription? Work through what the words require, what the purpose of the clause suggests, and what a reasonable drafter in 1954 would have said if asked. If the three approaches diverge, which should govern here, and what would that tell us about how far a preservation mandate reaches into the making of public memory? If you conclude the archive may not conduct the interviews itself, say whether the same clause would permit the archive to take in recordings made by the association, and why the line falls there.
