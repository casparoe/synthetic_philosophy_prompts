Consider the way the international civil calendar numbers years, and two features of it in particular. The scheme is anchored to a single historically contingent epoch — the traditional year of Jesus's birth, now usually relabeled CE/BCE — so that the timeline runs in two directions from a privileged point, forward into positive years and backward into negative years. And the scheme contains no year zero: 1 BCE is followed immediately by 1 CE. Because of that, "the first century" runs from 1 through 100, and by the same ordinal logic the year 2000 was not the start of the 21st century; yet astronomers quietly use a numbering that inserts a year zero, with 1 BCE as year 0 and 2 BCE as year −1. The same culture that finds a year zero unnatural in chronology has no trouble placing zero at midnight on a clock or at sea level on an altimeter. I want to know whether this is merely an arithmetical convenience or whether it presupposes something: a picture of time as a line radiating from a genuine divide, a view of a year as an ordinal place in a narrative rather than a cardinal quantity on a coordinate axis, a conviction that the epoch is an event rather than an arbitrary origin. What does the no-year-zero practice actually presuppose, as opposed to what it merely sits well with? Reconstruct the implicit view as strictly as the facts will bear, say which rival views of time it quietly rules out if any, and note whether its parts — the privileged epoch, the two-directional numbering, the refusal of zero, the ordinal counting of centuries — presuppose consistent or inconsistent things. Then tell me what would have to change, in the metaphysics or simply in the bookkeeping, for the insertion of a year zero to become forced rather than just an astronomer's shorthand, and whether the question "what should year zero be?" names a real problem or a confusion in need of dissolving.
