I am writing my fourth novel, and for the first time I am treating its conceptual scaffolding as seriously as its dialogue. The book follows a family whose wealth and social standing were created by an ancestor who committed an act of spectacular cruelty, and it tracks the different ways three later generations carry, disavow, or quietly absorb that fact. Halfway through the second draft I realised that I still had no clear idea what the phrase inherited guilt actually means. The phrase kept doing too many different jobs whenever I reached for it: legal liabilities that pass to a successor, a moral stain thought to run in a bloodline, a grandchild's felt shame, later generations' political obligations, and, in the theological background, the doctrine that human beings are born guilty of Adam's sin. I would like a working definition of inherited guilt that is precise enough to do narrative work: it should tell me whether a character who did not commit the original act can nonetheless be described as guilty, and in what sense.

I have six cases in mind, and I ask you to run the definition through all of them and report where it delivers surprising answers.

- The strong doctrine of original sin, on which every human being is born guilty of Adam's transgression.
- A woman whose grandfather helped organise a massacre; she feels intense, disabling guilt about it, though she never met him and has no legal exposure.
- A state that pays compensation and apologises for a genocide committed before any of its living citizens was born.
- A company held liable for pollution caused by the firm it later acquired, though no current owner or employee caused the damage.
- A child who knowingly keeps and spends money that her parent stole.
- A moderate doctrine of original sin, on which human beings inherit a damaged moral condition or inclination to sin, but not guilt itself.

I am fairly confident that the first case is a paradigm of the term and equally confident that the last is not; the four between them are precisely the cases I cannot settle. Please structure your answer in four titled sections: Proposed definition (roughly 250 words); Application to the six cases (roughly 400 words); Revisions and hard cases (roughly 250 words), indicating which of my prior judgements should be revised and where the definition gives out; and Bottom line for the novel (roughly 100 words), saying in a sentence or two whether the definition is usable for characterisation and which case it handles least well. Please write in British English throughout.
