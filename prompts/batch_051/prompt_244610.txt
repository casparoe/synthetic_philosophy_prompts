I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I have been trying to make my views about animal minds more explicit rather than relying on intuition. I have settled on a rough checklist that I use before I am willing to say that a creature has felt experience of any kind — that there is something it is like to be that creature, or at least that things can go well or badly for it in a felt way. The criteria I currently use are these:

1. Nociception or a functional equivalent. The creature has receptors or pathways that detect tissue damage or other harmful states.
2. Not a fixed reflex. The response to a harmful stimulus is modulated by context, satiation, learning, or competing goals, rather than being a stereotyped withdrawal.
3. Learning from negative events. The creature changes its future behaviour after an aversive experience in a way that is flexible, not merely associative in a narrow sense.
4. Integration. The creature behaves as a single decision-making system: information from different sensory channels is brought together and traded off against one another.
5. Trade-offs that reveal valuation. The creature will forgo food, mating, or safety to escape or avoid a harmful stimulus, which suggests the state carries negative weight for it rather than simply triggering a circuit.

Now take the honeybee. A honeybee has on the order of a million neurons. It navigates over kilometres, learns and remembers odours and locations, communicates the location and quality of resources to nestmates, and adjusts its behaviour in ways that do not look like hardwired reflexes. Yet it is also plausible to describe all of this as extremely sophisticated computation with no felt quality behind it — a set of tightly optimised control loops.

I would like you to go through my checklist criterion by criterion and tell me, for each, whether the honeybee plausibly satisfies it, where the uncertainty is, and what evidence would move the verdict. Then step back and tell me whether the checklist itself is doing the right kind of work: is it a reasonable operational test for the presence of felt experience, or does it smuggle in assumptions about what consciousness is? Would a creature that passes all five criteria be conscious in any sense, or only a good simulation of a creature that is? And is consciousness even the right category to apply to a bee, or should I be asking a more precise question — for example, about pain, or about the capacity for welfare? I am not satisfied with a bare yes or no; I want to know which criteria carry the load and where the genuine disagreement lies.

Please write your answer in British English.
