I spent my working life in the natural sciences, where I was trained to treat a theory as underdetermined by the data and then to settle matters by further experiment. I have recently been reading around Quine's radical translation, and I find myself unsure whether I have correctly reconstructed his argument for the indeterminacy of translation. I would be grateful for a careful assessment from a philosopher.

My reconstruction runs as follows. (1) In radical translation, the linguist has no prior dictionary and must rely solely on the observable verbal behaviour of speakers, that is, their dispositions to assent to or dissent from sentences under various stimulations. (2) Two translation manuals could in principle be drawn up that are incompatible with one another, one rendering the word gavagai as rabbit and another as undetached rabbit-part, say, yet both could be squared with the totality of all possible such behavioural evidence. (3) If two incompatible manuals can both fit all possible evidence, then there is no fact of the matter as to which manual is correct, and translation is indeterminate between them. (4) Consequently, there is no fact of the matter whether gavagai means rabbit or undetached rabbit-part.

I half suspect that step (3) is doing more than the premises entitle it to, but I cannot say exactly why without sneaking in an unexamined assumption about what it takes for a fact of the matter to exist. Am I right that the argument is flawed, or is it actually sound? If it is flawed, identify the precise step that fails, give the strongest reading under which that step might be defended, and say whether the argument goes through on that reading. Please also indicate how confident you are in your assessment, single out the point on which you are least certain, and answer in British English.
