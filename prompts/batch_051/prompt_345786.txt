I have been trying to work out whether the passage of time has any intrinsic moral significance, or whether it matters only because evidence decays and memories fade. My judgments about particular cases feel stable, but the principle behind them keeps slipping away from me. Consider these cases. A man who committed a murder at twenty-five is conclusively identified by DNA at sixty-five, after four decades of a quiet, lawful life; I think he should be prosecuted and imprisoned, with time barely discounting the sentence at all. A bank employee who embezzled a large sum at twenty-eight is discovered by auditors at forty-eight, after building a legitimate business abroad; I am much less sure that prosecution is still the right response, and I would in any case punish him far less than I would have at twenty-nine—though I cannot say what distinguishes his case from the murderer's. A bigoted post that someone wrote at sixteen should, when it resurfaces at thirty as she is running for office, count only a little against her, far less than the same post written at twenty-nine. A chemical company that buried toxic waste in 1975, whose responsible managers are dead or retired, should be liable today because the waste is still contaminating groundwater, even though the acts are decades old and the current shareholders did not commit them. I also believe a state owes some form of restitution or reparations for land taken from an Indigenous people in the nineteenth century, long after every original wrongdoer has died. By contrast, if one friend lent another five thousand dollars and neither mentioned it for fifteen years, I think the lender's claim is stale and the law is right to bar it, even if the debtor has since become wealthy. And if a forged signature on a will is discovered eight years after probate, with the estate distributed and the heirs' lives built around the inheritance, I think it is simply too late to overturn, clear as the wrong is.

The best principle I can formulate is roughly this: time should count against punishment and obligation only insofar as it has eroded the evidence, changed the wrongdoer into a different enough person, or allowed others reasonably to rely on the settled state of affairs. But that principle fits my own cases badly. The murderer and the embezzler both appear, by all outward signs, to have become different people over the decades, yet I want time barely to discount the one and to discount the other heavily; so the changed-person clause cannot be doing all the work. And the corporation and the state remain liable long after the evidence has degraded and the individual wrongdoers have left the scene, apparently because the harm is ongoing or the institution persists—considerations my rough principle never mentions. So I would like you to do better. State the best principle you can find that gets my verdicts right, or close; say precisely what it predicts for cases I have not listed, such as a concealed war crime committed in youth and revealed in old age, or a wrong committed by an institution that has since been dissolved; and tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and which I should revise. If the choice is between a complicated principle that saves every verdict and a simpler one that overturns one or two of them, say which side you would take and why.
