Ever since my sister got turned down for a rental and the letting agent muttered something about a "consumer risk file," I've been stuck on this. She eventually got a copy of what one of these data brokers holds on her: not raw stuff, but guesses—her openness to "aspirational messaging," her predicted Big Five traits, a flag saying she was "life-event vulnerable" because of a breakup, and an estimate of how much she'd pay before switching brands. None of it came from interviews; it was stitched together from loyalty cards, public posts, location pings, that sort of thing. The broker sells access to the file.

So here's what I want to think through. In a property theory class I took, we were told something is property if roughly three things hold. First, you can exclude people from it (a chair, you can keep others from sitting in it). Second, you can transfer it (sell it, give it away, destroy it). Third, there's some ground for the claim: you made it, or you were first to take possession of it, or it's bound up in your personhood. There was also a background idea that property is rivalrous—my using it leaves less for you—but that already seems shaky for ideas and credit scores.

Now take one slice of the file: the inferred personality profile. Go through the criteria honestly. Can she exclude the broker (or anyone else) from using it? Can she transfer it—give it to a competitor to shop around, or demand it be deleted? And on the grounding test, who has the better claim: her, because every input is an event in her life and the output is a claim about her inner character, or the broker, because they built the model? I honestly can't tell wether the right conclusion is that this profile is her property, or that the criteria are just the wrong tool for information and we need different ones. I'm less interested in what the law currently says and more in whether the concept of property can stretch to cover it without breaking.
