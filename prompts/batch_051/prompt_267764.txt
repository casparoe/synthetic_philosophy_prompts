I'm preparing a graduate seminar unit on epistemic modesty, and I keep getting stuck on a reformulation of the dogmatism paradox. The standard version runs roughly like this: if you know that p, then you know in advance that any piece of evidence which appears to tell against p is misleading; and since you know it is misleading, you can rationally disregard it. That seems to license an absurd degree of closed-mindedness, so something in the reasoning must go wrong. A colleague has suggested that the paradox is an artifact of describing things in terms of infallible knowledge, and that moving to graded confidence and conditionalization makes it dissolve into ordinary Bayesian updating. I suspect it doesn't dissolve but only becomes more explicit, and I'd like to work through the shift carefully before teaching it.

Here is the variation. Replace 'S knows that p' with the following. S is rationally 90% confident in p on the basis of her total evidence. S is about to observe the output of a single test whose profile she knows exactly: if p is true, the test reports 'against p' with probability 0.1; if p is false, the test reports 'against p' with probability 0.9. Say that an actual report of 'against p' is misleading just in case p is true, which is the same sense of 'misleading' the original paradox uses. Before the test, S can compute in advance exactly what posterior she would adopt upon seeing 'against p'. It is also tempting for S to reason as a dogmatist would: 'I am 90% sure p; a report of 'against p' is misleading whenever p is true; so I should regard such a report as probably misleading and not let it move me much.'

The test is run and it reports 'against p'. Compute S's rational posterior in p. Then work through exactly where the tempting dogmatist reasoning goes wrong, if it does: before observing the report, S was 90% sure of p, and surely a report she is 90% sure is misleading is not something she should update on as if it were decisive information. Does the paradox survive the move from knowledge to high confidence, does it get sharper or weaker, or does it dissolve into Bayesian updating? Finally, bring this back to the standard version: the usual diagnoses locate the flaw either in a closure step, or in the claim that one can know in advance that a piece of evidence is misleading, or in the move from 'this evidence would be misleading' to 'this evidence may be disregarded.' Which of those diagnoses does the credal reformulation support or undermine, and what should I tell my students about where the original argument actually goes wrong?
