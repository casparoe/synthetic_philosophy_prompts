I am a philosophy professor who works in the philosophy of medicine, and I have been asked to advise the medical-policy committee of a regional health insurer. The committee meets several times a month to decide whether a given condition should count as a disease and therefore be covered as medically necessary treatment, or instead be classified as normal variation, an ordinary life problem, or an enhancement. Its members are clinicians, actuaries, a patient representative, and a lawyer; none has philosophical training. Each decision is made in a single one-hour meeting, but the rulings affect thousands of patients and must survive appeals. I want you to draft the written procedure the committee will use.

The conceptual core of the procedure is the dispute between naturalist and normativist accounts of disease. Naturalism holds, roughly, that a disease is a value-free departure from species-typical biological functioning: a part or process failing to make its statistically typical contribution to the organism's survival and reproduction. Normativism holds that disease is not a value-free concept: calling something a disease involves judging that the condition is a biological dysfunction that is also a harm or serious disvalue for the person. The procedure must keep this distinction alive rather than quietly settling it. Give the committee a checklist or ordered set of tests, usable by non-philosophers in a single meeting, that says which steps the two accounts agree on, which steps they would answer differently, and which steps are bare policy choices. Mark explicitly the points where judgment cannot be eliminated.

Then apply your procedure to three cases: (i) asymptomatic essential hypertension; (ii) infertility in a patient who has never wanted children; and (iii) one further case of your choosing in which the procedure returns an answer the committee will dislike—for instance, a condition the committee is currently inclined to cover but your procedure excludes, such as non-pathological short stature, ordinary age-related decline, or ordinary grief. For each case, show how the checklist runs, which step does the deciding, which rival step would have flipped the classification, and what the committee should say to a patient who appeals. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: where it will misfire, which parts of the philosophical dispute cannot be proceduralized away, and why it is still worth writing the procedure despite that.
