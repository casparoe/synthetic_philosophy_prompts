So here's a situation I can't stop turning over. A friend and I did a one-off project together, and now we're splitting the payout. We each have to submit a choice seperately, with no chance to see the other's before committing, and after this there are no further dealings, no reputation effects, and nobody else will ever know what happened. The choice is just two buttons: fair, or grab. If we both press fair, we each get $100. If we both press grab, we each get $5. If one presses grab and the other presses fair, the grabber gets $150 and the fair one gets $0. The usual reasoning is that whatever he does, I do better by grabbing, so I should grab; he reasons the same way, so we both grab and walk away with $5 each, even though we'd both rather have the $100. But here's the wrinkle. We've both been reading the same rationalist blogs and forums for years, and we've argued about exactly this kind of case. We've each independently landed on the view that when two sufficiently similar rational agents face a symmetric choice, they'll do the same thing, so each should choose the option that is best if both choose it. As a starting point, suppose I'm nearly certain he reasons the same way I do and will land on the same button I land on. Should I press fair? And is that "sameness" a legitimate thing to reason from, or is it just magical thinking dressed up as logic? Then relax that: suppose I'm only about seventy percent confident he's my type in that way, and thirty percent confident he's a plain maximizer who will press grab no matter what I do. Does my button change? I'd like practical advice about what to actually do, plus the theory underneath, in particular where the symmetry argument stops being valid. Please answer in continuous prose only, like an email or letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
