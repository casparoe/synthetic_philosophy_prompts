I've been grading papers on On Liberty and it's made me notice something strange about how we actually treat people who try out new ways of living. We talk as if experiments in living are the engine of progress, and we retrospectively celebrate the ones that worked: the person who left a good job to open a restaraunt, the couple who moved into a van, the college drop-out who built something in a garage. But when the experiment is happening near us, before the outcome is in, we read the exact same person as self-indulgent or irresponsible. If it fails we say it was obviously a bad idea; if it succeeds we act like we saw the potential all along. The approval tracks the result, not the experimenting. My question is whether there's any justification for that asymmetry other than the flat fact that we like winners. Could it be defended on decent grounds — say, that a settled way of life has a default claim on us until somebody proves an alternative works — or should we admit the asymmetry is just hindsight bias and judge experiments in living by there expected value at the time they were chosen? And if Mill is right that failed experiments are part of how a society learns what alternatives are even possible, would he have to treat our ex post applause as one more form of the social tyranny he was writing against?
