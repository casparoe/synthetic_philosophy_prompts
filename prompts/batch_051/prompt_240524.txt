A colleague in the research group I coordinate is responsible for ranking short episodes of animal experience for a welfare database. For each episode she records two scores on 0–10 scales: a pleasure score, P, for how good the state feels, and an awareness score, A, for how fully and clearly conscious the animal is. She then issues pairwise judgments: one episode is better than, worse than, or on a par with another.

Her complete record so far is as follows.

1. When awareness is held fixed, she always ranks by pleasure. Given (P=8, A=4), (P=5, A=4), and (P=2, A=4), she prefers them in that order.
2. When pleasure is held fixed, she always ranks by awareness. Given (P=4, A=8), (P=4, A=5), and (P=4, A=2), she prefers them in that order.
3. When one episode scores higher on both dimensions, she always prefers the one that dominates. For instance, she ranks (P=6, A=6) above (P=2, A=2).
4. She has not yet judged any pair in which one episode has the higher pleasure score and the other has the higher awareness score.

The pair she is about to judge is exactly that kind of case:

- Episode X: P=2, A=6
- Episode Y: P=3, A=5

I want to predict her verdict. Spell out the natural rules that fit her record so far—at least an additive rule (P + A), a multiplicative rule (P × A), and lexicographic rules that give one dimension absolute priority—and say what each implies for X versus Y. Then tell me which verdict you would actually predict, with reasons, and specify the single most informative follow-up comparison I could put to her once her answer to this pair is known, to narrow down which rule she is really running.
