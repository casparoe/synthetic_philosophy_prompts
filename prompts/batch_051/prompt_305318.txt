I keep seeing people claim that when a language model confidently fabricates a citation or a legal case, it's bullshit in Frankfurt's sense—not lying, because the model doesn't know the fact and isn't trying to get you to believe something it disbelieves, and not a plain error, because a plain error is what happens when a system is actually aiming at truth and misses. I can't tell wheter that's a testable classification or just a way of talking.

So: how would you actually test whether hallucination is bullshit as opposed to error or lying? Suppose I have a model that hallucinates under some conditions. Design the best experiment or set of experiments you can to pull those three apart. I want concrete manipulations—something like comparing hallucination rates when the correct answer is put right in the context vs. when the prompt subtly signals that the user wants a particular answer, or fine-tuning for truthfulness vs. helpfulness and seeing what breaks, or probing internal states before and after the model has the relevant knowledge. What pattern would count as evidence for each category? Be explicit about the predicted difference.

And then be honest about what even the cleanest result would not settle. In particular: Frankfurt's bullshitter is defined partly by not caring whether what he says is true. Can "not caring" be operationally measured in a model, or does category (c) collapse into (b) once you go behavioral? Is there a residue here that no experiment gets at because it's about mental states, or is that worry itself a confusion about what the distinction was doing in the first place?
