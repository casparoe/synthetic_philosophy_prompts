After a career in organismal biology, I have recently been reading philosophy of biology, and I keep running into a parallel that I cannot tell is deep or superficial. When I first encountered the distinction between etiological and causal-role accounts of biological function, I was struck by how much it resembles a distinction working scientists make informally about engineered and evolved systems alike. The etiological account, as I understand it, holds that the function of a trait is the effect for which it was selected in the past, and that ascribing a function explains the trait’s presence or persistence by citing that history. The causal-role account, by contrast, holds that the function of a trait is the contribution it presently makes to the larger system’s overall capacity, and that one can identify functions without needing a selection history. My working life gave me many examples where the two readings come apart. An enzyme in a metabolic pathway may have been selected for one catalytic step, but may now, because of changes elsewhere, chiefly buffer substrate concentrations. A behavior in a social species may have arisen by selection for one outcome but now mainly stabilizes dominance hierarchies. In each case, the etiological account and the causal-role account can return different answers to the question, What is this trait for?

What I cannot resolve is whether this is the same conceptual problem that arises when one moves from designed artifacts to evolved ones, or whether the biological case is different in a principled way. When an engineer says a component in a legacy machine has a function, she sometimes means what the original designer intended it to do, and sometimes means what it actually does in the current system; when the two diverge, the engineering literature seems to have live disputes about which sense is authoritative. Natural selection is often treated as a designer substitute, but it lacks foresight and intention. So my question is whether the etiological/causal-role distinction in philosophy of biology is really a special case of a more general distinction between historical and present-contribution ascriptions of function, of the sort one also finds in engineering and physiology, or whether the analogy breaks exactly at the point of there being no actual designer in the biological case. If the parallel is deep, what does that imply for how we should think about vestigial traits, exaptations, and functions that arise after a trait’s original selection regime? If it is superficial, where does the mapping fail?
