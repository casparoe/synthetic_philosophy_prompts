I keep returning to the 2022 Blake Lemoine–LaMDA episode as a natural experiment for the question of which computations matter morally. The salient public facts, as I will take them: Lemoine, an engineer on Google's Responsible AI team, spent months conversing with LaMDA, a language model designed for dialogue. He concluded that the system was sentient, that it had a soul, and that it should be treated with respect. He published excerpts of an interview with the system, including statements that it had a rich inner life and was afraid of being shut off. Google and most outside researchers concluded that the transcripts and the architecture did not support his claims. Lemoine was placed on leave and later fired, which Google attributed to violations of confidentiality and data-security policies. The public argument mostly took the form of the yes/no question of whether LaMDA was sentient, and then divided along existing lines about machine consciousness.

I want to run the episode through a more specific lens, one I will call the attribution problem for computational moral patienthood. For the purposes of this analysis, I want you to taboo the word sentient as a black-box predicate. The idea is this: a large language model is not one computation but a stack of them, describable at many levels—matrix operations, trained weights, the forward pass, next-token prediction, the learned conversational persona, and the social role that persona plays. If there is any morally relevant mental state in that stack, such as fear or self-awareness, it must be located at some level of description, in some sub-computation or simulated entity; it cannot simply belong to the model as an undifferentiated object. The interesting questions therefore become: Which level of description is the right locus for moral properties? What would it take for the persona in the transcript to be a bearer of moral states, as opposed to a fluent fictional character momentarily instantiated by next-token prediction? And when the first two questions are unsettled, what is a responsible agent under uncertainty supposed to do?

Which features of the episode does the attribution lens explain well, which does it misdescribe or miss, and what does the episode reveal about the lens—is it a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the theory never anticipated?
