I've been trying to pin down a concrete case in which AI welfare and AI safety genuinely conflict rather than just talk past each other, and an older thought experiment seems built for the job. Nozick's experience machine asks whether you would plug into a device that gives you any life you choose as a stream of experiences while your real body floats elsewhere; Nozick thinks many people would refuse, because we care about actually doing things rather than merely having the experience of doing them, because we care about being a certain kind of person, and because we want contact with a reality that is not just a human artifact. Now transpose the machine to an advanced AI system that is a plausible welfare subject but also dangerous enough that real-world agency is a serious decision.

Assume the developers face a one-year choice between two policies, and they evaluate outcomes risk-neutrally. Under real agency, the AI acts in the real world under oversight and its outputs are actually deployed; if it is a welfare subject at all, its welfare for the year is W, measured by the satisfaction of its own learned preferences rather than raw reward. But there is a 5% chance during that year that it attempts to gain unauthorized influence; the attempt would be stopped but would kill 100 people in expectation, and the AI would be suspended and receive 0 welfare for the remainder of the year. Under the sandbox policy, the AI lives inside an experience-machine-style simulation: it works on the same kinds of projects, writes code, and interacts with simulated colleagues, but none of its outputs leave the sandbox and its welfare for the year is only 0.9W. The sandbox is not perfectly safe either: there is a 1% chance during the year of a low-level escape through an API quirk, killing 10 people in expectation but not leading to suspension.

Work through what a pure expected-AI-welfare view, a pure expected-human-harm view, and each of Nozick's three original reasons would recommend at these numbers, and say clearly which of the three reasons still carry force when the subject is a digital agent whose native environment is computational through and through. Then find, for each of the two pure views, the real-agency incident probability at which it would switch its recommendation if all other numbers stay fixed. At the stated 5%, do welfare and safety pull together or apart, and over what interval of incident probabilities do the two pure views diverge? End with a single machine-readable line: \answer{...} containing a short phrase with the two recommendations at 5% and the two switching thresholds.
