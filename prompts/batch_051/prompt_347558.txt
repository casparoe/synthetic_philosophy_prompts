After I retired from physical chemistry I started sitting in on the philosophy of physics seminar, and now Ive somehow ended up with a colloquium slot. The talk defends the claim that the thermodynamic arrow gets asked to do to much work. My line is this: entropy gradients plus time-symmetric microdynamics plus a low-entropy boundary condition explain why entropy rises toward the future, but they do not by themselves explain why we remember the past and not the future, why causes precede their effects, or why deliberation treats the past as fixed and the future as open. I argue those asymmetries are logically independent of the entropy gradient, and that closing the gap takes a time-asymmetric posit that the standard story slips in without labeling it. I think the real work is done by the boundary condition, which is just the temporal asymmetry wearing a different hat.

The audience is a mid-size philosophy department, heavy on metaphysics, with two philosophers of physics, a couple of Humeans about laws, one hardcore eternalist who thinks passage is an illusion, and one older faculty member who still takes presentism seriously. They know the basics (Second Law, reversible microdynamics, the Past Hypothesis) but wont care about technicalities from my old field; they will care about what is doing the explaining and what is being smuggled in. This is my first philosophy talk, so I'm still learning how they phrase objections in that room.
