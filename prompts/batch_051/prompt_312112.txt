I am a game theorist, comfortable with commitment devices, cheap talk, repeated games, reputation, and mechanism design. Restate the main philosophical answers to the question of why promises bind—for example, the conventionalist view that promising is a move in a social practice, the expectation view, the assurance view, and the view that promising is the exercise of a normative power to put oneself under an obligation—in that vocabulary, one by one. For each answer, say what translates cleanly, what survives only with distortion, and what has no counterpart in game theory at all. Then give me the residue report: does what was left behind show that game theory is missing something important, or that the philosophical answers are less precise than they look? In particular, I want to know whether the apparently central idea—that a promise creates an obligation that remains even when it would be profitable to break it and no punishment is possible—can be expressed in game-theoretic terms, or whether it is exactly the sort of thing the translation leaves behind.
