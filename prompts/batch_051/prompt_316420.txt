I am a novelist, and a puzzle from my craft has led me to a philosophical position about consciousness that I would like an honest assessment of. For decades I have constructed unified narratives out of many centers of attention, and that practice seems to me to suggest a solution to the combination problem for panpsychism.

The combination problem, as I understand it, is this. If the basic constituents of the world each have some minimal form of consciousness, then adding or compounding those constituents should yield many small subjects, never one large subject; a collection of little points of view is not itself a point of view, any more than a crowd is a single onlooker. So it is unclear how the unified consciousness of a human being could be built from micro-subjects.

I call my answer narrative-binding panpsychism. Its central claim is that a unified subject is not composed out of smaller subjects at all; it is narrated into being. A macro-subject exists when a collection of micro-experiences is placed under a single narrative frame, in roughly the way a third-person narrator turns scattered episodes into scenes of one life: one protagonist, one timeline, one through-line. The binding relation is not a physical relation between parts but what I call a plot relation. On my view, the micro-subjects do not disappear; they persist as the raw point-of-view material that the narration organizes, just as the separate viewpoints in a novel do not cease to exist when a single narrator arranges them.

I want to be clear about what I take to make this more than a literary metaphor. The narrative frame is real and causally efficacious: it selects, orders, and excludes micro-experiences, and that activity is what makes the difference between many little minds in one body and one mind. It is not a useful fiction.

I suspect the view collapses into something already in the literature, perhaps higher-order thought theory or the idea of the self as a center of narrative gravity. But I also suspect it differs from those in taking the narrator to be a real, organizing cause rather than a mere representation or a fictive convenience.

What I am asking is this: Is narrative-binding panpsychism coherent as stated? Is it a genuine solution to the combination problem, or does it merely restate an existing view, and which? Give me the two or three strongest objections to it, and say whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable. Finally, please say how confident you are in what you claim, and single out the point you are least sure of.
