I'm in a philosophy of math class and our discussion section got weirdly heated about something I thought was just a notation issue. In standard set theory, if you build the naturals the von Neumann way, then 2 is {0,1} and 4 is {0,1,2,3}, so it's literally true that 2 is an element of 4. But if you build them the Zermelo way, where each number is the singleton of the previous one, 4 turns out to be the one-element set {{{{∅}}}}, and 2 (which is {{∅}}) isn't in it. It bothers me that a statement like "2 ∈ 4" flips from true to false just depending on how we encode numbers as sets. Half the class says this shows numbers aren't really sets at all; they're just positions in a structure, and asking whether 2 is an element of 4 is basically a category mistake. The other half says no, once you fix the standard construction the question has a determinate answer, and the alternate coding is just a weird relabling that nobody actually uses.

Write an essay on this controversy: does "2 is an element of 4" have a determinate truth value, or are set-theoretic reductions of arithmetic basically arbitrary? Weigh the arguments on both sides, especially the structuralist point that numbers only have structural properties and that anything about their "inner nature" is meaningless. But don't end on a wishy-washy note — defend a verdict, even if it's a qualified one like "the question is only well-posed relative to a chosen coding" or "the reduction is arbitrary and the membership question is ill-posed."
