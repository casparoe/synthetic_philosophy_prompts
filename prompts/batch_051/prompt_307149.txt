Last night I spent a long evening arguing with a friend about whether falsifiability can really carry the burden of separating science from non-science. Neither of us changed sides, but by the end we had each refined our position well past where we began, and I would like to see those refined positions written down in their strongest forms, placed against one another in dialogue.

The first position is a procedural falsificationism, not a semantic one. It makes no claim that unfalsifiable statements are meaningless, and it does not pretend that science advances by naive refutation. It claims that what makes an inquiry scientific is a standing policy: the central claims of a field are held in such a way that some possible observation would count against them, and when a prediction fails, that failure is treated as information rather than explained away. The line between science and pseudoscience, on this view, is drawn not by whether this or that isolated sentence is falsifiable, but by whether a practice arranges matters so that its core commitments could lose.

The second position denies that any single criterion can do that work. It says falsifiability is at once too weak and too strong. Too weak, because a pseudoscientific enterprise can issue falsifiable claims and then surround each failure with enough qualifications to evade the consequence. Too strong, because real science routinely keeps a theory alive through anomalies — by adjusting auxiliary assumptions, doubting the instrument, or waiting for a better theoretical framework — and it is often right to do so. On this view, demarcation is a graded and interest-relative judgment about the overall style of a community: how it apportions blame between core claims and auxiliary assumptions, whether failed predictions tighten or loosen constraints, and where it sets the threshold between anomaly and refutation.

Write the dialogue so that both speakers are equally competent and neither is a strawman. They should not take turns delivering speeches; each must answer the other's actual objections. At some point the falsificationist has to face the objection that a failed prediction refutes only a conjunction of claims, never a single claim in isolation. At some point the critic has to face the worry that, without falsifiability as a shared norm, the label science threatens to become a merely sociological honorific. Each side should make at least one concession that visibly changes what the other argues next. The dialogue should end with the two speakers stating, in their own terms, exactly which disagreement survives their exchange.
