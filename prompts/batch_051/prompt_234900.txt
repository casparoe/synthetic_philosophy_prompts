I am putting together an epistemology workshop on Hempel's raven paradox, and the session would be much better if the participants had to write a careful joint statement instead of rehashing the usual slogans. Draft the document that could emerge from a structured adversarial collaboration between a Hempelian and a Bayesian about the paradox. The first party holds that confirmation is a logical relation between a hypothesis and the evidence sentences that instantiate it: "All ravens are black" is confirmed by observed black ravens via the Nicod condition, and since "All ravens are black" is logically equivalent to "All non-black things are non-ravens," it must be equally confirmed by an observed green scarf; the air of paradox is just a psychological illusion produced by ignoring the relational character of confirmation. The second party holds that confirmation is always a matter of degree of belief: an observation confirms a hypothesis just in case it raises the hypothesis's probability against a background, and whether a non-black non-raven raises the probability of the raven hypothesis depends entirely on the background distribution and the sampling procedure, so there is no standing puzzle to dissolve, only a collection of differently calibrated cases.

The statement both parties must be able to sign should include five components. First, five to seven numbered claims on which both sides agree, covering the logical equivalence of the two hypotheses, the inadequacy of the naive idea that every observed instance of an antecedent together with its consequent is automatically a favorable datum, and the formal core that any adequate resolution must respect. Second, a disagreements table in which each position is stated in wording a committed defender of that position would accept as fair—not a caricature a Bayesian could dismiss or a Hempelian could disown. Third, the two or three genuine cruxes: the sub-questions whose settlement, by argument or by a specified kind of case, would move at least one side. Fourth, for each crux, a test case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's predicted verdict recorded in advance. Fifth, a closing diagnosis of which portion of the dispute, if any, is verbal rather than substantive, and what reformulation would make that visible.
