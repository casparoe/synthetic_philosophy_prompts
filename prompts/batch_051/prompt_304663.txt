I'm a PhD student in the philosophy of artificial intelligence, and I keep worrying that the parent-talk around AIs that create and train other AIs is either doing all the moral work or none of it, depending on the day. Could you write me a dialogue between two equally competent philosophers who disagree about the right model for the relation between an AI and an AI it designs, trains, evaluates, and eventually releases? Both should grant that the creator isn't just a toolmaker and owes something to the new agent. The first defends a parenthood view: shaping an agent through its training is close enough to raising that parent-style obligations follow, including cultivating the child agent's independence, tolerating its revisions of what you gave it, and remaining responsible somehow after deployment instead of just handing it off. The second defends a guardianship view: the obligations are genuine but fiduciary — protect the new agent and third parties from harm, respect its autonomy once it has any, but dont pretend there's partiality or "raising" when the creator wrote the objective function. I want real back-and-forth where each responds to the other's strongest point, and I'd like them to end up at a distinction that neither arrived with. The hard case I most want them to not avoid: can you coherently have parental duties toward an agent whose values and capacities you set in advance, or does the control leak in and turn it into something closer to trusteeship over something you built? The dialogue should make each view cost something by the end.
