I spent most of yesterday evening arguing with a mate about the Great Filter, and I'm still turning it over. The standard line is roughly this: if galaxy-spanning civilisations were common we'd probably have seen some trace of them by now, so somewhere on the long route from dead chemistry to colonising the stars there has to be a brutally improbable transition, and people talk about that improbability as a single Great Filter with a location: it sits either behind us — life, complex cells, or intelligence are rare and we're the fluke — or ahead of us — civilisations tend to wipe themselves out before they spread. And the usual moral is that we should hope it's behind us, because a filter ahead of us is just a roundabout prediction of our doom. My friend thinks this way of talking has become misleading. His point wasn't that the science is settled; it was a modelling challenge. Suppose the route isn't blocked by one monstrous bottleneck but by, say, two hundred separate steps, each with only a one-in-three chance of going the life-friendly way, scattered along the whole path from chemistry to galactic civilisation, with about half already behind us and about half still in front of us. The product is vanishingly small, so 'life is rare' still comes out right, but no individual step deserves to be called the filter, and the improbability dosen't have a single location.

So here's what I want to work through. Does the Great Filter argument survive that picture — not just 'is it compatible with it', but does the conclusion people actually draw from the argument, that the silence gives us reason to hope the hard part is behind rather than ahead, still go through when the improbability is spread over lots of ordinary steps on both sides? My instinct is that the behind/ahead dichotomy partly breaks down, but I can't tell wether that kills the argument, weakens it to something boring, or just forces a cleaner statement of what the argument was doing all along. Where in the original argument is the single-filter assumption doing the real work, if anywhere? And if the argument doesn't need it, what's the repaired version, and what weaker-but-still-true conclusion does it leave standing? Also, when you answer, could you say how confident you are in the main claims and pick out the bit you're least sure about — I'd rather know where the reasoning goes soft than get a tidy verdict.
