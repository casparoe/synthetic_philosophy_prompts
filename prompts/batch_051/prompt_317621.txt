I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am working on a long-form piece about how to assess testimony for extraordinary claims: reported miracles, but also once-in-a-generation scientific results that would overturn what we thought was settled. The standard line I keep encountering is that Hume, in "Of Miracles" (Section X of An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding), proves that no testimony could ever rationally establish a miracle. The summary usually goes like this: a miracle is by definition a violation of a law of nature, and a law of nature is established by uniform experience, so the proof against any miracle is always as strong as an argument from experience can be.

I want to test that summary against a passage in Part I that seems to me to be doing something more careful. Hume writes:

"All probability, then, supposes an opposition of experiments and observations, where the one side is found to overbalance the other, and to produce a degree of evidence, proportioned to the superiority. A hundred instances or experiments on one side, and fifty on another, afford a doubtful expectation of any event; though a hundred uniform experiments, with only one that is contradictory, reasonably beget a pretty strong degree of assurance. In all cases, we must balance the opposite experiments, where they are opposite, and deduct the smaller number from the greater, in order to know the exact force of the superior evidence."

Read by itself, this looks like a general bookkeeping procedure for evidence: line up the experiments on both sides, subtract the smaller from the larger, and let the remainder measure the force of belief. But if that is the method, then in the case of a miracle the side against the event includes every observed regularity of nature, and the subtraction seems to leave the miracle side with nothing every time. Does that mean the textbook summary is right after all, and Hume does have an in-principle argument that testimony can never establish a miracle? Or is the real argument narrower, a claim about how testimony must be discounted rather than about whether the event is possible? And if it is narrower, does this passage actually give us a workable procedure for weighing the uniformity of nature against the reliability of witnesses, or does it quietly assume the very disparity it is supposed to demonstrate?

Please answer as an expert on Hume and early modern epistemology. Think carefully, and do not settle for the usual one-sentence verdict. Before committing to an interpretation, briefly lay out the two or three most plausible readings of the passage; identify any false presupposition or compressed simplification in the standard summary, including any that may be present in the way I have framed the question; and then work out, from the text, what Hume is actually licensing, what would have to be true on his account for testimony to win, and where, if anywhere, the procedure in the quoted passage breaks down.
