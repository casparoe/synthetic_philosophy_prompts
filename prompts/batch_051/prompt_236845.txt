Ordinary judgment draws a sharp line between photographs and paintings. If someone holds up a clear, unaltered photograph of a friend and says, "here is Maria" or "I can see Maria," almost no one treats this as merely loose talk; pushed on it, most people maintain that they are seeing Maria, not just a picture of her. If the same person holds up a faithful painted portrait of Maria, made from direct observation by a skilled painter, and says "I can see Maria," most people will correct them: they see a painting of Maria, not Maria. Yet the two cases are remarkably similar at the level of what reaches the eye. Both are flat, marked surfaces that carry information about Maria's appearance; both can be matched point-for-point to how she looked; and in neither case is Maria's present location visible. Meanwhile ordinary practice has no trouble countenancing "seeing" Maria through a window, in a mirror, through eyeglasses, or on a live security-camera monitor. So the contested boundary lies somewhere in the region of the live feed, the photograph, and the faithful painting — and the asymmetry is this: the photograph is treated as a way of seeing the person, while the painting is treated as a mere depiction of her.

Is anything available that justifies drawing the line exactly there, or is this a piece of folk optics that should be given up? The strongest candidate justification is something like this: a photograph is produced by the scene itself through a mechanical, largely belief-independent causal process, whereas a painting is produced through the painter's beliefs, intentions, and skill; so a photograph puts us in a genuinely perceptual relation to the past scene, while a painting only represents it. But that principle faces serious pressure from nearby cases. Photographs can be heavily retouched, lit, staged, and composed while remaining photographs. A painter can work with a camera lucida or trace projected light so faithfully that the result is nearly indistinguishable from a photograph and involves very little discretion. And a live video feed is not an imprint of a past scene at all, yet nearly everyone treats it as a way of seeing. I want the strongest version of the principle you can construct, tested against these cases, and an honest verdict: does it keep the photograph/painting boundary, move the boundary somewhere else, or collapse it? If you keep or move the boundary, say exactly what feature your principle makes do the work, and defend it against the hardest case you can think of.

As you answer, please mark which parts are relatively settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on perception and depiction, and do not present a contested view as the consensus; if you adopt a contested view, say that you are adopting it and why.
