Environmental, health, and safety regulators routinely decide whether a proposed rule or project is worthwhile by conducting a cost–benefit analysis. Analysts estimate what each affected person would be willing to pay to secure a benefit or avoid a harm, and what each would demand as compensation for a cost; they express these amounts in money and add them up across all affected persons. A regulation is judged to pass when the summed monetary benefits exceed the summed monetary costs, even though no money actually changes hands. The stated justification is the compensation test: the gainers could in principle fully compensate the losers and still come out ahead. In practice this forces agencies to trade one person’s avoided illness against another person’s extra year of life, or health gains in one population against compliance costs borne by another, as if the dollar amounts expressed a common unit of well-being.

What exactly does this practice presuppose about the possibility of comparing well-being, preference-satisfaction, or utility across different persons? Is monetization—measuring each person’s benefit by that person’s own willingness to pay—a way of avoiding interpersonal comparisons, or does it smuggle them in, given that a dollar can represent very different sacrifices for different people? Does a coherent cost–benefit analysis need full cardinal, interpersonally comparable utility, or only something weaker, such as the assumption that money is a neutral accounting device and that compensation is possible in principle? State precisely the view of interpersonal comparison that the practice commits its defenders to, if any; say which parts of the practice would remain defensible under the view that one person’s utility cannot be compared with another’s; and say what the practice would look like if it were rebuilt on the assumption that interpersonal comparisons are genuine but cannot be captured by dollar amounts.
