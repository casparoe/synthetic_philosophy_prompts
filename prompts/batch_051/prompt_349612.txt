I'm a philosophy of physics PhD student, and in three weeks I'm giving a lunch seminar at an institute where the audience is mostly working theoretical physicists: some high-energy people, some quantum information people, a couple of condensed matter folks, and exactly one other philosopher of science who will be sympathetic but is not going to save me. My thesis is deliberately provocative: that philosophy has been genuinely useful to the practice of science, not just in some hand-wavy context-of-discovery way, and that a lot of what physicists currently do — arguing about the interpretation of quantum mechanics, about whether string theory is even science, about what causality means in a lattice model, about whether fine-tuning is an explanatory vice — is philosophy under another name, and it gets worse when people refuse to admit they're doing it.

My rough sketch of the argument is: (1) the standard potshots from Weinberg and Hawking (and the birdwatching line usually attributed to Feynman) are aimed at a particular mid-century style of philosophy of science that prescribed how science should work from the armchair, which almost no philosopher of physics today endorses; (2) there are concrete cases where explicitly philosophical work changed what scientists did or argued about, like Mach's influence on Einstein, or the way Bell's theorem gets framed today; (3) the physicists in the room are already doing philosophy when they argue about whether a wavefunction is real or a calculational device; they just have a taboo against calling it that.

What I need from you is a prep sheet for the Q&A, because I've seen this room turn. The audience is not anti-intellectual — they read popular philosophy-lite books and care about conceptual clarity — but they genuinely believe academic philosophy is unfalsifiable, slow, and full of words that never cash out in a prediction, and they will say so in exactly those terms. Give me the objections they're most likely to raise and the ones that could actually wound me, phrased the way a particle physicist or a quantum information person would actually put them, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, with a short reply for each and a note on where my reply is weakest. Also tell me what I should concede up front so I don't look defensive, which single reply has to land for the whole talk to survive, and what the objection is I should be praying no one thinks to ask.
