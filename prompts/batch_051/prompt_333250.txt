I recently had a long argument with a friend about what newly created money actually is: whether issuing more money creates additional real resources and wealth, or merely creates additional nominal claims on a stock of real resources that is unchanged, and under what conditions new balances are experienced as liquidity rather than as lasting wealth. A concrete proposal emerged from that argument, and I want to understand how it would behave in practice rather than whether it is right in principle.

Consider the following mechanism. In an economy with an ordinary national currency, the central bank is required by statute to credit every adult resident with 500 units of newly created dated money at the start of each month. Dated money may be used to pay taxes and other government obligations at face value, and it is legal tender for settling debts denominated in the national currency unless the contract specifies payment in ordinary money. It expires ninety days after issue and is then void, so any unspent dated balances are extinguished. For spot purchases, sellers are free to accept it, reject it, or accept it at a discount, and banks are not required to accept it into ordinary-money accounts. Ordinary currency and deposits continue to exist alongside it and have no expiry.

Please walk through how this mechanism would actually play out. Trace how the velocity of dated money would change over its ninety-day life and what prices merchants would post depending on whether payment is made in dated or ordinary money. Consider how wage, rent, and debt contracts would be rewritten, and who would systematically end up holding dated balances when they expire, including what that implies about whether the scheme imposes its burden on those who are slower to spend or less informed. Examine how households and firms would try to convert dated money into durable claims before expiry and what that conversion demand would do to asset prices and the exchange rate. Identify where the mechanism would first be gamed, such as discount buyers of near-expiry balances, employers that pay wages late in the issuance cycle, or refundable transactions used to recycle dated money into ordinary money, and say whether any of these loopholes could be closed without destroying the purpose of the scheme. Finally, address the question that started the argument: under what conditions does this mechanism create additional spending and output, and under what conditions does it merely create nominal claims while leaving real wealth and real interest rates roughly unchanged?
