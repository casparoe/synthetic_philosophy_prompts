I just read a popular science piece about biological individuality and it left me feeling like I'd been sold an over-confident version of a reasonable idea. The author's summary of the new view is basically this: "Biologists used to say an organism was just a multicellular body running a single genome, but that view has basically collapsed. Natural selection doesn't care about cell walls or genetic similarity; it only needs variation, heredity, and reproduction, so whatever unit has those is an organism. That's why a cow plus its gut bacteria is now considered a single organism: the microbes are passed from mother to calf, and it's the whole cow-microbe system that actually survives and reproduces, not the cow alone. A Portuguese man o' war is the same pattern — it's a colony of zooids, but the colony is the unit that lives, grows, and reproduces, so it's an organism. Honeybee colonies are the clearest case, because worker bees are sterile and only the colony has a real lifecycle, with the workers functioning like body cells of a superorganism. The upshot is that an individual ant, a single bee, or even you (once you count your microbes) isn't, strictly speaking, an organism on the new view."

I don't know enough to know where the soft spots are — the author has a biology Ph.D., so I don't wanto push back on vibes alone. Could you go through it and tell me what a careful person working on this would accept, what is overstated or conflated, and whether the closing claim actually follows from the accurate parts? My own inclination is that the honeybee colony point is basically right and the cow-plus-microbiome part is where it gets loose.
