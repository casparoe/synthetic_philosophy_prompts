I am a novelist, not a linguist, and I have a conceptual knot I need untied before I put it in a novel. The book turns on a hearing woman who becomes a sign-language interpreter and has to defend, to her own family, the claim that her Deaf friends' signing is a genuine language and not an elaborate form of pantomime. I thought I understood that claim well enough to dramatize it, but when I tried to reconstruct the underlying reasoning, I built an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept, and I want to know where it goes wrong or what it costs to resist.

The argument runs as follows. The first premise is that American Sign Language is a full human language, on a par with English or Japanese, and not a pantomime, a code, or a manual translation of English. The second premise is that the only defensible basis for counting ASL as a language while excluding ordinary gesture is structural: a sign language has a finite lexicon of conventional signs and a combinatorial grammar that can build an open-ended number of sentences, with discrete recombinable units at the phonological, morphological, and syntactic levels and with the productivity that lets a person produce and understand novel sentences on the spot. The third premise is that these structural properties are not all-or-nothing; they come in degrees. The signed systems invented by deaf children who grow up without a sign-language model are not random pointing, since they contain stable signs with recurring meanings that get combined into structured, multi-sign expressions with something like a regular order, yet they are plainly simpler than ASL. A skilled mime, similarly, can produce sequences of movement that are productive, that express novel events, and that refer to absent people and things. So the very features that are supposed to separate a language from non-linguistic gesture show up, in attenuated form, in systems nobody calls languages. The fourth premise is that if ASL counts as a language in virtue of those features, then consistency requires counting homesign and skilled pantomime as languages too, since they share the features but to a lesser degree. The fifth premise is that this admission dissolves the distinction my character needs: language becomes a matter of degree along several dimensions rather than a difference in kind, with no principled point at which a system crosses over into being a language, and the concept stretches until it includes nearly any structured meaningful movement while saying very little.

For the novel I am committed to keeping the first premise; my protagonist's signing must come out as language, not mime, and I need that to be intellectually honest rather than sentimental. I am also inclined to accept that the line between language and gesture is graded, but I worry that this dissolves language into vacuity exactly when the character needs the term to carry weight. I would like you to tell me, as though writing a considered email, where this argument goes wrong, or whether it goes wrong at all; if it is valid, which premise is the least costly to give up, and what does giving it up cost the character's view of signing? For whichever step or premise you identify, say why it looked so plausible in the first place and what the alternative positions would have to pay to avoid the conclusion.
