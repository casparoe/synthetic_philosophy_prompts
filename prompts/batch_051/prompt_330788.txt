I am a novelist writing about the slow unmaking of a small coastal town, and the book keeps treating belonging as something real that is being lost. Before I let the novel commit to that, I want a working definition of belonging that gets the following cases right. I do not have a settled view on all of them.

- A midwife who has never left her mountain village, delivered most of its children, and is the first person called when someone dies.
- A traveler welcomed warmly into a week-long festival in a town where she knows no one, who leaves on Saturday and never thinks of it again.
- A refugee who has lived in a city for twenty-five years, raised children there, and speaks the language, but is still introduced to new neighbors as a guest from abroad; he considers the city home.
- The child of immigrants who has never visited her parents' country and cannot speak its language, yet says that country is where she belongs and feels foreign in the one where she was born.
- A man who spent a decade at a firm, felt entirely at home among his colleagues, and was told when he resigned that he had never really been family; within a year, none of them has been in touch.
- A recluse at the edge of a village who shuns every gathering and contributes nothing, but whom the villagers still check on in bad weather and speak of as one of their own.
- A weekly card game whose players know one another's histories and notice within two weeks when someone stops coming.
- A diffuse online movement whose members share a cause and a slogan but would not recognize one another on the street.

Please propose a definition of belonging, run it against each case, and say where it returns the wrong verdict or none; then revise it or defend the surprising verdicts. What I most need to settle is whether belonging is a matter of feeling, of being recognized, of shared activity, or of obligations that survive departure, since each option gives the novel a different account of what the town is losing.
