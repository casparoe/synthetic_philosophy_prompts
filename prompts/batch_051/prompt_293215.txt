I retired from experimental physics a few years ago and have since been reading philosophy of physics with the attention I could not give it while running a laboratory. The quantum suicide thought experiment, and the quantum-immortality conclusion sometimes drawn from it, seems to me a case in which an unnoticed step from the formalism to first-person prediction can do a great deal of work; I would like to examine that step with a variant I have not seen treated carefully.

Assume, for the purpose of the question, that the Everettian no-collapse interpretation is correct. In the present day, at a well-equipped research institute, the following experiment is conducted. One hundred volunteers, all technically competent and fully informed, each enter a separate sealed room. Over ten rounds, each volunteer's apparatus measures the z-component of a freshly prepared spin-1/2 particle that is in an equal superposition of spin-up and spin-down; if the result is spin-up, a fast-acting and irreversible lethal agent is administered; if spin-down, the volunteer hears only a click and survives. The rooms are isolated from one another, and the outside observers receive only the aggregate count of survivors at the end. The volunteers have agreed in advance on the branching structure and commit themselves to coherent self-locating reasoning about their own futures.

I want your reasoned prediction of how this plays out, with the argument made visible at every step. In particular: (1) What distribution of final survivors should the outside observers expect after the ten rounds, and why? (2) At the outset, and again after each round in which she is still alive, what should a given volunteer expect about the next round—and is that expectation experimentally distinguishable, from inside the room, from the bare assertion that the next measurement has a fifty-fifty chance of killing her? (3) After several rounds, any living volunteer can truthfully say that there is a branch in which she survives any finite number of further rounds. May she therefore expect to observe herself surviving them all, and may the living volunteers together expect to be in the branch in which all of them survive? If those two inferences come apart, identify exactly where the individual and the joint reasoning diverge. (4) For each link in your answer, separate what follows from the linear dynamics alone from what requires an additional assumption about personal identity, consciousness, or the meaning of rational expectation; say which predictions you regard as robust and which as guesses, and state what outside observation would confirm or refute them.
