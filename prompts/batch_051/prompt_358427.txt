I recently listened to a podcast whose host summarized the invention-of-tradition and imagined-communities literature in a single breath, as follows:

"Nations are recent constructions. Before the late eighteenth century there were kingdoms, empires, and villages, but no nations in our sense. Nationhood had to be built: mass print, standardized schooling, and newly invented rituals such as flags, anthems, and national holidays gave people the feeling of sharing an ancient past. That is what it means to call a nation an imagined community — a fiction held together by ceremony. Consequently, any claim to be an ancient nation is nationalist myth-making, and the deep traditions nations invoke are fake."

I find the debunking spirit attractive, but I have a nagging sense that this runs together things Hobsbawm and Anderson kept distinct, and that imagined means something more precise than fictitious. I cannot, however, localize the problems, and I want to know how much of this I should trust.

Is this a fair summary? Please give me an accuracy audit rather than a reading list. Go through it claim by claim and mark each as accurate, overstated, or distorted, saying in each case what Hobsbawm or Anderson would accept, qualify, or reject. Then assess whether the package's conclusion — that nations are fictions and their invoked traditions are fake — actually follows from the parts that pass muster, and state what a careful defender of either thinker would most object to.
