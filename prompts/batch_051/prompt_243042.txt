Since I began working with a proof assistant, I have been troubled by an asymmetry I cannot justify. In ordinary life, if someone says there is a way to repair a machine, or that some configuration satisfying a set of constraints exists, we expect them to be able to produce it or at least describe how to find it; an argument that merely shows that denying the existence leads to a contradiction would be treated as an admission that they have not actually found anything. Yet classical mathematics accepts such non-constructive existence proofs as genuine results: a theorem of the form "there exists an x such that P(x)" is counted as established even when the proof supplies no witness and no procedure for producing one. I know intuitionists and constructivists have long sided with the ordinary standard here, but I want to know whether the asymmetry can be defended. Is there a principled reason for it, or should the ordinary standard be exported to mathematics? If you defend the mathematical practice, defend it against the case where the existence claim is exactly what one needs: an existence proof with no witness is of no use when the object must actually be built.
