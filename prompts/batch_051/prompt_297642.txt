I'm a journalist covering economics and ideas, working on a piece about attempts to bring concepts from statistical physics into economics. In Ole Peters's 2019 Nature Physics article "The ergodicity problem in economics," he writes: "the prevailing formulations of economic theory — expected utility theory and its descendants — make an indiscriminate assumption of ergodicity." I want to test my reconstruction of the argument behind that sentence, because as I understand it, its conclusion is strong enough to make me suspect I have gone wrong somewhere. The reconstructed argument runs like this:

(1) An individual deciding whether to accept a risky prospect is deciding what happens to his or her wealth over time, not what happens across a hypothetical ensemble of parallel selves facing the same choice.

(2) Consider a repeated coin toss in which heads multiplies one's wealth by 1.5 and tails multiplies it by 0.6. The expected value of a single toss is positive, so standard expected-utility reasoning endorses taking the gamble.

(3) But the time-average growth rate is not the expectation: the repeated gamble's growth rate is less than zero, so an individual who keeps taking it will, with increasing probability, see wealth shrink rather than grow.

(4) Mainstream economic theory treats the time average and the ensemble expectation as interchangeable, which is precisely the indiscriminate ergodicity assumption Peters identifies.

(5) If Peters is right, then expected utility theory gives systematically bad advice to a single agent facing non-ergodic multiplicative dynamics, which covers a large part of real economic decision-making.

(6) But a theory of rational choice that tells a single agent to accept a sequence of bets that almost surely ruins that agent cannot be a correct general theory of individual rational choice.

I find all six steps individually plausible, but the conclusion seems to prove too much: it would mean that the standard apparatus of expected utility theory is not just incomplete but actively misleading across much of its domain. I can see several places where one might push back, but I want a diagnosis rather than a menu. Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? In your answer, please identify the specific step or inference you would reject, explain why that step looked plausible, and say which consideration would change your mind or where your diagnosis is most likely to fail.
