I've been trying to get clear on a worry about the teleosemantic program for naturalizing mental content, and I'd like your help working through whether its central argument survives a certain kind of case. Here is the argument as I understand it. A purely causal-informational account of content seems unable to explain how a state could mean something determinate rather than meaning everything it regularly co-occurs with, and unable to explain how the state could misrepresent. A frog's fly-detector, for example, can be triggered by a small dark pellet as reliably as by a fly; covariation alone seems to leave the content indeterminate. The teleosemantic move is to fix content by function: the detector mechanism was selected over its history because, under the conditions that explained its selection, its firing correlated with flies and downstream consumers used that correlation to guide successful snapping. Its proper function is therefore to indicate flies; in the pellet case it simply misrepresents. Content, on this view, is fixed by what the state was selected for, not by what it currently covaries with.

Now consider a case with no biological selection history and no designer assigning roles to states one by one: a vision model trained end to end by gradient descent, from random initialization, to answer questions about images. Once trained, its hidden units fire selectively for edges, object parts, scene categories, and the like. Those responses carry information, and the network's overall success appears to depend on some of them. Yet the training signal is a scalar loss applied globally. Nothing in the process selected a hidden unit because it indicated an edge; at most, whole networks survived or died by aggregate task performance, and the objective rewards loss reduction rather than accurate indication as such. So the function of any particular state, if it has one, was never assigned to it piecemeal by a history of selection-for.

Does the teleosemantic argument survive this development? I want to know which exact premise is under pressure: the premise that functions must be selected piecemeal, the premise that only a history of selection-for can fix content, or something else. Is the honest verdict that such hidden states lack representational content; must the account be extended so that a training objective or a designer's global intention can bequeath functions; or was the real work never selection history but something about current causal or predictive role? I care about the fate of the argument, not the engineering details, and I do not need citations or further reading.

- "The argument restated" (about 150–250 words): lay out the determinate-content and misrepresentation problems and how the selected-function reply is supposed to solve them.
- "Where the new case bites" (about 250–350 words): identify precisely which premise or inference the gradient-descent case pressures, and say how it pressures it.
- "Ways to repair the argument" (about 250–350 words): evaluate the main options for extending or restricting teleosemantics to handle the case, including their costs and what each would commit the view to.
- "Bottom line" (about 100–200 words): give your verdict on whether the argument survives, needs repair, or survives only in a restricted form, and state the strongest available version of the position in one or two sentences.
