Question: Explain what happens in Carroll's dialogue between Achilles and the Tortoise, and what, if anything, it shows about the justification of deductive inference.

Student answer: In the dialogue, Achilles wants the Tortoise to accept a conclusion Z from two premises A and B. A is the general principle that two things each equal to a third are equal to one another, and B says that two sides of a triangle are each equal to the same third thing. The Tortoise accepts A and B, but he won't accept the hypothetical C — that whenever A and B are both true, Z has to be true too — even after Achilles writes it down as a new premise. Once C is in the notebook, though, he still refuses to move to Z; he demands a further premise D saying that whenever A, B, and C are all true, Z has to be true too, and on and on. The lesson is that no list of premises can force an inference: a rule like modus ponens is not itself a premise, and treating it as one just generates another step that needs the rule applied. So what the tortoise really shows is that deduction cannot be rationally justified at all. At bottom, our acceptance of basic inference rules rests on nothing more than shared practical convention or habit.
