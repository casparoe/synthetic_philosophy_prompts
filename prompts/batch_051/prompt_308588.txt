I'm a novelist, and I have been asked to contribute a short piece to a writers' workshop on whether novelists can be said to learn moral things from their sources and from their readers. I am partway through a novel set in a community I was not raised in, so the question is no longer abstract to me; it has become part of how I write, revise, and negotiate with the people I am writing about. In preparing the piece, I found that I hold five commitments that I have never checked against one another, and I would like them audited before I have to present them in front of other writers. Here they are, as close as I can get to what I actually practice:

(1) I think moral knowledge can be acquired from testimony. If someone I trust, who knows a situation far better than I do, tells me that something done there is wrong, I treat that as a reason — sometimes a sufficient reason — to believe it is wrong, in roughly the way I would accept a knowledgeable friend's word about a historical event.

(2) But I also think there is something second-rate about moral deference that has no parallel in ordinary epistemic deference. A person who takes over a moral view wholesale from an admired teacher, without working through it themselves, seems to me to have failed in a way that a person does not fail when they accept an astronomer's account of a distant galaxy.

(3) In my own practice, I treat sensitivity readers as authorities about the moral character of my fiction. When a reader from a community I am writing about tells me that a scene is demeaning or harmful in a way I cannot see, I generally revise it; and I encourage other novelists to do the same, even when none of us can articulate from the inside why the criticism is right.

(4) I hold that my moral perception is systematically distorted by my own position and my own interests, and that the main remedy is to listen to — and sometimes to take on — the moral testimony of people whose lives and histories differ sharply from mine, until my own judgments come into line with what they report.

(5) I think real moral understanding cannot be outsourced: to genuinely understand something morally, I need to be able to give the reasons that make it true, not merely to hold the right view.

I want a consistency audit, not a lecture on which of these is correct. Go through the set and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be once each commitment is stated carefully; rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad; for each genuine tension, say which commitment would have to be revised or abandoned and what the cheapest repair would cost; and then tell me which single revision would do the most to make the whole set tenable. After your full discussion, close with a single line in the form \answer{...} naming the one commitment you would recommend revising first.
