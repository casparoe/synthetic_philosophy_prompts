I can't tell weather my friend's argument against advance directives is sound or just proves too much. He runs it like this. (1) A decision by me at one time can bind me at a later time only if the earlier and later selves are the same person. (2) By the time I have serious dementia, I will share almost no memories, plans, or character traits with the person who signed the directive. (3) On the psychological continuity view of personal identity, what makes two selves the same person is overlapping chains of psychological connections of the sort listed in (2). (4) So the later self is either a different person from the signer, or only borderline the same person. (5) Therefore the signer has no authority to bind the later self. I said this proves too much: I also share few memories or plans with my eight-year-old self, and someone prosecuted at 50 for a crime committed at 18 may have changed almost as completely as a dementia patient has, yet we still treat those earlier selves as binding. My friend says those practices are also in trouble, but he won't say which premise he would give up. Where exactly does his argument go wrong — and if it doesn't, which of those ordinary practices should we actually revise?
