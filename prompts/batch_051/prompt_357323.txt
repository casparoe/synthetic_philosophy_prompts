I've been leading a small reading group on the second Untimely Meditation — the one on history and life — and we keep getting stuck on whether the three attitudes toward the past he distinguishes really exhaust the field. As I have them: monumental history reaches into the past for exemplary human greatness, to make great things feel possible again; antiquarian history is the preserving, curator-like love of the past for its own sake, a kind of piety toward what one has inherited; and critical history drags the past before a tribunal, condemns whatever in it blocks life, and clears space for the present to act. Those are, as far as I can tell, the only three the text actually works with, and every candidate I've tried has collapsed into one of them or a blend.

What I want is a fourth way of relating to the past that is genuninely different from all three — I don't mean a sub-variety (a monumental history built on defeats instead of victories) or a mixture (an antiquarian stance with a dash of criticism), but different in what it wants from the past, in the need it answers, and in the kind of authority it lets the past have over the present. If you can build one, describe it concretely enough that I could recognize someone actually doing it, and then judge it by Nietzsche's own standard: would it serve life, or would it feed the historical sickness he's worried about? And if no coherent fourth exists, tell me that instead of inventing one, and show me why the three already carve up the space.
