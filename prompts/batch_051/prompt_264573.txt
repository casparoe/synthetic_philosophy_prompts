I am a novelist, not a philosopher. Over the past fifteen years I have published three works of historical fiction, and in each I have found myself making, under deadline pressure, a series of decisions for which I have never had a clear method: when is it permissible to invent dialogue, motives, or private scenes for a real person who is dead, and when does doing so wrong that person or their living descendants? I am not asking about the law — in England and Wales the dead cannot be defamed, so far as I understand it, and I know the position varies elsewhere — but about the obligations that remain once the lawyer signs off. What I want is a standing procedure I can run with respect to a named historical figure before I commit to a scene, both to keep my own choices consistent and to defend them afterwards to my editor, to descendants who object, and to the historians who review the book.

Please write it as an ordered set of questions or tests a novelist can actually run, with the questions in the order in which they should be asked and with the points where the procedure stops being rule-like and becomes a matter of judgement marked as such. It should distinguish at least the following, though you may add or cut as needed: adding undocumented circumstances to a documented life; representing a person’s inner states and motives; inventing events that contradict the known record; using a real name where a fictional name would serve as well; and the different ways a choice can cause harm — to the dead person, to living descendants, and to readers who will mistake the novel for history. The procedure should not assume its user is a historian, and it should be usable in the few days of a final edit.

Run the procedure on three decisions of the kind I face, lightly disguised but realistic: (i) a Victorian surgeon, well documented in public records, whom I want to show in an invented private quarrel with his brother over a patient’s refusal of treatment; (ii) a nineteenth-century servant mentioned in a single court record, whom I want to give an invented interior life and perhaps a name; and (iii) a controversial suffragist whose public life is thoroughly documented, whom I want to “round out” by inventing an adulterous affair. At least one of these should come out against what the novelist would prefer, and the procedure should say what to do then rather than quietly softening the rule. After the procedure and the applications, tell me how confident you are in the rules you have proposed, and single out the one point where you are least sure of your ground. Keep the whole response to at most 1,100 words.
