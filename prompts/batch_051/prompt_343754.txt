Been thinking about the old atomist arguments for atoms and void—the ones that go like this: motion needs empty space to move into, so since things plainly move, there must be void; and if you keep dividing a body you eventually hit bits that are solid all the way through, with no void inside them, so they can't be split, and those are the atoms. Fine. But it feels like quantum field theory undercuts exactly the parts that made the argument work. The vacuum isn't empty; it's a structured lowest-energy state of fields, with fluctuations and everything, and "particles" are just excitations of those fields, not hard impenetrable nuggets. Even impenetrability turns out to be an interaction effect rather than a primitive feature of matter. So my question: does the atomist argument survive quantum field theory in any interesting form, or is it dead? Work through it premise by premise—which exact premise does QFT bear on, is there a weaker restatement that still does the old work (e.g., is the spacetime/matter-field distinction enough to count as something "void-like"?), and in the end should we say the atomists were wrong about the details but onto somthing, or just wrong? Dont hand-wave that physics moved on; I want the argument taken seriously.
