Yesterday I spent a long evening arguing with a friend about a question that looks trivial and turns out not to be: is 2 an element of 4? I came away holding two positions at once.

I am genuinely attracted to mathematical structuralism. On that view, the natural numbers are not independently constituted objects whose internal makeup we might discover; they are places in a structure, individuated solely by their relations—successor, order, addition—and any collection of objects arranged in the right sequence can play the role of the numbers. One of the view's great virtues is that it dissolves the embarrassment that set theory identifies 2 with different sets depending on one's conventions. In the standard von Neumann construction, 2 = {0,1} and 4 = {0,1,2,3}, so 2 is an element of 4. In the Zermelo construction, each number is the singleton of its predecessor, and 2 is not an element of 4. Structuralism says there is no deeper fact that decides between them: the sentence "2 ∈ 4" has no answer until one has arbitrarily chosen a set-theoretic proxy, because the number structure itself comes with no membership relation among its positions.

And yet I keep returning to the intuition that there is a fact of the matter here. The question "is 2 an element of 4?" does not feel like asking whether the number 2 is blue, or whether Wednesday is heavier than Thursday. Once I have fixed the von Neumann construction, I can see determinately that 2 ∈ 4; once I have fixed the Zermelo construction, I can see determinately that it is not. But those cannot both report facts about one and the same object, number 2, unless the number has no determinate identity—and that conclusion feels like a price at least as high as the arbitrariness it was meant to avoid. My friend says this is the residue of a pre-structural Platonism that pictures numbers as little objects with hidden inner structure. I am not sure.

What I want is a negotiated verdict, not a vote for one side. Should the structuralist debunk my intuition by explaining why "2 ∈ 4" is a representation-relative pseudo-question that merely feels sharper than a category mistake? Or should the intuition push the structuralist to amend the view, holding that representation-relative mathematical statements can be determinately true or false relative to a chosen reduction, so that the fact of the matter is preserved rather than denied? Is there a third reading on which both positions are correct? Walk me through what each resolution costs, and what principle would tell us whether "2 ∈ 4" is more like "is 2 blue?" or more like "is 2 less than 3?"
