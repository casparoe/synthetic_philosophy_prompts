I'm designing a unit on philosophy of language and keep coming back to the film Arrival as a natural companion to Davidson's radical interpretation, and I'd like a serious critical reading that runs in both directions. The relevant idea is this: an interpreter confronts a speaker with no dictionary, no bilingual informant, and no prior shared language, and must build up a semantics from observable behavior and the objects and events in the shared environment; charity enters because the interpreter has to assume the speaker is by and large rational and mostly right about the shared world, or interpretation cannot get started. The film looks like a rare dramatization of exactly that predicament, but it also seems to endorse a strong claim Davidson is usually read as rejecting: that the language you inhabit can determine how you experience time and what you can think.

What I'd like is a reading of Arrival through radical interpretation that is faithful in both directions. Where does the film's interpreting process (the physical demonstrations, the shared referents, the slow accumulation of vocabulary and then structure) genuinely illustrate Davidson's picture, including places where it shows something his abstractions underplay, such as trust, urgency, and the political pressure on the interpreter? Where does the film part company with the picture, especially once the heptapods' written language is presented as simultaneous rather than linear and Louise's competence in it begins to give her access to what she experiences as memories of the future? Is the heptapod language the kind a radical interpreter could in principle interpret, or is the film quietly introducing the very idea of a radically alien conceptual scheme that Davidson thinks cannot be made sense of from inside interpretation? Does the film suggest charity has to be extended in a way Davidson did not anticipate, not by assuming the speakers are mostly right by our lights but by becoming able to adopt theirs, and if so, is that still charity or something else?

At the end, please mark where your reading is most likely to fail: name the interpretive claim about the film, or the premise attributed to Davidson, that would most change your overall assessment if it went the other way, and say what evidence or argument would make you revise the reading.
