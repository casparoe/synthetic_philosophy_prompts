I have spent the past year preoccupied with self-sufficiency, and I want a candid assessment of the idea this has produced.

I live in a country that recently suffered an abrupt shortage of a staple good when a neighboring state restricted exports; for several weeks ordinary supply chains failed, and my household got through it only because we had kept a deep pantry more out of habit than conviction. Around the same time, I ended a long relationship I had stayed in largely because I could not imagine being self-sufficient outside it. Since then I have been trying, in small ways, to make myself harder to cut off: financially, practically, socially.

Out of this I have come to think the debate between defenders and critics of self-sufficiency and autarky is misframed. It conflates two ideals. The first I call state autarky: the condition of actually not depending on others — producing most of what one consumes, refusing exchange, standing alone as a matter of policy. The second I am provisionally calling walk-away autarky: the maintained capacity to exit a dependence without ruin even when one expects never to exercise it. My claim is that most contemporary defenders of self-sufficiency, whether persons or nations, are not really aiming at state autarky. They are buying an option. The person who keeps hens and a generator is not trying to live outside the grid or the supermarket; she is trying to make it survivable if they fail, or if she leaves them. A nation that preserves a costly domestic capability it could import more cheaply is not confused about comparative advantage; it is paying a premium for a credible exit.

Two further claims come with the distinction. First, the standard efficiency arguments against autarky — comparative advantage, the gains from trade, the waste of duplicating capabilities — strike state autarky but leave walk-away autarky mostly intact, because walk-away autarky does not replace exchange; it pays a standing cost for an option on exchange's failure. Second, walk-away autarky has a cost structure its defenders rarely acknowledge: the option is expensive, it decays if it is never tested, and in personal life keeping one foot out the door may corrode the very relationships one insists on remaining free to leave.

It would not surprise me if this collapses into the economists' notions of resilience, optionality, or the right of exit. So tell me honestly: is walk-away autarky a real concept, or a metaphor with a balance sheet attached? Has it already been worked out under another name? And where does it break — what does it wrongly count as self-sufficiency, and which side of the original debate does the distinction actually favor?
