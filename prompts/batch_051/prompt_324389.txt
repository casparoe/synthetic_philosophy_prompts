I've been going down a rabbit hole about which curves mathematicians were willing to count as real. The old Greek rule, as I understand it, was that a curve or figure only properly exists if you can actually build it with a ruler and compasses in finitely many steps. So a circle is fine, but an ellipse is not, because a ruler and compasses can't draw one exactly. Then at some point in the 1600s people started saying no, that's too narrow: if an equation defines a curve, then the curve is legitimate even if no finite construction with ruler and compasses will ever trace it — a sine wave, a spiral, an ellipse defined by an equation. That strikes me as a much bigger change then it looks, almost a change in what "exists" means rather than just a new technique.

So here's the counterfactual I can't stop thinking about. Imagine that around 1700 the main scientific academies of western Europe somehow agree on, and actually enforce, a strict rule: only objects constructible with ruler and compasses in finitely many steps count as legitimate mathematical objects. Anything else that the newer coordinate geometry or the calculus produces — curves, lengths, areas, solutions — is demoted to useful notation, a calculating device for practical jobs like navigation or surveying, but not something that genuinely exists in mathematics. You can still use the new methods, but you can't prove things about their non-constructible outputs the way you can about constructible ones.

Do the academies hold the line, or does the rule get quietly redefined until it means nothing — and through which pressures? Where in your reasoning are you genuinely confident, and where are you guessing? For each prediction, what would have to be different for it to go wrong?
