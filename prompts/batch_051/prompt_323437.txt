I have been reading about Dōgen's conception of time as part of a larger project on Buddhist metaphysics, and I keep tripping over a gloss in an introductory survey of East Asian thought. The author summarizes Dōgen's notion of being-time (uji) roughly as follows: Dōgen rejected both the idea that time is an external container in which events happen and the idea that time flows past like a river; rather, every existing thing is itself a time-being, a momentary configuration that carries the whole of time within it. From this, the author says, it follows for Dōgen that the past and the future are not real in the way the timeless present is, that the ordinary distinction between earlier and later is an illusion, and that all times ultimately collapse into one. The author then extracts the practical moral that, since each moment is already complete, there is nothing to accomplish; sitting practice merely helps one notice this.

I find the opening plausible and useful, but the slide to a timeless present and the concluding quietism bother me — they seem to replace Dōgen's careful talk about the relation between being and time with something closer to a doctrine of pure presence, and then to draw a practical conclusion Dōgen himself would resist. I am not sure how much to trust that reaction. Could you audit this summary? Specifically: which of its claims a careful reader of Dōgen would accept, which are familiar simplifications or outright misreadings, where the author silently imports assumptions foreign to Dōgen, and whether the final claim about there being nothing to accomplish follows from whatever is accurate in the gloss.
