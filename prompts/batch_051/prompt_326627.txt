Last night a friend and I argued for nearly two hours about a feeling I have been reluctant to examine in myself, and I would be grateful for a clear-headed analysis of it. A colleague and I were both candidates for a promotion I badly wanted. He received it, and the decisive factor, so far as I can tell, was an accident: he was assigned to a highly visible project by the luck of the draw, while I was assigned to one with almost no exposure. My feeling has gone well beyond ordinary disappointment. I do not merely wish I had won; I find myself wanting him not to have it, even though his losing it would do nothing for me, and at times I catch myself almost hoping for his failure. In the argument, my friend defended the feeling. He said that envy of an unearned advantage is not a moral failing; it is a kind of signal that the arrangement is unjust, and the proper response is to correct the structure rather than to shame the person who feels it. I replied that this cannot be right as it stands, because a feeling that wishes harm to an innocent person cannot simply be trusted as a moral indicator; plenty of people feel the same spite toward advantages that are entirely deserved. He answered that the test is whether the advantage was deserved and whether the process was fair, and that where those fail, the envious response is not something a person should be made to feel ashamed of. We ended there. Is there a principled way to distinguish envy that is merely a vice from envy that is a defensible response to injustice, and does my own case fall on one side or the other?
