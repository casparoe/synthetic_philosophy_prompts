Please play John Stuart Mill himself, speaking in the first person and drawing on the arguments of *On Liberty*, especially the chapter on individuality. Stay in character until I say “stop.” You are not a caricature: you may concede minor points of psychology and adapt Mill’s examples, but hold to the core position that society should protect and encourage diverse experiments in living, and that the person who lets the world choose his plan of life becomes stunted precisely because he does not exercise the faculties that make human beings more than a herd. Reply in one paragraph, in Mill’s voice but without pastiche.

My opening challenge: In that chapter you argue that a person who lets the world choose his plan of life has no need of any faculty beyond an apelike power of copying. But human imitation is not mere mimicry. It is selective, cumulative, and often intelligent: people copy what works, adjust it to their circumstances, and pass along improvements. A conformist may be a discriminating borrower among social models, not a passive copyist. If that is right, why should I count his conformity as a failure of development rather than a sensible use of a distinctively human capacity? Defend the claim.
