I run a small "weird logic/philsophy problems" lunch session at my company. Most people are engineers, no formal philosophy background at all, and I don't want to lecture. I'd like to run an activity around Gregory Kavka's toxin puzzle. The basic setup: a billionaire offers you a million dollars tomorrow morning if, at midnight tonight, you genuinely intend to drink a nasty but harmless toxin tomorrow afternoon. You don't actually have to drink it to get the money; the payout happens once you've formed the intention. The catch is you can't just decide to intend something if you already know you'll never do it.

Can you design a 45-ish minute activity for 8–12 people that makes them feel this puzzle themselves, before anyone names it? I can offer a real small reward, like a $5 coffee card, for forming the intention. The unpleasant act could be something mild but genuinely unappealing, like drinking a shot of diluted hot sauce or eating a spoonful of Marmite. The rules should not require them to actually follow through to get the reward; the only thing that matters is whether they genuinely intended at the earlier time. But the hard part is verification. How would I set that up? Maybe they have to write something private, or do some kind of self-check, or maybe the debrief is where we separate the people who honestly couldn't form the intention from the people who just said "yeah I intend" and took the reward.

I want participants to run into the weird feeling: either they can't honestly form the intention, or they lie to themselves, or they try to hack it with a side bet or by getting someone else to threaten them. The debrief should map each of those reactions onto the actual parts of Kavka's argument, especially the point that intentions aren't under direct voluntary control and whether it's rational to intend something you know you won't do. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong when this is run (people not taking it seriously, someone actually doing the gross act out of stubbornness, etc.) and how I should handle it without ruining the exercise. And be honest about which part of the puzzle a live group can't reproduce and that I'll have to fill in myself.
