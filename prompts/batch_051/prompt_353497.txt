Two philosophers in our working group have spent a year defending rival answers to the equality-of-what question. One defends equality of resources in the broadly Dworkinian sense: the metric of justice is the bundle of impersonal and personal resources a person holds, assessed through an idealized auction of all resources plus hypothetical insurance against unchosen misfortune, with a sharp distinction between ambitions, for which people are responsible, and endowments, for which they are not. The other defends equality of capabilities in the broadly Senian sense: the metric is a person's capability set, the combinations of functionings she can effectively achieve, with resources treated as mere means whose value is mediated by personal, social, and environmental conversion factors.

Their working relationship has become collaborative rather than combative. They have found that they agree substantially against welfarism: both take expensive tastes, cheap tastes, and adaptive preferences to be decisive objections to equality of welfare, and both reject raw income or primary goods as the metric of egalitarian justice. A genuine disagreement remains about the correct currency and about the role of responsibility, but both suspect part of it may be verbal.

Write the joint statement they should co-sign at the end of a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Assume the reader is familiar with the debate but uncommitted. The statement must include:

1. A numbered agreed-claims section that is neither vacuous nor slanted: their shared rejection of welfarism and its grounds, their shared rejection of income and primary-goods metrics, and any positive constraints on an adequate metric that both accept.

2. A disagreements table. For each disagreement, give the neutral formulation followed by the formulation each side would accept for its own view. Cover at least: (a) whether the equalisandum should be resources or capabilities; (b) whether the ambition/endowment distinction is stable enough to do the responsibility work the resourcist needs, given that preferences and insurance valuations are themselves shaped by unjust background conditions; (c) whether disabilities and other conversion disadvantages are adequately handled by ex ante compensation in resources, or require the metric itself to change; (d) whether the capability approach can be operationalized without a canonical list of central capabilities, and what it would lose if it had to adopt one.

3. A cruxes section. Identify the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side, and attach to each crux a concrete case both accept bears on it. For each case, record each side's prediction in advance. Use at least: an impairment case involving equal resources but unequal ability to convert them into mobility; an expensive-taste case constructed so that it does not beg the question against either side; and a case in which a person's expectations have adapted to deprivation.

4. A closing assessment of how much of the residual dispute is verbal. In particular, address whether a sufficiently broad construal of resources absorbs the capability theory's conversion factors and thereby collapses into something close to capability, and whether the disagreement that remains is best located in the responsibility cut, in the specification of a capability list, or in something else.
