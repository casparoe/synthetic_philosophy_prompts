I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and my beat is machine learning and natural language processing. I'm preparing a long feature on what word embeddings have actually shown us about the nature of meaning, and I want to make sure I don't fool myself in either direction. I know the technical vocabulary well: static embeddings such as word2vec and GloVe, contextual embeddings such as BERT, the distributional hypothesis, context windows, masked language modeling objectives, cosine similarity, vector arithmetic of the king-minus-man-plus-woman kind, token-level versus type-level representation, and the fact that real embedding spaces are anisotropic, meaning the vectors are not spread uniformly in all directions.

I'd like you to do a systematic translation exercise for me, and then report what did not survive the translation.

Take three philosophical pictures of word meaning and restate each one as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary of embeddings and their training objectives:

1. The referential picture: words mean what they stand for, and grasping a meaning is at least partly a matter of what the word picks out. Include the externalist thought experiment of Twin Earth, where a physically indistinguishable duplicate of an English speaker means something different by 'water' because the local clear liquid is XYZ rather than H2O, and include the related idea that a linguistic community can defer to experts for the extension of terms like 'elm' or 'molybdenum.'

2. The use picture: meanings are grounded in the ways words are deployed and answered to in a language — the regularities of the moves one may make with them, including what applying a word licenses and what it rules out. Include the inferentialist version, which says the core of meaning is the pattern of material inferences between sentences — for example, that 'this is copper' licenses 'this conducts electricity' — rather than mere associations of co-occurrence.

3. The structuralist differential picture: the value or significance of a sign is not positive but wholly relational. A sign has significance in virtue of how it differs from the other signs in the system, not because it is a label attached to a thing. Meanings are contrastive positions in a closed system of signs.

For each of the three, give me the clean translation into embedding terms: which claims map onto statements about vector geometry, co-occurrence statistics, training objectives, or token prediction, and which claims map only with distortion. Be explicit about what you had to fudge, idealize, or quietly reinterpret to make the translation work.

Then give me the residue report. I want a clear list of the claims in each picture that have no counterpart in the embedding vocabulary, and for each one your judgment: does the failure to translate reveal something about meaning that embeddings genuinely lack — reference, truth, normativity, something else — or does it reveal that the philosophical claim was doing less precise work than its proponents think? I especially want you to address whether the embedding vocabulary is a neutral target into which the three positions can be translated, or whether it silently presupposes the use picture from the start, since embeddings are built from co-occurrence under the distributional hypothesis.

Please be frank about the places where the translation can go either way depending on technical choices — for instance, static versus contextual embeddings, or type-level versus token-level semantics — and do not resolve an ambiguity by picking whichever reading makes the philosophy come out looking better.
