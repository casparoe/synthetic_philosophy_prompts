I have recently been keeping a detailed symptom diary for a recurring pain condition, recording each episode with words like throbbing and stabbing, and urging myself to note whether today's pain is the same kind as Tuesday's. The practice has pushed me into Wittgenstein's private language argument, which I find more persuasive than I expected. As I understand it, the argument is that a sign cannot be given meaning by a private act of attending to one's own sensation, because such an act supplies no independent criterion of correctness: from within the private ceremony there is no distinction between applying the sign correctly and merely seeming to apply it correctly, and where that distinction collapses, the sign is not functioning as a word at all. Meaning, on this view, lives in a shared practice with public checks, not in an inner baptism of a sensation.

And yet I cannot shake the intuition that when I call today's pain the same kind as yesterday's, I am checking something real and private — a felt quality that only I can inspect and that holds my words to account. If I kept a sign for that quality and later began using it for a clearly different sensation, I would want to say I had made a mistake, and that the mistake was mine to detect in a way no observer could share. I am not looking for a declaration about which side wins. I want to know what would have to turn out to be true for the intuition to be the thing I should abandon, what would have to turn out to be true for the argument to be the thing I should narrow or give up, and whether there is a reading of the argument that lets private sensation constrain my vocabulary without falling back into the inner ceremony the argument targets.
