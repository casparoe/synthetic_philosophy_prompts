I write about the sciences and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am working on a piece about a claim I keep hearing from decision theorists and philosophers of action: that there is a deep tension between actively deciding what to do and assigning probabilities to one's own possible choices. As the slogan goes, one crowds out the other. The strong version of the claim is that you cannot coherently assign a probability to a choice you are still making; the act is either something you author or something you observe, not both. The weaker and more tractable version is that the attempt to predict oneself is not neutral: forecasting your own choice, especially under incentives for accuracy, changes the decision process and pushes you from the deliberative stance into a kind of self-observation. I want to bring this conceptual-sounding claim into contact with evidence, and I want to be honest with readers about where the evidence runs out. Please do not give me citations or a reading list; give me the experimental logic itself, self-contained.

Here is how I would frame the claim. Take a person facing a binary choice, say whether to take a smaller payment immediately or a larger payment in a month. In the deliberative stance they ask which action is best; the act is something they are settling. In the predictive stance they ask which action they will perform; the act is an event about which they might hold a degree of belief. The claim I want to test is that the predictive stance is loaded: assigning a probability to one's own choice makes people behave a little more like spectators of themselves and a little less like authors of the choice. To count the claim as supported I would need to see not merely that self-prediction is difficult or badly calibrated, nor merely that asking people to predict changes what they choose. I would need evidence that forecasting one's own choice specifically suppresses something recognizable as deliberation, while forecasting someone else's choice does not.

Please propose the best concrete test of that claim. You may assume I have access to an online subject pool and can run several conditions with real incentives. The test should isolate what is special about the self in self-prediction, ideally by including a condition in which people predict another participant's choice rather than their own. It should also specify measures of deliberation that do not rely on introspection alone: response time, optional information search, rate of overcoming an intuitive but wrong response, sensitivity of choices to independently elicited values, decision regret, or similar measures. Please say exactly what pattern of results would count in favor of the claim, what pattern would count against it, and where you would set the bar. Do not give me a test that merely measures whether people's choices match their predictions; that sounds circular. I want to know whether the predictive stance changes the decision process itself. Resist the temptation to conclude that the claim is purely conceptual and therefore untestable; I want the best partial operationalization, and then the honest remainder.

Finally, say what the test could never settle even if the results came out cleanly. My own suspicions are that no experiment can tell us whether there exists a stable pre-existing probability of a person's own choice that measurement merely reveals, or whether the rational-actor question is fully separate from the empirical one, or whether the two stances are impossible to occupy at once as opposed to merely difficult for ordinary humans. But do not let my hunches limit you.

Structure the answer as numbered sections, in this order and with roughly these lengths: 1. The claim, made precise (about 150 words); 2. A concrete test (about 400 words, with a compact table of conditions and measures welcome); 3. How to read the results (about 250 words); 4. What the test cannot settle (about 200 words); 5. Bottom line for a magazine audience (about 100 words).
