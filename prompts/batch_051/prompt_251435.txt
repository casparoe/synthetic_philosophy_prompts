I'm a PhD student in philosophy of physics, and I keep going back and forth on an argument I scribbled down while reading about whether causation belongs in the fundamental picture. It goes like this:

(1) The fundamental dynamical laws of physics are time-reversal symmetric — they don't distinguish past from future in the way a cause-effect pair seems to. (I know the weak interaction breaks this at some tiny level, but set that aside; the point is that nothing in the core dynamics looks like cause-before-effect.)
(2) Causation, as we ordinarily understand it, is temporally asymmetric: causes precede their effects, and we don't run the same story backwards and call the effect the cause.
(3) If causation were a fundamental feature of the world, then the fundamental dynamics would have to encode this asymmetry somewhere.
(4) But by (1), they don't.
(5) So causation is not fundamental — it's a coarse-grained, projectible, or perspectival notion, not part of the basic furniture.

The conclusion feels too quick to me, but I can't decide wether the bad step is (3) or something earlier. Step (2) also seems less obvious than it looks: maybe the asymmetry is in us, not in the world. I'd like someone to go through this properly. Please say exactly which step, if any, fails, and what the correct version of it would be. And while you're at it, I'd appreciate if you flagged which parts of this debate are actually settled among people working on causation versus which parts are live disputes — I keep hearing confidently stated but opposite views from different camps.

Format: please give exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
