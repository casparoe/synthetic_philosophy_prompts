I work on the program review committee of a conservation trust that funds large wildlife interventions and sometimes issues public statements opposing them. Over the past two years we have begun receiving proposals that involve genetic changes to wild populations that are meant to be self-sustaining or effectively irreversible: a gene drive to eliminate invasive rats from an island seabird colony, a de-extinction attempt for a recently lost bird, genetic rescue of an inbred species, and similar projects. The committee meets quarterly, reviews fifteen to twenty proposals a year, and must produce a written recommendation within a two-day meeting. It includes ecologists, a lawyer, and several donors, but no one trained in moral philosophy. Our staff prepare a short technical summary for each proposal, which we take as accurate, and the full board makes the final decision but is not bound by our recommendation. The stakes feel high in both directions: endorsing an irreversible intervention that goes wrong would destroy public trust and could damage ecosystems, but declining one could let a conservation crisis spiral. I want a reusable procedure, not a one-off set of verdicts.

Please write a decision procedure the whole committee could actually run on any proposal of this kind. Ground it in what you take to be the defensible core of conservation philosophy about irreversibility, precaution, the value of species and ecosystems, and the difference between restoring something and replacing it—including whether irreversibility should operate as a veto or merely as one factor among others—but keep the theory behind the scenes. The deliverable should be a checklist or ordered set of questions, with each step saying what counts as a sufficient answer to move on, where the procedure stops with a no, where it stops with a yes, and where it deliberately hands the decision to the full board because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. Be explicit about which distinctions survive the simplification and which judgment calls could not be eliminated. Then run the procedure on these three proposals: (a) a gene drive in an island population of invasive black rats, with a low but nonzero chance that a stowaway rat introduces the drive to mainland rat populations where the ecological consequences are unpredictable; if contained, the island's seabird colony recovers substantially. (b) a de-extinction attempt for the passenger pigeon that would create a genetically edited proxy from a surviving relative, release it into eastern forests, and likely foreclose several years of ordinary habitat-protection grants; the released birds would initially have poor welfare, while the expected ecological effects are incremental and roughly observable. (c) a genetic rescue of a small, inbred amphibian population threatened by a fungal pathogen, by introducing a disease-resistance allele from a larger population; the change would persist indefinitely in that population's genome but is not expected to spread beyond it.

For each proposal, show the procedure's steps and the final recommendation it yields. Make sure at least one case produces a conclusion you expect the committee to dislike—for example, clearing a proposal that strikes members as unnatural, or blocking an urgent-seeming rescue—and do not soften it; say explicitly why the procedure still gives that answer. End by saying what the procedure cannot do and what kind of proposal it is likely to mishandle.

You are a professional philosopher working on environmental ethics and the ethics of emerging technology. Before writing the checklist, state in one or two sentences the core principle you are operationalizing, and name one plausible alternative you are rejecting and why. Think carefully before you start writing. Hard limit: do not exceed 1,200 words.
