I help run a community garden that runs on shared labor — everyone's supposed to put in a few hours a month, and then everyone takes from the harvest. This summer we had our first real blow-up about the three or four people who never show up for workdays but always show up when the tomatoes are ready, and I've been asked to run a 90-minute workshop for about 20 members (mixed ages, not all of them philosophy people) before we rewrite the rules. I dont want to lecture at them — I want them to feel it.

Design a game or structured activity for that workshop that makes participants feel both the pull of free riding (the temptation to take without contributing) and the resentment of being free-ridden on. It should also force them to wrestle with the difference between someone who won't contribute and someone who can't, and — if there is a difference — between an ordinary free rider and a parasite who works the system on purpose. I'm fine with a hidden role or two (say, a "strategic parasite" and a "can't contribute" player) as long as the reveal happens in the debrief, not up front. Payoffs should use points or tokens, not real money, and the whole thing including debrief has to fit in 90 minutes. The idea should come out of what happens in the game, not out of me explaining it afterwards.

Give me a runnable activity with:
- "The setup" — materials and room, ~100 words
- "The game" — rules, roles, and round-by-round play, ~250 words
- "Payoffs" — the point or token scheme, ~100 words
- "The debrief" — mapping what happened onto the idea, ~250 words
- "What will go wrong" — predictable first-run failures and fixes, ~150 words
- "What the game can't teach" — what I'll have to supply in my own words, ~100 words

You're an expert on collective action and the ethics of cooperation, and you've run a lot of experiential workshops. Start by brainstorming three candidate mechanics and tell me in a sentence each why you picked the one you did; then give the full design. Think carefully about making the incentives do the teaching rather than the debrief.
