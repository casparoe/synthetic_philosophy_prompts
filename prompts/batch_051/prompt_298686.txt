I'm a science journalist — health and social science mostly — and a local journalism school asked me to run a ninety-minute session for working reporters on how to read a causal claim without either swallowing the press release or swatting everything away as "just correlation." I don't want the usual slide deck. What I actually want them to leave with is the shape of Pearl's ladder — association, intervention, counterfactual — the fact that P(Y|X) and P(Y|do(X)) answer genuinely different questions, and the sense that a structural causal model isn't a regression equation or a Bayesian network; its the asymmetry of the assignments that carries the causal content. The audience will have run one regression years ago and forgotten it, so notation has to be earned, not assumed. Don't explain the material to me; give me a teaching plan.

I want the order of ideas and what depends on what. I want a single carrying example that lets me show, with maybe a diagram and a bit of arithmetic, why conditioning on one variable closes an open path and conditioning on another opens a closed one — tell me what the example must have and what it must not have. Not smoking and lung cancer; it derails into 1950s history every time, unless you think that's actually a feature. The reporters will walk in with specific wrong reflexes, in particular "more controls is always safer," "an RCT is the only real causal evidence," and "if the mechanism story is plausible the study is causal." Tell me exactly where in the sequence each should be headed off, because I don't want to be correcting them after the confusion has already grabbed hold. Then say what I should leave out at this level — I assume actual do-calculus identification is off the table — and say so explicitly rather than letting it just not come up. End with one screen-up question that separates the people who can now see why an observational estimate went wrong from the people who've just learned to say "confounder" louder.

And commit: tell me which transition in that sequence is most likely to fall flat, what you'd accept as evidence that it failed, and what would make you drop the carrying example and reorder the rest rather than push through.
