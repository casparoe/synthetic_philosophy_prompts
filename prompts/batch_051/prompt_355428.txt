I keep noticing a strange asymmetry in how we treat wrong attribution in science, and I want to know whether its defensible. Cite the wrong person in a paper and its a scholarly error—people will correct it, maybe even call it sloppy. But the whole field runs on eponyms that are, by anyone's admission, wrong: Pell's equation, which Euler assigned to John Pell by mistake when Brouncker had done the work; Halley's comet, which had been recorded for centuries before Halley; Stigler's law of eponymy, which Stigler himself credits to Merton. Nobody campaigns to rename those, and using them doesn't seem like an error at all. So the same underlying mistake—attributing a discovery to the wrong person—is treated as a serious fault when it appears in a reference list and as harmless convention when it appears in the name of the thing. Is there a principle that actually distinguishes the two cases, or is this just a double standard we've gotten used to? I want the strongest defence of treating eponyms and citations differently you can give, and then an honest verdict on whether it survives a case where the wrong eponym is still being taught to students as though it tracked priority. Please write in British English.
