I am a PhD student in philosophy working on social choice theory and the formal modeling of rights, and I have been asked to help organize an adversarial collaboration between two groups who both work on Sen's liberal paradox but no longer engage with each other's arguments. The first group—call them the Paretians—treats the weak Pareto principle as non-negotiable for any acceptable social evaluation, and therefore concludes that the minimal-liberty condition has to be restricted: for instance, by excluding meddlesome preferences from the protected sphere, or by making a right immune only when others are indifferent to how it is exercised. The second group—call them the rights-as-constraints camp—draws the opposite conclusion: rights were misframed from the start as decision rules inside a preference aggregation. On their view rights should enter as constraints on the feasible set, or as game forms in which a right is a bundle of permissible strategies together with the resulting outcomes, with Pareto applied only after rights have fixed what remains open. Each side suspects the other of preserving a favored principle by quietly restating the problem.

Draft the joint statement these two camps would sign. It should open with a numbered list of claims both can accept, including a precise statement of the impossibility result that each endorses, a full presentation of the standard two-person book case (the prude, who wants nobody to read it and would rather read it himself than have the other read it, and the other, who most wants the prude to read it and would rather read it himself than have nobody read it), and the exact roles of unrestricted domain, weak Pareto, and the two private-sphere decisiveness conditions in producing a social cycle. Next provide a disagreements table in which each residual disagreement is stated in wording each side accepts for its own view, with a column indicating whether the disagreement is substantive or merely verbal. Then state the cruxes the collaboration will pre-register, covering at least the treatment of meddlesome preferences, the game-form reformulation of rights, and the question of how much rationality (transitivity, acyclicity, or something weaker) is being demanded of the social ranking. For each crux, specify what formal result, counterexample, or case both sides agree would bear on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. Where you conclude that a disagreement is terminological, say which one and why; where it is genuine, word it closely enough that neither side can later claim it was slanted.
