Economists have long defended models with obviously false assumptions—people with complete information, markets that clear instantly—by saying a model should be judged only on its predictions, not on the realism of its assumptions. That defense was worked out for models made of a few explicit equations, where assumptions and predictions could be pointed at separately. Now suppose an economist replaces such a model with a large machine-learning system trained to predict the same kind of outcome: it does well on data it was not trained on, but it has no hand-written assumptions, only a huge number of fitted parameters, and no one can say which economic relationships it has learned. Does the old argument survive this development? I want to know which premise is actually under pressure, whether there is a repaired version of the argument that says something weaker but still meaningful about black-box models, and whether predictive success can still do the justificatory work it used to when the assumptions are not even inspectable, or whether the argument simply no longer applies.
