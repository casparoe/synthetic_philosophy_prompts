I cover the science-and-ideas beat and am working through the ontology of generational labels — whether categories like Boomers, Millennials, and Gen Z track real kinds of people or are just convenient sorting boxes. Before I write the piece, I want to know what principle, if any, captures my own snap verdicts on a set of cases.

My verdicts:

(1) A child born at 11:59 p.m. on December 31, 1996, and another a minute later sit on opposite sides of the conventional Millennial/Gen Z line. I think nothing about the two children differs, and assigning them to different generations is arbitrary.

(2) Yet I think there is a genuine difference in kind between someone born in 1947 and someone born in 1965: the first came of age in the postwar boom and Vietnam draft, the second amid Watergate, stagflation, and the onset of AIDS. It would be perverse to lump them together.

(3) A 22-year-old born in 2002 but raised off-grid, with no smartphone until 18, is still Gen Z, in my view. Birth year settles membership; a generation is not a personality profile.

(4) A 68-year-old who makes TikToks all day and talks like a Gen Zer is still a Boomer, but the category seems to be doing no explanatory work for him.

(5) I treat "Millennial" as a made category when marketers use it to predict tastes, yet the prediction often works. I suspect the labels are constructed but become causally effective once adopted — the way "teenager" was constructed in the early twentieth century and then became real in its effects.

(6) I find a boundary keyed to a real event more principled than one keyed to a round number: I would take seriously a line between children who finished high school remotely during COVID and those who did not, even though that line cuts through Gen Z.

Is there one account of what makes a generational category a real kind that delivers all or most of these verdicts, and which of my verdicts does it force me to drop? Please also say what the account predicts for someone born in 1981, who is classified as late Gen X by some sources and an early Millennial by others — a case I keep reversing myself on.

Reply as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) state the candidate principle precisely; (2) identify which of my verdicts it forces me to drop; (3) show how it handles the tension between the arbitrary midnight boundary and the genuinely different 1947/1965 pair; (4) give its prediction for the 1981 case; (5) state the strongest objection to the principle.
