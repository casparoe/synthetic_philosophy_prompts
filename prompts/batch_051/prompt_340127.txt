I have been asked to lead a single one-hour seminar for first-year undergraduates in a humanities foundation course, on the idea that play and leisure are not merely instrumental goods—not just ways of recovering from work or making ourselves more productive later—but are worthwhile for their own sake. The session must stand alone: the students will not have read Huizinga or Pieper beforehand, and I am not assigning either text as preparation, though I may allude to their ideas in passing. My difficulty is not in finding things to say. It is that every point I want to make seems to depend on a distinction the students have not yet drawn, namely the difference between doing something for the sake of something further and doing it for its own sake. If I state that distinction at the outset as a definition, I suspect it will pass through the room unnoticed; if I wait too long, I suspect the first half of the hour will appear to be about nothing in particular.

Could you design the explanation for me, as a plan rather than a script? I would like to know how the components of the idea depend on one another and therefore what order they must enter in; which single ordinary example should carry the session, and what it must have and lack if it is to bear that weight without leading the students astray; which misconceptions I should expect from this audience—for example, that the position is a defence of idleness, that leisure simply is entertainment, or that an activity done for its own sake cannot have rules or standards—and at what point each should be intercepted; what question or task would show me that the central distinction has genuinely landed rather than been parroted back; and what I should openly decline to cover at this level, such as the theological commitments in Pieper or the historiography of play, and how to mark that boundary without making the tradition sound thinner than it is.
