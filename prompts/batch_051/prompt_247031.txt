I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, but I'm drafting a novel about a town that splits over a dam project, and I've gotten myself tangled in a moral question I can't get past. The project would flood a valley, and the strongest argument the conservationists in my book make is about the individual animals who will die or be displaced—the deer that drown, the foxes whose dens go under, the birds whose nests wash away. That's treated as the gut-punch reason the dam is wrong. But here's what nobody in the story, or in the real arguments I've read, ever seems to ask: are those animals' lives in the intact valley actually good? The valley is full of slow starvation, disease, predation, hard winter die-offs. If you tried to tally up the welfare of every individual creature living there, it might come out negative; some people who study this stuff argue that wild animal suffering is massive and that a healthy ecosystem can be a bad place to be an animal.

So here's the asymmetry. Individual animal suffering counts for everything when we're deciding whether to destroy a habitat, but it counts for almost nothing when we're deciding whether to preserve one, and I can't figure out what keeps those two judgments seperate. If the valley is a misery machine, why is flooding it a tragedy for the deer rather than a mercy? I want to know whether that asymmetry can actually be defended—or whether its just a psychological quirk we live with. Is there a principle that makes it coherent, maybe something about the value of species or ecosystems or natural processes over and above individual welfare? And if you find one, I want you to test it against the nearby cases where conservationists happily hurt individual animals for the good of a population or a landscape, and then give me an honest verdict: keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or drop it. And don't just hand me the standard line about intrinsic value in nature; I've heard it, and I want to know whether it actually survives contact with the deer.
