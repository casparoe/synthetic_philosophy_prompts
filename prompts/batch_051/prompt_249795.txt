In my recent reading on distributive ethics, I keep returning to the leveling-down objection. As I understand it, the objection runs as follows: if equality is valuable for its own sake, then a change that lowers the better-off to the level of the worse-off while leaving the worse-off exactly as they were must count as an improvement in at least one respect, and this implication is meant to discredit the view. Prioritarianism was introduced largely to avoid this result, by giving extra weight to benefits to the worse-off without ever treating equality itself as a good. Now suppose we adopt a conception of welfare on which advantage is partly positional: a person's standing is determined in part by their rank relative to others, not only by their absolute holdings. Does the leveling-down objection still go through under that assumption? Identify which premise of the objection the positional-welfare assumption bears on. If the objection survives, show precisely how it must be reformulated so that the worse-off are genuinely no better off. If it does not survive, say whether prioritarianism still has a distinct motivation, or whether it and a suitably revised egalitarianism now converge. Take a definite position and defend it.
