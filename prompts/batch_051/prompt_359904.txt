I sit on the canon committee of a small museum that each year displays one telling of the Orpheus and Eurydice myth as the canonical version, with a few alternate tellings in a side room. We grade submissions as standard, variant, or rejected: standard means the version is presented as canonical, variant means it is recognized as a legitimate alternative, and rejected means we do not regard it as an Orpheus story at all. I want our next ruling to follow from our own track record, but I suspect the track record underdetermines it.

Our rulings so far:

1. Standard. Orpheus honors the condition through the whole ascent and turns deliberately on the last steps because he believes he hears Eurydice falter behind him; she is drawn back forever.

2. Standard. Orpheus turns deliberately at the very threshold because his doubt about whether the gods have honored their word becomes unbearable; Eurydice vanishes and no reunion follows.

3. Variant. Orpheus turns at the threshold only because Eurydice calls out to him; the loss is final, but we judged that the story shifts the failure onto her and thereby changes the story’s center.

4. Rejected. Orpheus never looks back; the couple returns to the surface and grows old together. We held that without the broken prohibition the story is not the Orpheus myth at all.

5. Variant. Orpheus turns deliberately after stepping into the daylight while Eurydice is still in shadow; she is lost forever, but after his own death the two are reunited in the underworld. We held that the reunion erodes the finality of the threshold, so the tale counts only as a variant.

6. Rejected. The gods, moved by Orpheus’s song, never impose the condition, and Eurydice follows him out freely. We held that a release without the backward glance is a different myth.

This year’s submission runs as follows. Orpheus honors the condition through the entire passage. A few steps short of the exit he stumbles on a loose stone and reflexively turns his head to see whether Eurydice, who is following close behind, has also fallen. The turn is not a decision; it is a reflex, unmediated by doubt, longing, or any sound from her. She is still behind him, so the condition binds; she is drawn back and lost forever. There is no reunion and no later rescue.

What should we decide—standard, variant, or rejected—if we want to remain consistent with our own rulings? Which of the six earlier rulings is the closest analog, and what general principle about what makes a telling canonical would we be adopting under each of the three possible decisions?
