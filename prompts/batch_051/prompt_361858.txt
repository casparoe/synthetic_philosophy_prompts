I keep hearing people say that if we ever give AI systems legal personhood, the sensible template will be the corporation, not the human being — a corporation can't feel pain either, but we let it own property, enter contracts, sue and be sued, and sometimes even claim rights. That analogy is doing real philosophical work, and I want it pulled apart. Write me a short essay on what corporate legal personhood, taken as an actual institutional practice, presupposes about the concept of a person — and whether that scaffolding would transfer to an AI system. Pin down the practice first, then reconstruct its implicit theory of personhood. Be careful to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, since people tend to run those together. Then ask what does and doesn't transfer to an AI, and don't paper over the underdetermined spots: there's a few places where several different theories of legal personhood would license the same corporate practice, and if nothing forces a choice between them, say so. Don't worry about citing cases or statutes and don't give me a reading list; I only want the conceptual anatomy. Organize the response under these headings, in this order: "The practice" (~200 words), "What it strictly presupposes" (~400 words), "What it merely fits with" (~200 words), "The AI extension" (~350 words), "Where it's underdetermined" (~200 words).
