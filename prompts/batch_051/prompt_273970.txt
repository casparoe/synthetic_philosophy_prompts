I am trying to work out what principle actually governs my judgments about punishment, starting from my gut reactions to concrete cases. (1) A man lies in wait to shoot his business partner; the gun jams and the partner escapes unharmed. I think he should be punished, but less than if the partner had died. (2) A woman kills her business partner because a cartel has credibly threatened to murder her child unless she does it, and she sees no other way out. I think she should be punished, but far less than an ordinary murderer. (3) A town is about to erupt in a riot that will likely kill several people; the police know that convicting an innocent drifter of the unsolved crime would prevent the riot. I think convicting him would still be wrong, no matter how many lives it saved. (4) A nineteen-year-old in a first psychotic episode smashes up a neighbor's apartment; I think he should be involuntarily treated, not punished. (5) A ninety-year-old man who tortured and killed someone sixty years ago is now frail, poses no future danger, and his punishment would deter no one; I still think he should be punished. (6) An executive embezzles from his employees' pension fund, and the company later makes the employees whole; I think restitution is not enough and he should personally go to prison. Is there a principled account—retributivist, consequentialist, or a hybrid—that captures most of these verdicts? Please state the candidate principle precisely, say which of my verdicts it has to overturn or strain to save, explain what work each clause of it does, and apply it to the case I keep going back and forth on: two equally drunk drivers, one of whom happens to hit and kill a pedestrian while the other gets home without incident—should the lucky one face anywhere near the same punishment? Please write your answer in American English.
