I am a philosophy professor preparing a colloquium talk in which I defend the thesis that the simulation argument significantly dampens longtermist fanaticism: the view that the expected effect of present actions on the welfare of astronomically many future beings swamps all other considerations in evaluating those actions. My argument is not that we are probably living in a simulation. It is that the simulation argument's trilemma — that civilizations at our stage rarely survive to a posthuman stage able to run many ancestor-simulations, that such civilizations rarely have any interest in running them, or that we are very likely simulated — gives the fanatic principled reason to assign much lower probability to a long, influenceable future than the astronomical-stakes picture assumes, and thereby weakens the expected-value case for far-future dominance. The audience is a broadly analytic philosophy department: mostly decision theorists and epistemologists, with several longtermists sympathetic to the astronomical-waste argument and no one specializing in the simulation argument. Prepare me for the question period. Give me exactly seven numbered objections, each two sentences: the objection phrased as this audience would actually put it, and my best reply, with a note on what I can concede, what I must hold, and where the reply is soft. Order them by how likely and how damaging they are, and include the objection I most hope nobody asks. No introduction or conclusion.
