I am trying to work out what I think about survival and personal identity in cases of brain replacement and mind uploading, and my verdicts on particular cases seem to be in tension. I would like you to do reflective equilibrium for me: extract the principle—or a small set of candidate principles—that best captures my verdicts; state it precisely; say which clause handles each case; and tell me which of my verdicts, if any, the resulting account forces me to revise.

Assumptions. In every case, assume that artificial parts are functionally equivalent to the biological neurons they replace: each artificial neuron replicates the input–output profile of the neuron it replaces in real time, so the subject's behavior, memories, and dispositions are preserved unless a case explicitly says otherwise. Ignore practical questions about whether such replacement is technically feasible.

Cases and my verdicts.

1. The ten-year overhaul. My neurons are replaced one at a time over ten years. I am conscious through much of the process, I remember it, and I experience no discontinuity. At the end, every neuron is artificial. Verdict: I survive; it is me throughout.

2. The overnight overhaul. The same neuron-by-neuron replacement is compressed into an eight-hour procedure while I am under general anesthesia. I go to sleep with a biological brain and wake with an entirely artificial one, with my memories, plans, and dispositions intact. Verdict: I survive.

3. The swapped brain. While I am anesthetized, surgeons remove my biological brain and destroy it in the process, then position a fully assembled artificial brain in my skull and connect it to the same body. The new brain is a functional duplicate of the old one. I wake with the same memories and dispositions. Verdict: I survive, though the idea frightens me.

4. The destructive scan. A scanner records the exact functional architecture of my brain and destroys the brain in the process. Immediately afterward, the recording is activated as a computational process running in real time, connected to a robotic body. The resulting person talks like me, remembers my life, and says, "It worked." Verdict: I do not survive; the resulting person is a successor, not me.

5. The fork. A non-destructive scan produces a computational duplicate of me while my biological original continues to live. The duplicate and I then have different experiences. Verdict: the duplicate is not me; I am the biological original.

6. Fork, then the original dies. The non-destructive scan from case 5 is made, and one second later my biological original dies instantly from an unrelated cause. The computational duplicate remains, with all of my memories up to the moment of scanning. Verdict: I die with the original; the duplicate is not me.

7. The year-long archive. My brain is destructively scanned exactly as in case 4, but the recording is not activated until one year later. During that year, no instance of me exists anywhere. When the recording is activated, the resulting person remembers the scan and says the gap felt like no time at all. Verdict: I do not survive; the gap makes the case worse, not better.

My working rule has been something like this: I survive just in case there is a single continuous stream of mental life—or at least a continuous neural process capable of supporting such a life—whose later stages are causally dependent in the right way on my earlier mental life, and no competing continuant exists alongside it. Cases 1, 2, and 3 feel like clear instances of survival. Cases 5 and 6 violate the "no competing continuant" condition. Case 7 fails because the stream is interrupted. But case 4 is the problem: my working rule appears to classify it as survival, yet I cannot shake the verdict that I die. Is there a stable, non-ad-hoc account of survival that delivers all or most of these verdicts? If so, state it precisely, explain which clause handles each case, and say what it predicts about the further cases below. If not, state which of my verdicts, or which part of my working rule, must be revised—and defend the choice between a more complex principle that saves nearly all my verdicts and a simpler one that overturns some of them.

Further cases on which I want your verdicts, on the resulting account:

A. The teleporter. My body is disassembled at one location, the information describing its exact structure is transmitted, and a qualitatively identical body and brain are assembled from new matter at another location. No duplicate ever exists.

B. The interrupted overhaul. The eight-hour procedure from case 2 is performed, but halfway through, the partially artificial brain is placed in a metabolically inactive state for ten minutes, after which the procedure resumes. No duplicate exists, and the subject has no later experience corresponding to the shut-down.

C. The edited scan. As in case 4, except that before activation, technicians erase one episodic memory and replace it with a plausible constructed memory. The resulting person otherwise matches my psychology.

Please write the answer in American English.
