I am a retired experimental physicist, and over the past two years I have been reading my way into decision theory and the philosophy of probability. I convene a monthly reading circle of about twelve adults, mostly retired scientists and engineers, and I am responsible for the next 90-minute meeting. I would like that meeting to be a discovery session rather than a lecture, built around a game that has lately preoccupied me — the one sometimes called the Pasadena game. A fair coin is tossed until it first comes up heads; if the first head occurs on the nth toss, the player receives (−1)^(n−1) · 2^n / n dollars, with negative amounts understood as payments the player must make. The probability-weighted payoffs, taken in their natural order, give 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + …, which sums to ln 2. But this series is only conditionally convergent — the alternating sum converges, while the sum of the absolute values diverges — so the same terms can be reordered to converge to any real number, or to diverge to ±∞. The game therefore has no well-defined expected value in the standard sense, even though it is easy to specify and initially looks as if it has a tidy expected value.

I want the participants to run into that fact themselves before it is named, and no lecture up front: the term "Pasadena paradox" should not be spoken until the debrief. By the end of the session they should see two things: first, that a concrete, fully specified game can lack a determinate fair price; second, that the culprit is the failure of absolute convergence — the way the order in which one enumerates the same infinite list of outcomes changes the result. I recognize that live play must be a finite approximation: any fixed finite list of outcomes has the same total no matter how it is reordered, so the pathology lives strictly in the infinite schedule. A central design challenge is therefore to let participants handle the infinite schedule concretely — by computing terms, reordering them, or arguing about fair-ticket prices under rival enumeration rules — without the session collapsing into a lecture. Please also use only point-money or heavily scaled dollar amounts, since the actual payoffs are unbounded.
