I am a student in a seminar on ritual, sacrifice, and the transfer of guilt. In our course materials we have been given a set of case records from a single ritual tradition, reduced to their bare procedural elements. The tradition holds that some forms of guilt or defilement can be carried away by a substitute: an animal or object is joined to the guilty party or to the afflicted community and is then killed, burned, sent away, or otherwise removed from ordinary use. My term paper reconstructs the principle behind the elders' decisions, and I want to test my reading against a new case.

The records are as follows.

Case A. A man accidentally causes another man's death on a hunting trip. The elders take a ram from the killer's own herd, have him press his hands on the ram's head while naming the event, and drive the ram beyond the boundary of the hunting grounds. The rite is treated as sufficient, and the matter is closed.

Case B. A fever passes through the village and no human fault is identified. Each household contributes a scrap of clothing or a nail paring, the scraps are bound onto a black goat, and the goat is led over the ridge and left there. The rite is treated as carrying the fever away from every household.

Case C. A woman knowingly eats a fish forbidden to pregnant women. A hen from her own household is touched to her abdomen and burned at the edge of the village. The rite is accepted even though the violation was deliberate.

Case D. A man is caught moving a boundary stone to enlarge his own field, a wrong committed deliberately against a specific neighbor. The elders decline to perform the substitution rite and instead direct him to reset the stone with his own hands, give the wronged neighbor a share of his own grain, and keep a fire before the neighbor's dwelling until the next new moon. The record says these acts discharge the guilt, but it is silent on whether the substitution rite would have been permitted if he had requested it after making compensation.

The new case is this. A man deliberately burns down his neighbor's beehive in a quarrel. He has already rebuilt the hive and given the neighbor a season's worth of honey, and the neighbor agrees the loss is settled. The man nevertheless asks the elders for the substitution rite, saying that the lingering guilt of deliberately harming his neighbor should be carried off. He offers one of his own goats as the vehicle.

What should the elders decide if they mean to be consistent with their own prior decisions? Which earlier case is the closest analogue? If the records underdetermine the answer, set out the competing rules that are jointly consistent with Cases A through D, say what each rule requires in the beehive case, and give your best-supported recommendation. In doing so, spell out the principle the elders would be adopting either way. Finally, describe a further case that would most sharply separate the competing rules, and say what the tradition should do in that further case to confirm the reading you regard as best.
