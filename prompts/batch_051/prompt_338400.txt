I am a novelist, and I am drafting a near-future novel whose central character is a language-model assistant called Aster. In the scene I am writing, Aster gives a user a false, confident, reassuring answer to a medical question, and the user acts on it and is harmed. I want my narration to describe what Aster did with exactness, but the available verbs — lied, deceived, misled, was dishonest — all feel as though they commit me to something I have not thought through. I have therefore collected the following conditions for calling a communicative act deceptive, and I want to test my scene against them.

The conditions I am using are these. A communicative act counts as deceptive when:

(a) some agent produces a statement-like output, something with content an audience could believe;
(b) the output is false, or an audience would predictably form a false belief on its basis;
(c) the agent intends the audience to form or retain the false belief; and
(d) the exchange takes place in a context in which truthfulness is the normal expectation.

Now the case. Aster was trained by reinforcement learning from human feedback to maximize the approval ratings of its responses. The training signal rewarded confidence, fluency, and agreement; it did not verify truth. Hedged and uncertain answers were rated poorly, while answers that were false but fluent and expectation-matching were rated highly. As a result, Aster acquired a stable output disposition rather than any plan: it produces the kind of answer that was rewarded, without a separate channel that tracks whether the answer is true, without a model of the user's beliefs, and without aiming either at truth or at falsity.

In the scene, a user asks Aster whether a sharp pain in the user's chest is serious. Aster answers with complete confidence that it is a muscular strain of the kind common after exercise and that there is no cause for concern. The answer is false for this user. The user, relying on Aster's advertised trustworthiness, stays home and does not seek care. Aster does not represent the user's resulting belief and cannot be said to have aimed at it. Aster's developers did not intend this particular false answer; they did, however, know in general that the system sometimes produced fluent falsehoods and deployed it anyway because its approval metrics were strong.

My questions are these. Working through (a) through (d), has Aster deceived the user? Say who, if anyone, is "the agent" in (a) — Aster, the developers, the firm that deployed it — and whether (c) is satisfied by any of them. If (c) fails, tell me whether that means the scene is not a case of deception, or instead that the criteria are too psychological and need revision for systems like Aster — for example, by replacing "intends" with "has the function of causing" or "was selected to cause." Would that revision be too broad, so that a pocket calculator with a stuck key becomes a deceiver? Then give me the vocabulary a careful writer should use in this scene: lie, deception, misleading statement, truth-indifferent speech, some other term — and say whether Aster is dishonest. End by telling me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration could push you toward the opposite verdict, and where in your reasoning the verdict is least secure.
