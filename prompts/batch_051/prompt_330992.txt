Im a PhD student in history and philosophy of science, working on how research priorities get justified, and I keep going back and forth on a made-up case. Take the enabling statute of a national research foundation, drafted in the 1940s: 'The Foundation shall support only research that promises, within a reasonable period of years, to advance the economic, social, or public welfare of the nation.' Everyone agrees the drafters meant applied work—crop varieties, antibiotics, radar, that sort of thing. Now suppose a cosmology group submits a proposal to determine whether dark energy behaves like a cosmological constant or has changed over cosmic time, using distances and redshifts of type Ia supernovae. They say outright that there is no foreseeable application and that the payoff is purely that it constrains whether our basic model of the universe is right. Does the clause, read literally, reach that? Does it read differently as a general principle about what 'public welfare' includes? What would the drafters have said if you could have asked them about a universe whose expansion is accelerating, and how much should that matter? What would a reasonable reader take the clause to mean? I dont care how a real agency would fudge it; I want to know whether any coherent reading permits funding cosmology like this, and which reading is most defensible. Continuous prose only please, no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables, like an email.
