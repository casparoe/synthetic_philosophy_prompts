Ok so I've been around enough churches now to be honestly confused about baptism and I want someone to lay out what each way of doing it is actually commited to underneath. My aunt's church (some kind of mainline protestant, I think) sprinkles babies. They say the baby is being joined to God's family, and then around thirteen or so the same kid goes through confirmation. My friend's church does believer's baptism: you have to be old enough to confess faith on your own, they dunk you under the water, and they will re-baptize somebody who was sprinkled as a baby because they say the first one didn't count. So one church says it works on babies and the other says it can't work until you understand it. But here's what trips me up. The believer's-baptism church will happily baptize a seven-year-old who says the Jesus sentence while his parents are filming it, and nobody pretends that kid made some deep, independent decision. And the baby-baptizing church doesn't seem to think infant baptism finished the job either, otherwise why does the same kid get confirmed later? Both churches also treat baptism as a serious line to cross—some won't let you take communion or become a member without it—even the ones that insist baptism does not save you. If it's just an outward symbol, why do they act like it's urgent? And if it really does something to you before you can understand it, why do you need to confirm it later? I guess my question is: what theory of what baptism is (and what faith is, and what a person has to be like for God to deal with them) does each practice quietly commit you to, and which views does each one quietly rule out? Do the two practices actually presuppose different answers, or are they mostly the same answer on a different schedule? And what would you have to change in the practice if you actually bought the other side's theory?
