I come at this from software, so I usually treat proposals like this as systems: you specify the mechanism, then ask what behavior it actually produces, including the edge cases and the ways people game it. Here is a proposal I came across in a discussion about how societies handle contested commemorations. I have been reading about the different paths taken in Germany's Vergangenheitsbewältigung, usually translated as working through the past, the monument debates in the American South, the official-historiography disputes in Turkey, and the recurring fights in Japan over commemorations of the imperial period, and it struck me that most of these debates are settled by ad hoc political pressure rather than by any stable mechanism.

The proposal is this. A country institutes a mandatory review system for all public commemorations of historical figures: statues, monuments, street names, and the names of public buildings. Every twenty-five years, each commemoration is placed before a citizens' jury of thirty people selected by lot from the local population. The jury hears a fixed docket: two professional historians present the best current scholarship, and then any group, domestic or foreign, that claims the commemorated figure seriously wronged its members or its ancestors is guaranteed one slot to be heard, with the number of such groups limited only by the jury's total hearing time. Reauthorization requires a two-thirds vote. If the vote fails, the commemoration is not destroyed or hidden; it is moved to a publicly funded museum of contested memory and displayed together with the majority and minority written statements from the jury. The result is binding for twenty-five years, but a failed commemoration can be proposed again once after ten years, provided the proponents collect signatures from five percent of local residents.

Walk through how this system would actually behave. What does it incentivize among professional historians, activist and descendant groups, local politicians, and foreign governments? How would it be gamed, for example through strategic claims of historical injury, jury-selection effects, or the media and fundraising environment around each jury? What second-order effects would it have on which figures get commemorated in the first place, on the line between historical judgment and contemporary politics, and on the wider culture's relationship to its own past? And which of the failure modes can be fixed by adjusting the rules, as opposed to being baked into the mechanism itself?
