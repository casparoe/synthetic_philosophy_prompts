I keep noticing a move in the places I read — rationalist blogs, forecasting forums, long argument threads — and I can't tell whether it has a name or I've just stared at it to long. Somebody makes a strong evidential claim: that a policy raised rents, or that a mutual friend's apology was insincere. The reply is rarely 'here's where the evidence goes the other way'; it's usually 'the rational posture here is uncertainty, we just can't know.' Then the uncertain person is treated as having won the exchange, and the person who believed the evidence-backed thing as slightly gauche, even when the evidence was lopsided. The burden of justification is also totally asymmetric: 'I believe X' has to be supported, but 'I'm withholding judgment' is treated as free and safe, never in need of its own support, even though refusing to update on your evidence is itself a way of being wrong. I've watched the same shape in arguments about catastophic risk, about whether to leave a job, about what a dead author 'really' meant. Always the same skeleton: doubt gets moralized into a duty, certainty into a vice, and whoever performs the most uncertainty gets the most credit for rigor, regardless of what the evidence warranted.

Is there a name for this? I don't mean plain skepticism or agnosticism as positions, and I don't mean the older evidentialist rule that you shouldn't believe on insufficient evidence — that's a claim about when the evidence licenses belief, not about doubt being intrinsically virtuous. I mean the specific thing where suspended judgment is the default that never has to earn its keep, and confidence reads as a character flaw. My guess is the nearest concepts are somewhere in the ethics of belief or social epistemology, but I want the carving, not just a label: what exactly the phenomenon is, what it's not, and where the line is between 'weighing uncertainty correctly' and 'using doubt as a status move or an excuse not to act.' If no established term fits, what would you call it?
