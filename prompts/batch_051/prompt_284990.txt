AI alignment has harbored a long-running and mostly stalled argument over whether the problem should be defined narrowly — as getting systems to do what humans intend or ask for — or broadly, as getting systems to pursue human values, interests, or flourishing. Defenders of the narrow definition say that only intent, instructions, and expressed preferences are observable enough to be optimization targets, and that any wider definition smuggles in contested moral content without a testable operationalization. Critics argue that intent is the wrong target: people intend under false beliefs, fleeting moods, and limited reflection, so a system optimized against stated intent can systematically bring about outcomes the same person would disvalue, and calling that system aligned is a category error. The parties often agree on the engineering facts about instruction-tuning and learning from human preferences, yet neither side moves. My working hypothesis is that the word alignment is doing double duty here: one question is about what can be operationalized and measured, and another is about what should be optimized and whose authority counts.
