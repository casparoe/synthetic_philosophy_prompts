I am a PhD student in decision theory, and I am drafting a chapter on what the Allais paradox actually demonstrates about the independence axiom. I have become dissatisfied with the way discussions tend to treat the classic pair of choices as one unified phenomenon. In the first problem, most people prefer a certain $1,000,000 to a lottery that yields $5,000,000 with probability 0.10, $1,000,000 with probability 0.89, and nothing with probability 0.01. In the second problem, most people prefer a lottery that yields $5,000,000 with probability 0.10 and otherwise nothing to one that yields $1,000,000 with probability 0.11 and otherwise nothing. This is puzzling because the second problem can be obtained from the first by replacing a common 0.89 chance of $1,000,000 with nothing, and expected utility maximization requires the two choices to align.

I would like to rank candidate accounts of these and closely related choice patterns, not for purposes of historical exegesis but to decide how much weight to give each in my own modeling. The candidates I am considering are: (i) smooth generalized expected utility, in which local utility depends on the lottery being evaluated and indifference curves can fan out; (ii) rank-dependent utility or cumulative prospect-theoretic probability weighting, with small probabilities overweighted and moderate-to-large probabilities underweighted so that decision weights are subcertain; (iii) regret theory, in which choices reflect anticipated regret and rejoicing across states and therefore violate separability between common and differing branches; (iv) a two-factor account that treats the common-consequence version of the paradox as driven mainly by a certainty effect and the common-ratio version as driven mainly by a possibility effect; and (v) a re-description view on which the preferences are not violations at all once outcomes are re-individuated to include factors such as disappointment, or once event-splitting and framing effects are taken into account.

For each of the five candidates, please assign a score from 0 to 1 indicating how much you would trust it as a general account of Allais-type violations, where 0 means you would invoke it only as an ad hoc redescription and 1 means you would make it your default explanation. Then rank the five candidates and give one sentence of reasoning for each score. I am less interested in which account is historically correct than in which ones carry the most explanatory weight across variants, including cases with common consequences, common ratios, and very small probabilities.
