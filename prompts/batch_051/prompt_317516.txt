I retired a few years ago after a career in field ecology, and I've lately been reading philosophy of science with more patience then I ever had time for. The thing I keep worrying at is natural kinds — whether our categories carve nature at its joints or are just filing cabinets. I find I have strong gut verdicts about particular cases, but I can't recover the rule that produces them. Gold is a real kind: atomic number 79, no ambiguity. Water is a real kind: H2O. But 'tree' obviously isn't a kind — oaks and pines became woody independently of one another — whereas 'oak' and 'pine' individually feel like kinds. 'Fish' isn't a kind, since it's paraphyletic once you remember the tetrapods are inside it, yet 'salmon' is fine. Jade isn't a kind: I know it's two different minerals that happen to look alike, even though each of those minerals is a kind on its own. Species I want to call real kinds, even though species boundaries blur and sometimes two 'good species' hybridise, which sits awkwardly next to the crispness I like in gold and water. And I can't make myself treat 'planet' as a kind — the Pluto business felt like a vote on a definition, not a discovery — but 'star' feels like a kind. 'Vitamin' I'd call a real but functional kind, though I can't say why it survives while 'tree' doesn't.

What I'd like is for you to work out the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that best captures these verdicts, stated precisely enough for me to test on new cases. Tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon, and why. Then apply it to the ones I keep going back and forth on: 'mammal' (I'm inclined to call it a kind), 'neurotransmitter' (I'm inclined not to, but can't justify it), and 'weed' (surely not a kind — but on what grounds, exactly?). Please write the whole response in British English.
