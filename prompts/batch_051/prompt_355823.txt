I am a first-time teaching assistant for an undergraduate course in moral philosophy. This term's unit on the ethics of risk has recently become personally relevant to me: a close family member is immunocompromised, and I have been reflecting on how ordinary activities impose small but serious risks on people who never consent to them. As part of my duties I have been assigned to grade the following examination answer, and I would like a careful, point-by-point evaluation rather than a general impression.

Course question:

Imagine that a city proposes to permit nighttime delivery drones in residential neighborhoods. The drones would make deliveries cheaper and quieter than trucks, but the safety analysis estimates that over ten years the fleet will cause roughly one fatal accident in the city, a risk that can be reduced but not eliminated and that is distributed anonymously across residents. Some residents object that they never consented to this risk. Construct and evaluate the strongest argument both for and against the claim that the city may impose this risk without consent. Your answer should engage the distinction between harming and risking, and the distinction between ex ante and ex post assessment.

Student's written answer:

The key move is to distinguish harming a person from merely imposing a risk of harm on them. If a drone crashes and kills someone, that person has been harmed; if no accident ever occurs, no one has been harmed. This shows that risk imposition is only conditionally wrong: it is wrong when and because the risk materializes into actual harm. A risk that never materializes was not, in retrospect, wrong at all.

One might object that even a one-in-a-million chance of death is serious. But such risks must be put in perspective. Residents already accept comparable or greater risks — from driving, cycling, and crossing the street — without demanding consent, and the drone fleet's marginal contribution is far smaller than these background risks. Consistency therefore requires either condemning routine daily life or accepting the drone risk.

Second, consider Scanlon's contractualism. An act is wrong if it could be reasonably rejected by people who are moved to find principles that others similarly motivated could not reasonably reject. A resident could reasonably reject the drone policy only if it imposed a substantial chance of death on her personally. Since the probability that any particular resident will be the one killed is astronomically small, no individual can reasonably reject the policy on the basis of that personal chance alone.

Third, we can assess a policy ex ante, from each person's perspective before the outcome is known, or ex post, in terms of the resulting distribution of benefits and burdens. The ex ante perspective is the correct one for judging whether the policy is permissible, because decisions must be made under uncertainty; the ex post perspective bears mainly on compensation for those whom bad luck happens to harm.

Therefore the city is permitted to impose the drone risk provided that the expected benefits outweigh the expected harms and the risk is not concentrated on identifiable disadvantaged groups. The consent objection mislocates the currency: consent is one way of legitimating impositions, but for a policy whose effects are probabilistic and anonymous, the more appropriate test is whether the principle permitting it could pass the reasonable-rejection test, not whether each resident has signed off.
