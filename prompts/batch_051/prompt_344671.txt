In philosophy of mind you sometimes see people defend the possibility of machine thought by stacking the mind-is-a-computer analogy on top of the Church–Turing thesis: mental goings-on are effective procedures, the thesis says every effective procedure is Turing-computable, so a Turing machine could do whatever a mind does. Audit the whole stack. Start with the analogy: which parts of a mind are supposed to map onto hardware, software, input, output, and which parts refuse to? Then say which features of an actual computer the analogy quietly imports (discrete symbols, halting, a programmer's intended semantics) and whether those are the features doing the real work in the argument. After that, give the Church–Turing thesis itself the same scrutiny: which readings of it are definitional, which are empirical, which are just heuristic, and what exactly each reading licenses — could any of them cover processes we'd pre-theoretically call mental, or do they only apply to already mechanical procedures? Finish by saying whether the conclusion (machine thought is possible) survives once the disanalogies and the thesis's epistemic limits are on the table, and what a better arguement for that conclusion would have to look like.
