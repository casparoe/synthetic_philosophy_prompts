Two friends of mine got into an argument at the pub and I ended up having to referee, but I couldn't. The question was whether you could have come from a different sperm and egg. They asked me to settle it, so now I'm asking you.

(1) Ana said obviously not: if your parents had conceived a few days later, the child would have been your sibling, not you, because your life would have started from a different zygote. (2) Sam called that magical thinking: with the same parents and upbringing, the later child would have had a similar personality and been as much 'you' as you now are; identity can't hinge on a fertilisation timestamp. (3) Ana replied that similarity isn't identity — a table made from a different block of wood is a different table even if it looks the same, and people are no different. (4) Sam said people aren't individuated by there raw materials the way tables are, and anyway identical twins come from the very same sperm and egg yet are two different people, so gamete origin can't be what makes you the particular person you are. (5) Ana said he'd only shown that a shared origin isn't sufficient for being one person, which she never claimed; her claim was that being the same person requires the same origin, and twins don't touch that. (6) Sam said that's a retreat: if the same origin can still give two different people, then origin doesn't explain why you are you and not your twin, so 'you couldn't have come from different gametes' is just a slogan. (7) Ana answered that it still does real work because it rules out identity claims — a child conceived a year later can't be you — and that twin cases only show identity goes strange at the margins. (8) Sam said those margins are exactly the problem: if an early embryo splits, which of the twins was the one who was originally there? And a person whose body comes from two fused early embryos has two origins, not one, so the idea that each person has a unique gamete origin is literally false.

Please adjudicate this move by move. For each numbered move, say whether it is clearly mistaken, clearly right, or somewhere in between, and briefly justify the rating. Say what, if anything, both of them are missing, and who is more right overall. Please also separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present a contested view as the consensus. And I'd appreciate it in British English.
