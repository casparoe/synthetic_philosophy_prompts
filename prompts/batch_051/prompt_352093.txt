I teach an intro game theory course for philosophy students, about twenty people, one 50-minute session. I want an in-class exercise that makes them feel why iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies, the way it's first taught with only pure strategies in mind, can stop way too early: a strategy can be strictly dominated by a proper mixture of two others even when no single strategy strictly dominates it. I want them to run the elimination themselves on paper first, get stuck, and only then be told they're allowed to randomize, so the point hits them from doing it rather than from me saying it.

I've already worked out the 3x3 game, so design the activity around this matrix rather than building a new one—unless you spot an error, in which case say so and fix it.

(row player's payoff first, column player's second)

          X        Y        Z
A      (0,2)    (0,0)    (0,0)
B      (2,0)   (-1,1)   (-1,2)
C     (-1,0)    (2,2)    (2,1)

A is strictly dominated by a 50/50 mix of B and C, since that mix gives expected payoff 0.5 against every column, but A isn't strictly dominated by B or C alone. I also checked the columns: with A still around, no column strategy strictly dominates another (or even weakly dominates one). So pure-strategy IESDS eliminates nothing. But once you allow the mixed-dominance step and eliminate A, then X is strictly dominated by Y, then B by C, then Z by Y, and you land at (C,Y). Tell me if that sequence looks off to you.

What I need from you is the running of it: a stage-by-stage plan for the 50 minutes (paper and pencil? students paired or in groups? a vote?), a one-page handout, and a debrief that maps what they just worked through onto the formal distinction between strict dominance by a pure strategy and strict dominance by a mixed one, plus why letting mixtures in can let iterated elimination squeeze out much sharper predictions in this kind of game. The initial handout should only define pure-strategy strict dominance; don't introduce mixed-strategy dominance until after they've hit the wall, otherwise the whole thing becomes just a worked example. By the end I want them to see that 'no single strategy beats it' and 'no randomized plan beats it' come apart, and that one elimination can set off a cascade. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—I can imagine someone trying to eliminate a column strategy using a mixture that only weakly beats it, or students saying a mixed strategy is just playing randomly because you're indifferent. And be honest about the one thing the exercise can't itself establish: it makes it feel right that a rational player will never play a pure strategy that is strictly dominated by a mixed one, but doesn't prove it.
