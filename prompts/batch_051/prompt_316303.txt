I am a novelist, not a philosopher, and the vocabulary I am actually fluent in is the vocabulary of narrative craft: interiority, free indirect style, point of view and focalisation, motive as against reflex, subtext, scene and summary. I spend my working life deciding where the narration stands in relation to a consciousness — a character's, or none at all.

I have been trying to work out what Descartes' doctrine of the beast-machine amounts to when it is carried into that vocabulary. The position, as I understand it, is this: non-human animals are automata; their cries and their clever-looking actions are motions of matter without any accompanying thought; and the decisive outward evidence is that they lack real language and the flexible, reason-responsive action that language makes visible. I would like you to restate this position — and, where it helps, the standing commonsense objection that animal behaviour often reads as minded — in the vocabulary of narrative craft. Go through the relevant claims one by one. Which translate cleanly into claims about narration, point of view, and character? Which translate only with distortion? Which have no expression in that vocabulary at all?

Then give me the part I care most about: the residue report. What did not survive the translation, and why? Is the residue a sign that narrative craft tacitly presupposes the very interiority Descartes denies, so my vocabulary is rigged against him? Or does the failed translation expose something unclear or unearned in the beast-machine doctrine itself? Keep the translation honest: do not smuggle in a novelist's assumption that every behaviour has a mind behind it and then attribute the result to Descartes.
