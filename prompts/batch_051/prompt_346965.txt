In my seminar on religion and liberal democracy — usually 18 students, one 90-minute block — students can reconstruct the public-reason requirement almost verbatim, but they don't feel why critics say it is unfair to religious citizens. I want a structured role-play that makes them run into that objection themselves before it gets named, so the lesson arrives through what they actually do rather than through me lecturing. The scenario should be a municipal body deciding a genuinely contested local question — for example, whether to approve a supervised drug-use site, to permit a buffer zone around a clinic, or to renew public funding for a faith-based service provider. Each student gets a public role and a private comprehensive doctrine (some secular-humanist, some utilitarian, some theist from different traditions). The deliberation operates under a strict rule modeled on the duty of civility: only reasons that can be offered to other citizens as free and equal are admissible, and appeals to a comprehensive doctrine are ruled out of order. Please arrange the roles, scene, and rules so the burden of that constraint falls unevenly: secular participants can often state their underlying reasons without translation, while religious participants discover that their actual reasons keep getting disqualified or must be rewritten in a secular idiom, so that one or more of them will likely feel, without prompting, that the procedure is rigged. The debrief should then map what happened onto the distinction between public and nonpublic reasons, the translation proviso, the sincerity worry, the asymmetry objection, and the contested question whether the duty of civility binds ordinary citizens or only officials. I'd like concrete roles and materials, a timeline for the full 90 minutes, explicit guidance on what I should and should not say before the debrief, and an honest note on which feature of real public-reason debate a classroom role-play cannot reproduce.
