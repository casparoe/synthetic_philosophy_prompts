I serve on a university research-integrity committee that screens complaints about faculty research conduct and decides whether to dismiss them or refer them for a full misconduct investigation. I came to the committee after retiring from a career in the natural sciences, and I have lately been reading epistemology. A postdoc in the lab of Dr. M, a senior structural biochemist, has filed a complaint. Dr. M's lab published a well-received paper reporting that a particular protein complex assembles into a stable tetramer under physiological conditions. The postdoc spent eight months unable to reproduce the central result using the published protocol; she documented a careful series of negative experiments, which an outside collaborator reviewed for technique. When she brought the discrepancy to Dr. M, Dr. M replied in writing that the failure must reflect the postdoc's technical error, because the original result is known to be correct on independent lines of evidence, and she declined to investigate further. The complaint alleges that this refusal amounts to reckless disregard for whether the published claims are true. Our policy directs us to refer a complaint only if it alleges a plausible case of reckless disregard of that kind, and to dismiss good-faith scientific disagreements over evidence. The case strikes me as an instance of the dogmatism paradox: if one genuinely knows a proposition, then any apparent counter-evidence must be misleading, so knowledge can seem to license dismissing a failed replication out of hand. Please draft a short policy memo taking a definite position—dismiss or refer—setting out the reasoning, and giving an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side.
