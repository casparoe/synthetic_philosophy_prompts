A claim that recurs in thinking about writing systems, in both weak and strong forms, is that a script is not a neutral transcription of speech but a theory of the language it writes. On this view, an alphabet amounts to the hypothesis that the speech stream decomposes into a small inventory of segmental phonemes; a syllabary amounts to the hypothesis that the syllable is the unit speakers actually manipulate; and a deep orthography such as English's amounts to the hypothesis that morphological identity matters more than surface pronunciation. I want to know how much of this claim survives being operationalised, and I am not asking for a survey of the literature. Take the specific sub-claim that how phonemic an alphabet is is a real, measurable property of the script, and that this property does explanatory work rather than merely describing the script's historical or political origins.

Design the best concrete test of that claim you can. My own starting suggestion, which you should improve or replace, is a laboratory study with adults taught an invented alphabetic script for a small artificial language whose phonology is fully controlled. Run at least three conditions: one script that is genuinely phonemic for the language, one that is morphophonemic, and one that is deliberately built on a false phonological analysis — for example, a script that assigns distinct symbols to predictable allophones and thereby implies a phonemic contrast that no learner of the spoken artificial language should otherwise acquire. Measure, among other things, whether learners generalise the script to novel forms, whether they begin to treat the spurious contrast as real in tasks that have nothing to do with writing, and how much the script's structure rather than the spoken input predicts their behaviour. State precisely which pattern of results would count for the claim that the script is functioning as a theory, which pattern would count against it, and which pattern would only show that literate people can learn arbitrary conventions.

Then say what even the best version of this test could not settle, even in principle. In particular, address the distance between a script's behaving as though it encodes a phonological analysis and its actually encoding a correct one; the fact that the phoneme inventory of a language is itself partly a choice of analysis, so phonemicity can be measured only relative to a prior theory; and the case of a script that was phonemic once and has become false as the spoken language drifted without the spelling being revised. Is such a script a bad notation, or a failed theory — and can any experiment tell the difference? Please write your answer in British English.
