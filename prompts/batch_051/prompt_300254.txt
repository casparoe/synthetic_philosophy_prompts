I was sitting with two friends after a seminar on market efficiency, and they got into an argument that I've replayed maybe ten times without being able to referee it. Priya kept coming back to the no-trade theorem. Her version: if two fully rational people have the same prior and have no reason to trade except what each privately knows, then the moment one offers to buy and the other is willing to sell, each should treat the other's willingness as information and update, and the trade should unravel. So for her, the sheer volume of speculative trading is a standing puzzle for the idea that markets are full of fully rational agents. Jonah said the theorem only bites in a toy model. It assumes common priors, and it assumes nobody gains from the trade except through information; but actual traders differ in risk tolerance, time horizon, and balance-sheet needs, and they trade to rebalance, hedge, and shift exposure. Priya granted the hedging point but pushed back that most active trading is not hedging—it is a zero-sum bet on the same public information, where one side has to be wrong, and a rational person should wonder why they're so sure they aren't the wrong one. Jonah replied that the counterparty might just have a different prior—different background beliefs, different risk attitudes, different models—and there is nothing incoherent about two rational people pricing the same facts differently. Priya said that move made 'different priors' do too much work: once any disagreement can be re-described as a difference of priors, rationality becomes too cheap, because no possible market behavior would count against it. Jonah said that's just the standard subjective Bayesian picture, and common priors are a modeling convenience rather than a principle of rationality. Priya's last shot was that he'd conceded the real point: either markets are full of bounded rationality, or we have watered 'rational' down until it no longer constrains anything. Jonah said no—she had conceded the theorem never gets a grip on actual markets at all.

I genuinely don't know who won, and I don't want a balanced 'both sides have a point' answer. If you think they are ultimately using 'rational' in different ways, say which use is doing the better work in this debate.
