So I've been trying to figure out whether there's one principle behind the way I hear a bunch of "if" sentences, or whether I just have to clean up my gut. I'll give you my verdicts, and I want you to find the best principle (or maybe two or three candidate principles), state it precisely, and tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to give up.

Here are the cases.

Context: my neighbor's window got broken yesterday, and it had to be either the landscaper or my cousin, because nobody else was there.
(1) "If the landscaper didn't break the window, my cousin did." I accept this. It just follows from what I know.
(2) "If the landscaper hadn't broken the window, my cousin would have." I reject this. I'm pretty sure the landscaper did it, and my cousin had no reason to. The first and second feel completely different even though they look like variants of each other.

Now a pair that doesn't feel different at all:
(3) "If it rained last night, the game was cancelled." I'll say this even though I have no idea whether it rained; they always cancel for rain.
(4) "If it had rained last night, the game would have been cancelled." Also yes. These feel almost the same to me.

Then a pair where the first one breaks in a weird way:
(5) "If I took the other exit, I was on time." I know I didn't take the other exit. This isn't exactly false to me, I just can't bring myself to say it—the antecedent feels ruled out in a way that makes the whole sentence misfire.
(6) "If I had taken the other exit, I would have been on time." Perfectly normal to say, and I don't even have to know whether it's true.

Another like (1)-(2), shorter:
(7) "If the butler didn't do it, the gardener did." I accept it; I know it was one of the two.
(8) "If the butler hadn't done it, the gardener would have." I reject it; the gardener had no motive.

And then one where the mismatch vanishes again:
(9) "If you strike that match, it lights." I accept it; it's dry and it's a normal match.
(10) "If you struck that match, it would light." I also accept this. I dont feel any real gap between these two, unlike in (1)-(2).

My rough stab is: the plain "if did" versions go by what my evidence supports here in the actual world, while the "if had...would have" versions go by how things play out in the closest situation where the antecedent holds. That would explain (1)/(2) and (7)/(8). But then I'd expect (3)/(4) and (9)/(10) to come apart too, and they don't. So either the principle is wrong, or I'm misdescribing some of my verdicts.

Can you work out a principle that gets all of these, or a small set of candidate principles, stated precisely? Then tell me what it says about the case I can't make up my mind on: "If it doesn't rain tomorrow, the wedding will be outside." I accept that one. "If it didn't rain tomorrow, the wedding would be outside." I keep flip-flopping on whether that's fine or just weird. Does your best principle let that second one in, and does it force me to drop any of (1)-(10)?
