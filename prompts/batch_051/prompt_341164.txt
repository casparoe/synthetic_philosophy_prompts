The summary goes roughly: the Mu'tazila were the rationalists—moral status is built into acts themselves, so justice is good in itself and lying bad in itself, which is why God commands justice rather than the reverse; and unaided reason can figure out the moral landscape, so revelation doesn't add anything *moral*, only maybe some legal specifics. The Ash'arites are then described as the divine command side: good and evil are wholly fixed by God's command, and nothing whatsoever constrains God, so if He had decreed gratuitous cruelty it would simply *be* good, full stop. The post then says Ash'arism won the historical debate and that Mu'tazilism was essentially the view that ethics is independent of God, like an early secular humanism. I'm suspicious of the bits 'doesn't add anything moral,' 'nothing whatsoever constrains God,' 'won,' and 'early secular humanism'—they feel like one side's caricature of the other rather than a neutral gloss. But I don't know the material well enough to say where exactly the fair summary ends and the slant begins. Go through that summary piece by piece: which claims would someone who actually works on this stuff accept, which are subtle misreadings or overstatements, what would a careful Mu'tazili and a careful Ash'ari each object to, and does the bit about Ash'arism winning survive once the errors are fixed? Also, where do the two schools really part ways, as opposed to the places online discussions tend to mush them together?
