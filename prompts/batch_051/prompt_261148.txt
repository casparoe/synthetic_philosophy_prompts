I am an undergraduate taking a comparative federalism course that has spent several weeks on subsidiarity, and I am also finishing an applied statistics course on hierarchical modeling. I keep noticing a structural parallel between the two and cannot tell whether it is deep or merely a superficial kinship of the word aggregation. The version of subsidiarity we have studied holds that authority should sit at the most local level competent to achieve a given objective, with upward movement justified by cross-jurisdictional spillovers, economies of scale, or the need for uniform standards; fiscal federalism frames this as a trade-off between matching local preferences on one side and internalizing externalities on the other. The bias–variance trade-off frames estimation as a similar problem: fitting separate models to many small groups captures each group's idiosyncrasies (low bias) but yields noisy, unstable estimates (high variance), while pooling all groups into a single model yields stable but potentially misleading shared estimates (low variance, high bias). Hierarchical models resolve the tension by partial pooling, shrinking each local estimate toward the global mean in proportion to how noisy that group's own data is. On its face, the imperative to choose the right level of aggregation so that local information is used without mistaking noise for signal seems to do genuine work in both cases, and the shrinkage parameter in partial pooling looks strikingly like the continuous, proportionality-like judgment subsidiarity asks of courts and treaty-makers.

But I can also see reasons to distrust the analogy: subsidiarity is a normative, legitimacy-based principle about who may bind whom, not an optimal-estimation rule under a single loss function, and actual federal systems allocate powers categorically rather than tuning a shrinkage dial.
