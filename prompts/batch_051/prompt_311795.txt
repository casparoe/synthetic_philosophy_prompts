I've been going round in circles on the reductionism/anti-reductionism fight in the epistemology of testimony, and I honestly can't tell whether it's a real argument or two groups talking past each other. On one side people follow Hume and say our trust in what others report is just an induction from observed regularities between reports and the facts; on the other side people follow Reid and say testimony is a basic source of evidence, like perception, so you're entitled to believe people by default unless you have positive reason not to. The anti-reductionists keep pressing the circularity point — you can't build up the relevant track record of testimony matching reality without already relying on other people's word, so the reductive project can't get started on its own terms. Reductionists reply that this is just how the evidence is gathered, not a principled objection.

Here's the bit of Hume I keep coming back to, from An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding, Section X, Part I:

'This species of reasoning, perhaps, one may deny to be founded on the relation of cause and effect. I shall not dispute about a word. It will be sufficient to observe, that our assurance in any argument of this kind is derived from no other principle than our observation of the veracity of human testimony, and of the usual conformity of facts to the reports of witnesses.'

What strikes me is that Hume himself half-concedes the verbal point there, and then just carries on. So I don't want a verdict on whether reductionism or anti-reductionism is correct. I want a diagnosis of the dispute itself. Is it substantive, is it partly verbal, or are the two sides answering different questions — about justification versus entitlement, or about how trust actually develops versus whether we're rational to trust — under a single heading? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress, or should we stop expecting the two camps to move?
