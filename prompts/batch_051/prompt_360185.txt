I sit on the grants committee of a small family foundation, and I've developed a personal rule that keeps causing arguments. Two proposals will come in with the same success probability on paper—say both put the chance the project works at 80 percent—but one 80 is backed by years of trial data and several independent replications, and the other 80 comes out of a single model some consultancy we've never used before fitted to a tiny pilot. My rule is to treat these as completely different forecasts: I'll happily vote for the first, but I'm effectively a no on the second until we pay for more evidence, even though the numbers sit right next to each other. My defense is something I half-remember from reading Keynes: there's a difference between the probability you assign and the weight of the argument behind it. Probability says where you stand; weight says how much you actually have to stand on.

The rest of the committee says I'm double counting. If I've accepted the 80 as the probability, then the evidence is already in it, and equal probabilities ought to move a decision equally. Are they right, or is my policy defensible? Could a strict expected-utility maximizer treat these two 80s differently, and if so, where exactly does the thin evidence enter—in the estimate itself, in how much the estimate could shift with new information, or somewhere else? And if my rule is right, is there a principled way to say how much weight is enough, or do I just have to rely on gut feeling?
