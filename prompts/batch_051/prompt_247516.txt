I've been trying to think about the stock market crash of October 19, 1987 through the lens of no-trade theorems, and I want a disciplined analysis rather than the standard program-trading story I already know. The facts as I understand them: the Dow Jones Industrial Average fell more than 20 percent in a single day, there was no commensurate piece of public news, and yet trading volume was enormous. The Milgrom–Stokey no-trade theorem, the market version of Aumann's agreeing-to-disagree result, says roughly that rational agents with common priors should not be able to trade with one another purely on differences in information: your willingness to sell to me at my price is itself information that should move my posterior, and once it has, the mutually beneficial trade disappears. So Black Monday looks like a double puzzle from that standpoint—both the size of the price move, given how little news there was, and the fact that so much trade happened at all.

What I'd like is a careful mapping. Who are the agents the theorem would model that day, what counts as their private information, and exactly which trades does the theorem rule out? Then go through the theorem's assumptions—common priors, common knowledge of rationality, purely informational motives, the relevant sense of rational—and tell me which ones failed, and which failure does the most work in explaining the volume. In particular, I want to know how much of the day's trading should be classified as hedging rather than speculation on information, and whether the price action can be understood as the market aggregating information about fundamentals or about other traders' selling pressure. Finally, run the inference in the other direction: does the crash refute the theorem, or does it show the theorem is a baseline that forces you to name which assumption you are abandoning? And be honest about what the theorem cannot explain about that day, and whether any nearby model from the epistemology of markets does better.
