A colleague who works in the philosophy of set theory showed me the following argument, and I have not been able to find the defect in it. I would be grateful if you would work through it step by step and tell me which premise or inference you would reject, why that step looked attractive, and what rejecting it commits one to. Please write your answer in American English.

Take the unit interval [0,1]. Throwing a dart at it means selecting a real number uniformly at random, with distinct throws independent of one another. Fix any function F that assigns to each real number x in [0,1] a countable set F(x) of real numbers in [0,1]. The argument runs as follows.

(1) Throw two darts, obtaining points x and y.
(2) With x fixed, F(x) is a fixed countable subset of [0,1], hence of Lebesgue measure zero; so y lies outside F(x) with probability one.
(3) By the symmetry of the two throws, with y fixed, x lies outside F(y) with probability one.
(4) The set on which x and y are equal has measure zero, so the points are distinct with probability one.
(5) The exceptional outcomes in (2), (3), and (4) each have probability zero, so their union has probability zero.
(6) Hence, with probability one, x and y are distinct, x is not in F(y), and y is not in F(x); in particular, some pair of distinct points in [0,1] has both properties.

Now suppose the continuum hypothesis is true. Then [0,1] can be well-ordered in such a way that every point has only countably many predecessors in the ordering. Let F(x) be the set of predecessors of x under this well-ordering. Each F(x) is countable, so step (6) applies and yields distinct points x and y with x not in F(y) and y not in F(x). But a well-ordering is total: for any two distinct points, one precedes the other, which is to say that one point lies in the other's set of predecessors. This is a contradiction, so the continuum hypothesis must be false.

I find it hard to believe that an argument this brisk really refutes the continuum hypothesis, yet each step seemed plausible as I read it. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? If there is more than one defensible place to resist the argument, please compare the costs.
