Consider the following argument about small breaches of punctuality.

(1) If Nadia tells Ruth, "I'll meet you at 3," without qualification, and Ruth plans her afternoon around that, then Nadia has promised to be there at 3, and a promise normally gives the promisee a right to the promised performance unless there is an accepted excuse.
(2) Nadia arrives at 3:12. She has no excuse in the familiar sense: no traffic, no emergency, no forgotten prior commitment; she simply left too late.
(3) But in a loose, ordinary social arrangement, a bare appointment time is governed by a conventional grace period. A reasonable person in Ruth's position would understand "at 3" as "at 3, give or take ten minutes or so," and would not count an arrival within that window as a breach of the promise, even though she might notice it.
(4) If a reasonable promisee would not count an arrival within the conventional grace period as a breach of the promise, then that arrival violates no promise-based obligation.
(5) Therefore, Nadia's arriving at 3:12 without an excuse violates no promise-based obligation, which seems to conflict with the claim in (1) that she promised to be there at 3.

I find each premise individually plausible, but I also think chronic lateness is a real moral failing, so I cannot tell whether the argument is too clever by half or genuinely dissolves the apparent problem. Is the argument valid as stated? If it is not, identify exactly which step fails and say under what reading it fails—whether the trouble is an equivocation on "at 3," a suppressed premise about conventions overriding undertakings, a slide from "no felt grievance" to "no breach," or something else. If it is valid, explain how (1) and (5) are compatible, and state precisely what Nadia has promised on your reading.
