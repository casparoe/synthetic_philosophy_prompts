A familiar argument in reproductive ethics, the procreative beneficence argument, runs roughly as follows. When prospective parents using IVF must choose among several viable embryos, and when genetic information at hand gives them evidence about how the resulting child's life would likely go, they have a moral reason to select the embryo with the best expected life—not merely to avoid recognized disease, but to pursue the best available prospect. The argument was developed for a setting in which the relevant genetic information mostly concerned single-gene conditions with large, well-understood effects, so the relevant comparisons between possible children seemed comparatively straightforward.

Genome-wide association studies and commercial embryo screening have changed that setting. It is now possible to compute polygenic scores that aggregate thousands of common genetic variants, and some fertility services have begun to apply or advertise such scores for embryos, covering conditions such as schizophrenia, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular disease, as well as non-disease traits such as height and educational attainment. Polygenic scores, however, are much murkier epistemic objects than a single-gene diagnosis: their individual-level predictions are weak, they are sensitive to the reference population and to choices in how the score is built, and the ranking of a batch of embryos can shift when the scoring model changes.

My question is about the fate of the argument, not about what regulation should follow. Does the procreative beneficence argument survive this development in essentially its original form, does it need repair, or does it stop applying? Please identify the specific premise under pressure—whether it is the comparability of possible lives, the epistemic reliability of the selection criterion, the scope of parental beneficence, or something else—and say whether a defensible repaired principle can be stated: one that preserves the original idea without forcing parents to chase noisy score differences, and without collapsing into the conclusion that almost all selection is impermissible.
