Consider three people who each purchase and eat animal products produced under conditions involving comparable animal suffering and death. The first is an affluent urban professional in a high-income country, with cheap and abundant plant-based alternatives in the shop next door. The second is a pastoralist in a semi-arid region who keeps goats for milk and meat because the land will not support crops and imported plant foods are unavailable or unaffordable. The third is a low-wage worker in a wealthy country who lives in a food desert, has little time or transport, and buys the cheapest available calories, which are mostly animal-based. In much of our moral discourse these three are judged very differently: the first is treated as straightforwardly blameworthy, the second as having no obligation to change, and the third as somewhere in between or merely unfortunate. Since the harms to the animals are stipulated to be comparable, some difference among the agents' circumstances must be doing the justificatory work. What is that difference, and can it justify the asymmetry? A defensible answer should distinguish whether the alternatives affect the wrongness of the act or only the agent's blameworthiness, identify how scarce or costly the alternatives must be before judgment shifts, and test the proposed principle against adjacent cases. If no principle survives, say which way the asymmetry should be revised.
