Analyze the 2015 federal jury verdict against Robin Thicke and Pharrell Williams in the "Blurred Lines" litigation through the lens of the ontology of musical works. The facts to work with: after "Blurred Lines" became a hit in 2013, Marvin Gaye's estate claimed it copied Gaye's 1977 song "Got to Give It Up" not note-for-note but at the level of "feel" — the syncopated groove, the bass movement, the falsetto vocal placement, the party-with-cowbell texture — and a jury largely sided with the estate. Copyright law formally separates the musical work, as composition, from the sound recording, but the verdict seemed to treat the protected work as something thicker than melody, harmony, and lyrics. I want a careful mapping between this episode and the main positions in the ontology of musical works: (1) the Platonist or structuralist view that a work is an abstract sound-structure, roughly what a score or lead sheet captures; (2) the performance-based or nominalist view that a work just is its performances; (3) contextualist or "thick" views on which a work includes the sound-world, timbral choices, and performance means specified at its creation; and, if you find it useful, (4) views that treat recordings themselves as works. For each position, say what the verdict would have to count as the musical work, and which parts of the dispute it explains or misdescribes. Then reverse direction: what does this case show about the theories — does it confirm one, expose an anomaly in another, or fall in a place where they are all silent? Be disciplined about the mapping, and where the ontology has nothing to say about the legal question, say so rather than forcing it to speak. End with a committed verdict: which position, if any, best handles the case, and what you would have to believe about musical works to justify the jury's result.
