At last week's story meeting, two people on my desk got into it over a news brief. The reporter, Ben, had written a lede saying a new analysis found daily diet-soda drinkers had a 23 percent higher stroke risk over ten years. The editor, Priya, killed the brief: the absolute increase was small (about 2 in 1000 people to about 2.5 in 1000), and the study couldn't establish causation. Ben said he never wrote a false sentence, so it wasn't a lie. She said the whole point of a lede is to produce a belief, and the belief it produced—that diet soda creates a meaningfully higher stroke risk—is false; deliberately producing false beliefs with true sentences is exactly what misleading is, and in her book that's morally no better than lying. He said if that's the standard, every article we publish is a lie, because journalism just is selection and compression; nobody reads a 60-word brief as exhaustive. She said there's a difference between choosing a focus and burying the denominator on the one number you put in the lede. He said the true-under-a-framing versus made-up distinction is what separates journalism from PR, and if we collapse it, we're flacks. She said PR runs on true statements arranged to deceive; if "it's true" buys you a clear conscience, we're already in PR. He came back that if the absolute risk had been high, the same lede would've been fine, so her real objection is news judgment, not truthfulness. She said the news judgment is the moral part, because he knew how readers would over-read the number and used that inference as a tool—which is what liars do, except he gets to keep the defense that every word was accurate.

They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't. Please go through the argument in order and adjudicate each move: who is right about which specific point, where someone overreaches or misdescribes the other's view, and what both of them are missing. The real question here isn't whether the brief should have run but whether Ben's true-but-slanted framing is morally different from lying. As you do that, separate what's settled in the philosophy and linguistics of lying versus misleading from what's genuinely contested, and don't present a disputed view as if it were the consensus.
