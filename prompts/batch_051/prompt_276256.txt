I'm giving a paper next month at an environmental history workshop, and what I want from you is a Q&A prep sheet, not a redraft of the talk. My thesis is that historians should stop treating non-human factors — pathogens, climate shifts, volcanic events — either as inert background conditions or as full-blown agents with intentions, and should instead treat them as load-bearing processes: they do not deliberate or choose, but they can close off some historical paths, open others, and shift the costs of the options still available, and their causal contribution is not exhausted by the human responses they provoke. My argument, in short, is that the two familiar alternatives both fail. Environmental determinism ties outcomes to environmental inputs so tightly that it cannot explain divergence between similarly situated societies. The constructivist alternative treats a plague or an eruption as historically inert until humans interpret it, which strikes me as a parochialism about where historical causation can live. I develop this through three cases — an epidemic, a volcanic winter, and a slow climatic shift — in which, I claim, the non-human process changed the menu of options available to historical agents without dictating any particular choice from that menu.

The room will hold several distinct audiences, and I want to be ready for the objections this room will actually produce. The majority are environmental historians whose training makes them read any appeal to non-human causes as the first step back toward the determinism their field spent decades escaping. One climatologist will be there, visibly impatient with the way historians borrow the word agency for things that have no intentions. Two scholars in the room are sympathetic to actor-network theory and will likely think I have stopped halfway: if the volcano is doing real causal work, why not call it an actant, or else drop agency language altogether? And one senior political historian suspects the whole project is a way of letting humans off the hook for choices that were, in fact, choices. Prepare me for the objections I will actually get from this group — not the ones I would get at a climate-science conference or a posthumanism panel — ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, and phrased the way these particular people would put them. For each one, give me a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is softest.

Tell me as well what I should concede up front and what I should refuse to give, and include the question I should be most afraid of. Keep the entire prep sheet under 1,000 words.
