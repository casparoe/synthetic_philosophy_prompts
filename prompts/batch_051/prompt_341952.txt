I'm a software engineer with basically no formal philosophy background, but I got into reading about Bayesian stuff and came across something called van Fraassen's Reflection principle. The way I understand it, it says roughly that if you're rational, your current probability for some claim should equal the expected probability you think you'll assign to it later, after seeing more evidence. So if I now think that future-me, after the postmortem, will be 0.8 confident that the bug was in the cache layer, then current-me should already be at 0.8.

I've turned this into a habit at work and I want to know if it's defensible. Specifically: whenever I catch myself predicting what I'll believe after the next round of logs, metrics, or code review, I just adopt that predicted future credence as my current credence right away. I don't wait for the new evidence. The rationale in my head is that future-me is the same agent as me, just with strictly more information, and if I already expect him to be at 0.8, then staying at 0.5 until then is a kind of self-contradiction.

But this has led to some weird outcomes. Last sprint I was 0.4 confident our memory leak was in the transaction pool. Then I predicted that after seeing the profiling graphs I'd be 0.75 confident it was in the caching layer, so I immediately updated to 0.75 and wrote the cache fix before looking at the graphs. The fix happened to work, but that felt lucky, not smart. Another time I used the same rule to push back on a colleague: she gave a detailed argument for why the race condition was in the queue, but my reflection-based number said future-me would still blame the scheduler, so I told her I thought she was probably wrong. That was awkward because she was right.

I know the principle wasn't meant as a practical debugging heuristic, but I also can't tell exactly where my habit goes wrong. I'd like you to answer: is this policy rationally defensible? Under what conditions would a version of it be okay? And from the inside, how can I tell the difference between legitimately following a norm of self-consistency and just laundering a hunch by pretending it's what a future expert version of me will believe? Also, maybe it matters: my predictions about future-me are not very calibrated. If I expect to be 0.8 after seeing more data, the actual post-data me often ends up at 0.6 or 0.9. Does the whole point of Reflection depend on having good calibration about your own future credences, or does it survive even when those predictions are bad?
