I have been in a debate society this year, and in our sessions on international organization our coach has repeatedly used one comparison to settle almost every question we raise about sovereignty. Joining a confederation, she says, is like joining a club: you agree to pay dues, you accept the club's rules while you are on its premises or acting in its name, you remain free to do as you like at home, and you can resign whenever the arrangement stops suiting you. On this picture, a customs union is just a club whose one shared rule is that every member charges the same tariff to outsiders; the members remain the ones who made that rule, so their sovereignty is delegated, not surrendered. She uses this to argue that "joining" never changes who is ultimately in charge, and that fears about a confederation hardening into a state are confused, because no one would say a club absorbs its members.

I would like to know how far this analogy can actually carry that conclusion. Could you work through the correspondences — what plays the role of the dues, the clubhouse, the rulebook, the resignation letter, and the home that each member still owns — and then identify the places where the mapping breaks? I am especially unsure about three points: (1) a club can usually change its rules by majority vote, or even expel a member, while that member is still inside, which looks more like being governed than like delegating; (2) if entering a customs union means a member can no longer set a tariff at its own border while it remains inside, it is unclear what "leaving" remains free to do, since exit may cost more than staying; and (3) a real club has a purpose and identity supplied by its members, whereas a confederation may come to exercise powers nobody individually delegated, because the delegated powers interact in ways the members did not foresee. Are these genuine disanalogies, or only details that can be added back into the club picture without changing its verdict? And if the club picture does collapse under them, what would be a better analogy for what "joining" means — one that does not quietly assume from the start that ultimate authority always stays with the member?
