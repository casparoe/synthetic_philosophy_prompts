I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, working on a feature about the intuition that none of us could have been someone else's child. I want to test a two-part criterion of human origin against some real biomedical cases. The criterion: (1) Necessity — a person could not have developed from a sperm or egg other than the particular sperm and egg from which they actually developed; and (2) Zygotic beginning — a person begins exactly when a particular sperm fertilizes a particular egg to form a particular zygote, so that zygote is the person's first stage. Now consider three cases. Monozygotic twins: one sperm fertilizes one egg, then the single zygote splits and yields two distinct people. A tetragametic chimera: two different sperm fertilize two different eggs, then the two early embryos fuse and yield one person whose body carries cell lines from both. A child born after mitochondrial replacement: the nuclear DNA comes from one sperm and one egg, but the egg's mitochondria are replaced with those from a third person's donor egg.

For each case, check it against the two conditions and say what verdict the criterion yields, including where the conditions are silent, forced, or unable to distinguish the people involved. Then say whether any case shows the criterion needs revision (for example, anchoring identity in the post-split embryo rather than the zygote, or dropping the zygotic-beginning condition), or whether the criterion can be defended as a claim about biological ancestry rather than about individuation. End by saying what would change your mind and where your reasoning is most likely to fail. Keep the response under 450 words.
