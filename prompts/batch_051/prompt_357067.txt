Next week I'm giving a lab meeting talk arguing that a future visual brain interface could let people see genuinely new colors — not just new shades, but colors no one's every seen, outside the usual hue circle — by driving the two color-opponent channels in combinations that normal vision can't produce, like exciting both sides of the red–green channel at the same time. My line is that the old 'forbidden colors' idea only ever showed that the retina and early coding can't deliver those combinations, not that the cortex can't represent them, so the space of possible experience is wider than the space of colors you could ever get from light bouncing off objects. The audience is my old vision science group: mostly psychophysicists who grew up on the opponent-process model and have run forced-choice color discrimination studies their whole lives, a couple of fMRI people who will push on where exactly I'd stimulate and what the BOLD signal would even show, and my old advisor, who thinks 'qualia' is a word undergrads use to avoid doing experiments. They're friendly but they will pounce. I need a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the talk. Give me the objections in the order I'm actually likely to get them, phrased the way this crowd would phrase them, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Which ones should I concede up front rather than fight? And what's the question I should be praying nobody asks?
