In Book V of the Nicomachean Ethics, in the course of working out a conception of justice in exchange, Aristotle argues that money is a conventional device introduced so that unlike goods can be compared and exchanged. He treats money as a common measure and as a stand-in for need, and he remarks that its name, nomisma, marks it as something established by convention (nomos) rather than by nature. The argument was shaped by the money he knew: coined metal, a durable thing with uses of its own, circulating in a community whose members must trade shoes for houses and stand in relations of mutual need.

Now consider two developments Aristotle did not envisage. First, money has become largely fiat: unbacked paper, central-bank reserves, and ledger entries created by banks, with no intrinsic use and no direct tie to the community's need to exchange. Second, mainstream price theory no longer models exchange as requiring a common measure or a shared substance of value: prices are ratios that emerge from supply and demand, and comparisons across heterogeneous goods are handled with utility functions. On their face, these developments seem to remove both supports of Aristotle's argument: that money measures something common to the goods, and that it represents need.

My question is whether Aristotle's argument survives these developments, needs repair, or is dead. I want to know which premise of the argument each development actually bears on, whether the argument can be restated in a form that still does philosophical work, and what, if anything, remains of it for the ethics of exchange today — for example, for reasoning about exploitative wages, price gouging, or what makes a market exchange fair.
