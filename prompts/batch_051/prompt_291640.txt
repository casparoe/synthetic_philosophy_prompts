You are a professional philosopher who works on dispositions, causal powers, and counterfactual analyses. Please think carefully, and begin by reconstructing my proposal in its strongest form before you evaluate it.

I have been trying to formulate what I call the Two-Component Analysis of disposition ascriptions, and I want an honest assessment of it. Here is the idea. Take a simple disposition ascription like "This glass is fragile" or "This sugar cube is soluble in water." The standard starting point is a conditional: x is disposed to M in response to stimulus S iff, were x to undergo S, x would M. Familiar problem cases show this is inadequate. A fink removes the disposition when the stimulus occurs: a fragile glass is made shatterproof at the instant of being struck, so it is fragile a moment before but does not break. A mask leaves the disposition intact but blocks its manifestation: a fragile vase wrapped in foam is still fragile even though a firm strike does not break it. So the simple conditional analysis fails.

My proposal is that we have been treating "x is disposed to M in response to S" as a claim about the pair of object and stimulus, when it is really a two-component claim:

(C1) x has, throughout the relevant interval, a causal basis B that would bring about M in response to S.

(C2) The circumstances in which the ascription is evaluated include a standing condition of non-interference: nothing outside B is doing the causal work that would prevent M.

On this analysis, finks and masks are not the same kind of failure. A fink destroys C1 at the moment of S: the causal basis is gone when the stimulus arrives. A mask leaves C1 in place but defeats C2: the basis is still there and still would bring about M, except that something outside it intervenes. I think this explains why we say the finkish glass is fragile before the strike but not during it, while the masked vase is fragile even during the failed striking. It also gives me a restricted conditional analysis: if C1 and C2 both hold, then were x to undergo S, x would M.

I am also tempted by a further consequence: that no disposition ascription is purely intrinsic, because its truth always depends on C2's being satisfied by the environment.

I would not be surprised to learn this is close to something already in the literature. Please tell me (a) what the nearest existing views are, (b) the three strongest objections to the Two-Component Analysis, and (c) whether any of those objections is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable.
