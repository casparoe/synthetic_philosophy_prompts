So I write about science and ideas for a living, and the episode I keep coming back to is the 2006 IAU vote that demoted Pluto. I dont need another take on whether the definition was good or bad, or a replay of the public outrage. What I want is a careful case analysis of that episode through the three main theories of concepts: prototypes, exemplars, and the theory theory.

Quick background in case you need it: Pluto had been the ninth planet since 1930; in the 1990s and 2000s other sizable bodies turned up in the Kuiper belt, especially Eris; and in 2006 the IAU voted in a three-part definition — a planet orbits the Sun, is round from its own gravity, and has cleared its orbital neighborhood. Pluto fails the third condition, so it was reclassified as a dwarf planet. Planetary scientists promptly split, the public was annoyed, and a lot of working geologists still use "planet" in a way that includes Pluto.

Treat the episode as a natural experiment for the three theories of concepts. For each theory, tell me what it says was actually going on in the dispute. A prototype theorist presumably sees the category as organized around best examples (Jupiter, Earth), with Pluto as a weird peripheral member — so what does that predict about who resisted the demotion and why? An exemplar theorist would say people classify by implicit comparison to stored examples; then the disagreement is partly about which exemplars come to mind (Pluto next to Mars versus Pluto next to Eris). The theory-theory view says the concept is embedded in a causal/explanatory theory of what planets are and how they form, which is why the IAU's orbit-clearing criterion looked principled to dynamicists and arbitrary to geologists.

Be hard on each account. Where does prototype theory fail to explain why anyone needed a formal vote, or why the boundary mattered enough to fight over? Where does exemplar theory make the whole thing look too private and subjective, and miss the role of institutional authority? Where does the theory-theory over-intellectualize ordinary usage, or smuggle in the conclusion by choosing the scientific theory first?

Then run the analysis in reverse and tell me what the episode shows about concepts themselves. Was "planet" behaving more like a natural-kind term (something you go out and discover) or a pragmatic label (something a community decides)? Did 2006 reveal that the concept was never really shared, or that it was shared but vague? What does the fact that the dispute didn't end in 2006 tell us about concept change?

End with your bottom line as a short label in the form \answer{...}, e.g. \answer{prototypes best explain the public, theory theory best explains the experts} or \answer{planet is a stipulated kind} or whatever best states your verdict.
