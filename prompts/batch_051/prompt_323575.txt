I spent much of last evening arguing with a friend about whether chains of justification must come to an end. His position was foundationalist: every reason, he insisted, must terminate in a basic belief that requires no further support, since a justification that never bottoms out is an inference that never returns a verdict. Against this I sketched a coherentist view on which beliefs are justified by their reciprocal fit; he dismissed it as a cycle with no base case, and the conversation stalled before either of us could say precisely why a cycle is vicious.

Afterward I noticed that the three traditional positions in the regress debate map onto the three structural possibilities for a recursive definition or a dependency graph. Foundationalism resembles a recursion that terminates because every branch of every call eventually reaches a base case. Coherentism resembles a cycle of dependencies with no base case at all; and yet cycles are not automatically undefined in mathematics or programming, because a system of simultaneous equations can have a solution and a mutually recursive definition can denote a value through its fixed point. Infinitism resembles an infinite descending chain of dependencies; in a lazy language such a chain can still be productive, provided it is generated by a finite rule, the way an endless list is given by a seed and a step function rather than element by element.

I cannot decide whether this is a real correspondence or an analogy only at the level of diagrams. What would have to be true of epistemic justification for facts about termination, fixed points, and productivity to transfer to it? In particular: the lazy-list case seems to require a finite generator, and infinitists do not obviously possess one, which suggests the analogy might actually undermine infinitism — unless it shows that infinitism is coherent only if it supplies something like a generator. Where does the mapping break, and what does its breaking tell us about the regress problem itself?
