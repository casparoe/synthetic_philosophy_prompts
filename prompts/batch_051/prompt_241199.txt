I'm working on a short piece about the testimony debate and I'd like comments before I take it further. Heres the draft:

Most discussions of testimony set it up like this. Anti-reductionists say: if somebody tells you p and you have no special reason to doubt them, you get a default entitlement to believe p, much as you're entitled to trust your senses until something goes wrong. Reductionists say: no, your justification always has to come from background evidence that this person is reliable, along with your perception that they said p and your understanding of p. Without that, the bare fact that someone asserted p gives you nothing.

I have a hunch that both are right, but at different levels — not quite the usual local-reductionist compromise. Call the first level uptake. To hear an utterance as an assertion in the first place, rather than as just an acoustic event, you have to treat the speaker as someone answerable to the truth about p and as someone whose say-so is meant for you. That stance is not earned speaker-by-speaker; it's part of what makes linguistic exchange possible at all. Call the second level acceptance: the settled attitude you act on and reason from later. Acceptance should be sensitive to everything else you know about the speaker and the world. Uptake gets you to the starting line; acceptance decides whether you stay there.

If that's right, then the old debate has partly been a grain-size confusion. Anti-reductionism is really a thesis about uptake, and reductionism is really a thesis about acceptance. But I'm not sure the two levels are as clean as I want them to be.

So, comments specifically: where is the weakest load-bearing claim? I'm espically worried that I've just relabeled the familiar split instead of resolving it, and that the uptake/acceptance distinction is doing gerrymandered work. Also tell me if I'm being unfair to anti-reductionism—I'm going mostly from memory of how it's usually summarized, and I may be attacking a softened version of it.
