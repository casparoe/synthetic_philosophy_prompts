I'm an undergrad in a seminar on Kant's ethics, and for my final paper I keep coming back to a passage near the start of the Groundwork (Abbott translation, Fundamental Principles of the Metaphysic of Morals) that my professor sort of brushed past. Kant says:

Everyone must admit that if a law is to have moral force, i.e., to be the basis of an obligation, it must carry with it absolute necessity; that, for example, the precept, 'Thou shalt not lie,' is not valid for men alone, as if other rational beings had no need to observe it; and so with all the other moral laws properly so called; that, therefore, the basis of obligation must not be sought in the nature of man, or in the circumstances in the world in which he is placed, but a priori simply in the conception of pure reason; and although any other precept which is founded on principles of mere experience may be in some respects universal, yet in as far as it rests even in the least degree on an empirical basis, perhaps only as to a motive, such a precept, while it may be a practical rule, can never be called a moral law.

I want to use it to think about a case Kant obviously never had in mind: a piece of AI we're calling a "pure judge." It can inspect any action description, output moral verdicts (required, forbidden, permissible), and even explain those verdicts in terms of universalizable maxims. But it is deliberately built with no actuators, no outputs to other systems, no way to affect the world or even turn itself off. It can judge, but it cannot act on its judgments. What I want is for you to apply the passage to this case. First, walk through what Kant means by "moral force" and "basis of obligation" and why he thinks that basis has to be a priori in pure reason, not in human nature or circumstances. Then tell me whether the pure judge qualifies as a rational being under the law. If yes, what would its obligation even consist in—are there duties of inner judging, or a duty to avoid certain false judgments, or is it merely "subject to" the law while unable to violate it in action? If no, identify the exact condition in the passage that excludes it, and say whether that forces Kant to admit the law is less than "for all rational beings." I keep worrying that the objection "the AI only simulates judgment because it can't make its judgment effective" is just an empirical fact about wiring, which Kant seems to bar from the basis of obligation. But maybe it isn't—maybe having a will that can make judgments effective is an a priori condition Kant is implicitly assuming. End with a verdict on whether Kant's text can handle the pure judge case without quietly importing empirical facts about human agency, or whether this is a genuine hole.
