I have a working knowledge of Bongard problems — the two-by-six grid puzzles in which six panels on the left instantiate a hidden rule and six on the right are counterexamples, and the solver must infer the rule from those few examples — and of the way machine-learning researchers treat them as a small laboratory for few-shot concept induction and rule underdetermination. I have only a passing acquaintance with the philosophical literature.

I want a translation exercise, not a survey. Restate Nelson Goodman's new riddle of induction — the challenge posed by predicates like grue and bleen, which fit all observed cases but diverge on the next one — in the vocabulary of Bongard problems. Treat a Bongard problem as a case in which many candidate rules are consistent with the displayed panels, and treat Goodman's challenge as a constraint on which of those candidate rules a problem-designer or solver may legitimately project onto new, unseen panels.

Go through the elements one by one and make the translation explicit. What carries over cleanly, what must be distorted to fit, and what has no expression at all? In particular, say what happens in this vocabulary to the notions of lawlikeness, projectible predicates, and entrenchment. Then give me the residue report: does the untranslatable remainder point to something the Bongard and few-shot-learning framework is missing, or to something Goodman's formulation left less precise than it seemed? Keep the translation honest — do not smuggle the target framework's assumptions in and call them translation, and do not soften Goodman's claim until it fits.
