I'm a software engineer at a small startup, and I've ended up deep in a project where a bunch of reinforcement-learning agents have to negotiate over access to shared compute. I don't have a philosophy background, so the formal bargaining theory stuff is pretty hard going. I've basically got the picture that the Nash bargaining solution satisfies independence of irrelevant alternatives, while the Kalai-Smorodinsky solution instead satisfies monotonicity, roughly that if one party's options improve then its agreed share shouldn't go down. In our system the feasible set shifts every few seconds as jobs arrive and die, so this distinction isn't a toy example for us.

I'm trying to decide whether to build a prototype around a KS-style allocation rule rather than something Nash-like, and I'd like someone to actually force me to weight the implications instead of listing them. The two or three consequences that seem to matter for our case are: the allocation can move even when the new option is never chosen, which might make it easier to explain to auditors but harder to stabilise; the monotonicity property depends on cardinal utility numbers from our reward model, which I don't really trust; and the KS rule seems to require tracking the best-case outcome for each agent at each timestep, which could be expensive in a distributed system. Which of these should most change my engineering decision, and which are overrated? Defend the ordering, and say what would flip it. I want a defended agenda for where to spend the next quarter, not a survey of differences.
