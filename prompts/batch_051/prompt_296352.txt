I'm taking a combined course on Aristotle's four causes and the philosophy of biology, and I'm designing a handout that sorts people by where they think teleological explanation still earn its keep in the natural sciences. Please produce a branching decision tree rather than a prose comparison. The angle I want it to probe is the line between 'for the sake of' talk that is meant literally and teleological language that is only a convenient manner of speaking. The leaves should be: neo-Aristotelian realism about natural ends, teleosemantics (functions as selected effects), instrumentalism about teleological language, a Kantian-style regulative view, and eliminativism about final causes. The audience is fellow undergraduates who have read one chapter of Aristotle and one paper on functions; they should be able to answer every node without knowing these labels. Build the tree from the logical space rather than from the names: set out the questions and allowed combinations first, locate each position among them, and note any unoccupied combinations with a one-line diagnosis of whether the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Fix the diagnostic questions around three cases — the vertebrate heart, the peacock's tail, and the development of an embryo from a single cell — with at most one question using a non-living example such as water running downhill, to test how far respondents will extend 'for the sake of'. Keep the tree to five or six leaves and at most five questions. Flag any pair of positions the tree cannot separate on non-verbal grounds, and say which question, if any, separates them only as a doctrine about language. End with a final line reading 'Summary: ...' giving a one-sentence account of the tree's overall diagnostic logic.
