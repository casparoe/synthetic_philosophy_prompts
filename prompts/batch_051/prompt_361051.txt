I have been trying to work out whether my views about competitive institutions and competitive character actually hang together, and a passage from John Stuart Mill's On Liberty, Chapter V, has become the sticking point. Here is the passage:

“Whoever succeeds in an overcrowded profession, or in a competitive examination; whoever is preferred to another in any contest for an object which both desire, reaps benefit from the loss of others, from their wasted exertion and their disappointment. But it is, by common admission, better for the general interest of mankind, that persons should pursue their objects undeterred by this sort of consequences. In other words society admits no right, either legal or moral, in the disappointed competitors, to immunity from this kind of suffering; and feels called on to interfere, only when means of success have been employed which it is contrary to the general interest to permit—namely, fraud or treachery, and force.”

Here is where I have landed on the topic:

A. Competitive practices—examinations, hiring contests, markets for scarce goods, and the like—are justified only by their benefits to people outside the competition, not by any claim the winners have to the prize.

B. Mill is right in that passage: the losers in a fair competition have no moral or legal right to be shielded from losing, from wasted effort, or from disappointment.

C. Competitiveness, as a settled trait of character, is a vice. It disposes people to treat another person's success as their own loss, to feel diminished by others' flourishing, and to turn cooperative goods into zero-sum contests.

D. Competitive institutions cannot work well unless many of the people within them are competitive in something close to the sense in (C): they must care about winning, rank their performance against others, and be willing to endure repeated losses.

E. A well-formed person could participate in a competition with full seriousness and still feel no resentment or self-diminishment when a rival wins fairly; the desire to win and the desire that the better competitor win can coexist.

F. Social institutions should be designed, where feasible, to reward and cultivate virtue rather than vice, even at some cost in efficiency.

Run an audit of this set. Which pairs are in genuine tension, which pairs only look in tension once the terms are distinguished, and which commitment would have to give to make the whole package coherent? For each genuine tension, say how severe it is and what the cheapest repair would be, and then identify the single revision that would do the most to clean things up. I especially want to know whether (B) is doing more work than I think.
