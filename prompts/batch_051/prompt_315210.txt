I've been trying to work out what separates an inflected form of the same word from a derived form of a different word, and I'd rather proceed from my own judgments about cases than from a definition. Here is where I land.

- 'walk, walks, walked, walking' are forms of one word. So are 'go, went, gone'; the fact that 'went' looks nothing like 'go' does not make it a different word.
- 'cat, cats', 'dog, dogs', 'mouse, mice', 'child, children' are one word each. If anything, the irregular plurals feel more firmly one word, not less.
- 'tall, taller, tallest' are one word; the comparative and superlative are still the same word.
- 'quick' and 'quickly' feel like two words, even though -ly is completely regular and the meaning is highly predictable.
- 'sing' and 'singer' feel like two words; likewise 'walk' and 'walker', 'teach' and 'teacher', 'bright' and 'brighten'. They are mostly predictable, but they feel like new lexical items.
- 'happy' and 'unhappy' feel like two words, even though un- leaves the part of speech unchanged and merely negates.
- 'build, builds, building, built' are forms of one word; but 'building' in 'a brick building' feels like a different word from 'build', even though it has the same shape as the form in 'she was building'.
- 'email' as a noun and 'email' as a verb feel like the same word used in two ways, not two words. The same goes for 'walk' in 'she walked home' and 'she took a walk'.
- The noun 'John' in the phrase 'John's car' is the same word; the -'s is not really part of the word, since it attaches to the whole phrase, as in 'the man next door's car'. 'don't' from 'do not' is not a new word either.
- 'blackbird' is one word while 'black bird' is two; 'ice cream' feels like a single lexical item but is written as two words, and that mismatch bothers me.

I can already see the tensions. I treat -s, -ed, -er, and -ly all as regular suffixes, yet the first two produce the same word and the second two produce a different word. I treat a change of grammatical category as decisive in 'bright'/'brighten', but as no obstacle in 'email'/'walk'. I treat un- in 'unhappy' as creating a different word even though it changes no category, while I treat -er in 'taller' as not creating a different word even though it also changes no category. And the same -ing gives me the same word in one use and a different word in another.

Is there one principle—or a small set of candidate principles—that captures these verdicts, and if not, which of them should I give up? Please state the principle precisely, say which of the listed verdicts motivate each part of it, and apply it to the cases I have not settled: (1) 'greenish' from 'green'; (2) 'google' used as a verb; (3) 'driver' in 'truck driver'; (4) a hypothetical language in which one suffix marks plural on nouns when number is contextually relevant but can always be omitted, and on adjectives forms a noun meaning 'the ADJ one(s)'. Is that suffix inflection, derivation, both, or neither on your principle? Finally, say which of my original verdicts the principle cannot accommodate.
