My moral psychology professor keeps a running set of vignettes about weakness of will, and she has been giving me her snap verdicts as we go. So far she has ruled on six cases.

1. Lina resolved a month ago, and reminded herself this morning, to finish a fellowship application tonight. By evening, with the application open on her laptop, she starts a show as a break and keeps watching until midnight. The entire time she thinks she should be writing, judges that the application matters more, and is miserable at her own choice. Verdict: weak-willed.

2. Marcus and his partner have a savings account reserved for a down payment, and for years Marcus has insisted, including this week, that they must not touch it for a car. At a dealership he buys a sports car with that money, telling himself as he signs that the car is what will really make him happy and that the old rule was too rigid; he has no new information. Five days later he is sure it was a mistake and wishes he had kept to the plan. Verdict: weak-willed.

3. Priya has no resolution, promise, or plan about late-night desserts. Asked in the abstract, she says her health matters much more than a dessert. But when dessert is actually in front of her, she eats it, enjoys it, and does not later wish she had done otherwise; she says both the abstract preference and the concrete choice are hers. Verdict: not weak-willed.

4. Nadia has severe contamination-related OCD and washes her hands until they bleed. While washing, she clearly judges that she should stop and that continuing is worse, but she feels unable to stop. Verdict: not weak-willed.

5. Omar trained all year and planned to run a fall race. Two weeks before it, an MRI shows a stress fracture, and his doctor says racing would put him at risk of surgery and a long layoff. He withdraws. Verdict: not weak-willed.

6. Sana planned to phone her mother on Sunday. She got absorbed in finishing a project, the day passed, and she never decided not to call; she simply forgot until Monday morning. Verdict: not weak-willed.

Next week's case is Taylor. In February, Taylor, who is in a longitudinal experiment on delayed rewards, chooses $300 on July 1 over $200 on May 1. She makes no commitment, tells no one, and forms no plan around the choice. On May 1 she is offered the same pair again — $200 today or $300 on July 1 — and takes the $200 today. Her finances and circumstances are unchanged since February. In June she genuinely wishes she had waited for the $300.

Predict her verdict on Taylor and explain the reasoning. In particular, state which rule or rules she seems to be running about weakness of will, which other plausible rules fit her six earlier verdicts just as well, which of those earlier verdicts does the most work in narrowing the options, and describe the cleanest further case that would settle what is still open between the live candidate rules. Please write the answer in American English.
