Im rereading Turings 1950 Mind paper where he sets up the imitation game. At one point he worries that if telepathy is real, the person asking the questions might just psychically sense who the hidden human is and ruin the test. So he says the human should be kept in a room that blocks telepathy, and he adds that the statistical evidence for telepathy is strong enough that he has to take the idea seriously. I want to treat that move as a case study in how scientists handle an anomaly at the edge of a theory. What exactly was Turing committed to about ESP? Was he saying it exists, or just that its existence would matter for the test and the evidence was enough to make that a live possibility? Under something like a Bayesian account of evidence, how many independent Rhine-style guessing results, at what hit rate, would you actually need before adding an ESP-proof room made sense as a research design decision rather than a superstition? And is the telepathy-proof room an auxiliary hypothesis protecting the imitation game from being spoiled, or does it just make the test cleaner? Where does a legitimate experimental control end and an ad hoc patch begin? I want to know what Turings throwaway worry reveals about the demarcation problem: can you set up a rigorous test while staying agnostic about a weird phenomenon, or does taking ESP seriously in the design already blur the line between science and pseudoscience?
