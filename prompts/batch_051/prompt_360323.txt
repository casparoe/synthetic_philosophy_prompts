For a project on whether a nation can be guilty across generations, I'm building a fictional-but-realistic reparations tribunal and want to check its record for coherence. The tribunal's standing doctrine treats criminal guilt as dying with the perpetrators, but allows that the state may still be answerable for wrongs done in its name; the open question is what that answerability requires. Its rulings so far:

1. Railway corridor: in the 1910s the state forcibly cleared a district to build a rail line; the railway is still state-owned and profitable, and the displaced community still lives, largely poor and identifiable, along the corridor. Decision: a share of railway revenues, return of unused land, and a memorial.

2. Dictatorship massacre: a former regime massacred members of a religious minority; the successor state explicitly repudiated that regime, no property was seized, and the descendants are dispersed and of average means. Decision: a formal apology, a national memorial day, and full archive access; no individual payments.

3. Colonial grants: a colonial administration's land office granted common land to private settlers; the settlers' descendants still hold and profit from that land, while the original community's descendants remain identifiable and poor. Decision: the state must fund a development trust and buy back a portion of the land, with an apology.

4. Occupation requisitions: under a foreign occupation, the country's own administration requisitioned grain under duress; the affected communities persist but are not worse off than their neighbours, and nothing requisitioned remains. Decision: a formal acknowledgment; no payments.

5. Private expulsions: in the 1930s private landowners violently expelled tenant families; the state issued no order, took no land, and did not intervene. The descendants are poor. Decision: claim against the state dismissed, on the ground that the landowners' heirs, if anyone, bear the liability.

A new case is before the tribunal: in 1952 the state forcibly moved a community to build a dam; the dam was dismantled thirty years ago, the river was fully restored, and no ongoing asset or revenue can be traced to it. The descendants still live as an identifiable and genuinely disadvantaged group at the relocation site, and their disadvantage is traceable to the removal.

I sit on the tribunal and have to draft a decision that coheres with our own precedents. What should we decide, and which of the five rulings is the closest analog? For each plausible outcome, state which underlying principle we would be adopting. Then give your best prediction of how the tribunal should decide, and identify a further hypothetical case that would most cleanly separate the candidate principles. Finally, say how confident you are in that prediction and which point you are least sure of.
