I am grading a second-year ethics exam. One question and one student response are reproduced below.

Question (20 marks). The golden rule is often given in two forms: a positive one, roughly: do to others what you would want them to do to you; and a negative one, roughly: do not do to others what you would not want done to you. First, explain what is meant by the literal reciprocity reading of the positive form. Second, give two standard objections to that literal reading. Third, explain how a standpoint or reversibility interpretation is meant to avoid those objections, and say what that interpretation commits the rule to. Finally, assess whether the negative form is merely a stylistic variant of the positive form or a morally different principle; where it helps, you may compare the golden rule with Kant's universalizability test.

Student answer (transcribed as submitted).

The golden rule appears in both forms across many moral traditions. On the literal reciprocity reading, I should treat others exactly as I would want them to treat me, with my own actual preferences as the measure. This reading faces a famous objection: a masochist who enjoys being harmed would be entitled or even required to harm others, because that is how he would want to be treated. A second objection is that people have different preferences, so doing to someone what I would want done to me can leave their actual wants and projects unaddressed. The reversibility reading is meant to fix this. It says I should not import my actual preferences, but should imagine myself in the other person's position, with the other person's tastes, commitments, and vulnerabilities, and ask what I would then want. Once that is done, the masochist objection disappears, since the question is what I would want if I were the other person. Under this corrected reading the rule is essentially Kant's categorical imperative: both ask whether one can rationally will a course of action from the standpoint of anyone affected by it, and the difference between them is only presentational. That is why the golden rule has survived in so many traditions: they are all gesturing at the same formal test. The negative formulation is likewise only a stylistic variant of the positive one. Any positive instruction can be restated negatively and back again, so the two forms are logically equivalent; societies simply chose whichever phrasing fit their idiom. The negative form should therefore be regarded as a useful rhetorical alternative, not as a separate moral principle.
