I am trying to discover what principle actually governs when it is legitimate to hold a belief on less than decisive evidence. The familiar contrast between thoroughgoing evidentialism and the more permissive attitude toward belief often associated with William James does not, by itself, settle how I should judge particular cases, and I find that my reactions to concrete cases outrun any simple principle I try. By "legitimate" I mean that the person is not blameworthy for the belief and the belief does not violate an epistemic duty; I do not mean that it is true, or that it would satisfy an evidentialist's standard. Here are the cases, with my own verdicts:

1. A juror in a criminal trial with roughly balanced evidence concludes that the defendant is not guilty because the defendant's manner reminds her of her own grandchild. I judge this not legitimate: the juror's office requires her to be moved only by the evidence presented.

2. A spouse discovers ambiguous messages that could indicate an affair, and the total evidence is close to balanced. The spouse believes the partner is faithful, partly because trust is constitutive of the relationship and partly from long familiarity with the partner's character. I judge this legitimate, and not merely excusable.

3. A scientist believes her hypothesis is true before the decisive experiment, even though her colleagues put the odds near one in five, and the belief is what keeps her working on a five-year study. I judge this legitimate, provided she updates honestly when the results arrive.

4. A patient with a serious illness believes he will recover on evidence his doctor calls genuinely equivocal, because hope improves his chances. I judge this legitimate, but it feels different from the scientist's case, and I cannot yet say how.

5. A parent believes her daughter did not commit the crime of which she is accused, despite strong public evidence, on the ground that she knows the daughter's character from a lifetime. I judge this legitimate, but I also think it looks worryingly close to the juror's case, and I cannot yet state the difference.

6. A person continues to hold a claim that has already been refuted by well-established evidence, and defends the attitude as a permissible choice to believe because it gives comfort. I judge this not legitimate.

7. A teacher believes her struggling student can catch up to grade level despite prior test scores, and this belief changes how she teaches; the student does catch up. I judge this legitimate, and the self-fulfilling character of the belief seems to carry real weight in my verdict.

I have tried two candidate principles and neither quite fits all seven. The first says that belief beyond the evidence is permissible just to the extent that holding it makes the believed proposition more likely to be true. This handles the teacher and perhaps the patient, but it threatens the scientist: her believing the hypothesis does not make the hypothesis true, even if it makes a decisive test more likely to happen, and I am not willing to say her belief is illegitimate. The second says that roles carry their own epistemic norms, so the juror's office imposes stricter evidential duties than the spouse's or parent's position does. This handles the juror, the spouse, and the parent, but I cannot see how it distinguishes the patient from the comfort-seeker in case 6, since each can point to a need for belief without occupying an office such as juror or spouse.

Is there a single principle — or a small package of principles whose clauses are made explicit — that captures all or nearly all of my verdicts, and which of the seven, if any, does it force me to overturn? State the principle precisely enough that it yields verdicts on cases I have not listed. In particular, apply it to the case I keep going back and forth on: a person believes his close friend has not betrayed him when the friend's own account conflicts with strong circumstantial evidence, and the friendship itself is at stake. Finally, mark which parts of your account you take to be settled among people who work on the ethics of belief and which parts are genuinely contested, and avoid presenting a contested view as the consensus.
