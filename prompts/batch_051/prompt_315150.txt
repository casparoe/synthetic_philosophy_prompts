I'm a high school junior serving as the student member of my regional science fair's animal-research review panel, and I need to circulate a decision memo before Thursday's meeting. The panel is reviewing a project that adapts the infant violation-of-expectation paradigm to jumping spiders: a prey item is shown moving behind an opaque barrier, and the project predicts that spiders will look longer when the barrier is removed and the item is absent rather than present, which the student describes as evidence that the spider expects the object to persist — a rudimentary form of object permanence. One panel member invokes Morgan's canon, the usual rule that we should not explain an animal's behavior by a higher mental faculty when a lower one will do, and argues that the longer looking could be produced by simpler factors such as perceptual novelty, habituation, or a general response to a disrupted display; since these have not been ruled out, the board should at least require the interpretation section to be rewritten in neutral, non-mentalistic terms. Another member replies that Morgan's canon was meant to discipline armchair interpretation of behavior already observed, not to forbid an experiment designed precisely to give the mentalistic hypothesis a chance to fail, and that, taken as a demand to always prefer the lower-level description, the canon would rule out nearly all experimental work on animal cognition, since an ingenious lower-level redescription is almost always available. Please draft the memo recommending one concrete course of action — approve as written, approve with required changes, or reject — with a brief time frame and an honest engagement with the strongest consideration on the losing side. I'd also like you to say how confident you are in what you claim and to single out the point you are least sure of.
