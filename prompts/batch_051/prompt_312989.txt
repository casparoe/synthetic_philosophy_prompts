I'm working through Hofstadter and Sander's Surfaces and Essences in a small reading group, and one of the book's targets keeps nagging at me because I used to teach it as settled. A psychology textbook I once assigned presented the mind as a kind of mailroom: every concept is a labeled pigeonhole, and categorizing an object means checking whether it matches the defining features written on the slot before filing it away. On that picture, category membership is all-or-nothing, the boundaries between concepts are sharp, and the slots exist before any particular item arrives — which is supposed to explain why our categories are stable and why thought can be orderly. A friend in the group insists that Hofstadter and Sander spend much of the book dismantling precisely this pigeonhole (or filing-cabinet) analogy, and that it is not a harmless simplification but a positively misleading guide to how concepts work.

I'd like a careful audit of how far the analogy holds. Lay out, ideally as an explicit mapping, what corresponds to what between the mailroom and the mind's concepts. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the analogy's intended use from the ones that don't, with particular attention to the features of the source that get quietly imported: separate compartments, one slot per item, slots that are already there before the items arrive, a fixed external sorting rule, and the assumption that something either is or is not in a given slot. Given Hofstadter and Sander's positive view that analogy-making is the core of cognition, say whether the conclusion the textbook drew — that concepts have sharp boundaries and are applied by checking defining features — survives once those disanalogies are on the table. If it does not survive, propose a better analogy for what concepts are and how they get applied, and say what that replacement captures that the pigeonhole picture cannot. Please write the answer in American English.
