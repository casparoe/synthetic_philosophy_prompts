Okay so I've been reading about how Bentham had this whole under-the-radar line of thought on sexual morality—most of it never published in his lifetime—where he argued, on roughly utilitarian grounds, that consensual sexual acts between adults just shouldn't be the criminal law's business. His claim as I understand it: punishing them causes more pain (shame, secrecy, blackmail, ruined lives) than whatever offence or disgust is being policed, and mere disgust at how other people live isn't enough to justify state punishment. I want to stress-test that view against a real case, not just have it explained back to me.

Take Wolfenden and the partial decriminalisation of gay sex in England and Wales. For orientation, since I don't assume you can look this up: the Wolfenden Committee reported in 1957 and recommended that homosexual behaviour between consenting adults in private should no longer be a criminal offence, drawing a line between private conduct and public expression; the Sexual Offences Act 1967 then partly implemented that, decriminalising consensual acts between men over 21 in private, in England and Wales.

Analyse that episode through the Bentham view. What does the view say the actors were actually facing, and what should they have done? Which parts of the Wolfenden reasoning does it explain—for instance the committee's insistence that the law should stay out of private consensual sex but still police the public side—and which parts does it not capture, or even sit in tension with? Then run it the other way: what does this episode show about the Benthamite position—confirmation, anomaly, or a case it never really anticipated? I don't want a comic-book version where Bentham 'predicted' Wolfenden; I want a disciplined mapping of who the player is, what the options were, what counted as evidence, and where the view has to go silent rather than be stretched.
