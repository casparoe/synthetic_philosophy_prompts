I teach a graduate seminar in social ontology, and a colleague in political theory has shared with me the draft of a glossary article he is preparing for a handbook. His summary of Searle's account of money as an institutional fact reads as follows:

"Searle's central example is money. A piece of paper currency is money not because of its physical composition but because the members of a society believe that it is money and treat it accordingly. Money is therefore a social construction in the strongest sense: the fact that this paper is a twenty-dollar bill holds only relative to our collective attitudes, whereas the fact that it is made of paper holds independently of what anyone thinks. Searle's further claim is that statements about money are not objective in the way that statements about the paper are; they report conventions rather than facts. The paper remains money only as long as collective belief in it persists, so if everyone ceased to believe, it would simply revert to being a piece of paper. On this reading, Searle's distinction between institutional facts and brute facts just is the distinction between convention and reality."

I suspect this flattens a carefully drawn position, but since the article will be widely used I want to be exact before I raise objections. Could you audit the summary? In particular: which elements are faithful to Searle, which are defensible simplifications, which would Searle explicitly reject, and whether the closing identification of institutional facts with mere convention follows from anything accurate in the summary. I would also like a brief account of how a defender of Searle would answer the objection that, if facts about money are relative to our collective attitudes, then they are not real facts.
