I'm a philosophy of science PhD student, and before I read any more attempts to solve the reference class problem I want the specification laid out on paper, becaue every proposal I've read so far seems to be answering a slightly different question. The problem as I'm using it is about single-case probabilities. Suppose I want to know how likely it is that this individual borrower defaults within a year. The usual move is to slot her into a reference class—borrowers with her exact credit score, borrowers in her zip code, borrowers with her full payment history—and read off the frequency, but those classes give different numbers and nothing about the borrower herself picks one out. So I need a theory of what a single-case probability assignment even is, and under what conditions it reports a fact rather than a stipulation.

Write the desiderata such a theory must meet: the cases it has to get right, from the mundane borrower case to the genuinely novel one like the first launch of a new rocket design where every natural class is tiny or question-begging; the distinctions it cannot collapse, such as objective chance versus evidential support versus practical risk; and the formal constraints it can't flout, with the consideration or example behind each. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just the preferences of particular camps—frequentist, Bayesian, propensity, whatever. Flag any pair you suspect cannot be jointly met, and tell me which single requirement does the most to rule out the proposals on the table. Format: exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, no introduction or conclusion.
