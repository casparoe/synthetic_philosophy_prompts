I've been going in circles about pain asymbolia anyways. The background is this: some patients with certain brain injuries report that they feel pain, and can locate it and rate its intensity, but they don't seem to mind it. No grimacing, no pulling away, no asking for painkillers. One patient will say something like "it's there, it's a 7, it's sharp, but I don't care." I've been trying to turn this into an argument about whether the badness of pain is built into the pain itself, and I keep ending up here:

(1) If the badness of pain were intrinsic to the pain itself, then any two people in the same pain state would have to be in the same state of badness.
(2) Pain asymbolia patients are in the same pain state as ordinary patients; they report the same intensity, location, and sensory quality.
(3) But they are not in the same state of badness—the pain just isn't bad for them, or at least not in the familiar sense.
(4) So the badness of pain is not intrinsic to the pain itself.
(5) Therefore the badness of pain must come from a separate evaluative response, and a pain without that response isn't bad.

I think the argument is valid and I'm inclined to accept all the premises, but everyone I've tried it on says I'm eliding something. I suspect the trouble is somewhere around (1) or (2), but I can't make it stick. Can you go through it step by step and tell me whether it's valid, and if it is valid, which premise I should be least confident in and what the strongest version of that premise would be? Or, if the whole setup is confused—maybe I'm sliding between two senses of "pain state"—then just say that plainly and don't try to salvage it.
