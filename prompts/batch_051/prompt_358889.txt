Consider Kavka's toxin puzzle: you are offered £1,000,000 if, at midnight, you genuinely intend to drink a mildly unpleasant but harmless toxin tomorrow afternoon, and the money is yours whether or not you later drink it. The standard difficulty is that you cannot simply decide to form this intention, because you already believe you will not drink the toxin once the reward is secured. Now vary the case so that the intended act belongs to the ethics of small professional promises. You have agreed with a colleague that you will be on a 7:00 a.m. release call tomorrow. At 8:00 p.m. you are offered £100 if, at 9:00 p.m., you genuinely intend to be on that call. At 8:30 p.m. the call is cancelled and the colleague releases you from the agreement, so by 9:00 p.m. you know you will not join. The offer pays only if the 9:00 p.m. intention is genuine, not for a merely conditional intention to join if the call somehow goes ahead. Joining remains possible—you have the dial-in details and could sit on an empty conference line—but nothing further hangs on it. Run the original puzzle's argument, then run the same argument on this variant. Do the smaller stake, the social rather than self-harming character of the act, and the fact that it concerns ordinary punctuality change the analysis, or does the variant still work as a toxin-style puzzle about the gap between intending and doing? If it collapses, say exactly where.
