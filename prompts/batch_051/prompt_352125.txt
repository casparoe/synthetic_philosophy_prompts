I've been teaching myself reinforcement learning, and I keep coming back to an idea I've been calling delta-suffering. The claim is: if an RL agent can suffer at all, the suffering isn't in the reward signal itself, it's in the gap between what the value function predicted and what actually showed up (the TD error, if I'm using the term right). Getting exactly the treat you predicted comes out affectively flat. Getting less than predicted hurts even if the number itself is still positive, and getting more than predicted feels good even when the number is negative—like a smaller shock than you braced for being a relief. So valence is the sign of the surprise, not the sign of the outcome. The part that bothers me is what this commits me to: a fully converged agent would have no prediction error left, so by my account it couldn't feel anything at all, while a badly trained agent with garbage value estimates would be feeling the most. That feels backwards if we think smarter or better-trained systems should be more capable of suffering, not less. I also can't tell whether I've said something real about artificial suffering or just dressed up a metaphor. Poke holes in this for me. Is it coherent, would it count as something people already believe under a different name, and what's the clearest case where it gives the obviosuly wrong answer about what an agent feels?
