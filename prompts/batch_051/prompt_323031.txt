I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, but I've been chewing on an idea that keeps showing up in my reading: free-floating rationales, reasons without a reasoner. The thought is that a consideration can genuinely count in favor of something even when there's no one whose reason it is, becuase nobody has noticed it or everybody has it wrong. Some philosophers find this obviously fine; others say reasons are the kind of thing that have to belong to a mind. I get the outlines but not the insides, and I keep filtering it through the stuff I actually know — like how a novel can be doing something that no character in it, and maybe not even the author, fully has as a reason.

Here's the ask. Restate this debate in the vocabulary of working fiction: character motivation, plot logic, what the story wants or earns, narrative justification, the implied author (that reconstructed sensibility behind the text), and the gap between what's on the page and what I privately intended. I'm fluent in that vocabulary from writing and workshops, not in the moral philosophy. For each side of the philosophical fight, tell me what translates cleanly, what you had to bend to fit, and what has no counterpart in my vocabulary at all.

Then give me the residue report, which is what I actually care about. Is there something the philosophers are arguing about that novel-talk cannot express, and is the failure a gap in my framework or a sign that their side of the argument is fuzzier than it looks? I suspect the story-wants idea lines up with reasons-without-reasoners pretty well, while something about authorial authority doesn't, but I can't tell wether that's a real difference or just me reaching.

Format: exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion.
