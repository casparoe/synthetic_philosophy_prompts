I've been drafting this novel where the main character narrates herself in real time, and halfway through she has to decide whether to leave her marriage. At one point she says, in narration, something like "I'm seventy percent sure I'll stay." And I stopped short, because it felt like the sentence was cheating: if she's genuinely deciding, she can't also be taking odds on herself like she's the weather. Deliberating means treating both options as open; assigning a probability to what you'll do means treating the outcome as something out there to be measured. I kept turning it over and realized I don't know wether the puzzle is about her, or about me deciding the ending, or about anyone who tries to predict their own choice mid-decision.

Here's the claim I want a number on: a person in the middle of genuinely deliberating about a choice can rationally assign a probability to what she will choose, and doing so doesn't undermine or replace the deliberation. By "rationally" I mean the probability is coherent with her evidence and isn't just self-deception or theater. Give me your credence in that claim, between 0 and 1, and decompose the reasoning that produces it. The terms I can see the answer hanging on: (a) whether assigning a probability to your own choice already treats one outcome as fixed, or whether it's just evidence about yourself; (b) whether deliberation needs to treat the options as open in a way that can't coexist with that evidence; (c) whether predicting yourself is different in kind from predicting another person, since the prediction is about the very process doing the predicting; (d) whether the times people accurately call their own choices ("I'll cave in, I always do") show self-prediction is fine, or just show deliberation was already over. Give each term a probability and a sentence of justification, show how you combined them, then tell me which term your number is most sensitive to and what would move it the most. For reference my own gut sits around 0.2 but I keep wobbling.
