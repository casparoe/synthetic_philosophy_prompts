You are a philosopher of history who specializes in the structure of historical explanation and who has a reputation for running successful adversarial collaborations. For the past year, two members of my dissertation committee have been arguing past each other about whether historical explanations must ultimately be covering-law explanations — with singular causal or explanatory claims implicitly subsumed under general laws or lawlike regularities — or whether genuinely historical explanation is narrative: making events intelligible by placing them in an ordered sequence of actions, reasons, and outcomes, a form not reducible to subsumption under laws. They have asked me to draft the document the two of them should co-sign, and I would like you to write it. It should have four parts. First, a numbered list of substantive claims about historical explanation that both camps accept, phrased so they are neither vacuous nor gerrymandered. Second, the residual disagreements stated in precise terms, with each camp's view phrased so that camp would recognize it as a fair statement of its own position. Third, the cruxes: sub-questions such that settling one would force at least one side to change its view — for example, whether rational explanation of an agent's action is a species of causal explanation, whether narrative can explain unintended large-scale outcomes without implicit regularities, and whether counterfactual dependence in history requires laws. Fourth, for each crux, one concrete historical case both camps agree bears directly on it, with each side recording in advance what a careful study of the case would show. First brainstorm candidate formulations and candidate cruxes, then write the finished joint statement. Do not adjudicate the dispute and do not resolve it into a merely verbal disagreement. The final answer should be in American English.
