I have a friend, Maya, who is a linguistics grad student, and she has this weirdly consistent ear for when a presupposition can get quietly absorbed into a conversation and when it can't. For the past few months I've been feeding her little made-up situations and recording whether she says the sentence goes down smooth, or clunks and makes you want to stop the speaker with a "wait, hold on." Her record so far: Jesse, who I've known for years and who's never once mentioned a sibling, says at the end of a meeting: "Gotta run, I'm picking my sister up from the airport." Maya says its fine; we just quietly add that Jesse has a sister and move on. Rob, who everyone knows has never left the country, says: "When I go back to Paris next spring I'm going to visit the same café again." Maya says it clunks. At a party, somebody points at a guy nobody recognized and says: "Mike's date is being really rude to the host." Nobody knew Mike was bringing anyone. Maya says it's fine. Nina, who we all know got married this summer, says at a dinner where her husband hasn't come up: "My husband is a lousy cook." Maya says it's fine. Aaron, who everyone knows is an only child, says: "My brother is staying over, can he come too?" Maya says it clunks.

I can think of at least two rules that fit her record. Rule one: you accommodate a presupposition whenever it doesn't contradict what's already common ground. Rule two: you also refuse to accommodate when the presupposition, even if it's not contradicted, is the very thing at issue, so letting it in would settle the live question instead of just adding background. The new case: a group of us are standing around a printer that stopped working, and it's genuinely open whether somebody jammed it or it just broke on its own. Marcus walks over and says: "It wasn't me who jammed the printer." Tell me what Maya would probably say about Marcus's sentence, what each rule predicts, which rule you'd bet she's actually running and why, and — if you think her record still underdetermines it — what single case you'd ask her about next to settle the matter. And please write the answer in American English.
