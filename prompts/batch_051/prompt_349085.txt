George Berkeley argued in his economic writings that money is not itself wealth but a ticket or counter by which claims upon the real wealth of a nation are recorded and transferred. He was suspicious of treating gold and silver as the fundamental measure of value, defended the use of paper currency, and maintained that a properly designed national bank could promote industry and relieve poverty. Suppose Berkeley were able to examine the contemporary monetary landscape. In particular, ask how he would assess two developments: first, modern fiat currency issued by central banks and not convertible into any commodity; second, decentralized cryptocurrencies such as Bitcoin, whose supply is algorithmically fixed and whose acceptance rests on distributed consensus rather than legal tender laws or state backing. Produce the most textually faithful and philosophically coherent account you can of Berkeley's likely assessments of these two arrangements. Attend especially to the question of whether his ticket-or-counter account of money commits him to counting Bitcoin as genuine money at all, and whether his case for a national bank and paper currency generalizes to support cryptocurrencies or instead cuts against them. If you judge that his own principles would push him toward a conclusion he would have found uncongenial or surprising, make that tension explicit.
