I live in a shared house with four other people, and we've got an unspoken rule about the bathroom. If it's free, the door is left ajar; if someone's inside, the door's shut. Nobody ever agreed it, it just became how things are done. A month ago a new flatmate moved in and started closing the bathroom door behind her whenever she left. Everyone found this oddly annoying, and a couple of people said she was being inconsiderate. That struck me as strange, because all she was doing was failing to leave a door open.

Two friends of mine later got into a long argument about it and made me the umpire, and I honestly couldn't settle it. It went roughly like this.

Ravi: It's a textbook convention, in the sense David Lewis talks about. We leave the door ajar only because we expect everyone else to, because we all want to coordinate on a way to check whether the loo's free. Another rule would of been just as good — shut the door when it's free and knock, or use some little sign. So the new flatmate hasn't done anything really wrong; she's just broken an expectation.

Theo: That's exactly the problem. People didn't feel she'd merely broken an expectation, they felt she'd been rude. Lewis strips out all that ought stuff, so calling it a convention can't be the whole story. What we've got is a norm. It started as a habit, solved a small problem, got passed to new flatmates as "that's what we do", and somewhere along the way picked up a real ought.

Ravi: That ought is a later add-on. If the house had originally settled on the opposite scheme — closed means free, open means occupied — everybody would be just as sniffy about someone leaving the door open. The fact the felt wrongness flips when the historical fluke flips shows it's conventional, not a real rule.

Theo: Two things. First, not any old rule could have won and lasted. An ajar door is cheap and private; knocking has costs and a sign is silly. So it isn't arbitrary in the way a convention is supposed to be — it got selected for a reason, and selection with inheritance is what I mean by an evolved norm. Second, people follow it even when there's no coordination to do. Sam left the door ajar after cleaning the bathroom at two in the morning when everyone else was away. If it were only a coordination convention, that would be pointless. It looked more like following an internalised rule.

Ravi: Internalising a rule doesn't get you past convention either. We follow conventions without recalculating the payoff each time — that's just habit. And "selected for a reason" doesn't stop it being arbitrary in the relevant sense. There's still a whole family of other patterns that would be just as stable once everyone expected them. Your evolution story explains how we got the convention, but what we have is still a convention.

Theo: Maybe, but your analysis starts from "we want to coordinate". Ours started from half-conscious trial and error. A coordination convention exists to solve a coordination problem; if the problem disappeared, the rule would fade. Ours hasn't faded even though the problem is mostly gone — people leave the door ajar when nobody needs to know. That's the mark of a norm that has detached from its original function and lives a life of its own.

They started repeating themselves after that, so I left them to it.
