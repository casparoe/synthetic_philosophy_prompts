I am the clinical director of a nine-site community mental health network and I chair the committee that decides which diagnostic labels we adopt, revise, or retire in our intake interviews, treatment plans, supervision, and outcome reporting. The committee has seven members, all clinicians or administrators and none with philosophy training; it meets for two hours each quarter to act on proposed new labels and once a year to audit the labels already in use. The decisions govern how several thousand patients are described, medicated, reimbursed, and, we have learned, how they come to describe themselves. A compliance officer signs off on each decision and expects the reasoning to be written down in an auditable form.

We have been burned twice in ways that now look like the same phenomenon. After we adopted one label, patients built online communities around it and within two years the presenting picture had shifted enough that our criteria were selecting a different clinical group from the one we approved. With another label, recorded prevalence rose roughly tenfold with no evidence that the underlying condition had become more common, and I suspect the classification itself was helping to produce the cases.

I know enough of the literature to put a name to this: Ian Hacking's looping effect. Classifications of people are interactive kinds rather than indifferent kinds; the people classified become aware of the classification, respond to it, and thereby change the kind itself, which forces the classification to be revised, which sets off the loop again. I do not need a summary of that idea. I need it turned into a procedure my committee can actually run.

Write me a reusable decision procedure, grounded in the looping effect, sufficient for two tasks: first, in the quarterly meeting, deciding whether to approve, reject, or conditionally approve a proposed diagnostic label; second, in the annual audit, deciding whether an existing label should be left alone, flagged as unstable, reworded, or retired. The procedure must carry the theory in its steps and in observable tests; the committee cannot be asked to distinguish interactivity from indifference in the abstract, but it can be asked to look for specific, checkable signs.

Include in the procedure:

- a pre-adoption screen that forces the committee, before any vote, to predict how patients, families, clinicians, schools, payers, and online communities will react to the label, and to judge whether those reactions will stabilize or destabilize the category;
- a post-adoption monitoring schedule built around observable indicators that the classification is changing the people classified, not merely tracking an underlying condition, with each indicator phrased as a test the committee can apply to its own clinic data;
- an annual audit checklist for existing labels that separates better recognition, where more people correctly receive a label that already fit them, from looping, where the label is altering the phenomena, the prototype, or the population;
- explicit guidance for the hard cases a checklist will misfire on, such as a label that is diagnostically unstable yet therapeutically useful, or a patient community whose shared identity around a label is both clinically complicating and genuinely supportive;
- after each prescribed decision, a note marking the residual point that cannot be reduced to a rule and must be left to clinical judgment.

Then work the procedure through three cases: a proposed new label of the prolonged-grief type, where the live question is whether ordinary distress is being pathologized; an existing label whose presentation has visibly changed since the label became widely available; and a third case chosen so that the procedure returns a recommendation the committee will not want to hear. End by saying what the procedure cannot do: the questions it cannot settle, the loops it cannot anticipate in advance, and the point at which we should stop pretending the looping effect has been tamed.
