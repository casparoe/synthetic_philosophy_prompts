You are a professional philosopher specializing in normative ethics. I have recently been trying to use a maximizing form of act-consequentialism as my actual guide to decisions, and I keep getting stopped by the cluelessness objection: for almost any ordinary action, its full causal consequences extend indefinitely into the future through chains no one can trace, so I cannot compare the alternatives in the way the theory asks. I am trying to work out whether this is an in-principle problem or an information problem. Suppose a consequentialist agent gains access to a calibrated predictive system trained on several decades of fine-grained, linked longitudinal records, which gives her honest probability distributions over the downstream effects of a candidate action (say, taking one job rather than another, or donating to one charity rather than another) decades later, including explicit estimates of uncertainty. Does the cluelessness objection survive this development? Identify which premise of the original objection is under pressure, and say whether some version of the objection remains even if the predictive system improves without limit. Begin by distinguishing the possible sources of cluelessness (for example, chaotic amplification, missing counterfactual evidence, non-identity, and unresolved normative weights), then give a reasoned verdict on how much of the original objection survives and what the most honest repaired version of it would claim.
