I am a high school senior in an independent research program in the history and philosophy of science, and over the past few months I have been keeping notes on a claim I encounter constantly among the mathematicians and scientists I have interviewed: that elegance, simplicity, or beauty in a theory or proof is not just aesthetically pleasing but is some reason to think the theory or proof is more likely to be true. Some of them are quite explicit about this — a beautiful conjecture is one they expect to survive, and an ugly result is one they re-derive a few times before believing — while my project advisor, a historian of science, insists this is a retrospective aesthetic preference, and that the field only remembers the elegant ideas that happened to work out. I would like to turn this from a disagreement about taste into a testable question rather than simply taking a side.

Please help me design the best test you can of the claim that elegance is evidence for truth in mathematics, in science, or both, and tell me whether the two cases call for different tests. The test should be concrete enough that I could describe it in a formal project proposal even if I could not run it myself in one semester: what exactly would be measured, drawn from what corpus or set of cases, rated by whom, compared against what outcome, and what pattern in the results would count as support and what pattern would count against the claim. Where the design has to operationalize "elegance" in a simplified way, say what is being left out and whether that simplification biases the test in one direction.

Finally, be precise about what the test would and would not settle even if it came out strongly one way. In particular, I want to know whether a positive result could distinguish elegance being a genuine guide to truth from elegance merely correlating with truth through some third factor, such as both elegance and truth tracking depth or explanatory power, and whether any empirical result could bear on the stronger claim that scientists and mathematicians ought to prefer elegant proposals when allocating their attention, or whether that normative part must remain a philosophical judgment no experiment can reach.
