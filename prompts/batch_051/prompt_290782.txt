You are a moral philosopher who specialises in P.F. Strawson's account of the reactive attitudes and in the difference between holding someone responsible and treating them from what he called the objective stance. You are also good at turning distinctions of that kind into procedures a non-philosopher can run under time pressure. In what follows I am your client; do not lecture me on the history of the problem and do not cite texts, because I need the ideas converted into a working instrument.

I chair a weekly conduct review panel at a mid-sized engineering consultancy, with two other managers and an HR officer. We are about forty staff, and we see three or four incidents a month that could call for blame: a false statement to a client, one colleague humiliating another, a missed safety deadline, a leaked confidential document. For each incident we have roughly half an hour to decide what kind of response is appropriate before we settle on a formal warning, a performance improvement plan, an occupational health referral, or no action. Formal sanctions need the HR director's sign-off. My panel keeps slipping between two registers: sometimes we blame people for what we would not wish to be blamed for ourselves, and sometimes we avoid blame altogether by recasting the person as a performance problem or a welfare case. We want a decision procedure that tells us, on Strawsonian grounds, which register to be in and when neither register has been earned.

Write the procedure as follows. It should contain:

- a series of questions in order, each framed so a non-philosopher can answer it;
- for each question, what counts as a sufficient answer and which way that answer points — toward the participant stance (blame, demand, accountability) or toward the objective stance (management, support, treatment, removal) or onward to another question;
- stopping rules: where the procedure says do not blame, where it says blame is in order, and where it says this is a judgement for a named human being rather than something the checklist can settle;
- a short 'flowchart in words' so the panel can see the route at a glance.

In building it, preserve at least these Strawsonian distinctions without jargon: resentment, gratitude, indignation, and blame are ways of holding one another to demands we make as fellow members of a moral community, and they are not merely descriptions of a person's character or predictions of future conduct; the objective stance — treating the person as something to be managed, trained, cured, or avoided — is the proper fallback when the person cannot, in the relevant respect, participate in ordinary interpersonal exchange with us; ordinary adult life cannot be lived wholly from the objective stance, and the panel should be suspicious whenever it wants to adopt that stance purely because blame would be inconvenient; the truth of determinism, or the fact that an act was caused, is not a question the procedure should ask; and there is a difference between an excuse inside the participant stance (the act was an accident, a mistake, made under compulsion, or based on a false belief) and an exemption from it (the person is, for now or permanently, not a fitting target of the ordinary reactive attitudes because of immaturity, illness, incapacity, or something of that kind). The procedure should therefore distinguish two quite different findings: 'you are not to blame for this act' and 'you are not presently someone I can blame at all', and it should say what each commits the panel to doing.

Then apply the procedure to these three cases:

1. A junior engineer, instructed by her project lead and working against a tight deadline, signed off a drainage design without the required check. The design contained an error that a two-hour check would have caught; it was found before construction and no harm resulted. She is ordinarily careful and is now distressed.
2. An experienced engineer has now twice described a junior colleague's work as 'embarrassing' in front of the full project team. The first occasion drew only an informal word. His results are excellent, and he maintains that bluntness is how the profession works and that no one is hurt by it.
3. A project manager missed three consecutive safety-review deadlines over two months. Asked why, he said he had been unable to concentrate and was sleeping badly; occupational health reports signs of a depressive episode. No accident occurred, but site risk rose while the reviews were outstanding.

I must tell you in advance what my panel wants in case 2: quietly moving the engineer onto solo work where he cannot damage morale, because he is too valuable to lose and formal blame would make him harder to manage. I expect the procedure to tell us whether that instinct is defensible, and I expect to be told it is not. Do not soften that finding.

Work in stages. First produce a short planning section in which you set out the distinctions you will operationalise, the translation problems you expect, and the simplifications you are making. Then write the procedure itself, addressed to the panel. Then show it working on the three cases, including a case in which the panel dislikes the answer. End with an honest account of what the procedure cannot do and the conditions under which it will misfire.
