I'm a philosophy professor giving a law-school workshop paper in a few weeks, and I want a Q&A prep sheet aimed at this specific room, not a general audience. The paper defends this: when a written constitution genuinely runs out — the situation is neither covered by a rule nor a precedent nor a settled convention, so no existing legal rule tells you who may act — the right response is not "there's no law, so whoever can act may act," and not "let a court settle it as if it were ordinary interpretation." Instead, the official or body whose pre-gap powers are closest in subject matter should act provisionally, under a duty to pass the question to the ordinary lawmaker for ratification at the earliest moment it can sit; their acts count as lawful only in a derivative, trustee-like sense, and only if time-limited, reversible, and actually submitted for ratification. Judicial settlement is the fallback, not the default.

My compressed argument: (1) a gap in legal rules isn't the same as a gap in legal institutions — the offices and their defined powers still exist; (2) the point of rule-of-law constraints is to keep power non-arbitrary and government continuous, and acting through the nearest existing office under constraints serves that better than a free-for-all or a court inventing a "deep structure"; (3) the nearest-office test is more determinate and more grounded in actual institutional materials than judicial gap-filling. So it's the least bad default even though it is not clean.

The room: mostly public lawyers — constitutional and administrative law people in a common-law country with a written constitution — plus two political philosophers. The lawyers mostly think a written constitution plus judge-made doctrine is close enough to gapless, and where there is a genuine gap they assume the court's job is to fill it by adjudication. One philosopher is a positivist who will press that an official acting beyond the rules simply cannot be acting lawfully, full stop, derivative trustee-status or not. The other works on democratic legitimacy and will press that "provisional" action by whoever happens to hold the nearest office is still unilateral rule by someone nobody elected for this.

Give me the objections in the rough order each camp will actually raise them, phrased the way those people would phrase them — likelihood and damage weighted. For each: what I should concede on the spot, what I should hold, a two-minute reply, and where that reply is softest. Also tell me the question I'm hoping nobody asks and why it's the dangerous one. Throughout, separate whats settled among people who work on this from whats genuinely contested; dont present a contested view as if it were the consensus, and if one of my own moves is a minority view, flag it as mine.
