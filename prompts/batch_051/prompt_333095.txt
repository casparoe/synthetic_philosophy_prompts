I keep going back and forth on whether there's any real duty to double-check your beliefs before acting on them, and my gut verdicts on cases won't line up into a rule I can state. Walk me through what principle, if any, is behind these.

A doctor keeps prescribing an antibiotic the way she learned in residency, even though she vaguely remembers the guidelines changed a few years ago, and doesn't look it up before writing the script. I beleive that's clearly wrong.

A person repeats to a mutual friend that a coworker was fired for stealing, having heard it from one other coworker and never checked. Wrong too—and worse the more they dress it up as certain.

A juror in a serious assault case votes guilty mostly to get the deliberations over with and doesn't re-read evidence she's hazy on. That's one of the worst ones to me.

Someone recommends a restaurant to friends without checking recent reviews, and the place is bad. I don't think they did anything morally wrong; it's just unlucky.

Someone gives a few hundred dollars to a disaster-relief charity after a strong ad, without looking into what the charity actually does with the money. I want to say that's fine, or at most a little credulous, not a failure of duty. Praisworthy to check, not required.

A parent decides not to vaccinate their kid after watching some scary videos they never fact-checked, even after their pediatrician laid out the evidence. Blameworthy, full stop.

An entrepreneur has seen early market research suggesting their product will probably fail and keeps avoiding the newer reports because they're demoralizing, while telling themselves and their small team the plan is solid. I have the uncomfortable sense this is wrong too, but I can't say why it's worse than the charity case without just pointing at self-interest.

And one I can't settle: someone breaks off an engagement after seeing one flirty text from their partner to an ex, without asking about it. I think they owed their partner a conversation, but calling it a moral failure like the juror's feels off.

The rule I keep trying is something like: you're required to check before acting when the expected harm of being wrong, times your influence over the outcome, is large compared to the cost of checking—with extra weight if you've taken on a role that demands accuracy. But depending how I dial the numbers, it either makes the restaurant rec a moral issue or lets the parent and doctor off too easy.

Is there a principle, or a small set of them, that captures most of these verdicts even if it means I have to abandon a couple? State it precisely, run it through the hard pairs, and tell me which verdicts it can't accommodate. Then apply it to cases I can't settle: a manager firing an employee after one anonymous complaint; a teacher reporting suspected abuse based on a child's odd offhand comment without asking the child; and someone who avoids reading about climate change because it would force them to change how they live.
