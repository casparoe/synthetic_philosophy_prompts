Exam question:

Consider classical electrodynamics coupled to a charged scalar field. The action is invariant under local U(1) gauge transformations, where the gauge field and charged matter field transform as A_μ(x) → A_μ(x) + ∂_μ α(x) and ψ(x) → e^{iqα(x)} ψ(x), with α(x) an arbitrary smooth function of spacetime. Taking α to be a constant gives a global U(1) phase rotation of the charged matter field. Noether's first theorem associates a conserved current with each continuous global symmetry of the action. Noether's second theorem applies when the symmetry parameter is an arbitrary function of spacetime and gives identities relating the equations of motion. Many philosophers of physics regard the local gauge transformations as descriptive redundancies, since they relate representations of the same physical situation, while regarding the global U(1) phase rotation as the symmetry whose Noether current is the conserved electric current. Explain the relationship among the global U(1) symmetry, the local gauge symmetry, and the law of charge conservation. Then assess whether charge conservation is explained by a symmetry that is, according to this view, also a descriptive redundancy.

Student's answer:

Noether's first theorem says that every continuous symmetry of an action yields a conserved current. Electrodynamics is invariant under local U(1) gauge transformations, and this is a continuous symmetry, so by the first theorem it directly yields the conserved electric current. Charge conservation is therefore explained by gauge symmetry. One might object that gauge symmetries are just redundancies connecting equivalent descriptions, but that cannot be the whole story: a merely formal redundancy could not explain a physically robust conservation law. The fact that the gauge symmetry does this explanatory work shows that the redundancy interpretation is too deflationary.
