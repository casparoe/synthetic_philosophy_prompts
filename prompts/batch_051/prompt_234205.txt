I'm in a historical methodology seminar this semester, and we've spent two weeks going back and forth about whether non-human things can be historical actors. My professor gave us a working definition with four conditions. Something counts as a historical actor, on this definition, if (1) it's a discrete, bounded thing or process rather than a diffuse background condition; (2) it has its own internal dynamic or tempo that isn't just a reflection of human decisions; (3) you can trace specific downstream effects to it that would not have happened without it; and (4) its role doesn't evaporate into "something people reacted to"—it has to keep doing causal work in the story even after you've described all the human responses. So intentions aren't required, but the thing also can't just be whatever humans happened to respond to.

The case I keep getting stuck on is the 1815 eruption of Mount Tambora. The volcano blew up in April, injected a huge amount of sulfate aerosols into the stratosphere, and cooled the planet enough that 1816 became the so-called year without a summer—June frosts in New England, harvest failures across western Europe, famine in parts of Switzerland, and the political and social fallout that followed. On its face it nails the definition: a discrete event, a geophysical logic totally indifferent to what anyone wanted, and a causal chain you can follow crop by crop. But the eruption only became "historical" through the vulnerability of the societies it hit; without particular grain markets, poor-relief systems, and patterns of land tenure, Tambora might have been just a cold summer and a bad harvest. So does Tambora qualify as a historical actor under the four criteria? Go through them one at a time, be honest about which ones are doing the real work, and tell me whether this case shows the definition is too permissive, too restrictive, or just vague at the edges.
