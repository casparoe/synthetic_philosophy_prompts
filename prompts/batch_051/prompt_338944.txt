I am drafting a methods note for a small group of foreign-policy analysts, and before I circulate it I want my own views stress-tested for consistency. I hold all of the following, and I cannot yet see where the trouble is.

(1) Most invocations of Munich or Vietnam in policy debate are doing rhetorical work: they make a policy conclusion feel settled without adding real evidence.

(2) Nevertheless, I think the Munich lesson is a sound default for cases that share the German case's structural features, such as a revisionist power that is signaling further demands and has the capability to act on them.

(3) I regard the Thucydides trap as overused as a label, but I accept its underlying claim as a genuine regularity: rapid shifts in relative power between an established power and a rising challenger make conflict likelier, other things equal.

(4) I also think there are no meaningful base rates for the outcomes that foreign-policy analogies try to predict, such as wars or deterrence failures; each case is nearly unique, which is what makes simple lessons of history unreliable.

(5) At the same time, I believe the right corrective is to replace a single dominant analogy with explicit comparison across a reference class of past crises scored on a fixed set of dimensions.

(6) I treat the emotional grip of a historical parallel as inversely correlated with its diagnostic value: the more a parallel seizes me, the more I distrust it.

Please begin your response with your bottom line in the first sentence, stating your overall verdict on whether this set hangs together and which single revision would be least costly. Then work through the tensions in descending order of severity; for each pair, say whether the conflict is genuine or only apparent, and specify what would have to give. Do not restate my list or open with preamble.
