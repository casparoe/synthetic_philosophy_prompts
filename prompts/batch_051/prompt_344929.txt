I'm putting together a graduate seminar on generic statements, and I want to begin from a constraint list rather than from any particular semantics. Please write the desiderata that an adequate account of English bare-plural generics must meet, where the phenomenon sets the bar rather than any school. The data your list has to discipline include at least the following: the truth of "ducks lay eggs" although only a minority of ducks—mature females in season—ever lay; the truth of "mosquitoes carry malaria" although only a small fraction of individual mosquitoes ever carry the parasite; the fact that two different properties can be equally rare in a kind and yet differ sharply in whether the corresponding generic is acceptable, so that a purely statistical truth condition is at once too weak and too strong; kind-level cases such as "dodos are extinct" or "the dodo is extinct," which cannot be predications over members; the contrast many speakers hear between bare plurals and indefinite singulars, where the drop in acceptability from "mosquitoes carry malaria" to "a mosquito carries malaria" forces a choice about what indefinite singulars quantify over; and the inferential profile of generics—what a competent hearer may and may not conclude about an arbitrary member from "ducks lay eggs" or from "mosquitoes carry malaria."

For each requirement, state the case or consideration behind it, say explicitly what it rules out (for example, a simple majority or high-prevalence truth condition), and mark it as non-negotiable or as a camp-specific extraction from the data that a serious account might reject. Then tell me which single requirement does the most to eliminate the leading families of theories, and whether any subset of the requirements is jointly satisfiable only by giving up something an account would otherwise want—that is the part I most want to see. A brief scorecard of existing approaches is fine, but keep the weight on the criteria and their motivation.
