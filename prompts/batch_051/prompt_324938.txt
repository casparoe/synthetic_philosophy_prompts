I am a PhD student in political philosophy, and I serve as the graduate-student representative on my university's Student Opportunity Allocation Committee. Each month the committee distributes a limited pool of new funds — roughly $300,000 per year in total, awarded as a few dozen recurring grants — among competing academic and student-support proposals. The committee's charter borrows the language of Rawls's difference principle: departures from equal treatment or equal distribution across the university's members are justifiable only if they are to the advantage of the least advantaged members of the university community. In practice, however, we deliberate by intuition, precedent, and advocacy, and we regularly split on cases the charter should decide.

Please turn the stated principle into a decision procedure the full committee can actually run. The procedure will be used by committee members who have no formal training in political philosophy, in meetings of under ninety minutes with five to eight proposals on the docket. It must specify: the questions to ask in order; what evidence or argument counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure returns a 'no' and stops; and where it hands the decision to the committee because the remaining question is a value judgment rather than something a procedure can settle. The procedure must also state how it identifies the least advantaged group in this setting, how it compares different kinds of expected benefit to that group, and how it uses the veil of ignorance as a discipline on self-interested advocacy without pretending committee members can forget their actual positions. Be explicit about the point at which the procedure is no longer applying the difference principle faithfully because Rawls framed it for the basic structure of a whole society, and say which proposals the committee should decline to adjudicate on that basis.

Then run the procedure on three docket items we will actually face in some form: (a) a proposal to create a small merit-based scholarship for top-performing doctoral students; (b) a proposal to expand emergency bursaries for students at risk of leaving the university over unpaid rent or food costs; and (c) a proposal to fund a new laboratory for a department that is already among the best-resourced on campus while several departments cannot afford basic teaching materials. Work each case through far enough to show what the procedure recommends and why. Make sure at least one worked case produces a recommendation that cuts against my own initial preferences, and say explicitly which preference it cuts against and why the procedure nevertheless returns that result. The committee needs a document it can defend both to the provost, who signs off on our allocations, and to the students and faculty whose proposals we reject.
