I work as a contracts administrator and negotiator. I have no academic background, but I have become fluent in the practical vocabulary of contract formation, capacity, consideration, assignment of liability, indemnity, and related notions. In my spare time I have been thinking on my own about moral responsibility, and the argument that keeps troubling me is Galen Strawson's Basic Argument. As I understand it, the argument runs like this. What a person does flows from the kind of person one is, meaning one's beliefs, desires, dispositions, and abilities. To be ultimately morally responsible for an action, in the sense that would make blame and punishment truly deserved rather than merely useful, one would have to be responsible for the kind of person one is. But to be responsible for the kind of person one is, one would have had to shape it, and any shaping would itself flow from a character one already had and did not shape. That sets up an endless regress with no starting point, and the conclusion is that no one is ever ultimately morally responsible for anything.

I would like you to restate this argument, step by step, in the vocabulary I actually use every day: contract formation, capacity to contract, parties, consideration, liability, assignment, indemnity, and the rest of the toolkit. I am not asking for a loose analogy decorated with legal terms; I want a systematic translation, and I want it kept honest. For each step of the argument, tell me which parts carry over cleanly, which carry over only under distortion, and which have no counterpart in the contract vocabulary at all. Then give me the residue report: what does not survive the translation, and should I read that as a defect in my vocabulary or as a sign that the untranslatable parts of the argument were less clear or less substantial than they looked? In particular, I want to know whether the notion of desert at the center of the argument has any real equivalent in the way liability and obligation actually function in contracts, and whether the regress looks more or less compelling once it is restated as a problem about who could ever have formed the original agreement.
