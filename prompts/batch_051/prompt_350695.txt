I keep running into a reading experience I don't have a name for, and I'd like to know whether the literature has one. When a work of fiction stipulates something descriptively impossible — say, that in this world light travels at walking speed, or that a character is a literal minotaur — I accept it effortlessly as true within the story and get on with the plot. But when a fiction seems to stipulate something evaluative — for example, that a character's small, gratuitous cruelty is not merely something the character takes to be fine, but something that is true in the story's world: that the cruelty really was right, or noble, or beautiful — I find I cannot do it in the same way. The strange thing is that the obstacle is not that I dislike the content. I can read an unreliable narrator who regards his cruelty as noble and feel no resistance at all, because I experience the evaluation as his: it is a fact inside the fiction that he thinks this. The resistance appears only when the story itself, as I understand it, asks me to treat the evaluation as part of how things are in the fiction, rather than as the posture of some mind within it.

Does this asymmetry have an established name, and is it the same phenomenon as simply refusing to go along with a story's ideology? If the closest concept in the literature fits only imperfectly, I'd like to know exactly where the edges are: does the same resistance arise for aesthetic evaluations (for instance, a fiction in which a clumsy, derivative painting is stipulated to be a masterpiece), for epistemic ones (a fiction in which a plainly invalid inference is stipulated to be sound), and does the pull of genre change it — say, a fable or a tall tale where inverted moral evaluations are part of the game? If there is no established name, what would you call it, and how would you define it so that it excludes ordinary distaste and unreliable-narrator cases?
