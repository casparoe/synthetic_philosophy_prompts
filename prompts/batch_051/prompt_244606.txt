I am a philosophy professor assembling materials for a graduate seminar on the political philosophy of AI alignment, and I want a model of constructive disagreement for my students. The assignment concerns two families of answers to the question of whose values an aligned system should serve. The first, which I will call preference aggregationism, holds that the alignment target should be a social welfare function defined over the considered preferences of all morally significant parties—living humans, and plausibly future people and some nonhuman animals—where the familiar difficulties of preference elicitation, malformed or adaptive preferences, and Arrow-style impossibility are treated as problems of procedure and formal design to be solved within the aggregationist program. The second, which I will call liberal neutrality about value alignment, denies that an AI should be in the business of maximizing aggregate preference satisfaction at all. On this view the system's role is to sustain a rights-respecting, non-paternalistic framework within which individuals and communities actually deliberate about and pursue their own conceptions of the good, and the only values it may enforce are constraints acceptable to all reasonable persons. The two camps should not be treated as caricatures: the aggregationist may fully agree that raw majoritarian polling is unacceptable, and the neutralist may agree that a mere refusal to aggregate still leaves an AI that has to do something when values conflict.

Please draft the joint statement such an adversarial collaboration would produce. Include (i) a numbered list of agreed claims, substantive rather than vacuous, covering at least what an aligned AI must not do, which agents or interests must somehow be represented, and which familiar failures of aggregation both sides reject; (ii) a disagreements section in which each residual disagreement is stated in wording each side would accept as a fair formulation of its own view, including the aggregationist's reply to the neutralist and the neutralist's reply to the aggregationist; (iii) a list of cruxes—sub-questions whose settlement would move at least one side—with, where possible, the cases, arguments, or empirical tests both sides agree would bear on those cruxes; and (iv) each side's recorded prediction before those tests. Pay particular attention to whether any part of the dispute is merely verbal, especially whether terms like neutrality and preference satisfaction can be specified without already presupposing an evaluative stance. At the end, add a short methodological note stating how confident the statement is in its formulations and singling out the one point you are least sure both camps would actually sign.
