Read the cursed videotape in Ringu / The Ring (the Japanese original or its American remake) through the lens of the debate over whether cultural variants genuinely replicate, or whether 'meme' is only a metaphor. The premise common to both versions gives you a piece of media whose propagation is enforced rather than chosen: a viewer dies in seven days unless a copy of the tape is made and shown to a new viewer. Treat the tape as the film's own model of a meme, and push on what the model has to add before a cultural object can replicate like an organism. Does the tape behave like a Dawkins-style replicator — a unit of information that survives by getting itself copied — or does the film show that the replicator picture only works because it has silently inserted the two things ordinary cultural items lack: a ghost with intentions, and a lethal penalty for hosts that fail to copy? Track the details that matter: the near-perfect fidelity of the copies, the absence of variation and selection among rival versions, the way the tape has authored content and a biography rather than a lineage of mutations. Where does the meme lens genuinely illuminate the film's horror, and where does the film push back — for instance, by making transmission the re-enactment of a singular, authored event rather than the copying of a mutable unit? I want a reading that takes the film's actual mechanics and the philosophy of cultural evolution equally seriously, and that ends by saying what the film shows about the limits of the meme metaphor that a bare argument would miss.
