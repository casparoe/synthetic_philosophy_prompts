Say there's a draft international agreement, call it the Convention on Climate Intervention — it's made up, so don't go looking it up — and it contains this clause: "No Party shall maintain a climate intervention measure except with the continuing consent of the Conference of the Parties."

Now the case. A coalition of small island states has been running a stratospheric aerosol injection program for a decade, with the Conference's consent the whole time. The cooling worked, maybe too well: several big emitters quietly slowed their own decarbonization on the assumption that the aerosols would keep bailing them out — exactly the moral hazard people warned about. Last year one of those emitters, now under a new government, formally withdraws its consent and demands the program be wound down. The plain text seems to require that: once consent is gone, "maintaining" the program is a breach. But the program can't be stopped quickly without termination shock, a rapid snapback of warming that would hit the objecting state as hard as anyone. A safe wind-down might take a decade, which is a decade of maintaining without consent.

So work through what the clause actually requires here. Give me the plain-words reading, the purpose reading, what the drafters would most likely have said if someone had put this exact scenario to them during negotiations, and what a reasonable Party would have understood. Does "maintain" cover the continuation of a ongoing program, or only the initial deployment? And is the moral hazard backstory relevant — does the objecting state get to invoke its own loss of consent to force a crash exit when it's partly the moral hazard problem made flesh? One more thing: if the text and the purpose point in opposite directions here, which should govern, and what does that tell us about who really decides the endgame?
