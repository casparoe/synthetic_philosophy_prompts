I help govern a mid-sized online community—roughly twenty thousand active members—built around long-form political and philosophical discussion, with an elected moderation team, a written code of conduct, and a reputation score that affects visibility and voting weight. I have also been rereading the Republic, and I cannot stop thinking about Glaucon's challenge in Book II: that people are just only because they cannot get away with injustice, and that someone freed from detection would behave no differently from an unjust person. This has become unexpectedly practical for me, because a fellow moderator has proposed an institutional version of the thought experiment, and it now has real support among the membership.

The proposal is an annual "Gyges account." By a transparent lottery, one member is granted, for one calendar month, a special status. During that month their activity is uncoupled from their standing identity: their posts and votes appear under a single anonymized handle, and the moderation team is forbidden to sanction anything done from that handle while the status is active, unless the platform would be legally required to intervene. At the end of the month, the anonymized logs are preserved but sealed, and may not be used as evidence in any future moderation action, reputation adjustment, or recall. Everyone knows that exactly one member is the Gyges account and can read its posts as they appear; no one knows whose account it is. The status carries over the holder's ordinary privileges—so if a long-standing, high-reputation member wins, the Gyges account posts with the visibility and voting weight of a senior member.

I am not asking whether we should adopt this; I want to understand how it would actually run. Walk through the effects as precisely as you can. What does it do to the holder's behavior in the first days, in the final week, and in the week after the status lapses? What does it do to everyone else—how do people read the Gyges account's posts, treat newcomers or suspected holders, and adjust their ordinary conversation around the knowledge that one anonymous, unaccountable voice is present? Where does the scheme get gamed first: campaigns to get a particular member selected, coordinated use of the Gyges handle to launder reputation or punish rivals, attempts to bait or unmask the holder? Which failure modes could be patched without making the status meaningless? And if it ran for several years, what would the community's trajectory tell us about whether Glaucon was right that the fear of being seen, rather than justice itself, does the real work?
