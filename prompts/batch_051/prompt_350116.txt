Last night I had a long argument with a friend, an internist, about where the line between health and disease lies. He took the naturalist view that disease is simply disordered biological functioning: a part or process is failing to do what it is biologically supposed to do, and everything else is politics. I spent the evening objecting that his account quietly imports value judgments at every turn — in choosing the reference class for what is normal, in deciding how much deviation from a norm is too much, and in selecting which dysfunctions count as illnesses while others pass unnoticed. After I had exhausted my objections, he asked me what rule my own committee actually uses. I could not answer him.

Here is why that bothers me. I am the medical director of a regional health insurer and I chair a monthly committee that decides whether newly proposed conditions count as diseases under a policy that covers the treatment of disease and excludes what we call normal variation, development, or lifestyle. We see eight to a dozen proposals per month, each with a sponsoring clinician and often a patient group behind it. The rulings must be defensible to subscribers, to employer clients, and occasionally to external reviewers, and I have final sign-off, so an arbitrary decision is ultimately my error. The committee is composed mostly of physicians, plus a lawyer and an actuary, and none of them has any interest in philosophy.

I want you to write a decision procedure we could actually run on each proposal in under an hour. It should translate the naturalism-versus-normativism dispute about health and disease into an ordered series of questions for people with no philosophical training: what to ask first, what counts as a sufficient answer to each question, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it must hand the decision to a human because the remaining question is one of values rather than fact. In doing so, keep whatever distinctions from that dispute survive the practical translation and drop the ones that would only add noise. Mark every step at which judgment cannot be eliminated, and say who on the committee is best placed to exercise it. Be candid about where the procedure will misfire, and about what it cannot decide at all.

Then run the procedure on three of the proposals currently before us: a request to classify age-related decline in testosterone as a disease and cover treatment; a pediatric group's proposal to classify idiopathic short stature in otherwise healthy children as a disease and cover growth hormone; and a patient-advocacy proposal, pressed by one of our large employer clients, to classify mild-to-moderate age-related hearing loss as a disease and cover hearing aids, which we presently treat as an excluded assistive device. For at least one of these, I expect an honest application of your procedure to deliver an answer my committee will not like; when that happens, do not soften it. State what the procedure requires and why.

Please write this as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter or a long email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
