In my reporting on cognitive science I keep encountering the view that introspection is a form of inner perception: the mind has a faculty that looks in on its own states and reports them roughly as vision reports the world, a picture then used to license treating people's sincere first-person reports as authoritative evidence about their minds. I want to audit that analogy rather than repeat it. Please give an explicit correspondence table — what is supposed to play the role of the sense organ, the stimulus, the object perceived, the correctness conditions, and the independent check — and then identify where the mapping breaks down, separating the disanalogies that actually threaten the conclusion from those that are merely superficial. In particular, address how the analogy fares given that introspection has no dedicated transducer, that attending to a mental state often seems to alter it, and that introspective reports mostly cannot be checked by a second observer the way visual reports can. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion — that introspection is a reliable, perception-like source of evidence about the mind — survives the audit, whether the analogy was carrying it all along, and whether there is a better analogy that would do less damage.
