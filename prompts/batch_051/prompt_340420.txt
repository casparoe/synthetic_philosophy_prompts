Something's been bugging me since a chat with a friend who's in a history of science program. It seems like old mathematicians and scientists get graded twice, by two different rubrics. When a claim of theirs lines up with something we now believe, people rush to say they anticipated it — Greek atomists, somebody 'almost inventing' calculus a few centuries early, whichever example you've heard. The past thinker gets judged by our modern standards, and it makes them look prescient. But when the same thinker says things that don't line up with anything we now think, the story flips: suddenly it's anachronistic to judge them by our standards, they were asking a different question inside a different framework. So a hit is scored on our rubric and a miss is excused by theirs.

In my job that would be crazy. If I ship a codebase where one function is a clean recursive descent parser and the rest is a pile of bugs and half-understood borrowings, no one says I anticipated recursive descent. They say I got lucky, or that the pretty function was doing something simpler than it looked. So I want to know: is there a principled line that makes this asymmetry defensible when the author is Aristotle instead of me? Maybe there is — maybe ideas travel across conceptual frameworks differently than code, or maybe the two verdicts are answering different questions. But if not, shouldn't we be symmetrical, either anachronistic everywhere or anachronistic nowhere? Don't give me a reading list, just the argument. And as best you can, seperate what people who actually study this have settled from what's still a genuine fight, without making one side of a live fight sound like the consensus.
