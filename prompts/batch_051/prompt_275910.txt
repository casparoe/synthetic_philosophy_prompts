I keep running into a move in philosophy-of-science discussions that strikes me as too quick, and I'd like someone to slow it down and take it apart. Someone will invoke the slogan "survival of the fittest" and then dismiss natural selection as empty: if "fittest" just means "those who survive," they say, the slogan reduces to "the survivors survive," and a principle that explains nothing. I suspect the sentence is really several different claims wearing one coat, and that the tautology charge only sticks to some of them.

Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims "survival of the fittest" could be used to express — from the vacuous to the substantive — and for each reading say whether it is trivially true, an empirical claim that could turn out false, or simply a category mistake; and whether it actually licenses the tautology objection. I'm especially interested in where the propensity interpretation of fitness fits into this: the view that fitness is a probabilistic disposition to survive and reproduce, not an actual tally of who in fact survived. Does reading the slogan through that lens dissolve the tautology worry, or only relocate it? And which reading, if any, would actually support the dismissive conclusion that natural selection is an empty principle?

I'm a longtime reader of rationalist blogs, so I'm comfortable with technical vocabulary and with views being given unfavorable precision before they're rejected. Please do the same here: separately flag what is settled among philosophers of biology and what is genuinely contested — the claim that fitness is best understood as a propensity is widely held but not unanimous — and resist presenting a contested reading as the consensus.
