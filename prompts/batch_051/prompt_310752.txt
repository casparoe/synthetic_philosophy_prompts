I keep getting drawn to the anti-aggregation view about rescue cases: if I can save either one stranger or five strangers, and no one has any special claim on me, the right thing is to give each person an equal chance — so flip a coin. The argument winning me over is that persons are separate; there is no sixth entity 'the five' for whom dying is five times as bad, so the fact that there are five of them shouldn't make any individual's claim stronger than the one's. Each person has a claim on me, and each claim is exactly as strong as any other. But I also can't shake the conviction that if the coin sends me to save the one while five die, I've done something wrong — 'just save the five' feels like it's tracking something real, not just a failure to absorb the argument. Which side should yield? Give me the strongest version of both, and then tell me what it would cost to debunk the intuition as confused aggregation, what it would cost to amend or give up the anti-aggregation principle, and whether there's a reading on which 'each claim is equal' and 'save the five' can both be true.
