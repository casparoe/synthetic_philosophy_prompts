In Federalist No. 78, Alexander Hamilton defends constitutional review by arguing that a limited constitution can only be preserved through a judiciary that voids acts contrary to its plain limits. The passage our seminar spent the most time on reads as follows:

By a limited Constitution, I understand one which contains certain specified exceptions to the legislative authority; such, for instance, as that it shall pass no bills of attainder, no ex post facto laws, and the like. Limitations of this kind can be preserved in practice no other way than through the medium of courts of justice, whose duty it must be to declare all acts contrary to the manifest tenor of the Constitution void. Without this, all the reservations of particular rights or privileges would amount to nothing.

My question is whether this argument survives the way judicial review actually operates in contemporary constitutional law. Hamilton seems to justify the practice only for cases in which a statute contradicts something fairly explicit in the Constitution—its "manifest tenor." But modern courts regularly invalidate legislation under clauses whose tenor is far from manifest, such as due process, equal protection, or structural principles inferred from the constitutional order as a whole. Does the argument Hamilton gives still support that broader practice, or has the practice outrun its original justification? I would like an account of which premise is doing the essential work in his argument—for example, the assumption that a constitution has a fixed and ascertainable tenor, the premise that courts are merely enforcing the people's prior act of self-binding, or some other premise—and whether that premise can be repaired or replaced. If Hamilton's argument cannot be extended to the non-manifest case, what is the strongest available justification for judicial review in that case, or should we say the practice lacks a defensible foundation there?
