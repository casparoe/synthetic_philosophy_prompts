I'm a retired geophysicist. I spent my career doing data analysis and thinking hard about error bars, and now that I've gotten into philosophy I keep tripping over the same knot of questions about wether and when you're entitled to believe something. I'd like you to help me find the principle behind my reactions — or tell me which reactions I should give up.

Here are the cases and my verdicts, reported honestly rather than as a worked-out view.

1. The lottery. A fair drawing, 1,000 tickets, exactly one winner. For any particular ticket I believe "that ticket will lose." I don't believe "every ticket will lose," because I know one of them must win. I feel no tension in holding both at once.

2. The preface. Suppose I wrote a book, and in the preface I say, truthfully, that I believe each chapter's main claims, but I also believe the book contains at least one mistake somewhere, though I can't point at it. I find that completely reasonable.

3. The blood test. My doctor tells me a positive result from this test is wrong in only 1 case out of 1,000. The result comes back positive. Do I believe I have the disease? I find I don't — I'd want a confirmatory test before I'd say "I believe I have it." I realize this sits awkwardly next to case 1, where 999 out of 1,000 was good enough for belief.

4. The detective. There's strong, independent evidence for two claims: that the butler was in the house at midnight, and that the butler had the only key that fits a brand-new lock. Each claim is about as probable as a losing ticket in case 1, and the detective has no general reason to distrust either source. If he said "I believe each of those two things, but I don't believe the butler was in the house at midnight and had the only key," I'd call that irrational — and yet the same shape of move feels fine to me in the preface. I want a principle that treats cases 2 and 4 differently, but I can't say what the difference is.

5. The long proof. A mathematician has gone through a 200-step proof and each step individually looks right, but she won't say "the theorem has been proved" until a colleague checks the proof end to end. That strikes me as sensible, not inconsistent.

I've read around this topic a bit, so please go past the usual labels and do the work. My working rule so far was: believe P when your probability for P is at least 0.999, and don't require belief to be closed under conjunction. Cases 1 and 2 fit that rule. Case 3 doesn't, since it's 0.999 and I don't believe. Case 4 doesn't either, since refusing the conjunction there looks wrong. And I can't tell whether the rule really captures what's going on in case 5. So I need something better.

You are a professional epistemologist who works on the formal structure of rational belief. Please answer in four sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths:

- "The data, sharpened" (about 150 words): restate the philosophical backbone of my cases and say precisely which pairs of my verdicts pull against each other.
- "Candidate principles" (about 350 words): state two or three precise principles that might cover my verdicts, and say which case motivates each clause.
- "Tests and new cases" (about 300 words): run those principles on my five cases, and then on at least one new case of your own — something in the same spirit I haven't mentioned — that would seperate the candidates.
- "What I'd keep and what I'd drop" (about 200 words): pick the best candidate, state just what it rules about my five cases, and say honestly which of my verdicts it cannot save, if any.

Take your time and think carefully. Before writing, brainstorm the two or three most plausible principles you might defend, note the strongest objection to each, and only then settle on your final view. Write it so a former geophysicist can follow without being talked down to.
