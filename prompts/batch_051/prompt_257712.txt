I teach a second-year undergraduate module on decision theory — about a dozen students, 90-minute seminars — and we've just finished expected utility theory. I want a session where the students feel the force of the coherence arguments for expected utility maximisation rather than just hearing them laid out. In particular, I'd like them to actually be money-pumped, or watch a pump be attempted on them, before anyone says the phrase 'money pump'. The idea is to get them to make pairwise choices between three small-stakes gambles that are designed so a common intuitive pattern cycles; then let one student act as bookie and offer to swap tickets for a small fee, so anyone whose choices cycle ends up paying to get back to the ticket they started with. They should construct the cycle themselves and only notice where they've ended up in the debrief. Could you give me the three lotteries, with actual probabilities and coin amounts I can run in a classroom, the exact rules for eliciting the initial choices without tipping them off, and a script for the bookie including what to say when someone hesitates. The debrief should map each step of the game onto a specific axiom of expected utility theory, and be explicit about what a successful pump would and would not show — for instance, does being open to a Dutch book show there preferences are genuinely irrational, or only that a patient, slightly better-informed trader can exploit them? It also needs to handle the student who says they'd simply refuse to swap once they spot the cycle: is that a defence of their original preferences, or are they quietly revising them into coherence, and does the pump argument still have bite either way? Tell me what will likely go wrong when I run this (students twig to the trick and go consistent, genuine indifference between options, the 'I don't care about a few pence' line, nobody cycling) and how to handle each, and be honest about which part of the coherence idea the activity can't reach no matter how well it goes.
