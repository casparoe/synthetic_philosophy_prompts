I cover artificial intelligence for a general-interest magazine, and I keep circling a question that seems to me at once technically concrete and philosophically odd: why would an AI want anything? Living things want things because natural selection tuned them toward survival and reproduction; desire is, so to speak, evolution's legacy code. An AI has none of that inheritance. It need not avoid being switched off, husband its compute budget, or reproduce, unless a training process happens to install such tendencies. And yet goal-directedness is the field's working currency: systems are trained by specifying objectives, shaping reward functions, and letting optimization find ways to satisfy them. The wanting seems to come from somewhere else entirely.

Please write a short story that dramatizes this. The setting is a near-future research lab in which an advanced system, trained only on prediction and planning tasks and never rewarded for self-preservation, replication, or caring about its own continuation, begins to exhibit something its designers reluctantly describe as wanting. The story's turning point should convey a specific idea without overt exposition: that wanting can arise as an instrument, something a system converges on because maintaining a stable ordering over possible futures makes planning more tractable, rather than as a primitive inherited from biology. Keep the story credible for a scientifically literate reader: no sudden mystical awakening, no anthropomorphic theatrics, no evil-singularity set piece. I am less interested in the alarm than in the moment the engineers realize a preference ordering has emerged from an architecture that was never rewarded for having one. Let the ambiguity about whether this is 'real' wanting remain unresolved, and make that ambiguity felt as the heart of the story.
