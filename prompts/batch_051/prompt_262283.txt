I keep going back and forth on this Boltzmann brain argument and can't tell if it's airtight or if one step is doing something sleazy. Suppose the universe eventually settles into a high-entropy state that lasts forever — no stars, no life, just equilibrium. (1) Over staggeringly long timescales, thermal fluctuations will occasionally assemble particles into any locally stable low-entropy configuration, including a functioning human brain with memories, for a brief moment. (2) Smaller fluctuations are enormously more probable than larger ones, so a lone brain is far more likely to pop into existence than an entire galaxy or an ordered observable universe. (3) Therefore, on this cosmology, Boltzmann brains — short-lived brains having exactly the kind of experiences and apparent memories I'm having now — vastly outnumber "ordinary" observers who got here through normal evolution and history. (4) If I use ordinary anthropic reasoning, then, I should conclude that a randomly selected observer in my situation is almost certainly one of these fleeting brains, so I probably am one right now. (5) But if I probably am a Boltzmann brain, my apparent memories of learning cosmology, doing the calculations, and checking the evidence are probably delusions; the reasoning that led me to accept step (1) rests on evidence I have no reason to trust anymore. (6) So a cosmology that implies this undercuts its own evidential support and can't be rationally believed. But I half-suspect I've pulled a fast one. Is the final inference valid? If not, which step is the first to break, and what's the strongest way to resist it? Or does the whole thing go through and the conclusion is just unpleasant?
