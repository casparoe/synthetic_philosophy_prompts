For a school research project on how groups turn individual views into a single collective decision, I have been working through a line of reasoning my teacher sketched in passing. It seems to prove that majority rule takes individually rational preference rankings and produces a collectively rational ranking, and I cannot tell whether the argument is sound or quietly assumes the very thing it is trying to establish. Here is the argument as I have reconstructed it.

(1) Suppose there is an odd number of voters, and each voter has a strict preference ranking over three candidates, A, B, and C. Each ranking is complete — for any two candidates, the voter strictly prefers one to the other — and transitive: if the voter prefers X to Y and Y to Z, then the voter prefers X to Z. Call a ranking with these two properties rational.

(2) Define the social ranking by pairwise majority rule: for any two candidates X and Y, society prefers X to Y exactly when more than half of the voters prefer X to Y. Because the number of voters is odd and nobody is indifferent, one of the two candidates always commands a majority.

(3) It follows that the social ranking is complete, since majority rule yields a definite verdict for every pair of candidates.

(4) The social ranking is also transitive. Suppose society prefers X to Y and Y to Z. Then more than half of the voters prefer X to Y, and more than half prefer Y to Z. Any two majorities drawn from the same set of voters must overlap in at least one voter. That overlapping voter prefers X to Z, by transitivity of his or her individual ranking. Therefore a majority of voters prefer X to Z, and society prefers X to Z. The same reasoning applies to every triple of candidates, so the social ranking is transitive.

(5) Therefore, pairwise majority rule always converts a collection of rational individual rankings into a rational social ranking. Majority rule is, in this precise sense, a fully rational procedure for aggregating preferences.

A friend of mine thinks the flaw, if there is one, must be in step (2), because majority rule compares candidates two at a time and ignores the full shape of each voter's ranking. Another friend suspects step (4) does something suspect, but neither of us can state the gap cleanly. My teacher said this connects to Arrow's impossibility theorem, which I have not yet studied properly, and which apparently shows that several reasonable-sounding conditions on a fair voting system cannot all hold at once.

I would like a committed judgment rather than a survey of possible difficulties. Is the argument valid as written? If it is not valid, please identify the first numbered step that fails and explain exactly what goes wrong in that step, as precisely as you can. It would also help to know whether the conclusion in step (5) might still be true even if the proof does not establish it, and under what restricted conditions it would be true.
