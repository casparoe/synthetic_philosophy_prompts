Two members of my political theory seminar got locked in an argument over a passage in Hannah Arendt's "Lying in Politics: Reflections on the Pentagon Papers" (1971). The passage they were arguing about is this one:

"A characteristic of human action is that it always begins something new, but this does not mean that it is ever permitted to start ab ovo, to create ex nihilo. In order to make room for one’s own action, something that was there before must be removed or destroyed, and things as they were before are changed. Such change would be impossible if we could not mentally remove ourselves from where we are physically located and imagine that things might as well be different from what they actually are. In other words, the ability to lie, the deliberate denial of factual truth, and the capacity to change facts, the ability to act, are interconnected; they owe their existence to the same source, imagination.

For it is by no means a matter of course that we can say, The sun shines, when it is actually raining (the consequence of certain brain injuries is the loss of this capacity); it rather indicates that while we are well equipped for the world, sensually as well as mentally, we are not fitted to it as one of its inalienable parts. We are free to change the world and to start something new in it. Without the mental freedom to deny or affirm existence, to say “yes” or “no”—not just to statements or propositions in order to express agreement or disagreement, but to things as they are given, beyond agreement or disagreement, to our organs of perception and cognition—no action would be possible; and action is of course the very stuff politics is made of."

Maya opened with the claim that the passage is aimed at readers who treat political lying as an avoidable corruption. On her reading, Arendt is not excusing lies but explaining why moral outrage has never eliminated them: the very capacity that lets politicians change the world also lets them deny facts, so lying is a standing structural possibility rather than a foreign intrusion. She took the sentence linking lying and action to a single source in imagination as her main evidence.

Daniel objected that Maya's reading makes the essay self-defeating. If lying and action originate in the same capacity, he said, then Arendt has no principled way to separate the political actor who changes the world from the liar who falsifies the record; on Maya's own reading, the essay's exposure of deception presupposes a line that the passage erases. A passage that cannot distinguish the Pentagon Papers' lies from politics itself, he said, cannot ground a critique of those lies.

Maya replied that the distinction is still available inside the very passage. Action changes what is the case; lying changes what people take to have been the case. They share a source without sharing a result. The passage says only that both require the capacity to step back from things as they are and imagine them otherwise; it does not say that altering the world and denying a fact are the same deed.

Daniel came back that this merely relocates the problem. A lie, once told, is itself a new event in the world, with consequences like those of any other act, so denying what was is itself a way of changing what is. If the source is the same and the product is the same in kind, he asked, what is left of the distinction except the winner's word about what happened?

The seminar ended before either gave ground. Adjudicate the argument as it actually went. For each move, say who is right about what; identify which of Arendt's distinctions each speaker is using or missing; and say what the quoted passage, read in the context of the essay, does and does not commit Arendt to.
