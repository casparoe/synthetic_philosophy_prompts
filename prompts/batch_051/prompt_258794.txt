After I retired from geochemistry and finally got around to reading legal philosophy, I kept tripping over the same thing: the argument about strict liability and mens rea seems to have reproduced the same moves for well over a century. People who think punishing without fault is unjust treat it as nearly definitional that criminal liability requires a guilty mind, while the other side says that is a historical slogan and not an argument, and that modern regulatory offenses simply need strict liability. Nobody moves. I was reading Holmes’s The Common Law, and this passage caught my attention: “The law embodies the story of a nation’s development through many centuries, and it cannot be dealt with as if it contained only the axioms and corollaries of a book of mathematics.” That sounds like he is saying the dispute is partly about how much doctrinal history should constrain present practice. But I can’t tell if the real impasse is normative (retributivist versus consequentialist), conceptual (what counts as punishment rather than regulation), empirical (whether strict liability actually deters or over deter), or something else.

My working hypothesis is that the debate has stalled because the two sides are answering different questions under one word, “punishment.” Is that right, or is there a substantive disagreement hiding underneath? I’m not asking for a summary of positions. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself: what kind of disagreement is this, and what would actually count as progress—a distinction, a test, a formal result, a change of question—or is it the sort of impasse that should not be expected to move?
