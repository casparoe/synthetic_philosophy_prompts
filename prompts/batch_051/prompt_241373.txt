Ive been going down a rabbit hole trying to steelman the old guilt-culture/shame-culture distinction instead of just vaguely gesturing at the West internalizing norms while honor cultures don't. The thing that keeps bugging me is that the same memory will feel like guilt when I replay it alone at 3 a.m. and like shame the instant I imagine someone specific hearing about it — say, a teenage shoplifting incident that feels like a cold private wrong until I picture my brother finding out, at which point it becomes this hot, face-level thing. I used to file those as two different emotions, but the parsimonious move now looks to me like this: there's one underlying distress signal (a detected gap between how I behaved and a standard I was supposed to meet), and the different 'emotions' are just that signal pointed at different simulated judges. Guilt is the version where the simulated judge is a model of yourself; shame is the version where it's a model of other people whose respect you actually need; embarrassment is the version where it's everyone in the room and the standard is mere social competence. You can move one memory through all three just by changing which eyes you imagine on the replay, which is a thing I definitely do, and I don't think that's an accident of introspection.

The cultural corollary would be that a shame culture trains people to run the simulation against external eyes (reputation, honor, being seen), while a guilt culture trains them to run it against their self-model, and the two are endpoints of a continuous parameter rather than different kinds of thing. Then "can a culture choose its moral-emotional profile?" becomes an engineering question: can you redesign the default simulated audience by changing what gets detected, publicized, and punished? Public shaming rituals push toward shame; anonymity and private apology push toward guilt; the lever is never "create the emotion" but "change whose gaze gets installed as the default judge." And the whole thing leaks, because a shame episode gets internalized into guilt when a humiliated person reruns it alone, and guilt re-externalizes into shame the moment a society adds mass surveillance and pile-ons.
