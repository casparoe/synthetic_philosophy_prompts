I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, so I end up at events where people argue about things like formal epistemology. Last month I watched two regulars at a meetup go back and forth about Jeffrey conditionalization, and it's been bugging me since. Here's the argument as best I remember it.

Hannah said: suppose your neighbor bangs on your door at 2 a.m. and says he's pretty sure he saw a fox in your yard. You don't get to just conditionalize on "a fox was in the yard," because you didn't observe that; you observed your neighbor telling you something. But you also shouldn't conditionalize on "my neighbor said he saw a fox"—well, you could, but that's not what people actually do, and it requires liklihoods you usually don't have. What you actually have is a shift: your credence that a fox was in the yard goes from, say, 0.05 to 0.35. Jeffrey conditionalization takes that new credence as input and revises the rest of your beliefs by holding conditional probabilities fixed.

Ravi jumped in: that's exactly the sleight of hand. Accepting a new P(fox) as an input, with no story about where it came from, is just changing your mind without giving yourself evidence. You've learned something concrete—your neighbor said such-and-such—and if you were a careful Bayesian you'd model that, with priors over his reliability, what he'd say in fox versus no-fox worlds, and so on. If you can't do that, the problem is an incomplete model, not evidence of a new kind. And if you can do that, ordinary conditionalization is enough, so Jeffrey conditionalization is either unnecessary or ungrounded.

Hannah said that misses the point of the fox case. The news your neighbor brings is precisely that your model was incomplete: you can't yet spell out likelihoods for his utterance, but you still learned something, and you can report it as a new probability of fox. Jeffrey conditionalization is the conservative update that revises just enough to accommodate that new probability.

Ravi came back: conservative along which metric? Holding conditional probabilities fixed is only one kind of minimality. And it's a substantive assumption—maybe your neighbor's arrival at 2 a.m. should also alter P(someone else heard a noise | fox), because it tells you foxes are active at night around here. If rigidity is wrong, Jeffrey wants you to keep conditionals that the evidence itself has changed. So the method solves a fake problem by assuming away the hard part.

Hannah admitted that rigidity could fail, but said every update rule has a rigidity-like assumption somewhere—ordinary conditionalization holds likelihoods fixed too. The question is which inputs you can take, and denying Jeffrey just because the input isn't a proposition learned with certainty is too strict.

They asked me to adjudicate and I couldn't. Can you go through move by move and say who's right about what, where the argument goes wrong, what they're both missing, and who's more right overall?
