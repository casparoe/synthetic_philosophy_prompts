I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and next month I am presenting an essay to a joint research workshop whose regulars are demographers, economic historians, and a small group of development economists. The thesis I am defending is that the standard dismissal of Malthus as simply falsified is too quick. My claim is that the Malthusian constraint—diminishing returns to land pressing on living standards as population grows—did operate for most of human history, that the industrial escape did not refute the constraint but changed the conditions under which it operated, and that the phrase "right but early" can be made intellectually respectable rather than unfalsifiable, provided it is disciplined: a delayed mechanism earns credit only when the postponement was specified in advance and carries new observable implications, not merely a longer timeline. In the talk I will press three moves in sequence: first, that pre-industrial economies showed the characteristic Malthusian pattern of living standards tracking population; second, that the escape is better described as a shift in how strongly technology responded to population pressure than as the death of the constraint, since the constraint reappears whenever that responsiveness stalls; and third, that neo-Malthusian environmental claims should have to say in advance what would count as the constraint failing to reappear, and by when.

The audience will not be a general public audience. The demographers in the room are committed to the demographic transition being a distinct regime shift, and they will resist treating fertility decline as merely an endogenous escape valve, because that seems to reverse Malthus's own behavioral assumption that fertility rises with material improvement. The economic historians care about institutions, trade, and the fact that wages began to rise well before fertility declined in the canonical Western European case; they will argue the escape cannot be explained by the mechanism whose arrival it supposedly preceded. The development economists will be less interested in the history and more interested in whether my discipline for "right but early" licenses or undermines contemporary resource pessimism, and they will press hardest on methodology: what makes one theoretical rescue legitimate and another ad hoc.

Please prepare the Q&A prep sheet I will actually use, not a general defense of the thesis. Give me the eight or so objections I am most likely to hear from this audience, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way a demographer, an economic historian, or a development economist would put it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what to concede up front so the question period does not spend its first ten minutes on points I have already granted, what I should hold even at the cost of appearing stubborn, and which of my three moves is the weakest and how I should prepare to defend it rather than hope nobody notices. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
