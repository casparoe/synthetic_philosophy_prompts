In my philosophy of language seminar we've been stuck on indexicals for two weeks, and the standing claim is that no human language could do without them—that 'I', 'here', and 'now' are ineliminable because you need some expression whose reference shifts with the context of utterance to tie a sentence to the speaker, place, and time. The usual argument, as far as I can reconstruct it from my notes, assumed something like face-to-face talk: a speaker and hearer in a shared physical situation, an utterance at a particular moment. I've been trying to figure out whether that argument survives two things it didn't assume. First, a huge amount of language now lives in logs: a chat app already attach the username, the timestamp, and the earlier messages, so you can often replace 'I' with 'the user who sent the message two lines up' and look it up in the record rather than needing a shifter in the sentence. Second, a language model produces perfectly ordinary uses of 'I' and 'now' even though there's no stable self, body, location, or moment inside the model; the deictic center has to be pasted in afterward by the interface or the reader, and its not obvious it's doing the same work a real speaker would. So here's the thing: does the classic ineliminability argument still go through, or does it now only establish something weaker, like you need some context-tracking device, which might just as well be a timestamp column or a user ID field rather than a pronoun? If the argument survives, say which premise the new cases don't touch. If it doesn't, say what the repaired claim should be. If I've set the argument up wrong—like it never assumed what I think it did, or these developments don't actually cut against it—just tell me that instead of politely playing along. Oh, and American English please.
