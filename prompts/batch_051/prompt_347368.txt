I'm a novelist, and a physicist friend has been poking at the one thing I need to believe to do my job. I write slowly, and the part I love is the feeling that at any moment the next sentence could go a bunch of different ways and I'm choosing it. My friend says special relativity makes that feeling an illusion, and he gave me an argument I can't get out of my head:

(1) In special relativity, there's no absolute "now"; whether two far-apart events happen at the same time depends on your reference frame.

(2) Take event A: me sitting at this desk right now. Take event B: a flare on a star far away, which in my frame is simultaneous with A.

(3) Take event C: me writing the last page of this novel tomorrow afternoon, an event in my future.

(4) If that star is far enough away, B and C are spacelike separated, which means there's a legal reference frame, moving relative to me, in which B and C happen at the same time.

(5) But no frame is more real than any other. So the "now" that contains C is just as real as the "now" that contains A.

(6) So tomorrow afternoon already exists the same way this moment does. The future is fixed like the past is fixed.

(7) Which is absurd — or if it isn't absurd, then my whole sense of writing as choosing is a con I'm running on myself.

Something in (1)–(6) has to give, but every step looked solid when he said it. I'd rather reject almost any premise than give up the future being open, but I don't know what I'd be paying. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? And please tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would make you switch your diagnosis, or where youre reasoning is most likely to fail.
