I have been trying to work out, for myself, what would make it legitimate to say that something has a mind of its own, the way I take other people to have minds. The rough list I keep coming back to has four points: (1) the thing has an inner point of view, a way things feel from the inside; (2) it shows signs of caring about pain and pleasure in ways that suggest genuine concern rather than mere reflex; (3) it can act on its surroundings with purpose, correcting course when things go wrong; and (4) it can convey something it wants or perceives, even in a very limited way. So far this list has struck me as workable, but then I learned about split-brain patients, and the case has been bothering me.

As I understand it, in severe epilepsy a surgeon sometimes cuts the bundle of fibers that joins the two hemispheres of the brain, and afterward the two sides can no longer share information directly, though they still share a body. The left hemisphere usually does the speaking; the right hemisphere can rarely speak but, in experiments, can answer questions by pointing, drawing, or moving the left hand. In one setup, the two sides view different pictures, and the silent right hemisphere makes the left hand point to an object that matches the picture it saw, while the speaking side, when asked why the hand pointed to that object, immediately produces a story that has nothing to do with what the silent side actually saw. It is as though one side of the brain is a separate perceiver and chooser, and the other side talks about the body as if it were the only one making the choices.

That case sits right on the edge of my four tests. I am not asking whether a split-brain patient is one person or two in a medical or legal way; I am asking whether, by the four criteria above, the silent hemisphere should count as a second mind, a second subject of experience, living inside the same skull. I would like you to go through each criterion honestly, indicate where the evidence underdetermines the answer, and then say whether the case shows that my four tests themselves need revising, for instance, whether the ability to put things into words is doing too much work in my list, or whether I have mixed up the question of what I could ever tell from the outside with the question of what is actually there. Please write your answer in American English.
