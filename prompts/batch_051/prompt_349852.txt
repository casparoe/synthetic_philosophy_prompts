As a novelist writing a book about somebody who walks away from a comfortable, tightly managed community, I keep coming back to Lois Lowry's The Giver and I don't fully trust my own read of it. Could you step in as a philosopher who works on Mill's On Liberty, take your time, and maybe brainstorm a little before you answer. I want to read the novel through Mill's argument that a fully human life requires more than being kept safe and supplied—that it requires choosing and testing out a way of living for yourself (what he calls experiments in living)—and that a community that makes everyones choices for them, however painless it looks, eventually goes stagnant. Where does the book actually dramatize that argument, and where does it complicate or resist it? The community really has eliminated hunger, war, and cruelty, after all, which isn't nothing. Is Lowry ultimately on Mill's side, or is she showing a cost to experiments in living that Mill doesn't fully count? Don't just rehash the plot; get into the philosophy. I'm trying to figure out wether my own protagonist is a Jonas or just a fool.
