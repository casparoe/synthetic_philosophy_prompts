As a novelist, I'm working on a scene where a small town revives an old scapegoat rite, and I keep getting stuck on whether the thing actually does anything to guilt or just gives people a story to tell themselves. I've been reading around in Leviticus. The KJV of Leviticus 16:21, as I remember it — not exact, but close — says Aaron lays his hands on the head of a live goat, confesses over it all the iniquities, transgressions, and sins of the children of Israel, puts them on the goat's head, and sends the goat into the wilderness by the hand of a fit man. Frazer and others treat this as a literal transfer of evil, but I want to know if that transfer is a real psychological thing or just a ritualistic way of talking.

So here's what I'm after: how would you test whether a scapegoat rite like this actually reduces or displaces guilt, blame, or moral discomfort in the participants, rather than merely changing how they talk or making them feel briefly relieved? Give me a concrete experimental design, say what result would favor each reading, and be honest about what a positive result would and would not show. I especially want to separate three mechanisms: confession, public cleansing, and actual transfer of guilt. Could a test distinguish them, or is the notion of transfer too underdefined to test at all? And if it is underdefined, what operational definition would be least unfair to the original text?
