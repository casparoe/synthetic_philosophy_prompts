I convene a small adversarial-collaboration program in the philosophy of science, and the current pair has been working for several months on the epistemic significance of computational irreducibility. They have already agreed on a working characterization: a system is computationally irreducible when, for some well-defined class of questions about its future states, no general procedure can answer those questions substantially faster than simulating the system's dynamics step by step; in that sense the system's own evolution is its most efficient description. What they cannot settle is what follows. One participant — call this the strong-irreducibility view — holds that computational irreducibility sets a principled limit on explanation: for such systems there can be no genuine shortcut, no compressed theory, and no understanding that outruns running the system itself, so the ideal of science as the discovery of shorter laws fails for a large class of real systems. The other participant — the explanation-optimist — accepts the formal notion but denies those consequences, arguing that explanation does not require compressing a trajectory; it can isolate mechanisms, invariant constraints, coarse-grained regularities, and statistical laws that remain informative even when no shortcut predicts the micro-trajectory. The two keep returning to the same exhibits: a cellular automaton whose large-scale pattern has resisted every closed form they have tried; turbulent flow, where physics works with coarse-grained descriptions rather than molecule-by-molecule simulation; and an agent-based market model in which individual runs diverge while aggregate statistics stay stable.

Draft the document this pair should be able to issue after a good-faith collaboration. It should contain: (1) a numbered agreed-claims section covering the mathematics of irreducibility, what has and has not been shown, and the distinctions between incompressibility of a single trajectory, incompressibility of a class of predictive questions, and incompressibility of an adequate explanation; (2) a disagreements table in which each residual claim is stated precisely, and each camp's own position is worded in a form that camp would sign; (3) a cruxes section identifying the sub-questions that would move at least one side if settled, each paired with a concrete case, dataset, or argument that both sides agree would bear on it, and each side's advance prediction about what that case would show; and (4) a short closing note on any part of the dispute that is verbal rather than substantive. Number the sections, use columns for the table, and do not blur the disagreement into a middle position.
