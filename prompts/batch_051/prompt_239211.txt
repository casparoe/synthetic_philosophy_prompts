I run a fortnightly rationality meetup, mostly people who've read a fair bit of the Sequences but haven't done much formal philosophy. We've got 75 minutes, between 8 and 14 people, and I want a practical session on Dutch books that doesn't start with the words "Dutch book". The aim is for people to feel the force of the argument in their gut: they should end up having accepted a set of bets that loses no matter what, and only then have it pointed out that their own credences were what made that possible. No lecture up front, and they shouldn't hear the term untill the debrief.

I'd like a concrete runnable design: roles, materials, the exact propositions to use, instructions a facilitator can read out, and a timeline. The key constraint is that the participants themselves set the prices. So maybe one or two people act as market-makers, or everyone writes down the lowest odds at which they'd buy and the highest at which they'd sell. I'm not sure which setup produces the cleanest artefact; please choose one and justify it briefly. The payoff scheme has to work without real cash, so tokens, points, sweets, or just losing face.

Then I want a debrief that maps what happened in the room onto the formal claim: if your degrees of belief don't satisfy the probability axioms, there's a set of bets each of which you'd accept individually but which guarantees a loss. Walk through the exact bets that actually got made, and show where additivity was violated. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time: people give vague intervals, or they smell a trap and refuse to commit, or the whole thing becomes a debate about whether probabilities are internal states. How do I handle each of those without railroading anyone? And be honest about what the game can't reproduce: the real argument targets an idealised agent whose acceptances are stable over time and whose betting odds reveal credences, whereas live humans can change their minds or say the stakes were too small to count. What does the facilitator need to supply afterwards to bridge that gap? A short second-round variant where the trap is less obvious would be good too.
