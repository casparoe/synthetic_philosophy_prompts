Suppose a deliberative group adopts the following explicit scorekeeping rule for its spoken exchanges. The conversational scoreboard—the common ground of accepted presuppositions—is kept in writing and visible to everyone. Before a speaker may assert any sentence, the speaker must enter onto the scoreboard every presupposition the assertion would standardly carry that is not already there: presuppositions triggered by definite descriptions, factive verbs, change-of-state verbs, iteratives like "again" or "too", clefts, and the like. Each newly entered presupposition is then open for objection for one minute. It is added to the common ground only if at least one other participant seconds it and no one objects; the rules do not require objectors to state a ground. Silence does not accommodate: an entered presupposition that is neither seconded nor objected to simply fails to enter the common ground, and the would-be asserter must withdraw or reformulate. Once a presupposition is on the board, it may be removed only by a seconded, unopposed motion to strike it.

Treat this protocol as a mechanism rather than a piece of conversational etiquette, and trace how the practice would actually behave. In particular: what becomes of the usual process of accommodation; which presuppositions can never get onto the board and why; how speakers restructure what they would otherwise assert so as to smuggle or launder presuppositions; what strategic uses participants will find for seconding, objecting, and motions to strike; what happens to information normally conveyed quietly through presupposition rather than asserted; and where the first major breakdown under ordinary conversational pressure occurs. Conclude with a final line of the form \answer{...} containing a one-sentence statement of the mechanism's deepest structural vulnerability.
