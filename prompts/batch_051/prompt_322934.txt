I am trying to work out the difference between a consideration that is a genuine reason to hold an attitude—to admire something, to hope for something, to resent someone, to believe something—and a consideration that is merely a reason to try to get myself into that attitude. My working principle is this: a fact is a reason for an attitude only if it bears on the object of the attitude in the way that makes that kind of attitude fitting; considerations that bear only on the advantages or disadvantages of being in the state are reasons to act so as to produce or remove the state, not reasons for or against the attitude itself. I would like you to test this principle against the following cases, repair it if it needs repairing, and tell me which of my verdicts, if any, I should give up.

Here are the cases with my verdicts.

1. A vicious but powerful person threatens to destroy everything I value unless I genuinely admire him. I am sure I have strong reason to take whatever steps might bring about admiration—a drug, hypnosis, whatever works. But I do not think the threat gives me a reason to admire him. He remains despicable, and admiration of him would be unfitting.

2. A colleague handles a hostile meeting with far more patience and fairness than she owed. The fact that she did this is a reason to admire her, and it is not merely a reason to make myself admire her; it makes admiration appropriate.

3. A rich eccentric offers me a large sum if I come to want a dish of mud for its own sake. I have every reason to try to induce the desire if I can. But the money is no reason to want the mud; it does not make the mud worth wanting.

4. The same person offers me the same sum to feel contempt for a mild-mannered neighbor who has never done me any wrong. Again I say: reason to induce the contempt, no reason to feel it. Here I feel especially certain, precisely because the neighbor has done nothing contemptible.

5. My friend's child is in surgery. I think the fact that the child's survival would be a good outcome is a reason to hope for it; and the fact that my visible hope would comfort the parents is a reason to want to be in a hopeful state. But I am not sure that last fact is a reason to hope, as opposed to a reason to try to induce hope. The two considerations point the same way, and I find the line between them blurring.

6. A tyrant offers to release prisoners I know to be innocent if I come to genuinely believe that his regime is just, though all the evidence I have shows the opposite. I say: the offer is a reason to try to get myself to believe, if I can, but no reason to believe; evidence is the only thing that counts as a reason for belief.

7. A physician tells my friend that cancer patients who are fully convinced they will recover do measurably better, even with treatment held constant, and that in his case the clinical evidence is genuinely uncertain. Intuitively I want to say that my friend has a reason to be confident. But my principle would say that the effect of confidence on recovery is a fact about the state of confidence, not about the illness, so it is only a reason to induce confidence. I cannot tell whether to abandon that intuition or loosen the principle.

8. Someone deliberately and seriously insulted me years ago. I think the insult is a reason to feel resentment toward him. I also think that carrying the resentment is ruining my health and my other relationships, and I find myself wanting to say that these facts are a reason not to resent him. But my principle seems to force me to say they are only a reason to try to stop resenting him, not a reason against the resentment itself. Is saying that I have a reason not to resent him just a harmless way of putting the point, or does the principle misclassify it?

Is there a single principle that captures most of these verdicts, and if so, what exactly is it? Which of my verdicts does it require me to revise? State the principle precisely, show how it handles each case, and then apply it to a further case I have not listed: a man's son has been lost at sea for weeks and the evidence strongly indicates he drowned; continuing to hope keeps the man functional enough to care for his other children. Does he have a reason to hope, a reason to try to make himself hope, both, or neither, on your preferred principle?
