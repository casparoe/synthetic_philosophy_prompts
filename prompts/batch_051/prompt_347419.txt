I keep going back and forth on whether the shift from foraging to farming should count as a 'mistake.' I've been working with three criteria for calling a large historical transition a mistake: (1) people at the time had a feasible alternative they could have stuck with, (2) the main harms were foreseeable enough to be avoidable, and (3) it left most of the people who lived through it worse off by their own lights. Take the spread of farming in the early Fertile Crescent. Health markers get worse—shorter stature, more signs of nutritional stress, more infectious disease—and the labor of planting and grinding grain seems at least as heavy as foraging and more monotonous. The 'alternative' argument gets some support from the idea that many hunter-gatherers were affluent in a specific sense: not rich in goods, but having few wants and satisfying them without constant work. On the other side, farming fed far more people per acre, it spread into places where people plausibly could have kept foraging for a while, nobody at the start had seen where the process would end up, and the people whose health declined are not the same people who later had the advantages of larger, richer societies. Go through my three criteria and say whether agriculture comes out as a mistake on them. Then tell me whether the case shows any of the criteria need revising—for example, is 'worse off by their own lights' doing real work when we can't ask them, and does it matter that there was no single decider, just lots of small local choices over generations?
