I've been reporting on the way every app and service now wraps itself in contracts of adhesion that nobody negotiates and almost nobody reads. The classical argument for enforcing contracts goes something like this: a contract is binding because the parties voluntarily took on the obligation — offer, acceptance, consideration, and actual assent. That argument was built for a world where two parties could look at the terms, haggle, and walk away. The new development is that the assent event has been redesigned entirely — shrinkwrap said opening the box counts, clickwrap makes you tap a button that says I agree, browsewrap says using the site at all counts, and now we have dark patterns, terms that auto-update with an email no one opens, and user agreements longer than the Constitution. What I want to know is whether the voluntary-assent justification for enforcing contracts survives that redesign, or whether it's been quietly replaced by a different argument — maybe constructive notice plus inertia, or efficiency. Which exact premise does the redesign put pressure on: that people actually understand the terms, or that its fair to treat a click as a signal of consent even when they dont? And is there a repaired version that still says something weaker but real — like, enforce these contracts for reasons of coordination but stop claiming they carry the moral force of a promise? If the old argument is dead, say so; if it just needs a patch, show me the patch and what it gives up.
