I am a PhD student in philosophy of science working on reduction and the autonomy of the special sciences. In a seminar on Dennett's distinction between cranes and skyhooks, I built the following argument, and I now find it suspicious because its conclusion seems to demote nearly all non-fundamental explanation to the status of promissory notes. I would be grateful for a diagnosis of where it goes wrong and an account of what it costs to give up the offending step.

(1) A skyhook is an explanatory posit that does real explanatory work while floating free of any underlying mechanism — it lifts without being supported from below. Skyhooks are illegitimate.

(2) A higher-level explanation counts as a crane, a legitimate bottom-up explainer, only if its explanatory regularities are at least in principle derivable from lower-level mechanisms, ultimately physical ones.

(3) Many central explanations in the special sciences have no such derivation, even in principle. Natural selection explains adaptation without any derivation from biochemistry and microphysics; price adjustment explains market coordination without a derivation from the microphysics of individual agents; and the role of working-memory limits explains performance deficits without a derivation from neurochemistry. These patterns are realized in radically heterogeneous lower-level materials and depend on higher-level organization in ways that seem to resist translation downward.

(4) Therefore, by (2) and (3), these explanations are not cranes.

(5) Since every legitimate explanation is either a crane or a skyhook, and skyhooks are illegitimate, it follows that these special-science explanations are illegitimate as they stand: acceptable only as promissory notes for a future derivation.

(6) But that conclusion is hard to accept. Natural selection, price adjustment, and working-memory explanations are paradigm cases of non-miraculous, mechanistic explanation — exactly the sort of explanatory work the crane/skyhook framework was introduced to vindicate.

(7) So at least one of (1), (2), (3), or the dichotomy in (5) must be given up.

Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep the anti-skyhook principle in (1) and the verdict in (6). I can see that (2) is demanding, but I cannot yet articulate why it is anything other than the reasonable demand to show that a crane is built out of smaller cranes, all the way down. Is the argument valid as written, and if so, which premise is least costly to reject? Or have I slid somewhere — for example, between an explanation's being realized by lower-level mechanisms and its being derivable from them — in a way that the numbered steps conceal? I would also be interested in where the line between legitimate reductionist demands and greedy reductionism lies, since I cannot tell whether my argument enforces the former or the latter.
