I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas for a general audience, and I'm drafting a longform piece on physicalism — the view that everything that exists is physical, the mind included. The obstacle I keep hitting is the first word. Before I can explain to readers whether physicalism is true, false, or empty, I need a working definition of "physical" that actually does some sorting.

I'd like you to propose a definition and then run it against the cases below, saying for each whether it comes out physical and why. My own working assumptions are in parentheses, but I'm open to being argued out of them.

1. A quark, an electron, a gravitational field, spacetime itself. (Surely physical.)
2. The quantum wavefunction. (I'm inclined to call it physical, but it looks more like a probability distribution or a law than like a thing in the world, so I don't want that taken for granted.)
3. A gene, described functionally. (I'd call it physical, but it doesn't appear on the inventory of fundamental entities in any physics textbook.)
4. Shannon information — the bits in a signal, measured without regard to what carries them. (A thing in the world, or an abstraction over a physical channel?)
5. A felt pain, or the smell of coffee. (The constraint: a definition that excludes these settles the mind–body problem by stipulation; a definition that admits them too easily makes physicalism trivial.)
6. The number 2, or a mathematical proof. (Presumably not physical, even if doing mathematics happens in physical brains.)
7. A hypothetical ectoplasm that reliably bends spoons, occasionally registers on instruments, but is posited by no current physics and no completed physics. (Would it count as physical, and on what rationale?)
8. A hypothetical entity posited by some future, completed physics that we cannot currently conceive of. (This is the trap I keep circling — what I understand to be Hempel's dilemma: if "physical" means what current physics says, physicalism is hostage to the likely incompleteness of current physics and so probably false; if it means what an ideal completed physics would say, the doctrine threatens to become empty. I want the definition to thread that needle rather than just pick a horn.)

After running the cases, tell me which verdicts you are least confident in, and either revise the definition in response or defend the surprising verdicts. Finally, say a bit about whether "physical" should be fixed once and for all, or whether its content properly shifts as physics itself shifts.
