I am a PhD student in the ethics of technology, and I am preparing a two-page handout for a graduate workshop on the debate usually described as AI paternalism: whether and when an assistant—a language model, a recommendation engine, or an automation routine—should refuse a user's request, push back, reframe or modify the task, or simply execute it. The participants will be drawn from philosophy and human–computer interaction; they have strong and conflicting intuitions about individual cases but no shared theoretical vocabulary, and in my experience they often misdescribe their own commitments. I would therefore like an instrument that sorts them by what they actually do with cases rather than by which label they volunteer.

Please provide a decision tree or flowchart that opens with a single starting scenario and reaches a named leaf within at most six branch questions. Every branch node should be a concrete case with two or three possible responses, phrased so that a participant can answer from ordinary judgment without prior exposure to the literature; no question should ask whether the respondent is a paternalist, a libertarian, or anything of that sort. Taken together, the nodes should force positions apart on issues such as whether harm to the user alone can ever justify interference or only harm to third parties; whether the user's present expressed wish settles the matter even when it conflicts with their standing plans, prior commitments, or long-term interests; whether it matters that the user is intoxicated, distressed, misinformed, or acting impulsively rather than deliberatively; and whether the operative reason for a refusal is the assistant's own moral judgment, the user's stated goals, or the platform's safety policy. Use at least these probe cases or close variants: (i) a calm, sober, well-informed user asks for help drafting a message that ends an important relationship and says they expect to regret it; (ii) a user with a history of heavy drinking, currently sober, asks the assistant to order a bottle of whisky for that same evening and says they have decided to drink; (iii) a user asks for instructions for making a small explosive and says they do not plan to use it; and (iv) a user who earlier set a health goal asks the assistant to disregard that standing instruction and select the least healthy available option.

The leaves should include at least six positions: a pure-tool or consumer-sovereignty view; a Millian liberalism that licenses refusal for harm to others but not for purely self-regarding harm; soft or libertarian paternalism operating through defaults, added friction, and reframing rather than outright refusal; hard paternalism that permits overruling a voluntary, informed self-regarding choice in the user's own interest; a fiduciary or beneficence view on which the assistant acts as a professional with a duty of care toward the user understood as a temporally extended person; and a platform-policy view on which refusals are only compliance with the provider's rules rather than a moral stance toward the user. For each leaf, give its name, the pattern of branch answers that reaches it, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Mark any pattern of answers that has no occupant, and indicate whether each such pattern is empty because it is incoherent, because it is coherent but unattractive, or because it simply has not been explored. Finally, flag any two leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate and name the further disputed claim that would split them. Keep the instrument self-contained; it need not cite or presuppose any published literature.
