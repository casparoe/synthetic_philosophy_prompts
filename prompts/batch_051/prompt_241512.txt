I cover the science-and-religion beat, and the place where historical criticism touches doctrine keeps producing the same puzzle. Consider a reader who holds a scripture to be revealed and yet treats the instruction to leave part of a harvest for the poor as a live obligation while dismissing the rule against garments of mixed fibers, a few verses away in the same chapter, as a dead artifact of its culture. Historically, the two rules sit side by side in the same legal collection, were addressed to the same community, and show the same ancient Near Eastern background; there is no reason to think the original audience heard one as weightier than the other. But the modern reader sorts them into "living revelation" and "dated history" without, apparently, holding a rule that does the sorting. I want to know whether any principle can justify that asymmetry. Spell out exactly what is being claimed when one passage is retained as revelation and its neighbor is archived as history, then give the strongest candidate justification—genre, internal trajectory, later tradition, moral development, canonical hierarchy, or some other criterion—and test it against a case in which the same criterion points the wrong way: either it would demote a much-loved command or it would force retention of a rule the community has long abandoned. Close by saying whether the asymmetry should be kept, revised, or given up, and what "revelation" must mean if the asymmetry is to be principled rather than post hoc. Keep the response to 500 words or fewer.
