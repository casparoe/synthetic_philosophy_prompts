Since I packed in the lab work I've been reading Davidson on metaphor, and I keep coming round to the line that a metaphor has no special meaning over and above the literal one: a sentence like 'the new manager is a bulldozer' means only what it literally says (strictly, it's false), and everything the metaphor lets me see is a matter of use, of effects it has on me, not of content it carries. As someone who spent a career asking what would falsify a claim, I want the same audit for that view: what exactly would count against it?

Lay out the defeaters — observations about how metaphors are understood, findings from psycholinguistics and developmental work, facts about novel metaphors, the way metaphors seem to fall into systematic families where one domain structures another — and rank them from fatal to merely awkward. Tell me which of these a committed Davidsonian would concede as counting against the view, and which they would dismiss as a change of subject. In particular, I want to know whether the conceptual-metaphor people's evidence (the systematic mappings, cross-linguistic regularities, metaphors clustering the way they do) is evidence that metaphor is doing meaning-like cognitive work, or whether a Davidsonian can soak all of it up as effects after the literal meaning has done its bit. And at what point does that use/meaning distinction stop paying its way — when do the supposed effects become so content-like, so embeddable under negation and disagreement, that calling them non-meaning starts to look like a dodge? I'd also like it put in terms of differential predictions: where, if anywhere, do Davidson's view and a meaning-based view actually come apart empirically rather than just in terminology?

Then be honest about insulation: has the position been built, with the use/meaning split, so that no processing result could ever touch it, and is that a feature or a reason to stop taking it seriously as an empirical claim? Finally, seperate what's actually settled in this area from what's genuinely contested among people who know the topic — I suspect the philosophers and the cognitive scientists point different ways, so don't pass off one camp as the consensus.
