I've been reading rationalist-adjacent biology takes for years now and I've basically converged on a pretty strong adaptationism: if a trait shows complex functional organization (a wing, an eye, a behavioral pattern), the default explanation is that natural selection built or maintains it, and appeals to drift, developmental constraint, or "it's a spandrel" have to meet a high bar. I find the Gould and Lewontin spandrels critique mostly irritating—the motte is "byproducts can exist" (obviously) and the bailey seems to be "so we shouldn't reach for selection, and a lot of adaptationist stories are just Panglossian" (which is way stronger than their argument). My position, slightly more carefully: selection is the only known process that can put together *cumulative* functional complexity, so a trait that looks designed should be treated as an adaptation unless there's positive evidence otherwise.

What would actually count against this view? I don't just mean "re-explain the spandrels paper to me." I want the strongest possible case: which observations, experimental results, or theoretical arguments should genuinely make me lower my credence, and by how much? Sort them into "this would be decisive" vs. "this is just awkward." Candidates I can already half-see: neutral theory and the molecular clock implying most molecular change is invisible to selection; developmental bias where some forms are much easier to reach than others; constructive neutral evolution where multi-part complexity allegedly builds up through drift and loss without any selection for it; cases where a trait was confidently labeled an adaptation for decades and then turned out to be something else. Also, the pattern where "vestigial" organs keep turning out to have functions—I read that as evidence for my side, but I realize I might be cherry-picking the cases that got popularized, so tell me what I'm ignoring.

I want an honest assessment of how exposed my view actually is. Does it predict anything a "constraint-and-drift-first" view doesn't, or does it stop predicting once you add all the usual qualifications (spandrels can be co-opted later, selection can be frequency-dependent, constraints channel rather than replace selection, etc.) and become unfalsifiable? And if it has become unfalsifiable, is that a problem for a view of this kind, or just what a good null hypothesis looks like? I'd personally be most moved by concrete cases where functional complexity arose without selection, or where selection was shown unable to produce something people thought it could. But maybe that bar is to high.
