I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've been trying to get my head around a policy a small, fast-aging country is debating. It's called a "filial contact mandate." Every adult child with a living parent has to document at least one meaningful contact with each parent per month — a phone call over ten minutes, an in-person visit, a personal letter, or a care task — through a government app. The parent confirms in the app that the contact happened and was meaningful. If the child misses the monthly quota, they pay a substantial fine into the national elder-care fund. There are two escape valves: adults can get a waiver by certifying that a parent was abusive, and parents can opt their adult child out entirely, no questions asked, though the reason gets recorded somewhere. The politicians pushing it say it's just turning what decent families already do into official recognition, and that it targets grown children who vanish for years while the state picks up the tab. Some philosophers consulted on the bill have pushed back with a view they call the friendship model of filial duty: adult children don't owe parents repayment for the raising they got, but they do owe the kind of responsiveness and care friends owe each other, which isn't the sort of thing a quota plus a fine can produce. I can't tell whether the scheme assumes one model or the other, or whether that even matters once the app exists.

Put on your philosopher-of-the-family and mechanism-design hat and think carefully. Start by brainstorming who the actors are and what each of them can do with the app, the confirmation power, the fine, and the two escape valves. Then walk me through what this actually incentivizes, how it gets gamed first, what second-order effects it has on the relationships it's supposed to protect, where it fails hardest, and whether any of the failure modes can be fixed without gutting the scheme. I want the weeds, not the generic "families are complicated" take.
