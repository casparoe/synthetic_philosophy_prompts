I write literary fiction, and I've ended up with a pretty specific rule about my own disgust. When I'm deciding whether a character's action is supposed to read as wrong, I use my own first reaction as the test: if I picture writing the scene and I feel a wave of revulsion, I take that as strong evidence the act is morally off, and I write it that way—or I refuse to write it at all. If I picture the scene and feel nothing much, I generally let the character do it and let the reader judge. Over time this has hardened into a shortcut: I trust my disgust more than my stated moral principles, because my principles feel like arguments I invent after the fact, while disgust feels like a lifetime of social learning compressed into one quick signal. I also treat a small list of subjects as basically sacred—desecrating something childlike, messing with graves or photographs of the dead—and I don't even try to argue about those; I just won't write them as anything but violations. My partner says this is a bad moral epistemology. Disgust evolved to keep us away from spoilage and parasites and it misfires on novelty, sex, and strangers; treating it as a moral gauge is how you get taboos and puritanism, not how you find out what's actually wrong. I half-believe her, but I also don't have a better fast method for deciding what my characters ought to do without turning every chapter into a seminar. Is my shortcut defensible? If it isn't, is there a restated version that keeps the speed of the feeling without importing all its biases—and how would I tell from the inside when my disgust is tracking something morally important rather than just tracking unfamiliarity?
