I'm a high school student, and in three weeks I'm leading a forty-five-minute session of my school's philosophy club. The participants are juniors and seniors, mostly strong students, but none of them has studied decision theory or epistemology. I've chosen to run the session on the idea of transformative experience in roughly the sense contemporary philosophers discuss: an experience that someone cannot know from the inside before undergoing it, and that may also change what that person cares about, so that decisions about whether to undergo such experiences put pressure on our ordinary ways of choosing. I've already read about this in some depth, so please do not explain the idea to me; plan how I should teach it.

I need an instructional sequence, not the explanation itself. Please specify the following.

First, the order in which the components must be introduced, and for each step, what depends on the previous one. I want the session to build so that the central difficulty feels forced rather than imposed.

Second, one concrete carrying example that the whole session builds around, with a justification of what that example must have and what it must lack in order to do the work. Please do not use the stock example of deciding whether to have a child, and do not use the vampire example; choose something whose stakes a high-schooler can feel without being so personally heavy that it shuts down discussion.

Third, the misconceptions this audience is likely to bring or form, and the exact point in the sequence where each should be headed off. In particular, I expect many of them to think the sensible response is simply to gather more information and then decide; I want them to feel the force of why that response misfires for this kind of choice, and I want that to happen through the carrying example rather than through an abstract argument.

Fourth, one question I can ask in the last five minutes that would show me whether they have grasped the distinction rather than merely learned the catchphrase.

Finally, what I should explicitly set aside at this level and label as a simplification, so the forty-five minutes do not sprawl.

One more constraint. I do not want the takeaway to be the slogan that some choices are life-changing and you cannot know what you are getting into. I want the participants to leave able to separate three things: ordinary uncertainty; epistemic transformation, meaning that the only way to know what the outcome is like is to undergo it; and personal transformation, meaning that the person doing the evaluating after the choice may not share the standards of the person deciding now. Show me where in the sequence it becomes necessary to draw each distinction, and how the carrying example motivates each distinction at that point rather than as front-loaded terminology.
