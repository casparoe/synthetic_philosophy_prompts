In a long-running argument about whether fads, bubbles, and sudden consensus should be diagnosed as failures of individual rationality or as something that emerges from individually reasonable choices, I have been pressing the following line. Before I deploy it again, I want a validity check.

Set up: people decide in sequence whether to adopt or reject a technology. The technology is either good or bad. Each person observes a private signal about its quality and the full public history of earlier adoptions and rejections, and everyone knows that earlier decision-makers followed the same rule. Signals are informative but noisy. Adopting a good technology yields a gain, adopting a bad one yields an equal loss, and rejecting yields nothing.

(1) For any history of choices and any private signal, Bayes' rule yields a unique posterior probability that the technology is good.

(2) A rational agent adopts when this posterior is above the break-even threshold.

(3) There are histories after which the public evidence favors adoption strongly enough that any contrary private signal still leaves the posterior above that threshold, so adoption is optimal regardless of one's own signal. In the standard binary-signal version, where the private signal is one of two values, two early adoptions is such a history.

(4) Therefore each agent who joins the herd is doing the uniquely rational thing given their information. They are not ignoring evidence; they have updated on it, and it simply failed to move them off the action the public history already mandated.

(5) Hence an information cascade is just a sequence of individually rational Bayesian choices. Once one has said that each person did as well as their information allowed, there is nothing left to criticize: there is no distinct sense in which the outcome is suboptimal, and talk of herding mistakes an outside-view pattern for an inside-view failure.

Where, if anywhere, does this argument go wrong? Does (5) follow from (1) through (4), or is there a suppressed premise doing the work? If a step fails or an inference is illicit, say exactly which one, give the corrected version, and state whether the corrected argument can still support (5) or whether the conclusion must be weakened.
