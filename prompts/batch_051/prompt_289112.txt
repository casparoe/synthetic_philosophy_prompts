Consider the familiar equal-opportunity case for inheritance taxation: large financial bequests give their recipients an unearned competitive advantage in life, and taxing estates at death is one way to prevent family wealth from entrenching unequal life chances across generations. Now suppose that the main vehicle of dynastic privilege has shifted away from the bequest at death and toward transfers made during the donor's lifetime: privately funded schooling, university and early-career support, help with housing deposits, tax-advantaged gifts, and the social capital that affluent parents transmit to their children. These transfers are spent early, are comparatively difficult to observe, and may do more to convert money into durable advantage than an inheritance received in mid-life. Does the equal-opportunity argument for inheritance taxation survive this development? Does it need repair, or does it collapse into a broader argument about taxing wealth and privilege rather than estates? Identify which premise of the original argument is placed under pressure, and state a repaired version, if you think one exists, that preserves whatever was sound in the original. The focus should be on what happens to the argument, not merely on policy. Please write your answer in British English.
