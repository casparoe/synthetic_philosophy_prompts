I'm preparing a philosophy of law seminar and want to teach Sidney Lumet's 1957 film 12 Angry Men through one philosophical lens: the dispute between epistemicism and supervaluationism about vagueness. The standard the jury is applying—guilty beyond a reasonable doubt—is a vague threshold. Epistemicism would say that the standard has a sharp boundary though no juror is in a position to know where it lies; supervaluationism would say that borderline cases of the standard are neither determinately met nor determinately unmet, since the standard permits several equally admissible sharpenings. Read the film against these two options. Does the gradual accumulation of doubt read better as the jury working out a fact that was there all along (the epistemicist picture), or as the jury collectively constructing a precisification of reasonable doubt through its successive votes (the supervaluationist picture)? Does the unanimity rule make vivid the supervaluationist idea that truth for such a claim is truth on every admissible sharpening, or is that merely a loose analogy between different jurors' thresholds and different sharpenings of one standard? I need the reading to be honest in both directions: the theory should illuminate something non-obvious about how the film works, and you should say plainly where the film resists the theory—in particular, where it leans on uncertainty about what happened rather than vagueness in the standard of proof. No plot summary or quotation; I want the critical reading.
