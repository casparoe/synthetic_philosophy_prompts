So I'm trying to pin down whether disgust can ever count as a moral response, not just a sensory one. I keep coming back to Darwin, in The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals, chapter XI. He talks about how disgust starts from things offensive to taste, but then the word gets extended to anything that excites a similar feeling through smell, touch, or even sight. At one point he mentions that a native of Tierra del Fuego touched some cold preserved meat and showed disgust at its softness, and Darwin felt disgust at the food the man had touched. So a sensory reaction, maybe a bit of retching movement around the mouth, can attach to something because of contact with a person or category.

Here are the criteria I've been using for a moral emotion: (1) it's directed at a wrongdoing or violation, not just something unpleasant; (2) it involves an appraisal that could be wrong or right; (3) it motivates avoidance or punishment in ways that track the supposed violation; (4) it's at least partly shaped by cultural learning and concepts like purity or sacredness; and (5) it's not merely a reflexive reaction to bad smells or sights.

Now consider a borderline case. A person raised in a community with strong purity rules is served a meal that contains ingredients that are perfectly safe and taste mild, but that the community regards as violating a sacred prohibition. The person feels a wave of revulsion, a slight gagging sensation, and wants to leave the room. They later say they feel contaminated and that the meal was wrong to serve. But there is no harm, no breach of ordinary moral rules about hurting people, and they can't articulate why the prohibition has force beyond saying it's sacred. Does their disgust qualify as moral by the criteria? Or is it better classified as a non-moral disgust reaction that gets rationalized after the fact? If it fails, which criterion or criteria should I adjust?
