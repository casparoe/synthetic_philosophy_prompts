For the past several years I have helped lead a small adult-education hour at our church. The room is mixed: lifelong members, newcomers, and a few people who will read only the King James Version and treat other translations with suspicion. I have never studied theology or philosophy at a university; I read and think on my own time. Next month I have forty-five minutes to lead a session on a question that has been worrying me privately: can a translated text be inspired?

Two facts seem to pull in opposite directions. The earliest Christians treated the Septuagint — a Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures — as Scripture, and, as I understand it, the New Testament writers quote the Old Testament in forms that often follow that Greek rendering. That seems to show that translation, by itself, cannot destroy whatever it is that makes a text Scripture. But the King James-only members of the group are pressing something I do not want to wave away: a congregation's life is built around particular words — memorized, argued over, sung in the old phrasing — and saying "the meaning survives in any decent modern translation" is too cheap, because it treats the actual words as disposable. I want the hour to honor both observations without pretending they are the same problem.

I suspect the discussion will collapse unless the group first separates three ideas they currently run together: that a text is inspired, that a translation is faithful, and that a translation is authoritative for a community's worship and teaching. I want people to leave able to say what they think about whether a translation can be inspired, without reaching for one of those slogans.

Please plan the session for me. Give me the order in which these components should be introduced and what each later step depends on; one concrete case to carry the whole discussion (I have been considering the Septuagint, but tell me what a carrying case must have and what it must not drag in if it is to do this work); the misconceptions I should expect from this particular room — the telephone-game worry that every translation loses something essential, the opposite thought that if the original is inspired then every faithful translation is equally inspired, the older-is-purer instinct behind King James-onlyism, and any others — and the point in the sequence where each should be headed off; a check I can use at the end to tell whether people have understood the question rather than simply picked a side; and what I should openly leave out, such as technical manuscript history, so the hour does not sink under its own weight.
