Write a decision tree, for a handout in an upper-level ethics seminar, that sorts a reader into a position in the moral particularism–generalism debate from their answers about cases rather than from any label they prefer. Begin by laying out the space of answer combinations the questions allow, then locate the named positions within it. The tree must put exactly four questions to the reader, in whichever order you judge most diagnostic, anchored in these three cases: a promise extracted under threat; a lie told to a would-be murderer at the door; and a surgeon's incision that causes pain. The questions should concern (1) whether one and the same feature—that an act was promised, or that it causes pain—can count in favor in one context and against in another; (2) whether there are true, exceptionless moral principles; (3) whether, absent exceptionless principles, there are nonetheless true defeasible principles to the effect that some feature always counts for or against an act; and (4) whether moral competence is best explained by the internalized application of principles or by a trained sensitivity to what is salient in the particular case. The leaves must include at least absolute generalism, Rossian pro tanto generalism, Dancy-style particularism, and a pragmatic generalism on which principles are indispensable heuristics but not truth-makers. For each combination of answers that no live position occupies, add a one-sentence diagnosis—incoherent, unattractive, or merely underexplored—and flag any two positions the tree cannot separate on these four questions alone.
