I serve on the capital-planning committee of a regional water authority. At next Tuesday's meeting we are to adopt the evaluation framework for a proposed flood-protection and groundwater-recharge project whose main benefits will not arrive for thirty to eighty years. The staff analysis discounts future benefits and costs at a constant annual rate of four percent, our longstanding default. At that rate the project's central benefits -- avoided mortality and morbidity in major flood events, long-term aquifer recovery, reduced emergency-relief spending -- shrink to very little in present-value terms, and the project fails the benefit-cost test. Last night I spent a long time arguing with a friend about precisely this. He is an economist and defended discounting even distant effects, appealing to people's ordinary preference for benefits sooner rather than later, the likelihood that future generations will be richer, and the fact that the future is uncertain. I replied that none of these establishes what our default quietly assumes: a pure time preference that counts otherwise identical benefits as less valuable merely because they arrive later. We did not settle it.

Draft the short decision memo I will circulate to the committee before Tuesday. It should recommend a concrete course of action -- retain the four percent constant rate, adopt a lower constant rate, adopt a schedule of declining rates for distant effects, or remove certain distant benefits from the standard discounting framework -- and it should give the committee a defensible bottom line with a time frame for implementation. It should also state, in one paragraph, the strongest consideration in favor of the practice we would be changing, and answer it rather than dismiss it. Write the memo so the committee can act on it, not as a survey of the theoretical dispute itself.
