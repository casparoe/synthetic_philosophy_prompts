I've been trying to actually engage with the Mencius–Xunzi debate on human nature, but the standard framing ("is human nature good or bad?") seems like one of those questions where the words are doing too much work, and I want to step back before picking a side. I'd like you to write the desiderata a theory of human nature in that debate has to meet. Not a summary of positions, not a comparison essay—the spec first. What should any adequate account of xing be able to deliver, distinguish, and survive contact with, and why? Treat the passage below as the key pressure point, not decorative ancient prose.

Here is the passage, from the Mencius, Gaozi I, Legge translation:

"Mencius replied, 'Water indeed will flow indifferently to the east or west, but will it flow indifferently up or down? The tendency of man's nature to good is like the tendency of water to flow downwards. There are none but have this tendency to good, just as all water flows downwards. Now by striking water and causing it to leap up, you may make it go over your forehead, and, by damming and leading it you may force it up a hill - but are such movements according to the nature of water? It is the force applied which causes them. When men are made to do what is not good, their nature is dealt with in this way.'"

A few things I want the desiderata to handle, because they are what I keep getting stuck on:

- A theory of human nature needs to say what counts as evidence about a thing's nature. Mencius uses the water analogy, but water only flows downhill if it is in a gravitational field, and "down" is not an intrinsic property of water. So what is the analogous move for humans? Is it a ceteris paribus claim about what a person does absent interference, a claim about what the normal course of development converges to, a claim about first-person moral phenomenology, or something else? I want the requirement spelled out such that the water analogy does not just beg the question against Xunzi, but also does not make "nature" so behaviourist that Mencius's point evaporates.
- There is a distinction the debate often collapses: between what a thing is like in its raw baseline state and what it is like when well-cultivated or fully realised. Any adequate theory needs to say whether human nature is a set of initial dispositions that can be shaped into anything, or a normatively loaded potential that can be thwarted. If the latter, the theory owes us an account of how we identify thwarting without importing values under the table.
- The role of acquired habits and culture: Xunzi keeps pointing to models and ritual as necessary because the raw stuff is bad; Mencius points to sprouts that need tending. Both seem to accept that the raw stuff is not by itself sufficient for full virtue. A desideratum should therefore require a theory of xing to specify what the environment supplies relative to what the person brings, without making "environment" do all the work or none of it.
- Each side seems to use different normal cases: Mencius likes the child about to fall into a well; Xunzi likes the warped wood analogy. I want a meta-requirement: the theory has to say which range of human behaviour counts as the relevant data set and why. That means addressing whether ordinary bad behaviour is a distortion caused by bad institutions and poverty, or a baseline that institutions exist to correct, or something in between.

Then, if you think some subset of these requriements is jointly unsatisfiable—or that one requirement does the most to exclude one of the two positions—flag that. I suspect there is a load-bearing ambiguity in "human nature" between a descriptive statistical claim and a teleological claim, and it would be useful if the desiderata forced that apart. Do not spend most of the answer scoring Mencius and Xunzi against the list; I will do that part myself. Just tell me briefly where the list prunes them if you like.

One more specific thing: I would like you to treat "nature" in the Mencian passage as the sort of thing a machine-learning person would call a biased prior or an inductive bias, not as an essence with a built-in telos. If that reading is wrong or contested, the desiderata should say what it can and cannot explain.
