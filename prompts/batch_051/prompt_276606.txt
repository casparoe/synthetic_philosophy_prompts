I have been developing a distinction in the theory of decision under uncertainty and would like an honest assessment of it. The point of departure is the standard Ellsberg three-color problem. An urn contains 90 balls: 30 red, and 60 that are black or yellow in unknown proportion. A bet on red pays if a red ball is drawn; a bet on black pays if a black ball is drawn. Most people prefer the bet on red, although under the natural midpoint estimate of the black–yellow composition the two bets have the same probability of winning. The usual explanation is ambiguity aversion: an aversion to unknown probabilities as such.

I suspect this explanation is too crude, and that the operative property is not unknown probability but what I call adjudicability. A gamble is adjudicable, on my usage, when the event that settles the gamble also settles every uncertainty the gamble required its maker to take on. The bet on red is adjudicable: after the draw, you know whether you won, and the unresolved black–yellow composition turns out to have been irrelevant to your bet, so nothing the bet relied on remains unknown. The bet on black is not adjudicable: whatever ball is drawn, the size of the black set—the very quantity on which the bet turned—remains unknown. The gamble resolves the immediate uncertainty of the draw while leaving its own epistemic basis unresolved, so that even after the fact you cannot determine whether your bet was favorable.

My claim is that the Ellsberg pattern is driven largely by a preference for adjudicable gambles rather than by a blanket aversion to unknown probabilities. In the standard design the two properties are confounded, because the known-probability bet is adjudicable while the unknown-probability bet is not. To see that they can come apart, consider an urn with an unknown composition where the experimenter commits to revealing the composition after the draw regardless of the outcome. On the usual ex ante reading, the bet remains exactly as ambiguous as before, but it becomes adjudicable: you will eventually learn the probability on which the payoff turned. My account predicts materially weaker aversion to such a bet than to an otherwise identical bet whose composition stays hidden, whereas unadorned ambiguity aversion, evaluating the two bets solely by their ex ante ambiguity, predicts no difference.

I would like to know whether this distinction already exists in the decision-theoretic literature under some name I have missed, and whether it collapses into one of the familiar accounts—maxmin expected utility, smooth ambiguity, regret theory, or preferences over the timing of uncertainty resolution. Is the psychological claim coherent in its own right? Where does it break, and can you construct a case in which adjudicability and ambiguity pull apart in a way the account handles poorly?
