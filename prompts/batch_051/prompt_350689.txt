So I've been doing a lot of birding this spring — mostly crows and starlings around my neighborhood — but it got me rereading about Hempel's raven paradox, and now I'm supposed to write something for this tiny philosophy-and-poetry zine my friend runs. The zine's theme this month is confirmation, and I keep coming back to how wierd the paradox is: the hypothesis all ravens are black is logically equivalent to all non-black things are non-ravens, so the white sneaker on my porch ought to count as evidence about raven color. Hempel basically bites the bullet and says yes, it's a confirming instance, even when that feels wrong, and I want a poem that lives inside that uneasy feeling instead of just name-dropping the paradox.

Could you write a villanelle for me — the nineteen-line kind, five tercets and a closing quatrain — where the two refrains are those two forms of the hypothesis, set apart in italics? The point of using a villanelle is that the refrains keep coming back separately and then end up side by side in the last two lines, which is what the equivalence condition does to the reader anyway. Use the early tercets to build the ordinary confirmatory picture (a black raven, an instance doing its job), let the middle tercets bring in the white sneaker or a green apple as a confirming instance of the contrapositive, and save the final quatrain for the uneasy collision of the two refrains. It should be accurate enough that someone who knows Hempel wouldn't wince — so please dont dismiss the paradox by saying ravens are just defined as black; the logic has to be respected. American English spellings, please.
