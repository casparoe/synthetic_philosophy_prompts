I am a novelist at work on a book whose central preoccupation comes from the situationist critique of virtue ethics: the worry that the stable moral traits we ordinarily invoke — honesty, compassion, courage — are less robust than we suppose, and that small, seemingly superficial features of a situation do much of the explaining when a person acts well or badly. The novel will function as an argument by narrative against the assumption that character is a single global trait that travels with a person; I want it instead to render the possibility that what we call character is a patchwork of local, situation-sensitive dispositions that can come apart in surprising ways. To help me find the right register, I would like you to write a short story of roughly a thousand to fifteen hundred words that works as a precise literary test of that idea.

Make the protagonist a woman in her forties who has, with some justification, long thought of herself as honest and compassionate. Over the course of one ordinary afternoon, give her four small opportunities to act in keeping with that self-conception — returning a dropped wallet, letting someone go ahead in a queue, quietly correcting a mistake she has made at work, checking on an elderly neighbor — and vary only the fine-grained situational variables across those episodes: whether she is running late, whether she is being observed, whether acting would embarrass someone, whether it would require her to break a comfortable social script. Her conduct should vary noticeably from occasion to occasion, and its shifts should track those minor variables rather than any single change of heart. Then end with a scene in which someone close to her sees only one of these episodes and explains it in confident characterological terms, so that the reader experiences the gap between the situational machinery the narration has exposed and the trait explanation the character reaches for. Do not use the standard experimental set pieces of this research tradition, and do not make her a hypocrite, a villain, or a victim of coercion; the discomfort should come from how slight the causes look when they pivot her away from the person she takes herself to be.
