I cover books and culture for a living, and every time a city council or an arts council has a funding fight, the same two arguments get trotted out: reading fiction makes people more empathetic, so fund it; and people already buy the novels they value, so public money is just a subsidy for the tasteful. Both of those feel like they're smuggling in a full theory of what fiction is for without ever stating it. So before I write another one of these stories, I want the actual spec: what would a theory of the public value of fiction have to get right to be good enough to settle a funding question—not a private reading question, but a "should the state pay for this" question? I'm not asking you to rank the existing positions; I want the requirements list itself. The cases it has to handle (the brilliant novel almost no one reads, the mediocre bestseller, the propaganda novel that's beautifully written but it's politics are repugnant, the reissued dead author, the kid whose only exposure to narrative is a school library), the distinctions it can't collapse (the value of reading fiction vs. the value of writing it vs. the value of just having it exist, private benefit vs. public benefit, fiction as a genre vs. "literature" as a status category, ex post masterpieces vs. ex ante bets), and the empirical facts it can't contradict—like most published fiction gets read by almost no one, and the research on reading and empathy is way more contested than the op-eds let on. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and flag which ones are negotiable and which aren't. Then tell me which single requirement does the most work to kill off the popular positions—the empathy argument, the revealed-preference argument, the great-books-as-public-good argument. A table is fine.
