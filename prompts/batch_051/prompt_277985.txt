I work on the metaphysics of economic institutions, and I keep returning to a standard reading of Aristotle's account of money: that money holds by convention (nomos) rather than by nature (physis), and that it functions as a measure which makes goods of unlike kind commensurable for exchange. A contemporary descendant of that view claims that a unit of account is not like a thermometer measuring a pre-existing magnitude called value, but a collective device that works only while participants mutually expect it to keep working. Setting aside the exegetical question of what Aristotle himself meant, I want to see how far the empirical core of that descendant claim can be tested. Design a concrete study — historical, comparative, experimental, or some combination — that would distinguish a purely conventional unit of account from one anchored to something independent of collective belief, such as a commodity or a pre-existing scale of need. State clearly which pattern of results would favor each reading, and then say what a result favoring the conventionalist would still not establish, because part of the thesis is conceptual rather than empirical.
