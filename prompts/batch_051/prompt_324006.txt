I'm a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, and I've been drafting a short essay on the Kelly criterion and what is sometimes called geometric rationality. Below are my working notes. I would like comments on them, particularly on:

(a) accuracy — where the argument gets the mathematics or the standard positions wrong, or states them too strongly;
(b) whether the central distinction between growth-optimality and log-preference does real work rather than relabeling a familiar debate;
(c) whether the final note about fractional Kelly is sound.

Please keep your comments to at most 500 words.

Working title: Kelly is a property of the process, not a preference

1. The standard story: for repeated bets with multiplicative returns, the long-run growth rate of wealth is governed by expected log return, and the policy that maximizes this growth rate is the Kelly rule. Rationalist treatments often slide from this to a stronger conclusion, namely that log wealth is in some sense the correct utility function.

2. I want to separate three claims that these discussions run together. Growth-optimality: the Kelly stake maximizes the asymptotic exponential growth rate of capital under repeated proportional betting. Log-preference: log wealth is the correct utility function, full stop. Ruin-avoidance: Kelly never risks ruin. My thesis is that only growth-optimality deserves the name geometric rationality, and the other two do not follow from it.

3. Growth-optimality is a structural fact about multiplicative return processes plus a limiting argument. Under the usual ergodicity assumptions, the Kelly bettor's wealth comes to dominate the wealth of any other fixed-fraction policy almost surely. But this justification is instrumental and environment-relative: it tells you to care about log in order to capture long-run growth across many trials. It does not establish log as the correct utility for one-off gambles. A person could consistently be a long-run growth optimizer and still reject a single large bet with positive expected log value, because that single bet is not a draw from an indefinitely repeated multiplicative process. The slide from environmental optimality to utility function is, I think, made too quickly.

4. Ruin-avoidance is the weakest of the three. If ruin means literally reaching zero wealth, any fixed-fraction rule with fraction strictly between zero and one keeps wealth positive forever, so Kelly is not distinctive in this respect. If ruin means falling below some positive floor, the relevant calculation changes, and it is no longer obvious that full Kelly minimizes that probability. What Kelly distinctively controls is the asymptotic growth rate, not the probability of a finite-horizon catastrophe.

5. This bears on fractional Kelly. Rationalists often treat half-Kelly or quarter-Kelly as a psychological risk-management overlay on the normatively correct full-Kelly rule. But in the continuous-time limit, betting a fraction f of the Kelly stake is exactly the optimal policy for an investor with constant relative risk aversion γ = 1/f; half-Kelly is optimal for γ = 2, quarter-Kelly for γ = 4, and so on. If that holds, someone who says I accept full Kelly but bet half for reasons of temperament is better described as someone whose behavior reveals a power-utility risk aversion of 2, while their stated position still sounds log-flavored.

6. If the argument works, geometric rationality is a constraint on how a decision rule aggregates over time in ergodic multiplicative environments, not a theory of the utility function. The real disagreement between full-Kelly and fractional-Kelly bettors is an ordinary disagreement about risk aversion, and it cannot be settled by appeal to the long run once the horizon is finite or once the bettor cares about something other than asymptotic growth.
