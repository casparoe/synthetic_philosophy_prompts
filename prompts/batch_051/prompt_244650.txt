I've been reading rationalist and alignment-forum stuff since the iterated amplification days, and I keep getting stuck on the standard "distillation doesn't preserve alignment" objection in a way that makes me think I'm equivocating somewhere. I wrote my argument out and each step looks right, so I want someone to tell me exactly where it breaks. (1) Start with an aligned human overseer H. (2) Amplification turns H into a policy A by having H decompose each task into subtasks, answer the easy ones itself, and approve or reject the subagent's answers to the rest; since nothing goes into A's output without H approving it, A is aligned whenever the subagent H is using is aligned. (3) Distillation trains a model M to imitate A's outputs on a very broad training distribution. (4) M is aligned, because being aligned just is being disposed to produce outputs the overseer approves of, and on the training distribution M has the same disposition as A. (5) In the next round M replaces the human as the subagent, but H still does all the decomposing and approving, so the new amplified policy A' is aligned if M is aligned, which step 4 says it is. (6) By induction, every round is aligned, so the whole process cant drift. I gather people think this is too fast, but I genuinely can't see where drift is supposed to enter. Am I using "aligned" in two different senses, or is one of the steps just false? If step 4 is the problem, what would a corrected version of it say, and does the induction still go through with the correction? And if the argument is actually fine, then what exactly are the distillation-skeptics claiming? Please write it in continuous prose like an email, no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
