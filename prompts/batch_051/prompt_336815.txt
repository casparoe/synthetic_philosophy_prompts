Medical research ethics committees standardly hold that a randomized controlled trial may begin only when the arms are in a state of clinical equipoise: the relevant expert community is genuinely uncertain which arm will prove better, because randomizing a patient to a treatment believed to be inferior would violate the clinician's duty to the individual patient. Yet the same committees routinely approve designs that sit uneasily with that principle. They approve placebo-controlled trials for conditions that already have an established effective treatment, so long as the period without that treatment is short and the risk of lasting harm is judged low. They approve trials in which one arm is, on earlier and smaller trial evidence, likely to be worse for side effects but might still turn out better on an efficacy endpoint. And they approve add-on trials in which every participant receives standard treatment while some are randomized to an additional, untested intervention with no current evidence of benefit. In each case, participants are informed of the alternatives and consent to random assignment.

Reconstruct the implicit ethics of the clinician-investigator's role that would make this pattern coherent, or argue that it is not coherent. Be specific about what the practice strictly presupposes concerning (a) whether the duty to the individual patient is waivable by the patient's consent, (b) the moral difference between briefly withholding a proven treatment and knowingly randomizing someone to a treatment likely to be inferior, and (c) whose uncertainty matters: the expert community's, the individual clinician's, or the participant's own understanding. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and identify the points at which rival theories—a fiduciary duty to each patient, a utilitarian division of moral labor, a contractualist account of permissible risk imposition—would each license the same protocol. Finally, say what would have to change in trial design and ethics review if the governing principle were instead a strict rule that each participant be given the treatment that maximizes her own expected medical benefit, and what would change under a strict rule that no one may ever be randomized to an arm she would not choose under full information, regardless of consent.
