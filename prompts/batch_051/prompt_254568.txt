I'm a science journalist and I've ended up with a pretty strict rule about the pieces I write: a historical analogy can suggest a mechanism, but it can't support one. If someone tells me a policy is 'Munich 1938 all over again', that an intervention is 'another Vietnam', or that the US and China are sliding into a 'Thucydides trap', I treat the analogy as a pointer to a possible causal story, but I won't let it move my credence until the mechanism is stated in terms of the present case and I can say what would show the analogy has broken down. My editor says I'm being precious, and a historian friend says the whole point of an analogy is to smuggle in a mechanism you cant yet articulate, so by the time you can state it precisely you no longer need the analogy. He also says most historical analogies are meant to be partial, so demanding a disanalogy test is holding them to a standard nobody intends. Is my rule defensible, or am I dressing up a temperamental dislike of a genre of argument as a principle? And if it's too strict, what would the defensible version be?
