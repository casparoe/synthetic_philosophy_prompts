I'm on the governor's public health emergency review board and we've been asked to weigh in on a specific proposal before it goes out. There's an outbreak of a novel influenza-like illness in two counties, case counts doubling every ~3 days, but the legislature is out of session and won't reconvene for two weeks. The governor wants to issue an executive order imposing a 10-day lockdown of those counties and a de facto border check on roads leaving the metro area, using the state's general emergency management statute. The statute gives the governor broad powers during a declared emergency to take necessary measures to protect public health and safety, but it explicitly lists quarantine orders for specific individuals and premises, not whole regions; our state attorney general has said privately that the regional lockdown likely exceeds the text of the statute. The governor's general counsel is arguing that, whatever the text says, it's exactly the kind of situation where the executive has to act for public good even without clear legal authorization, and she keeps invoking Locke's chapter on prerogative in the Second Treatise. Locke's basic idea, as I understand it, is that the executive may act outside the law for the public good when the law cannot foresee all emergencies, and that the people judge afterward whether the action was justified. I can see the force of that, but I'm worried it becomes a blank check.

Draft a short decision memo for the board, recommending whether to approve the governor's proposed order, approve with conditions, or reject. It should engage seriously with the Lockean prerogative argument and also with the rule-of-law objection that legality is not just a technicality. The rationalist part of me wants a decision-procedure that doesn't just say "in emergencies we trust the executive," so if you can give criteria for when emergency powers cross into illegitimacy, or how to build in retrospective judgment, that would be useful. I need it for Wednesday; no need to cite anything, just make the reasoning usable for non-philosophers on the board.
