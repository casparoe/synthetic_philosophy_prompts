I'm a software engineer who runs a small platform team on a service where regressions are expensive. In our weekly one-hour incident reviews we keep hitting the same argument: someone puts forward a fix that demonstrably works — tests pass, the symptom disappears — but they can't say why it works beyond hand-waving. Half the team says a correct fix is a correct fix; the other half says an unexplained fix isn't really ours and will bite us later. I know philosophers have worked on the difference between knowing something and merely being right about it, and on why knowing is supposed to be worth more than just being right. I want that translated into a decision procedure my team can actually run to decide when a working-but-unexplained fix may ship and when it must be held. Give me the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops at accept or hold or reject, and where it hands off to a human because the question is no longer factual. Make it usable by a tired on-call engineer with no philosophy background, but keep it honest to whatever is actually settled about why knowledge is more valuable than true belief, rather than collapsing into trusting your gut.

Then apply the procedure to three cases: (1) a senior engineer ships a one-line config change that ends a production outage but can't say which property of the change mattered; (2) a junior engineer correctly identifies a race condition but can't articulate the interleaving that causes the failure; and (3) a fix whose author gives a confident explanation that later turns out to be false even though the fix itself holds. Include at least one case where the procedure forces a decision I'll be inclined to resist. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot decide, including where my demand for explanations stops being about reliability and becomes a values judgment. Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who study this, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
