I'm a retired physicist — spent my career around instruments and equations — and lately I've been reading philosophy of computation on the side, and I keep tripping over the same puzzle. People talk about the Church-Turing thesis as if its a law of nature, but also as if its a definition or a mathematical claim that can't be tested. The version I mean is roughly: anything that can be computed by any physically realizable device can also be computed by a Turing machine. That sure sounds empirical to me, because if someone built a device that computed something no Turing machine can, it would refute it. But I've also seen people say thats an empty formulation, because an effective procedure just means whatever a Turing machine can do.

Anyway, the thing I actually want help with is this: how would you test the physical version of the claim, not the mathematical one? Suppose an engineer shows up claiming to have a hypercomputer — say, a gadget that performs an infinite sequence of steps in a finite time by running each step twice as fast as the one before, or some analog system that is supposed to settle the halting problem for arbitrary programs. What concrete protocol would you put their device through? What outcome would count as a decisive result one way or the other, and what would a positive result actually show about the Church-Turing thesis? I'm especially interested in the residue: what part of the thesis would remain untested no matter how the machine behaves, and would we end up reinterpreting the thesis instead of simply giving it up?

Act as an expert in the philosophy of computation and think carefully — maybe start by brainstorming two or three candidate operationalizations before settling on one. I'd rather have a careful answer than a tidy one.
