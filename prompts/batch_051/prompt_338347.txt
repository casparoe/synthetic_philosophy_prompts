Act as a professional philosopher who works on aesthetics and the ethics of cultural production, not just someone with general opinions. I want you to think carefully about this one—brainstorm a bit before you settle on a verdict, and show your work.

I keep seeing a move in disputes about cultural appropriation in art, and I want it audited. The move goes like this: when, say, a novelist from a dominant culture lifts an origin story from a small Indigenous community without asking, or a painter adopts another culture's religious motifs and sells the canvases, that person is basically walking into somebody's house and walking out with the furniture. The conclusion is then treated as obvious: it's theft, and no amount of artistic transformation or "making it my own" changes that.

I'm not asking whether the conclusion is right so much as whether the analogy is actually doing the work people think it is. Give me the audit. In particular: what exactly maps onto the house, the owner, the furniture, the act of taking, and the loss to the original owner? Where is the mapping tight, and where does it snap? What does the theft frame quietly import—scarcity, a single identifiable owner, a right to exclude, deprivation of the original, maybe a clear ledger of who ended up with what—that might not survive contact with how cultural practices actually transmit? Then tell me whether the conclusion, that this kind of borrowing is wrong in just the way theft is wrong, survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether it was being carried by the analogy the whole time. If the analogy is bad for that purpose, say what a better one would be and what it would license instead. A table of correspondences is fine if it helps.
