I'm in a rational choice seminar and we've spent two whole classes on the self-torturer, and I think I've finally worked out an argument that shows rational preference doesn't have to be transitive. My study partner says it's step 2 that fails but he wont say exactly why, and I want to know whether the argument is actually valid or which precise step breaks.

Here's the version of the case we've been using. A person has a dial that goes from 0 to 1000 in one-step increments. Each step up adds a tiny bit of pain, small enough that at setting n you genuinely can't tell the difference between n and n+1. You get $10,000 for each step up, but the dial only goes up, never back down. At the end, at 1000, the person is in pretty awful pain and would pay a lot of the money to be back at 0; but at any given setting, taking the next step feels like free money.

(1) For any setting n, the self-torturer rationally prefers turning the dial up one step and gaining $10,000 to staying where he is, since the extra pain is imperceptible and the money is a real gain.
(2) Transitivity of rational preference says: if someone rationally prefers B to A and C to B, then they are rationally committed to preferring C to A. Applying this principle iteratively down the chain from 0 to 1000, the self-torturer is rationally committed to preferring setting 1000 with the full payout to setting 0 with nothing.
(3) But he isn't rationally committed to that: he would refuse a one-shot deal that jumped him straight from 0 to 1000 for the same total money, and at the end he would pay much of the money to undo the sequence.
(4) So the same sequence of rational preferences commits him to preferring the endpoint and not preferring the endpoint. Contradiction.
(5) Therefore rational preference isn't transitive, and the standard money-pump argument against intransitive preferences must be the thing that's broken.

Is this valid? If not, which step fails? If you think (2) is the culprit, give the strongest reading of (2) on which it is true, and say whether the argument still goes through on that reading. If you think the whole thing is valid, then tell me exactly where the money-pump argument goes wrong.

At the very end, after your explanation, give me a single line in exactly this form: \answer{...} where ... is either "valid", "step N fails" (with the number), or "valid on the strongest reading of step 2" if that's the resolution.
