Here is an argument I cannot find my way out of. (1) To deceive someone into believing p, the deceiver must believe that p is false and must intentionally bring it about that the other ends up believing p. (2) In self-deception, a person is both deceiver and deceived. (3) So a self-deceiver must at once believe that p is false (as deceiver) and come to believe that p (as deceived). (4) A single subject cannot simultaneously believe that p and believe that not-p, at least not while remaining minimally coherent. (5) Nor can the contradiction be escaped by saying she gradually biased her own evidence-gathering until the false belief took hold: intentionally biasing herself toward p requires knowing that she is steering herself away from the truth, and a process known to produce a false belief cannot yield sincere conviction in the person knowingly running it. (6) Therefore self-deception is impossible, and apparent cases must be redescribed as weakness of will, ordinary ignorance, or insincere self-report. I take that conclusion to be unacceptable, since self-deception seems real and common. Which premise should I give up, and what does each repair cost? In particular, spell out what is sacrificed by a view that denies self-deception must be intentional, and what is sacrificed by a view that partitions the mind into semi-autonomous structures — and say which option leaves us with the phenomenon we were trying to save.
