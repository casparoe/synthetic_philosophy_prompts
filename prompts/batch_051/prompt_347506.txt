Take the canonical Bayesian convergence setup as a baseline. Two observers, A and B, are about to watch tosses of a coin whose bias p is fixed but unknown. They share a Bernoulli likelihood: each toss is modeled as an independent flip with the same p. They begin with different smooth priors over p, and they both update by conditionalization after each toss. The standard result is that, as the sequence grows, their posterior distributions over p come to agree: the initial prior contributes less and less, until two initially different priors have washed out.

Now vary one part of the setup: what the observers get to see. Suppose the full sequence of n tosses is observed not by A and B but by a Summarizer. The Summarizer examines the whole sequence and then reports a selected summary of it. A and B see only the summary and the sample size n, never the raw tosses. Take as a running example a summary such as the length of the longest consecutive run of heads, or the proportion of heads in the consecutive block of 100 tosses with the highest proportion of heads.

Consider two versions. In the first, the selection rule is fixed and fully known to both observers: they know exactly which summary the Summarizer computes and reports, so the report is just a known deterministic function of the full data. In the second, the selection rule is unknown: A and B know only that the Summarizer saw all n tosses and selected a summary, but not the rule by which the summary was chosen.

I want an analysis of how the standard convergence conclusion changes across these versions. In the known-rule version, do A and B still converge to the same posterior over p, and does the prior still wash out—just more slowly, or not at all for some summaries? In the unknown-rule version, is Bayesian updating even well-defined on the information as stated, and if not, what extra model of the Summarizer must each observer adopt? Does the identity of the selection rule matter in the limit, or does disagreement about the Summarizer simply become a new kind of prior that refuses to wash out? Use the contrast between the two versions to state precisely what the old slogan that given enough shared evidence, priors wash out really needs in order to be true. Then apply the point briefly to the real-world version of the claim: scientists reading the same published literature, or citizens reading the same news, where what is shared is always a summary selected by a process whose rule is not fully known.

Please structure the reply in the following five sections, in this order and with roughly these lengths: (1) The baseline result, about 200 words; (2) The selected-summary setup and the two versions, about 250 words; (3) What survives and what breaks, version by version, about 450 words; (4) What an observer must add to recover convergence, about 250 words; (5) Verdict on the slogan and its real-world import, about 150 words.
