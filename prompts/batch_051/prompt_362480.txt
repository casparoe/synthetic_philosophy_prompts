Alright, put on your social-choice-theorist hat—I want someone who'll actually do the sums rather than hand-wave. A five-person grant comittee has to pick one of three bids, A, B and C. One member (the chair) proposes this procedure. Everyone submits a strict ranking of the three bids. After the rankings are sealed, the chair draws one ballot at random from a bag and destroys it unread. The remaining four ballots are scored by Borda count (3 for top, 2 for middle, 1 for bottom). If there is a tie for top Borda score, it is broken by the chair's ballot if that ballot survived; if the chair's ballot was destroyed, the tie is broken by a fair coin. The chair reckons this is a neat trick: because the rule is now random, it escapes the Gibbard–Satterthwaite impossibility, and because no one knows whether their ballot will count, everyone may as well just rank honestly.

I'm sceptical. Take me through how this would actually play out. Start by stating the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem in one or two sentences, and say exactly what 'strategy-proof' should mean once a rule is randomised (expected utility, not certainty about the winner). Then work through two or three small preference profiles to check whether a voter can still gain from misreporting under this scheme; show which manipulations survive the random deletion and which, if any, genuinely disappear relative to plain Borda count. Does the random deletion change the set of possible winners at all, or only the expected influence each voter has? Comment on the chair's tie-break: is it a dictator in disguise, and does it give the chair an extra incentive to vote tactically themselves? Finally, is there a way to fix the scheme by changing what happens to the deleted ballot or the tie-break, or is the only clean strategy-proof random rule the usual random dictatorship (pick a voter at random and implement their top choice)? Don't just tell me the mechanism is manipulable—show me the profiles and the arithmetic.
