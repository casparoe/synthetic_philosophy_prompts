I'm a retired zoologist — spent most of my career on marine invertebrates — and I've only recently gotten into philosophy of biology, so forgive me if I use terms loosely. Something keeps nagging me. Field guides treat a Portuguese man o' war as a single animal even though it's a colony of zooids; lichens get treated as single organisms even though they're a fungus plus an alga or cyanobacterium. But nobody treats a cow together with its rumen microbes as one organism, and nobody calls a termite and its gut flagellates a single animal, even though the termite would starve without them and the cow couldn't digest cellulose without its microbial population either. So we have an asymmetry: some tight collectives get counted as one individual, and other, seemingly equally tight collectives get counted as an individual plus its passengers. I realize the man o' war is a same-genome colony while the lichen is a cross-species partnership, so maybe two different principles are doing the work — but in that case I want to know why both principles earn the label "one organism" while the cow and termite collectives, which also look tightly integrated, don't.

I'm not asking for the definitions of "superorganism" or "holobiont" — I know those words exist. I want to know whether the asymmetric usage tracks any real biological or philosophical line, or whether it's mostly historical accident and convenience. Is there a principle that puts the man o' war and the lichen on one side and the cow-plus-rumen-microbes and termite-plus-gut-flagellates on the other? If you defend such a principle, test it against the case of an aphid and the obligate bacteria inside its cells, or a leafcutter ant colony and its fungus garden, because those seem to sit right on whatever line you'd draw. And I'd like you to say (a) how confident you are in what you claim and (b) which point your least sure about.
