I run a monthly evening reading group at my company for software engineers who want to think about self-reference and formal systems. The group is about a dozen people, mostly without a philosophy background, and we have 90 minutes. I'd like a hands-on activity that makes the group actually run into the problem behind the Liar paradox instead of hearing it described: that a set of sentences referring to one another's truth values can look perfectly well-formed and yet permit no consistent assignment of true and false.

I picture the activity as a deck of labeled cards, each carrying a sentence that refers to other cards by label: a few cards that assert plainly checkable facts about the room as base cases, a card that says of itself that it is false, a card that says of itself that it is true, and loops of various lengths in which each card says the next is false. The group's task is to tag every card TRUE or FALSE, subject to the constraint that each card's tag must agree with what its sentence says. I'd like them to discover for themselves, before anything is explained, which configurations admit no consistent tagging, which are forced once the base facts are fixed, and which are consistent in more than one way — and to feel the difference between odd and even loops.

Spell out the rules, the materials, and how long each phase should take. Then give me a debrief that maps what happened to the genuine philosophical fork: keep truth two-valued and forbid this kind of self-reference, allow sentences that are neither true nor false, or allow sentences that are both. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong when I run this with engineers rather than philosophy students, and which part of the philosophical issue the activity cannot get them to feel and I will have to supply in words.
