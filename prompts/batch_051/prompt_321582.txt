I am writing something on whether the value of wild nature is intrinsic, relational, or instrumental, and I keep finding that the word "wild" does two jobs at once: it describes how independent a place is from human intention, and it commends the place as worth protecting. I would like a working definition of "wild" (or wildness) that an ecologist, a restoration practitioner, and a policy officer could use without talking past one another. Please test your definition against these cases: (1) a heavily visited summit in a national park; (2) a grouse moor kept open by rotational burning; (3) a derelict gasworks covered in spontaneous vegetation; (4) a "rewilded" former farm where introduced cattle and horses act as proxies for extinct herbivores and are culled to control their numbers; (5) a river restored by re-meandering, with flows controlled by sluices and plants reintroduced from nursery stock; (6) an exclusion zone around a decommissioned nuclear plant where people are barred and large mammals thrive; and (7) a suburban garden planted only with native species and kept free of weeds. Some of these I take to be clearly wild, some clearly not, and some I genuinely cannot settle. For each case, say what your definition yields and why; if it returns a verdict I would reject as clearly wrong, either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict. I would also like to know whether the descriptive sense ("independent of human intention") and the evaluative sense ("worth protecting for its own sake rather than for what it does for us") can be separated, and which of the cases move between the two senses. If the request rests on a confused premise, for example if "wild" simply cannot do one of these jobs, say so plainly rather than forcing a definition to fit. Please write the answer in British English.
