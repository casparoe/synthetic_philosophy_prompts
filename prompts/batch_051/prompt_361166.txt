A national climate service proposes the following rule for its public projections. It will issue a quantitative regional projection only when at least two-thirds of the models in its current ensemble agree on the sign of the change in the target variable, such as mean precipitation or days above 40°C, over the next thirty years. When the threshold is not met, it will publish neither a central estimate nor a probability distribution, only a statement that the sign of the change is unresolved. In addition, any model that lands in the minority on more than half of the published sign votes over a rolling ten-year window has its weight in the next ensemble halved. Analyze this as a mechanism rather than a moral position: what does it incentivize among the groups that build the models, among users such as water managers and insurers who need a number to plan against, and among the service's own scientists? Where is it likely to be gamed first, and does the rule ultimately select for better climate models or for models that track the consensus? Finally, say whether the failure modes can be fixed without abandoning the aim of speaking only when the models are robust.
