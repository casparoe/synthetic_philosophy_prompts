A friend in my political philosophy group keeps pressing this and I can't find the exit. It's about Mill's harm principle.

(1) The harm principle says the only legitimate ground for restricting anything someone says is preventing harm to others.

(2) Speech can harm people without touching them: a doxing campaign that gets someone swatted, medical misinformation that makes people skip treatment, sustained harassment that drives a classmate to a breakdown.

(3) So the harm principle has to allow restricting those cases.

(4) Now consider someone who loudly and constantly mocks another student's religion in the dining hall, or puts up a poster the student finds deeply violating. The only difference between these and the harassment case is that the harm is less severe and less direct.

(5) But severity and directness are differences of degree, and a principle has to draw a line of kind if it's going to do principled work.

(6) So either the principle is toothless — if psychological harm doesn't count, then a harassment campaign whose only concrete damage is a documented psychiatric injury can't be restricted, because nothing physical happened — or it swallows free speech, since almost anything can cause someone real distress.

(7) Either way, the harm principle isn't a workable limit on speech.

I refuse the conclusion but I cant find the step doing the damage. Which premise or step should I reject, and what does giving it up cost? I'd most like to keep (1) and still have harassment law come out fine; if that costs me an account of what 'harm' means, I want to know how big a bill that is.
