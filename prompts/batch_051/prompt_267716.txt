Rousseau's core argument for the state runs something like this: in a legitimate polity we obey laws that express the general will — what each citizen would will in common once they set aside their purely private interest — and that's what makes even coercive enforcement of those laws compatible with freedom rather than an attack on it. The argument leanes pretty hard on a specific picture of how opinion gets formed: citizens get the facts, deliberate together, and are kept from hardening into partial groups, because once factions form each one mistakes its sectional interest for the interest of the whole. Now change one thing: you're in a polity where nearly all political information reaches people through personalized algorithmic feeds, so there's no common factual world, and microtargeted mobilization assembles de facto factions faster than any law could suppress them. Does the general-will argument survive this change, need repair, or is it dead?
