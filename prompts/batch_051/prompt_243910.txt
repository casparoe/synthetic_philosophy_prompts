I have been teaching a graduate seminar on language death and language policy, and I find that the revival of Hebrew is a case my students keep using to argue past one another. Let me set it up carefully. By the late nineteenth century, Hebrew had not been a spoken vernacular for well over a millennium, surviving almost entirely in liturgy, study, and writing. Over roughly a century, through a movement conventionally associated with Eliezer Ben-Yehuda and the Zionist project, it became the native language of millions and the dominant public language of the State of Israel. But this success coincided with, and in part drove, the marginalization of several living Jewish languages — Yiddish above all, but also Ladino, Judeo-Arabic, and others — which were actively discouraged as the new Hebrew-speaking society consolidated.

I would like you to analyze this episode through the lens of the thesis that linguistic diversity is valuable and that language death is a loss. Begin by distinguishing the main versions of that thesis: the instrumental argument that languages encode distinctive knowledge, conceptual resources, and forms of expression; the identity argument that a language can be a repository of a community's history and self-understanding; and the ecological analogy that treats linguistic diversity as valuable in something like the way biodiversity is. Then run each version against the Hebrew case, saying clearly where the episode supports the thesis, where it strains it, and where the thesis is simply silent. In particular, what should a proponent of the value of linguistic diversity say about the fact that one celebrated reversal of language death was also, from the standpoint of the Jewish linguistic landscape, a large reduction in diversity? Does the case suggest that the value actually at stake was self-determination, national identity, or ordinary welfare rather than diversity as such? I want a disciplined case analysis rather than a historical narrative: separate what the episode can test from what it cannot, and be willing to say that the diversity frame misdescribes the case if that is where the analysis leads.
