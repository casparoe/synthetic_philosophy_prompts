I'm a graduate student in the philosophy of mathematics, and for the past few weeks I've been arguing with a friend in theoretical computer science about whether a sufficiently powerful program could do mathematics the way a human mathematician does. My friend keeps pressing the computationalist line, and I keep reaching for the anti-mechanist argument in the tradition of Lucas and Penrose: that Gödel's incompleteness theorems show no formal system can capture what a human mathematician can see. The argument has forced me to write out, for the first time, the commitments I actually hold in this area. I was planning to circulate a one-page statement of where I stand before our department's work-in-progress seminar, but now I suspect the list does not hang together, and I would like an audit before I embarrass myself in front of the seminar.

Here are the commitments, as precisely as I can state them:

1. Physicalism about the mathematical mind: my mathematical thinking is entirely realized in the physical activity of my brain.
2. Computational sufficiency: in principle, a Turing machine could simulate my brain's information processing well enough to produce the same mathematical judgments, proofs, and pronouncements I produce, without any non-computable ingredient.
3. Gödelian transparency: for any consistent formal system that is strong enough for arithmetic and that I can explicitly specify, I can reason about that system from the outside and come to see both that its Gödel sentence is true and that the system is consistent.
4. The anti-mechanist lesson: since I can out-Gödel any formal system proposed as a candidate for capturing me, no single formal system captures my mathematical competence, and my mathematical understanding is not algorithmically characterizable.
5. Fallibilism about mathematical knowledge: any mathematical claim I accept, including a claim that some formal system is consistent, could in principle turn out to be wrong; absolute mathematical certainty is unavailable to me.
6. Semantic determinacy about arithmetic: arithmetic has a determinate intended interpretation, the standard natural numbers, and every sentence of the language of arithmetic has a definite truth value on that interpretation; accordingly, a Gödel sentence of a consistent system is straightforwardly true rather than indeterminate.

I would like a careful audit of this set. Work through the pairs and tell me which commitments pull against each other, how severely, and what the cheapest repair would be in each case. Are there any pairs that look flagrantly inconsistent but are only apparently in tension once the terms are clarified? And if I were willing to revise exactly one of the six commitments, which single revision would do the most to make the set coherent, and what would it cost me? I am not attached to any particular one of these in advance, so tell me plainly where the damage is.
