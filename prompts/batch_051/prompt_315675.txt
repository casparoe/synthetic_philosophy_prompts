In a seminar on early Christian ethics, we have been wrestling with the apparent contradiction between Paul and the Epistle of James on justification. Our instructor offered a framing I had not encountered before. On this framing, the works that James insists cannot be separated from a living faith are not the observances of the law that Paul criticizes — the boundary-marking practices of circumcision, food regulations, and Sabbath keeping — but the concrete acts of mercy James himself names: looking after orphans and widows in their distress, feeding the hungry, clothing the ill-clad. The proposal, then, is that Paul and James are not flatly contradicting each other. Paul denies that law-observance secures one's standing before God; James denies that correct belief can float free of practical care for the vulnerable. The apparent clash dissolves because the same word is being aimed at two different targets.

I find this exegesis attractive, but I want to know what I would be committing myself to if I adopted it wholesale. Please lay out the most important implications of this reading — theological, ethical, and hermeneutical — for how one should understand the relation between faith and works, and for how one should read the two letters side by side. Answer as a numbered list with exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
