I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a long feature about the growing industry of charter cities, private zones, and seasteading ventures. The practice I want to understand is this: several states now license or propose to license privately governed zones within their territory—special economic zones, common-law enclaves, autonomous development regions—while a smaller seasteading movement tries to build floating platforms beyond territorial waters. In the Honduran ZEDE model, for example, private developers were given authority to run their own court systems, security arrangements, and tax regimes within a host state. Supporters describe such projects as a way of opting out of the state through consent and competition; critics call them enclaves that replace citizenship with customer status. What I want from philosophers is not a verdict on the politics, but something more basic: what view of political obligation and state legitimacy does this practice presuppose? Does it commit its proponents to the view that the state's authority is legitimate only if its subjects consent, or only if it performs well enough, or only if exit is possible? Do the different parts—private arbitration, the host state's grant of autonomy, the marketing of zones to foreign investors—presuppose consistent things, or is there a hidden conflict between contract-based authority and territorial sovereignty? And I am struck by an apparent inconsistency: these projects are advertised as escapes from politics, but they depend at every point on a sovereign act that creates and protects them. Is that a real conceptual problem, or just a rhetorical one? Finally, what would have to change in the practice if the presupposed view were replaced by a rival one—for instance, a view on which political membership is not a contract and exit is not a legitimate way to dissolve communal obligations—and where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, so that several different theories of legitimacy would license the same institutional design?
