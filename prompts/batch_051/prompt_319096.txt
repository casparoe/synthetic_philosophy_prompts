I am a novelist writing a book in which four relatives — an Anglican vicar in England, a German municipal clerk who pays church tax, a French schoolteacher shaped by laïcité, and an American civil-liberties attorney — spend a summer disputing how the state should relate to religion. I want each character's stance to be a coherent position rather than a national cliché, so I need a decision tree that sorts church-state views by what the state may actually do. Build the tree from the diagnostic questions on which the classic settlements genuinely divide, not from the country names. Aim for roughly seven branch questions and no more than ten leaves. Split each branch on a concrete claim: whether the state may maintain or fund a particular church; whether it may collect a compulsory membership tax for religious bodies and remit the proceeds; whether clergy may act as civil registrars; whether religious instruction or insignia may appear in state schools; whether the state may promote a shared civic ritual with religious trappings. The leaves should include at least an established church with toleration, church-tax pluralism, laïcité, strict separationism, accommodationist neutrality, and a non-confessional civil-religion view if you judge it coherent enough to be a leaf. Where a combination of answers has no real defender, say so and diagnose whether it is empty because the combination is incoherent, unstable, or merely historically unexplored. Give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and flag any two positions the tree cannot separate along with the further question that would sever them.
