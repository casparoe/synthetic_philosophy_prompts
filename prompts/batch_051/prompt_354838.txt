I'm a PhD student in philosophical logic, and I've been trying to decide whether the standard argument for dialetheism from the semantic paradoxes is still live. As I understand it, the argument runs roughly this way. Naive semantic principles—say, that a sentence is true just in case what it says is the case—seem inseparable from ordinary talk about truth and denotation. The liar and related paradoxes show that classical logic cannot keep those principles without contradiction and therefore, via explosion, triviality. The dialetheist's challenge is that every consistent escape route rejects something with at least as strong a claim on us as the principle the dialetheist relinquishes, namely the principle that no contradiction is ever true. One can restrict self-reference, but that looks arbitrary; one can weaken the naive semantic principles, but that abandons part of semantic competence; or one can accept that some contradictions are true and adopt a paraconsistent logic in which contradictions do not explode. The dialetheist concludes that this last option is the least-cost response, not a fringe one.

The development that worries me is the family of substructural responses that has since been worked out. These theories keep the naive semantic principles intact and keep the usual truth-functional behavior of the connectives, but they reject structural rules of inference rather than any principle specifically about negation: most importantly, contraction (the rule that lets an argument use a single assumption twice) or cut (the rule that lets one chain a proof through an intermediate lemma). They are consistent, they handle the liar, and they require no true contradictions—but at the price of giving up inference patterns that even many nonclassical logicians treat as fixed.

My question is whether the familiar dialetheist argument survives this development. Does it still establish that accepting true contradictions is the least-cost response, or has the availability of a consistent competitor that retains naive semantics changed the comparison enough that the argument must be repaired or is simply no longer sound? Please take a definite position rather than laying out the options: say clearly whether the argument survives, needs repair, or is dead, identify which premise of the argument the substructural development bears on, and say whether and in what form the dialetheist could plausibly restore it.
