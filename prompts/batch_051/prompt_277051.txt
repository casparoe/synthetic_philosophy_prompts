I am a retired biochemist who came to philosophy late; I now convene a small discussion group of scientists and physicians, none of us formally trained in philosophy, who are working through the philosophy of punishment. Two members have crystallized into opposing camps, and rather than let them continue trading talking points, I want to force the exchange into the form of an adversarial collaboration. Please draft the joint statement they would sign.

The first camp is a desert-based retributivist of this precise sort: an offender's culpable wrongdoing is a necessary condition of justified punishment, and desert is the positive ground of punishment, not merely a ceiling on its severity. The second is a harm-reduction consequentialist: punishment is justified when and because its expected effects reduce the future frequency or seriousness of wrongdoing through deterrence, incapacitation, or reform. This consequentialist accepts two side constraints, considered consistent with that position: never punishing a person known to be innocent, and never imposing a sentence more severe than the wrong committed—though this proportionality limit is grounded in the long-run consequences of maintaining a system that respects it, not in desert.

Format the document as follows. First, a numbered list of the strongest specific claims both parties agree on, including cases they would treat identically and practices both would refuse; the list should contain no merely verbal or banal points, and each item should cost at least one party something. Second, an exact statement of the remaining disagreement, with each position phrased in the wording its own side would accept and with each side's denial of the other's wording made explicit. Third, a disagreements table of cruxes: the sub-questions whose answers would move at least one side. For each crux, specify the case or argument both parties accept as genuinely bearing on it, and record in advance each side's prediction of what that test will show. Include at least three cruxes, one of which must be whether the proportionality ceiling admits a consequentialist grounding that does not covertly import the very desert judgment it was meant to replace; each side must record in advance what it predicts such a fully specified grounding will look like. Where an apparent disagreement is really a dispute about words—for instance, about whether regulatory fines, civil penalties, or institutional detentions count as punishment at all—say so explicitly, and move any substantive issue underneath it into the disagreements table.

Do not resolve the debate, do not declare a winner, and do not recommend readings. Where a crux turns on an empirical matter, such as the marginal deterrent effect of a sentencing practice, write the unresolved empirical question into the crux rather than guessing its answer.
