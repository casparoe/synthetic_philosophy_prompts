I am giving a departmental colloquium next month defending the claim that the supererogatory — acts that are morally good but not required, such as risking serious harm to save a stranger — is not a stable deontic category. My argument has three moves. First, the standard ways of drawing the line between the required and the supererogatory, whether by expected cost, sacrifice, reasonable expectations, or the strength of blame we direct at someone who omits the act, either settle somewhere arbitrary or turn out to be tracking how blameworthy the omission is rather than whether the act was required. Second, if the threshold is set by what a decent agent can be expected to do, then what counts as supererogatory is a fact about social expectation, not about the act itself. Third, the work done by the supererogatory category is better done by two independent axes: how good the act is, and how blameworthy or excused its omission is.

The audience is a philosophy department with a strong ethics group: roughly half broadly Kantian deontologists who take duty to be fixed by what a rational agent can consistently will, a third consequentialists who will say the supererogatory is just loose talk about acts that produce more good, and the rest virtue ethicists who will suspect I have replaced ethics with a ledger. They are exacting and unsentimental, and they will not grant me anything about the nature of requirement for free.
