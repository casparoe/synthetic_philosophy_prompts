I've been working through the modal version of Kurt Gödel's argument about rotating universes, and I find I now hold the position myself. The view is this: because the general theory of relativity admits cosmological solutions describing rotating matter distributions in which spacetime contains closed timelike curves — timelike paths along which an observer could in principle travel into their own past — the passage of time cannot be an objective feature of the world. Nothing in reality picks out a global 'now' that advances; temporal becoming is ideal, or at best a local matter of convenience. My core premise is that whether a world possesses an objective cosmic time order ought not to hinge on a contingent and quantitative fact such as how much the matter of the universe happens to rotate, yet on the relativistic picture it does, and that is enough to demote temporal passage from a genuine feature of reality to an appearance.

What would count against this? I want the evidence and arguments that should move me, sorted by how decisive they would be, not a restatement of the view. Which formal results would be fatal — for example, a theorem showing that this class of solutions is somehow physically excluded, or a demonstration that closed timelike curves do not by themselves threaten a tensed or moving-now conception of time? Which objections are merely awkward, such as the observation that the actual universe appears to rotate very little or not at all? What does the position predict or explain that its rivals — presentism, a moving-now view, or a robust eternalism — do not? I would also like an honest assessment of whether I have qualified the view into unfalsifiability by saying, for instance, that any apparently objective requirement on time can be absorbed into local physics, and whether that degree of insulation is or is not a problem for a metaphysical view of this kind. If you had to identify the single load-bearing premise whose failure would bring the whole argument down, what would it be?
