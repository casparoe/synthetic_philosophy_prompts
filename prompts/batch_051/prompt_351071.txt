I'm giving a seminar next month in a comparative political economy workshop, and I need to prepare for the question period. My thesis is that the Nordic model is not an exportable package. Its high-tax, high-transfer equilibrium rests on a pre-existing stock of generalized trust and low-corruption administration that predates the postwar welfare state, and attempting to import the policy bundle into a low-trust society tends to produce a fiscal trap: compliance and tax capacity erode faster than the policies can generate the trust that would eventually sustain them. I am not claiming the Nordic model is undesirable, or that trust is permanently fixed, or that no policy can ever build trust. I am claiming the causal order is trust-before-transfers, that the bundle is closer to a cultivar than to a kit, and that below some trust threshold the same policies are self-defeating in ways that make the threshold hard to cross from below.

My argument runs roughly as follows. First, the generalized trust and administrative integrity that matter in the Nordic countries predate the expansion of the welfare state and have deeper roots in state-building, religious and ethnic homogeneity, and local associational life. Second, a high-tax, high-transfer state requires very high voluntary compliance and a low tolerance for free-riding, both of which are outputs of trust rather than inputs to it. Third, in a low-trust setting the same bundle invites evasion, informality, and distributive resentment, which shrinks the tax base and forces either benefit cuts or coercive enforcement, both of which damage trust further. Fourth, the export argument therefore mistakes a coincident equilibrium for a lever: welfare-institutions-build-trust mechanisms that operate at the margin in already high-trust societies are weak evidence about what happens far from that equilibrium.

The audience is a joint political-economy and economic-sociology workshop. Several members work on welfare-state feedback effects and believe universal programs and active labor-market policy can raise trust and social capital over the long run; they will object that I have turned a two-way loop into a one-way arrow. One comparativist has written on flexicurity as an exportable design and will press me on Denmark specifically. One development economist studies tax morale in low-capacity states and will be skeptical of any story that leans heavily on culture rather than enforcement capacity. The group is broadly sympathetic to Nordic social democracy, so the objections will be friendly in tone but empirical in force, and they will care most about endogeneity, the measurement of trust, and whether my account leaves any policy path out of a low-trust trap.

Give me a prep sheet, not a defense. Order the objections roughly by how likely and how damaging they are, phrase each one the way a member of this audience would actually put it, and for each give a reply I can deliver in about two minutes plus a note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me what I should concede up front and what I should hold firm on. Include the question I should be hoping nobody asks, and say why it would be hard to answer.
