A friend walked me through this argument and I cant find the exit. I don't have any background in philosophy, so I might just be confusing myself, but here's the argument as best I can reconstruct it.

(1) Suppose I'm the only person who can help, and I have to choose between saving one stranger or saving five strangers; I can't save both.
(2) Each of the six people stands to lose exactly one thing: the rest of their life. Nobody can lose more than one life, and none of the five is somehow in a position to lose five lives.
(3) So the one person's claim on me looks exactly as strong as any given one of the five people's claims. Each claim is for the same thing: a whole life.
(4) But then the five don't, together, have a stronger claim on me than the one has. Claims don't add up across separate people: five equal claims for one life each are still five separate one-life claims, not a single five-life claim.
(5) So there isn't any more reason for me to save the five than the one, and saving the five just because there are more of them would be treating the one as less important than each of the others. The fair thing is to give each side an equal chance, say by flipping a coin.
(6) But the reasoning doesn't stop at the rescue case. It would also mean a doctor with one dose of medicine should flip a coin between one patient and a ward of a hundred, and that an emergency planner should treat a threat to a thousand people and a threat to one person as morally the same size. That seems to give up on the whole idea of triage.
(7) So something must have gone wrong somewhere before the triage conclusions, but I can't pin down which step it is.

My friend says the lesson is just that numbers don't count, full stop, and the triage stuff is what you swallow if you're consistent. I'd rather keep the idea that in the original rescue case the five outrank the one, but I want to know what keeping that actually costs me. Which step do I reject, and what happens to the rest of the argument once I do? Also, when you answer, could you separate which parts of this are settled from which parts are genuinely contested among people who actually work on this stuff, instead of making a contested position sound like the consensus?
