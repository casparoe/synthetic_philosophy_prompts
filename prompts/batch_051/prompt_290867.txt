I am an undergraduate in an upper-level seminar called The Ethics of Simulated Worlds, and we have spent the past few weeks working through a disagreement about whether virtual objects and virtual harms are real. One side argues that they are not real in any morally serious sense: nothing that happens inside a simulation touches a physical body, the goods that are lost or stolen are made of code, and the participants consented to enter a space where actions are play. The other side replies that this is a dodge, because a humiliating raid, a destroyed guild, or the theft of a rare item can be a real event in someone's biography, and calling something a game does not by itself settle whether anyone was wronged. I keep circling back to a case in which the metaphysics and the experience pull apart, and I think Orson Scott Card's Ender's Game stages it more sharply than most of what we have read.

I would like to read the novel through the lens of that debate. The central reversal is that Ender believes his final command exercise is a simulation, while the battles he directs are real, and he destroys an entire species under the impression that he is only playing. Surrounding that reversal are the places where the book treats game spaces as psychologically serious events, such as the battle room and the mind game he plays at Battle School, along with the anonymous online personae used to shape real-world politics. The novel's aftermath is likewise structured around Ender's conviction that what he did was a real harm demanding something from him. I do not want an exercise in matching the book to the philosophy, though. Does the novel actually take a position on when a harm counts as real? Which scenes would you set side by side to show the limits of the thought that pretending strips an action of moral weight, and where does the reading break down because Ender's situation is deception rather than simulation? Finally, what would you say the novel adds to the philosophical debate that the arguments alone tend to miss?
