I keep coming back to a small puzzle I first met in the sentence It's raining, but I don't believe it. There is no contradiction in it: the rain and my state of mind are two different facts, so both halves could perfectly well be true at once. And yet anyone who actually said it, just like that, would sound as though they had lost track of their own mind. Lately I have been noticing how much wellness writing and self-help culture encourage people to voice their inner splits aloud — to "own" their contradictions, speak their doubts, and treat every gap between the world and their feelings as something brave to confess. It seems to me that if you followed that ethic honestly, you would end up teaching people to say things like My marriage is fine, but I don't believe it is, and to regard that as the most authentic sentence they could produce.

I would like you to write a piece of deadpan satire in that spirit: a mock book proposal for a self-help method called the Moore Method, which presents the sentence It's raining, but I don't believe it as the gold standard of honesty and instructs readers to append but I don't believe it to their sincere claims. Please keep it to roughly 700 or 800 words and format it as a real proposal: a title and subtitle, a paragraph selling the promise, three chapter summaries, and two invented endorsements. The irony should remain completely straight-faced, and it should stay legible without any winking or explicit explanation.

The comedy should come from the actual logic of the puzzle rather than from a straw man. Use genuine variations of the sentence — how it behaves in the first person present tense as against the past tense or the third person, or what happens when both halves are forced into a single utterance — and let the absurdity emerge from the examples. A reader who has followed this puzzle before should feel that you understand exactly what makes the sentence odd, and a reader who has never heard of it should still find the proposal funny and mildly disturbing.
