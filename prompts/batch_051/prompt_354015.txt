I write fiction. Research for a novel about how families and nations dispute the past has repeatedly shown me a pattern I do not have a name for, and I want more than a label: I want the phenomenon carved at the joints.

When historical atrocities are discussed, the same person — sometimes within a single paragraph — will do two things. First, they will refuse the very idea of ranking such events: to place them on a scale is obscene, each evil is singular. Second, they will use language that quietly restores a scale: they will call one event unprecedented, or the gravest crime in history, or they will deflect an opponent's analogy by itemizing differences, which is itself a comparative judgment. What is curious is that the speaker does not feel the second move as a ranking. It presents itself, to them, as the same act of reverence as the first. Comparison is simultaneously forbidden and performed.

I am not asking whether ranking historical evils is defensible. I am asking what this double stance is called and how it is best analyzed. Is there an established name or body of thought for a principled refusal to compare that nonetheless leans on comparative commitments embedded in words like unprecedented or unique? If there is, what distinguishes it from ordinary inconsistency, from hypocrisy, and from whataboutism? If there is no exact fit, I would ask for a coined term and a precise definition, including boundary cases that separate it from those neighboring phenomena — sharp enough to guide the narration of a character who cannot see the tension in himself.
