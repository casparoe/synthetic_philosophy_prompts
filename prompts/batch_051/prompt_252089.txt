I am a research engineer who works with Bayesian models and decision theory daily: priors, likelihoods, posteriors, model uncertainty, loss functions, policies. I have been reading Wittgenstein’s later remarks on religious belief, especially the Cambridge lectures he gave around 1938, and I want to see how much of his position I can express in my own formal vocabulary. In one passage he contrasts someone who believes in the Last Judgement with someone who treats it as an ordinary empirical hypothesis. The religious believer, as he describes him, does not hold the belief as a probable or improbable prediction about a future event; the belief is not suspended on evidence in that way. It has an unshakeable place in the believer’s life, regulates how he lives and what he takes seriously, and does not get revised the way a scientific or everyday expectation does. For example, the believer may say he has no doubt, while the non-believer may say there is no proof, and according to Wittgenstein the two are not simply assigning different probabilities to the same proposition; they are doing something different with the words.

I want a systematic translation. Could I model this sort of religious belief as a prior with probability one? As a policy or a value function? As a constraint on the loss function or on the set of admissible hypotheses? As a background assumption that is not updated but conditions all updates? For each candidate translation, tell me what carries over cleanly, what is distorted, and what has no expression in my vocabulary. Then give the residue report: is the residue pointing to something my Bayesian framework cannot see, or is it pointing to a place where Wittgenstein’s contrast between religious and ordinary empirical belief is less sharp than he makes it sound? I want the translation to be honest, not a metaphor that smuggles in Bayesian assumptions and calls them equivalent.
