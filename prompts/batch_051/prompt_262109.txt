A mathematically literate person will reject a proof whose final step appeals to the very theorem it was meant to establish, and will not accept an endless chain of lemmas as a proof of anything. The same person will use an ordinary dictionary without unease, even though a dictionary taken as a whole is a closed system in which every word is ultimately defined through other words; and the same person will accept that a child's string of why-questions may properly end in the claim that things are simply that way. The first pattern is the Münchhausen trilemma usually associated with Hans Albert: any attempt to justify a claim seems to end in infinite regress, circularity, or a dogmatic stopping point. Everyday semantic and explanatory practice, however, tolerates all three of these endings as unremarkable, while formal proof and philosophical justification treat them as disqualifying.

Is there a principle that justifies this asymmetry, or is it only a division of labor we have stopped noticing? I want the strongest formulation of the principle you can find, tested against nearby cases—coherentist accounts of epistemic justification, the mutual support among the parts of a mature scientific theory, and a dictionary that defines every one of its words only through other words—and a candid verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, revised, or abandoned.
