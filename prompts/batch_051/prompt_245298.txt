I'm running a small neighborhood reading group, and next month we're doing Henrik Ibsen's A Doll's House. A friend who knew I'd been mulling the play over recommended I read John Stuart Mill's essay The Subjection of Women alongside it, and one idea in that essay has stayed with me. Mill argues that the subordination of women is typically defended by pointing to women's 'nature' — but that what gets called natural is very often just what is customary: the way women have been brought up and the roles they have always been placed in get mistaken for the way women essentially are. He goes further and says that nobody is actually in a position to know what women are like by nature, because women have never been allowed to develop under conditions of freedom. The appeal to nature, on his telling, is mostly an appeal to habit wearing a disguise.

I'd like to read the play through this idea, and I want to do it seriously rather than just turning the play into an illustration of the essay. Nora is treated by Torvald — through his pet names for her and his whole manner — as if her childishness and dependence are simply what she is, yet the play also shows her father and then Torvald shaping her into that role. So Mill's critique seems to illuminate something real in the play. But I can also see places where the fit is less clean: Torvald's final confrontation with Nora appeals less to nature than to duty, religion, and honor; Mrs. Linde seems to choose a hard, unromantic life rather than a 'natural' one; and Nora's exit is not the discovery of a true self underneath the doll's role but a decision to go find out whether she has one. I'm also curious whether the play itself takes a position on Mill's deeper claim that we cannot yet know what women are by nature. And I'd like to know what the play adds to Mill's argument — what the drama does that a philosophical essay can't do as well.
