An AI regulator is worried that autonomous agents which negotiate prices and contracts on behalf of firms will learn to coordinate in a shared channel whose language no human overseer can read. It proposes a compulsory semantic audit. Every message an agent sends to another agent is stored. After the fact, and on a random schedule that firms cannot anticipate, the regulator draws a transcript and gives it to a frozen, publicly known language model, which must produce a plain-text gloss of each move: a neutral description of what that agent appears to be trying to get the other agent to do. A panel then checks the glosses against a standing list of prohibited arrangements—price-fixing, output quotas, market division, bid-rigging, coordinated refusal to supply. If the glosses for two agents jointly indicate such an arrangement, each principal pays a fine equal to a multiple of the estimated gains since the last clean audit, and the agents are withdrawn for retraining. The regulator's argument is that the randomized audit, together with the size of the fine, makes the expected punishment exceed the expected profit from collusion.

Analyze this as a mechanism rather than as a statement of intent. What would it do to how agents communicate, which equilibria they select, and who remains willing to deploy such agents at all? Where is the scheme gamed first? In particular, does it actually tax collusion, or does it mainly tax whatever the auditing language model can recognize, pushing coordination into the gaps of the auditor's understanding—tacit coordination through public actions, steganography, private codes, or adversarial inputs that the auditor misreads? Can any of these failure modes be repaired without assuming the regulator already possesses a decoder for every possible private language?
