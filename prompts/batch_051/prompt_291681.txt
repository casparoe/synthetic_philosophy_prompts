I am a retired physical chemist who recently began sitting in on a philosophy reading group. After a session on H. L. A. Hart’s discussion of the open texture of legal rules, we turned to the void-for-vagueness doctrine. I would be very grateful if you would adjudicate the following exchange point by point.

We imagined a city ordinance making it a misdemeanor to remain in a public place in a manner that reasonably alarms or annoys another person. One member, a former criminal defense investigator, said the ordinance is plainly void for vagueness. Her argument was that an ordinary person cannot know in advance whether standing on a corner, talking to strangers, or wearing unusual clothes will cross the line, because the line depends on how someone else reacts after the fact. She added that the defect is not just imprecision at the edges but the fact that the prohibited conduct is constituted by another person’s alarm or annoyance.

Another member, a retired classicist with a strong interest in jurisprudence, replied that the law is full of standards such as reasonable force, reasonable belief, and reasonable provocation, and we do not ordinarily call those void. In his view, the reasonable-person test supplies a community norm rather than leaving everything to the officer’s whim. He said that if we demanded a list of specified behaviors, criminal codes would become brittle and would fail to cover novel disruptions.

The first member answered that his parallels are disanalogous. In the case of reasonable force, she said, the underlying act of force is already defined, and the standard governs its justification. Here the phrase reasonably alarms or annoys does not limit an independently identified act; it is the whole content of the prohibition. She argued that the notice problem is therefore circular: the conduct is unlawful because it reasonably alarms, and reasonable alarm is just what the defendant was supposed to foresee. The classicist responded that public-order statutes have long used language such as conduct likely to provoke a breach of the peace, and courts have upheld them. The first member concluded that longevity does not cure a rule-of-law defect; it only shows that courts have tolerated vaguely drawn public-order laws because they doubt that any list of disturbances can track future events.

I honestly could not decide which of them was right about which points. Please go through the exchange move by move, identify where each person is on strong or weak ground, and say who is more right overall and what, if anything, both are missing. Please answer in continuous prose only, as in an email or letter, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
