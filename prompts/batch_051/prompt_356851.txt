I keep going back and forth on the knowledge norm of assertion, and I'd like your take. The classic line, which got its sharpest defense from Williamson, is that you shouldn't flat-out assert that p unless you know that p. The arguments for it were mostly built around a single speaker who composes a sentence and stands behind it. But most of the asserting that actually happens now doesn't look like that: people retweet or share claims they haven't checked, send AI-drafted paragraphs after a quick skim, or speak for a committee where no one member knows the entire position. I don't just want the usual "that's epistemically bad" complaint. I want to know whether the knowledge norm survives this, and in what form.

Does the rise of amplification and AI-assisted speech put pressure on a specific premise of Williamson's argument, or does it just change the examples? If the norm survives intact, say why these new acts are either not assertions at all or are just constant violations. If it needs repair, give me the best repaired version—maybe a split between asserting something outright and amplifying or relaying it, with a weaker norm for the second (reason to think it's worth passing on? grounds for thinking it's probably true?). Or does the whole idea of one norm for assertion fall apart once assertion comes in these distributed and half-endorsed forms? I care more about which premise takes the hit and whether something weaker but still interesting can be saved then about the practical online advice.
