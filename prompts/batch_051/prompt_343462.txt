I keep going in circles on Skolem's paradox and I want someone to audit this reconstruction of it — specifically I cant tell whether the reasoning is valid and the conclusion is uncomfortable, or whether there's a slide hidden somewhere in the middle. Here's the argument:

(1) Assume ZFC is consistent. By the downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem it has a model M whose domain is countable. So there are only countably many things M treats as sets at all.

(2) Since M is a model of ZFC, it satisfies every theorem of ZFC, including Cantor's theorem. So M satisfies the sentence "there is no bijection between the set of natural numbers and the set of all subsets of the natural numbers." Let R be the object in M that M takes to be P(ℕ) — the set of reals, if you wish to code reals as subsets of ℕ.

(3) Consider Ext(R), the collection of objects in M that M regards as members of R. Since every one of those objects is an element of M and M's domain is countable, Ext(R) is countable from the outside: in the real world we can enumerate x0, x1, x2, ... of everything M thinks belongs to R.

(4) So the set of things M calls real numbers is, from the outside, countable, even though M itself says it is uncountable.

(5) But M is not a defective model of ZFC. Every first-order statement about R and ℕ that ZFC proves is true in M, just as it is true in the actual universe of sets. So there is no first-order fact about uncountability that the real universe has and M lacks; the outside countability of Ext(R) is not the kind of difference ZFC can state.

(6) Therefore "really uncountable" is not something first-order set theory can say at all. Uncountability is model-relative: a set can be uncountable from inside a model and countable from outside, and neither viewpoint is more correct.

Is this argument valid? If not, which step or steps fail? In particular I worry about two places: step (4), which seems to smuggle in an outside viewpoint while saying "from the outside"; and step (5), which might overstate what M and the real universe share. What is the strongest true version of the argument, and what does it actually establish about the real numbers?
