Consider the familiar philosophical-zombie test case for the thesis that conceivability is a guide to possibility: a world physically exactly like ours but containing no phenomenal consciousness. Now vary the case by making the absence partial and singular rather than global. Two subjects, each in a first-ever encounter with the color red, are looking at the same ripe strawberry and are physically identical at that moment and indistinguishable in behavior and function. Each sincerely reports that the strawberry looks red. The only stipulated difference is that one of them has the qualitative reddish character of the experience, while the other—a partial zombie with respect to this one state—lacks it entirely. Assess this variant using, first, the standard two-tier account of conceivability (roughly, conceivability as a priori coherence versus conceivability that survives full knowledge of the actual world, including a posteriori identities) and, second, the standard physicalist reply that the residual gap is epistemic, analogous to the a posteriori necessity of water's being H2O and explainable by the special character of phenomenal concepts. Does this partial-absence variant strengthen, weaken, or merely relocate the original argument from conceivability to possibility? Which premise of the original argument now carries the main weight, and what is the strongest first move for the physicalist against this smaller target? State how confident you are in each of your main claims, and end by singling out the point you are least sure of.
