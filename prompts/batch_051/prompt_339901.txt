Two friends of mine, Dan and Priya, got into it over whether greed is a vice. I dont have a horse in it and stayed out of it, but I'd like to know where the argument actually landed. Here's the back-and-forth, pretty much as it happened.

(1) Dan said a greedy person is never satisfied. He gets the raise, the bigger house, the promotion, and it immediately becomes the new floor. He compares himself upward and can't enjoy what he has. That's not a small quirk; it's a stable disposition, a vice — it makes his own life worse and makes him bad to be around.

(2) Priya said that exact same restlessness is what keeps an economy alive. Contented people don't start businesses, cut costs, or work the extra shift to develop a new product. If you got rid of the desire for more, you'd get a poorer, less inventive society. So the trait Dan is calling a vice is actually the engine of prosperity, and calling it a vice is just moralizing about something that on balance does good.

(3) Dan replied that this doesn't disarm his point; it just describes the same thing at two levels. Something can be bad in the person and good as a side effect. A suspicious, envious colleague who double-checks everyone's work might catch a lot of errors — the suspicion is still a vice, even if the office benefits from it. So he can concede that greed produces prosperity and still say it's a vice. Those aren't rival descriptions of the same thing; they're answers to different questions.

(4) Priya said that "vice" isn't a neutral label — it licenses discouraging or suppressing the trait. If suppressing greed reliably made everyone poorer, the case for suppressing it looks weak or absent. And Dan's side-effect framing presumes the benefit is accidental. With greed, the benefits aren't a fluke; they're regular and predictable. A reliable, predictable contribution isn't a side effect, it's a function. So he's the one who needs to explain why a trait with a dependable social function should still be condemned.

Could you adjudicate this move by move? For each move, say whether the arguer is on solid ground or where exactly the argument slips, then say who comes out ahead overall and whether there's anything important both of them are missing.
