Consider the following counterfactual. In 2026, a well-funded institute attempts a literal implementation of the Humans Consulting HCH (HCH) idealization from factored cognition: a recursive tree of human subagents in which each node receives a short task and a bounded context, decomposes non-leaf tasks into a handful of simpler subquestions, delegates those to fresh workers, and then synthesizes their written answers. The pilot tree has depth 7 and branching factor 4 (roughly 16,000 nodes). Every node is filled by a competent but replaceable generalist; each worker sees only their own question and, for internal nodes, compressed subanswers, cannot see the tree or know their position in it, has about 1,500 words of input budget, and works for at most 90 minutes. The root tasks are a long technical review of a genuinely unsettled area of biology, a legal risk analysis for a multinational acquisition, and a 10,000-word strategic brief on a geopolitical instability—all longer than any single node can hold in working memory.

Predict how this literal system would perform relative both to the HCH idealization and to a single excellent expert given the same total person-hours. In particular: (a) where do errors, ambiguity, and incentive misalignment compound most across levels; (b) does recursion buy anything beyond parallelized labor—that is, can the system sustain an argument or notice a pattern that no individual worker could, and what is its effective reasoning horizon; and (c) how fragile is the system to workers who shirk or subtly game their instructions. Distinguish claims you regard as robust from claims that depend on specific assumptions about human cognition, the decomposability of the tasks, or how well-run the institute is. End with a one-sentence summary of your overall assessment, on a line beginning "Summary:".
