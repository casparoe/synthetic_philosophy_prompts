I'm a PhD student working on moral uncertainty and AI governance, and I've sort of talked myself into a view I want pressure-tested. The view is roughly this: when the live moral theories (or the relevant stakeholders) disagree, the only defensible interventions are safe Pareto improvements — changes that no live value system counts as making things worse and at least one counts as making things better. The appeal is obviuos: it's the one kind of change you can't end up regretting no matter who turns out to be right. What I want is a map of what would actually count against this, beyond the obvious point that the set of such improvements might be small or empty. What would have to be true — empirically, formally, or conceptually — for the whole approach to be misguided rather than just hard? Which objections would a defender of the view have to concede count against it (even if they think it survives), and which are just people importing assumptions the view doesn't share? Are there cases where a 'safe' Pareto improvement turned out to be a mistake, or a rival position that predicts something this one can't? And be honest about whether the view has been qualified to the point of unfalsifiability, and if it has, whether that's a problem for a view of this kind. I'd personally find it most persusive if you could show me a case where the move was called safe by this criterion but was actually a mistake once you adopted a value system the criterion had excluded, ignored, or couldn't represent.
