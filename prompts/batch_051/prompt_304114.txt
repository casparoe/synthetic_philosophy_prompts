I have recently been put in charge of designing a home curriculum for a child who has missed several years of formal schooling, and I keep thinking about Alan Turing's proposal in his 1950 paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence that an intelligent machine should be built as a child machine and educated rather than constructed with an adult mind already in place. I do not trust my own paraphrase, but the passage I mean is the one in the section on educating machinery where Turing compares the initial state to a child's mind before education, with relatively little built-in structure, and says the business of teaching is what turns it into an adult mind. I want to work out what a theory adequate to that proposal must actually require.

Please produce the desiderata such a theory of education—understood as the process by which an initially near-unorganized system becomes a competent thinker—must meet. The phenomena it must explain include: why the initial state must not be entirely blank if learning is to get started, but also why it cannot already contain the adult competencies if the education is to be genuine rather than a stage-show; what role the teacher's interventions play; what it means for the same curriculum to produce divergent outcomes; and what failure modes, such as rote imitation, overfitting to the teacher, or refusal to generalize, must be explainable. For each requirement, give the consideration or case that makes it a requirement, and mark it as non-negotiable, or as a preference of Turing's approach, or as a demand added by later machine-learning practice that Turing did not anticipate. I especially want to know whether any subset of these requirements is jointly unsatisfiable for a single learner—whether, for example, the demand that the initial state be simple enough to be built but rich enough to afford learning already hides a contradiction. I am not asking you to score particular named theories; the criteria are what I need.
