I've been arguing with a friend about Nozick's experience machine, and it struck me that the standard presentation quietly assumes a homogeneous hedonic condition: you plug in and receive something like a stable stream of pleasant experiences of one general kind. But that assumption is exactly what the heterogeneity problem for hedonism calls into question. The problem, roughly, is that pleasure may not name a single felt quality, or even a single natural kind. The pleasure of solving a proof, of scratching an itch, of schadenfreude, of relief at not being found out, of a runner's high, of hearing a joke land — these appear to share little in their phenomenology, their time-course, their physiology, or their role in the rest of one's mental life. Perhaps they have only a family resemblance, or only a shared relation to desire or to a liking system, rather than a common pleasant feel.

So vary the thought experiment. Suppose the machine cannot sustain any single continuous hedonic state. Instead, it delivers a carefully sequenced kaleidoscope of maximally heterogeneous pleasures: each episode has a different qualitative feel, a different duration, a different place in your motivational economy, and no felt continuity with the one before it. By the machine's design, the only thing the episodes have in common is that, while each lasts, you endorse it as you would any pleasure. Does the standard experience-machine argument still cut the same way? Please work through whether the machine becomes more attractive, less attractive, or equally attractive once its pleasures are heterogeneous rather than homogeneous, and what this tells us about whether the heterogeneity problem and the experience-machine objection stand or fall together.

Please answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. The points should cover: (1) what argumentative force the original machine is supposed to have, and which premise the variation puts pressure on; (2) whether the kaleidoscope machine's appeal goes up, goes down, or stays the same, and why; (3) how a hedonist who claims pleasure is a single felt quality should analyze the variant; (4) how a hedonist who accepts that pleasure is heterogeneous should analyze it; and (5) what the variant reveals about whether the heterogeneity problem matters for the broader case for hedonism.
