Here's a thing I've been chewing on. The classic split-brain argument runs something like this. Sever the corpus callosum, flash an instruction to only one hemisphere, and the patient can carry it out with the hand that hemisphere controls; ask the speaking hemisphere why, and it confabulates a reason that has nothing to do with what actually happened. Each hemisphere can act on information the other can't report, so — the argument goes — the patient has two separate streams of consciousness where a normal person had one, and the unity of consciousness is a contingent bit of wiring rather than a necessary feature of a mind. Fine. Now suppose instead of a permanent lesion we get a reversible way to dampen interhemispheric communication in healthy adults, something you can set anywhere from fully connected to fully disconnected, with the subject awake and able to report throughout and afterward. At the middle settings you do not get a clean split into two selves. You get one subject who reports feeling hazy and slightly unreal, who does things with one hand they can't explain, who still insists the actions were theirs, and whose speaking self confabulates an explanation on the spot. My question is whether the original argument surives that development. Does it show the argument was always overreading the lesion data — that behavioral disconnection was never enough to establish two streams — or is the zero-bandwidth case still doing the real work and the middle settings are just noise? Which premise exactly is under pressure (the idea that each hemisphere is a full agent, the idea that acting without verbal access means a separate consciousness, the idea that unity of consciousness is all-or-nothing, something else), and is there a repaired version of the split-brain argument that says something weaker but still worth saying? If the original argument is dead, say what it should be replaced with.
