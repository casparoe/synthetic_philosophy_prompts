Lately I've been trying to sort out when turning my attention inward actually helps and when it's just picking a scab. I've kept a running list of my own verdicts on cases, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, captures the pattern:

- Journaling each evening about what went wrong socially that day and what I could do differently: fine, genuinely useful.
- Lying in bed at 3 a.m. replaying an awkward comment from a party, trying to figure out just how bad it was: not fine. It loops without going anywhere and I end up feeling worse.
- Doing a CBT-style thought record where I write the situation, the automatic thought, the evidence for and against, and an alternative thought: fine, often the most useful thing I know how to do.
- Asking "why am I like this, why cant I just be normal" in the bottom of a low mood: not fine; it digs the hole deeper.
- Meditating with the instruction to notice whatever is present and name it without trying to fix it: fine, maybe better than fine.
- Reconstructing a tense conversation with my boss to work out whether she is actually angry or I'm imagining it: I go back and forth. Sometimes it produces a concrete next step; other times it spirals into guessing at her mental states.
- A structured work postmortem after a failed project, with causes and lessons written down in a shared document: fine.
- The same failure, but alone at night thinking "am I just not cut out for this": not fine.
- Tracking my mood in an app three times a day: fine, almost mechanical, no problem.
- Scanning my body every hour to check whether the anxiety is back: not fine; it turns anxiety into something to be anxious about.

My working rule was that useful self-examination is concrete and answerable while rumination is abstract and loops, but I cant make that fit the boss-conversation case (concrete but it can spiral) or the mood-tracking case (it's about feelings, not a task, but it seems harmless). And then there's the case I can't settle: last month I made a long list of every way I might have let down an old friend over the years, trying to figure out what I owe them. It felt bad while I was doing it, but it did lead to one real apology and a clearer sense of a pattern, so I don't know whether to file it under useful self-examination or rumination. Is there one principle that gets most of these, and what would it say about the friend-list case? I'd rather you tell me which of my verdicts it can't accommodate than watch a principle get tortured to fit all of them, and if the principle has several clauses, say which clause is doing which work.
