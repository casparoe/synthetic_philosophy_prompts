Construct a thought experiment that separates two explanations of why people treat purity violations—desecrating a sacred object, handling the dead, eating a taboo substance—as wrong. Position A holds that such acts can be wrong in themselves: purity is a source of reasons not exhausted by any consequence of the act. Position B holds that purity norms have only instrumental value, functioning as rough proxies for avoiding contamination, disease, and the loss of social boundaries, so that once those consequences are removed, any residual sense of wrongness is a misfiring with no normative force. The scenario must be realistic and single-person, with no observers and no material or biological consequences; it must not stipulate how the character feels but must place them in a choice where A and B make different predictions about their reflective judgment or overt behavior. It should force a verdict about whether an initial feeling of disgust or aversion survives reflection, rather than merely recording the feeling. After presenting the scene, state exactly what each position predicts and why, identify the single feature of the case that does the separating work, and explain how a defender of whichever position the case cuts against would try to neutralize it. The point of the case is to test whether a purity judgment retains any normative force once every instrumental rationale has been stripped away, so a case that smuggles in a hidden consequence, or that simply stipulates the character's answer, fails the assignment.
