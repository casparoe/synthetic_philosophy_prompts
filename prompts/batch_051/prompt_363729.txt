A friend and I spent a long evening on the question of whether effort has any value apart from the results it buys, and he left me with an argument I have not been able to shake.

(1) If effort is valuable in itself, then that value must be able to guide action: there must be at least some cases in which the fact that a course of action would demand more effort counts in favor of choosing it.
(2) But a course of action chosen because it demands more effort is undertaken for the sake of the effort rather than for the sake of the end.
(3) Effort undertaken for its own sake is not the kind of effort that could be valuable in itself. The struggle worth calling good is striving directed at something beyond itself; once the effort becomes the point, it collapses into self-imposed toil, and toil is not what anyone values.
(4) So the only effort that could be valuable in itself would be effort whose value cannot guide our choices.
(5) A value that cannot guide our choices is not a value in the practical sense; it is idle to call it valuable in itself.
(6) Therefore effort is not valuable in itself. Whatever is good in struggle is either good because of the results it produces, or it is an incidental by-product of wholehearted pursuit, and it disappears as soon as we try to aim at it directly.

I want to reject this conclusion: I think there is something genuinely good about effort that is not merely instrumental. But I cannot see which premise I should surrender. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
