Every year I teach a seminar on language and categories, and every year the grammar unit leaves me with a nagging feeling I can't quite articulate. My students arrive having been drilled that words fall into one of a small number of parts of speech, each with its own defintion: nouns are persons, places, or things; verbs are actions or states; adjectives modify nouns; prepositions express relations of place, time, or direction. The practice around them—dictionaries, SAT prep, grammar checkers, ESL placement tests—treats these categories as if they carved the language at its joints. But the moment you apply the definitions, the joints stop lining up. Running names an action but it's a noun in Running is bad for my knees. Redness names a property, not a person, place, or thing. Explosion names an event. The same form jumps categories from sentence to sentence, and dictionaries respond by listing run as a noun and a verb in seperate entries, as if that settles it. Meanwhile words like concerning get labelled prepositions even though they're transparently verb-derived connectors, and Latin textbooks insist that every language worth taking seriously has the same cases doing the same jobs.

So here's what I want to get clear about. What philosophical picture of categories does this whole practice presuppose? Does it actually commit its users to the view that parts of speech and cases are natural kinds with real, definitional essences and sharp boundaries—or is the essentialism just a pedagogic fiction, with the practice being compatible with prototype structure, family resemblance, or purely distributional definitions? Reconstruct the implicit theory, be explicit about where the practice genuinely needs it and where it's just underdetermined, and then say what would concretely have to change in a grammar textbook or a parser if we rebuilt the whole thing on the distributional view: a noun is whatever can pluralize, follow a determiner, and so on. Would anything real be lost, or just the comfortable illusion that grammar is about meanings rather than positions?
