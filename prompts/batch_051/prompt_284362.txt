Construct a thought experiment in which an irreducible value pluralist is forced by her own theory to act more ruthlessly than a reductive value monist — the opposite of the usual direction. Work with the following two positions, spelled out so that the exercise is self-contained.

Reductive value monism: there is exactly one fundamental value, namely well-being. Every other candidate value — honesty, promise-keeping, gratitude, loyalty — is valuable, when it is, only insofar as it serves well-being. Once all consequences are counted, the right act is the act that best promotes aggregate well-being.

Irreducible value pluralism: well-being is a fundamental value, but it is not the only one. Fidelity — keeping one's serious commitments, including commitments made to a person who has since died — is a separate fundamental value with weight of its own. That weight is not cancelled simply because breaking the commitment would produce more aggregate well-being; the pluralist weighs fidelity against well-being, and sometimes fidelity wins.

Ruthlessness, for this exercise, is defined as follows: an act is ruthless to the extent that it knowingly accepts concrete, foreseeable losses to the well-being of identifiable living people for the sake of a principle. The scenario must make the pluralist — not the monist — the ruthless one by this measure.

Specifications:
- The scenario must be realistic and ordinary: a family, a workplace, a small nonprofit, a literary estate, or the like. No trolleys, no wars, no science fiction.
- Write the scenario in one paragraph.
- The promise should be one whose performance no longer benefits the promisee — the standard case being a solemn promise made to someone now dead — so that the pluralist's reason to keep it runs through fidelity alone and cannot be relabeled as a welfare benefit to the promisee.
- The pluralist's ruthlessness must fall out of the structure of her theory, not out of a fanatical or idiosyncratic second value. The promise should be one a reasonable person could regard as binding even at real cost.
- The monist's calculation must be run honestly. Breaking the promise should carry some genuine welfare costs — disappointed expectations, damage to trust, the agent's own unease — and the monist must count them, yet still conclude that breaking the promise maximizes aggregate well-being.

After the scenario, provide these sections in order:

1. The verdicts (roughly 250 words). State exactly what each theory prescribes, then run each theory's reasoning step by step. Show, using the details of the case, why it is the pluralist's prescribed act rather than the monist's that meets the definition of ruthlessness. Do not simply assert this.

2. The load-bearing feature (roughly 100 words). Identify the single feature of the case that makes the two theories come apart in this direction, and say what would happen to the case if that feature were removed.

3. The pluralist's defense (roughly 150 words). Present the strongest attack a defender of the pluralist verdict could make on the case — an illicit assumption in the setup, an incompleteness in the monist's calculation, a challenge to the definition of ruthlessness — and assess briefly how far that attack succeeds.
