Take the final push to eradicate smallpox — say 1967 to 1977 — and run it through the importance/tractability/neglectedness framework used in cause prioritization. I know the basic facts: in 1967 smallpox was still endemic in a few dozen countries, something like 10 to 15 million cases and a couple million deaths a year; the WHO launched an intensified program; the big tactical shift was from mass vaccination to surveillance-containment (find a case, vaccinate a ring around it); the last natural case was in Somalia in 1977 and the disease was certified eradicated in 1980. The whole thing cost maybe a few hundred million dollars.

What I want is a proper ITN audit, not a just-so story. Score it as a funder in 1966 would have, and then again with hindsight. On importance: the current burden was real but small next to malaria or TB, so does the framework only say it was worth doing because eradication buys you all future cases avoided? On tractability: did the containment strategy make it more tractable than it looked, and is that a point for or against the framework? On neglectedness: here's the part that bugs me. The endgame looks like increasing returns — the last case was worth vastly more then the first case because it flipped the system to "gone forever." The neglectedness heuristic normally says marginal money does less good as a space gets crowded, but with eradication the marginal value went up as the campaign progressed. Does ITN have a clean way to model that, or does it assume you're buying units of a continuous good rather than finishing a binary job?

Then give me the two-direction verdict: what does the framework get right about the episode, what does it miss or mangle, and what does the episode show about the framework — is it a vindication, an anomaly, or evidence that "neglectedness" is quietly doing most of the work?
