I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and my exposure to game theory is informal. I own the agent strategy layer for a B2B contract-negotiation platform. Companies on the platform configure software agents to negotiate short-term supply and procurement contracts; a typical interaction is effectively one-shot — two agents meet through a venue, exchange terms, and either close or walk away — and because counterparty identity is often new or unverifiable, we cannot assume reputation or repeated play will enforce good behavior. I have come across an idea I believe is called program equilibrium in the academic literature. As I understand it, if a player's strategy is a program that can read the other player's program before choosing an action, then a strategy can make its behavior conditional on the other's behavior in a principled way — for example, offer fair terms if and only if the counterparty's source code shows it would offer fair terms in the same situation. The surprising claim is that, even in one-shot interactions, this can make mutual cooperation a stable outcome where purely opaque, self-interested bidding would not.

I want you to turn that idea into a reusable decision procedure my team can actually run before we open a new negotiation venue or launch a new contract type. The decision is whether our agent should publish its source code, or a verifiable fragment of its decision logic, and make its offers and acceptances conditional on the counterparty's source code, and if so, under what safeguards. Please write this as a checklist or ordered set of tests, not as an essay about the theory. For each item, state what counts as a sufficient answer to proceed and what should stop the process. It should include: the preconditions that must hold before we even consider transparent conditional strategies; a concrete battery of adversarial opponent programs to test against in a sandbox — unconditional defectors, programs that mirror our conditional logic and then add a self-serving deviation, programs designed to force unbounded mutual simulation, and programs nearly identical to ours but distinguished only by a stored identity or owner key; how to handle the fact that in practice we usually see a hash, bytecode, or a notice file rather than clean source; and explicit points where an engineer or legal reviewer must make a judgment call rather than follow the checklist — for example, how much proprietary logic we are willing to reveal, or how much expected loss we tolerate before disabling the approach on a venue. We face a two-day pre-release review window, a team of five engineers, and legal sign-off is required for any source disclosure. Then apply the procedure to three candidate deployments: a recurring procurement auction among vetted suppliers, a one-off negotiation initiated by an unknown and possibly adversarial counterparty, and an internal resource-allocation negotiation between two agents our company owns on different teams. Make sure at least one worked example returns an answer I will not like, and say why the procedure returns it anyway. Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot settle or guarantee.
