I keep coming back to this bit from W.K. Clifford's essay 'The Ethics of Belief':

"When an action is once done, it is right or wrong for ever; no accidental failure of its good or evil fruits can possibly alter that. The question of right or wrong has to do with the origin of his belief, not the matter of it; not what it was, but how he got it; not whether it turned out to be true or false, but whether he had a right to believe on such evidence as was before him."

Am I right that he's saying a belief's wrongness doesn't depend at all on whether it turns out true? Because that feels extreme. I've got three little rules I actually notice myself using day to day: (a) believing a thing because someone I trust told me, (b) keeping a belief I grew up with and not going looking for stuff that might knock it down, (c) believing only after I've tried to find what's wrong with it and couldn't. Could you rate each of those three from 0 (never ok) to 10 (always ok) under his standard, and in a sentence or two each say where it lands? I'd like it in British English please, and don't just say they're all bad — I want a proper score per rule.
