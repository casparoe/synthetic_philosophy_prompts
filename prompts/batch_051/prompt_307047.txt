I'm a PhD student in philosophy of mind, and I keep coming back to the dispute between illusionists and realists about phenomenal consciousness, partly because it feels like the same moves get made every year without anyone shifting. On one side, realists say there is a what-it-is-like character to experience—the hurtfulness of pain, the reddish look of a tomato—that is not captured by any story about functional roles, representations, or access, and that any theory which denies it is quietly changing the subject. On the other side, illusionists say that what introspection presents as an ineffable qualitative residue is itself a kind of systematically misleading representation: there is only the representational and functional machinery plus our reactive, access-like sense that there is something more, and the real job is to explain why that sense is so stubborn.

The strange thing is that both camps seem to agree on nearly all the empirical facts about brains and behavior, and even on most of what people report about their own experience. The disagreement seems to be over whether those reports and the accompanying sense of a qualitative residue are data to be respected or an appearance to be explained away. My working hypothesis is that this is not really an empirical dispute and not exactly a verbal one either: it is a disagreement about what an explanation of consciousness has to do, and about who gets the default position. But I honestly can't tell whether that is right or just a way of being charitable to both sides, since people on each side tell me no amount of neuroscience or introspection would change there mind.

I want a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, not a survey of positions and not a verdict on who wins. Is this a case where the two camps are answering different questions, where introspection is treated as evidence by one side and as part of the illusion by the other, or where there is a hidden factual question about how reliable introspection actually is? What would count as progress—a conceptual distinction, a new experimental result, a formal argument, or just a definition of phenomenal consciousness both sides could accept? And be candid: given your diagnosis, is there any realistic route to resolution, or should we expect the debate to stay stuck. Put on your philosopher-of-mind hat, brainstorm a few candidate diagnoses before committing to one, and then tell me where your own diagnosis is most likely to fail and what argument or consideration would change your mind about it.
