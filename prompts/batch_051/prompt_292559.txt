Suppose that on 1 January 2026, a large common-law jurisdiction enacts the following reform. The law of homicide ceases to distinguish between killing by act and killing by omission. A person is guilty of homicide for failing to prevent a death whenever two conditions are met: she knew the victim was in immediate danger of death, and she could have intervened without significant risk or cost to herself as judged by an objective reasonable-person standard. The statute states that moderate inconvenience and moderate financial loss are not significant: a bystander who can throw a life-ring to a drowning stranger must do so, while no one is required to accept serious bodily danger or to surrender an organ or other bodily material. The omission is graded exactly as an act with the same mental state would be: murder where an act would be murder, manslaughter where an act would be manslaughter. The duty is general, not tied to any pre-existing relationship, profession, or role.

Predict, with reasoning, how this jurisdiction's legal system and social life look after thirty years. In particular, address the following. (1) How do police, prosecutors, and courts manage the expansive liability, and where, in practice, do new distinctions arise to replace the old act/omission line? (2) How do ordinary people and institutions reorganize so as not to know about, or be cheaply able to prevent, strangers' emergencies? (3) What happens to emergency medicine, triage, rescue services, family care, and the ordinary ethics of public spaces? (4) Where is the reform likely to be narrowed, nullified by juries, or effectively repealed? Make your chain of reasoning visible, and for each main prediction indicate whether it is robust, conditional on contested assumptions about how courts will interpret knowledge and reasonable cost, or largely speculative. End with a one-sentence summary of your overall prediction as the final line, beginning "In sum:".
