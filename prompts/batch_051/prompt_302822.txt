Last month I served on the jury for a criminal trial, and I have not been able to stop thinking about one feature of the verdict: it required us to treat the defendant as blameworthy in a way that went beyond merely saying he caused a bad outcome. After the trial, a colleague pressed a general argument on me — she called it Strawson’s Basic Argument — and I have been reconstructing it ever since. I will give it in my own wording, since I am asking about the reasoning itself rather than about who first formulated it. By “truly morally responsible” throughout, I mean responsible in the desert-entailing sense: the sense in which blame or praise would be ultimately deserved, not merely useful.

(1) What a person does in any situation is the product of the way she is at that moment — her beliefs, desires, values, character, and dispositions — together with whatever chance factors operate.
(2) To be truly morally responsible for an act, a person must be truly morally responsible for the features of the way she is that produced the act; chance, after all, cannot itself contribute responsibility.
(3) A person can be truly responsible for the way she is only if, at some earlier time, she intentionally brought it about that she is that way.
(4) But an act of bringing about a change in oneself is itself something she does, and so it is likewise the product of the way she was before that change.
(5) Therefore the same requirement recurs for that earlier way, and for the earlier one before it, with no point at which the regress can stop.
(6) Since the regress can never terminate in a way for which she was responsible, no one is ever truly responsible for the way she is.
(7) Therefore, by (2), no one is ever truly morally responsible for anything she does.

I keep oscillating. On the one hand, when I recall how much of the defendant’s situation had been fixed long before anything he could have chosen, the conclusion feels true and even humane. On the other hand, the jury’s distinction between the deliberate and the accidental seemed to track something real about him, not merely about the amount of harm, and I am reluctant to conclude that the law’s notion of fault was empty.

My question is this: does the argument succeed? Please give me a precise diagnosis. If the inference from (1)–(6) to (7) is valid but a premise is false, say which premise it is and explain on what reading it can be rejected without simply changing the subject. If a step appears to follow only because a term such as “responsible for the way one is” is being used in two different ways, say where that happens and what the two senses are. And if you think the argument really does go through, say so plainly and explain what, if anything, a person in my position should make of the kind of blame embedded in a criminal verdict. I have a vague suspicion that step (3) is doing too much work, but do not let that suspicion steer you if you think the genuine fault lies elsewhere.
