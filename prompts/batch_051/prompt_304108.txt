You are an epistemologist who has worked on the epistemology of testimony and who has served as a neutral convenor for adversarial collaborations between opposing camps. You are drafting a document for two philosophers: a reductionist about testimonial justification (the view that a hearer's entitlement to accept testimony is always at least partly inferential, resting on positive background evidence of the speaker's or the type's reliability) and an anti-reductionist (the view that a hearer can be defeasibly entitled to accept testimony on the speaker's say-so alone, absent defeaters, without needing positive evidence of reliability).

Before writing the final statement, spend some time brainstorming candidate formulations. List the claims each side is tempted to attribute to the other in a slanted way, then try to produce formulations each side would accept as a fair statement of their own view. Discard formulations that are vacuous or that only one side could sign. Then assemble the final document.

The deliverable should be self-contained, with no citations or reading lists.

Section 1: Numbered agreed claims. These must be claims both camps can sign without doing violence to their commitments. Cover at least: what testimonial belief is, how it differs from belief based on independent evidence about the speaker, when defeaters require withdrawing or downgrading trust, the extent to which ordinary cognition depends on others, and anything else a neutral characterization of the terrain requires. It is acceptable, and often necessary, to include claims that are conditional or that explicitly abstract away from the disputed question. Where one side signs only with a caveat, write the caveat into the claim itself.

Section 2: A disagreements table. Its rows should be the individual points of contention. Use four columns: the issue; the reductionist's formulation; the anti-reductionist's formulation; and whether the disagreement is substantive or possibly verbal, with a one-sentence explanation for each possibly verbal verdict. The central row should be whether testimonial justification is ever basic or default, or whether it is always partly owed to background reasons for trust. But do not stop there. Include rows on: whether positive reasons for reliability are required or only the absence of defeaters; whether the reasons in question must be propositional or may be merely dispositional or sub-personal; whether the speaker's act of telling itself contributes something beyond the evidence it provides; and whether the dispute changes shape once one separates testimony about the speaker's own mental states, testimony about ordinary observable events, and testimony in expert contexts.

Section 3: Cruxes. For each crux, give a neutral statement of the sub-question, a concrete case or class of cases both camps would agree bears on it, the reductionist's predicted verdict with rationale, and the anti-reductionist's predicted verdict with rationale, worded so each side would own its respective formulation. Include at least these cruxes: a hearer who accepts an ordinary report from a complete stranger with no evidence either way about that individual's reliability; a small child acquiring beliefs from a caregiver before the child could have a track record of the caregiver's reliability; and a hearer with strong statistical evidence about the reliability of a class of speakers but no evidence about the individual speaker. Then add one further crux of your own choosing. For each crux, state what sort of consideration would move each side, and specify in advance what each camp predicts about the case.

Finish with a short caveats paragraph noting where the two-camp framing breaks down — for example, local reductionism, or positions that grant a default entitlement to children but shift to reductionism for adults — and say whether the adversarial collaboration treats those as variants of one camp or as further positions. Throughout, aim for exactness rather than conciliation or escalation: the point is to locate the disagreement precisely, not to make it look smaller or larger than it is.
