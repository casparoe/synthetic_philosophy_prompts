For a seminar on medieval Islamic philosophy I've been working through al-Ghazālī's discussion of causation in The Incoherence of the Philosophers, where he argues that what we habitually call causes are not genuinely productive: when cotton meets fire, the burning is not brought about by the fire itself but is created by God on the occasion of the contact, and the regularity we call a causal law is only God's customary way of acting, which He could suspend at any moment. I keep half-recognizing this picture in literary treatments of dreamt and unreal worlds, and I want to make that intuition into a term paper on Jorge Luis Borges's story "The Circular Ruins," from Ficciones. In the story, a wizard trains himself to dream a son into bodily existence and finally succeeds; the youth is sent out into the world with his origins erased, and it is revealed that he can pass through fire without burning, which shows he is only an image. But at the end, as the temple burns around the wizard, the flames leave him untouched as well, and he realizes he is himself being dreamed by another.

I'd like a genuinely philosophical reading of the story through al-Ghazālī's occasionalism, one that treats both the text and the doctrine with equal seriousness and does not reduce either to an illustration of the other. Where does Borges dramatize the denial of secondary causation in a way that actually illuminates it, and where does he import commitments al-Ghazālī would reject or complicate? On al-Ghazālī's view, every created thing depends for its continued existence on God at every instant and no finite thing is a true agent, so what could it even mean within that framework for one person to be less real than another, or for a dreamt person to be a phantom rather than a genuine cause of his own actions? Does the story's implied regress of dreamers—the wizard dreamed by someone else, presumably by another in turn—read as a secular inversion of occasionalism's single sustaining God, and is the ending's horror, or its strange relief, philosophically earned on al-Ghazālī's terms? I am less interested in influence-tracing or biography than in what the story shows about the occasionalist picture that the arguments alone do not.
