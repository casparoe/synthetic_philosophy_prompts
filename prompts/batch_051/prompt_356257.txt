You are an professional philosopher working at the intersection of moral psychology and institutional design. You have been asked to analyze a proposed governance mechanism for a mid-sized membership organization. Treat the proposal as a piece of institutional engineering rather than as a moral thesis to approve or condemn; your task is to trace how it would actually behave in practice. Begin by brainstorming the relevant actors, incentives, and possible failure modes, and only then settle into an organized written analysis. Answer in American English.

The mechanism is as follows. A professional association of roughly 3,000 members, whose credential is valuable but not legally required, has a recurring problem with expelled former members seeking readmission and with long-running resentment between those members and the people they harmed. Under the proposed rule:

1. An expelled member may petition for reinstatement once per year after a two-year waiting period.
2. The petition triggers a secret ballot of all members in good standing, with three options: "forgive," "withhold," or "abstain."
3. Reinstatement requires both that at least 60% of non-abstaining voters vote "forgive," and that no member named as a direct victim in the original finding votes "withhold."
4. After the decision, the full tally and every member's vote are published.
5. A successful reinstatement seals the offense from all future disciplinary use within the association, though it cannot control members' personal memory or private dealings.
6. Any member who votes "withhold" must submit, within seven days, an anonymized written reason to a rotating Reinstatement Committee. The reason must be forward-looking: framed in terms of risk, safety, or likely future conduct. A reason the committee judges to be purely backward-looking or retributive is rejected as invalid, and the vote is then treated as an abstention.

Analyze this mechanism. In particular: what does it incentivize for (a) offenders seeking reinstatement, (b) direct victims, (c) third-party voters with no direct stake, and (d) the committee itself? Where is the system most likely to be gamed first? What patterns of forgiveness and refusal—real, performative, coerced, or strategic—would you expect it to produce? Trace the interactions among the 60% threshold, the direct victims' veto, the publication of individual votes, and the committee's power to invalidate backward-looking withholds. Identify the substantive account of forgiveness that the mechanism quietly builds in, and say what happens when members do not share that account. Conclude by distinguishing the failure modes that could be fixed by tinkering with the rules from the failure modes that are baked in once forgiveness is turned into a formal voting procedure at all.
