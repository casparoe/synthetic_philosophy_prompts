I'm a PhD student in philosophy of science, working on predictability in nonlinear systems, and a freind in the geosciences department walked me into an argument that now looks like it proves too much. It's not originally hers — it's one of those arguments that floats around whenever climate and chaos come up — but she wrote it out cleanly and I can't find the exit. Here it is, numbered.

(1) A system has sensitive dependence on initial conditions — the butterfly effect — when arbitrarily small differences in its initial state grow until two trajectories that began arbitrarily close are eventually as far apart as randomly chosen states of the system.
(2) The weather is like this: on the standard picture the atmosphere sits on a strange attractor, and nearby states separate in roughly the way Lorenz saw, which is why forecast skill drops off after a week or two.
(3) If a system is chaotic in that sense, then predicting its exact state at time t requires pinning down the initial state to a precision that grows exponentially in t.
(4) No measurement has infinite precision, so for any weather model there is a finite lead time beyond which the forecast is no better than a guess — the error bars just swallow everything.
(5) Climate is just weather considered over long timescales and large spatial scales: the same atmosphere, the same ocean, the same equations, the same attractor.
(6) So the same reasoning should carry over: climate models, too, have a finite horizon beyond which they're useless. The only difference is where the horizon sits.
(7) But climate science does issue projections over decades to centuries — global mean temperature, regional precipitation distributions, extreme-event frequencies — and we treat these as informative enough to bet policy on.
(8) Therefore either those projections aren't really informative (in which case the butterfly effect quietly refutes much of climate science), or one of the earlier steps is false.

I'd like to keep (4) and (7): weather really is unpredictable in that way, and long-range climate projections are not astrology. But the argument seems to make keeping both impossible, so something upstream has to give. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? And if I want to keep both (4) and (7) intact, what do I have to say about the relationship between weather and climate that the argument as written is assuming away?
