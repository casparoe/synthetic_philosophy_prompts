I have been trying to work out whether a line of reasoning sometimes used in discussions of insect welfare—the scale argument—survives scrutiny. The idea is that the vast number of insects swamps even a low per-individual probability of sentience. By sentient I mean having at least minimal subjective experience. Here is the argument reconstructed as seven steps.

(1) At any given time there are at least 10^18 insects alive.

(2) For any individual insect, a reasonable observer should assign at least a probability of 10^-6 to the proposition that it is sentient.

(3) Since each individual insect has at least a 10^-6 chance of being sentient, the expected number of sentient insects alive right now is at least 10^18 × 10^-6, which is 10^12.

(4) If the expected number of sentient insects is at least 10^12, then it would be unreasonable to be less than overwhelmingly confident that there really are at least 10^11 sentient insects alive right now.

(5) Therefore, we should be overwhelmingly confident that at least 10^11 insects are sentient right now.

(6) If we should be overwhelmingly confident that at least 10^11 insects are sentient, then we ought to give substantial weight to insect welfare in decisions that affect insects.

(7) Therefore, we ought to give substantial weight to insect welfare in decisions that affect insects.

Go through the argument step by step. Is it valid? If not, exactly which step fails, and under what reading? If it is valid, is it sound—and if not, which premise should a critic reject first, and for what reason? I am not asking whether the conclusion is welcome; I am asking whether the inference has a technical defect, and if so, precisely where it is.
