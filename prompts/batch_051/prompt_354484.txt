I'm a novelist, and I keep getting dragged into this argument with another novelist in my building — he's just come back from observing a citizens' assembly, and his whole position these days hangs on one analogy, and I can't tell whether I'm being unfair to it or whether he's using it to win the argument without actually earning it. His line is: a lottery is just random sampling. We trust an opinion poll to represent the whole country precisely because the sample is drawn at random; a self-selected magazine poll, or a poll where the interviewers pick whoever looks approachable, is junk. So choosing a legislature by lot is the same move: the chamber becomes a statistically representative cross-section of the public, and elections are just a giant self-selection bias — the only people who stand are the ambitious, the well-funded, and the weirdly certain they should govern. So he says a lottocratic assembly wouldn't just be fairer; it would be epistemically better, because it would think the way the country actually thinks, in the same proportions. He's now writing a novel about cool it would be if the entire upper house were drawn by lot.

I want to audit the analogy instead of just arguing with him at the bar. Lay out what corresponds to what: in the lottocracy case, what plays the role of the population, the sample, the sampling frame, the interviewer, the survey question, the estimate, and the sampling error. Then go through the places where the mapping starts to fail — especially the fact that a poll measures opinions people already hold, while an assembly has to make decisions after people argue with and persuade each other, and the fact that a representative is supposed to act for people, not merely resemble them. Which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion he wants (that sortition is a better selection method than election), and which are cosmetic? Does his conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the polling analogy carrying the whole thing? If a random-sample-of-citizens isn't the right source analogy, say what a better one would be and whether it helps him or hurts him. And at the end, tell me how confident you are in your main claims and pick out the one point you're least sure of.
