I've been working through the normative-uncertainty literature and the surrounding forum discussions, and I keep extracting the same argument from the material — one that seems to prove the whole research program is trying to solve a problem that cannot exist. I find the conclusion unacceptable, but I cannot locate the step that breaks, and the argument is embarrassingly simple.

(1) The standard proposal says: when I am uncertain between moral theories, I should maximize expected choiceworthiness. I assign each live theory a credence, let each theory score the available actions for choiceworthiness, take the credence-weighted average score of each action, and choose the action with the highest average.

(2) Expected choiceworthiness is meaningful only if the theories' scores share a common scale. Suppose I am 60% credent on act-utilitarianism and 40% on a promise-centered deontological view, deciding whether to break a promise to save a stranger. The utilitarian score and the deontological score are expressed in different units; averaging them without an exchange rate is like averaging dollars and joules.

(3) A genuine exchange rate between theories could only be fixed by a standpoint outside both theories — something that tells me how much of one theory's value is worth one unit of the other theory's stringency, without already being one of the theories.

(4) No such neutral standpoint exists. Any exchange rate I adopt just is a substantive moral commitment, since it settles, in effect, how much a promise weighs against a life. That commitment is itself one more first-order moral view, in competition with the theories I was supposedly uncertain between.

(5) Therefore intertheoretic comparison is impossible even in principle, and expected choiceworthiness is undefined for any real case.

(6) Yet real choices under moral uncertainty keep arriving, and something must settle them.

(7) Whatever in fact settles them — a felt weighting, a hedging intuition, a temperament — functions as an operative exchange rate between the theories, and so by (4) it is a further first-order moral view.

(8) Consequently, anyone who claims to be morally uncertain is, in the only sense relevant to action, already committed to a further moral theory. Moral uncertainty as a distinct state is impossible.

If (8) is right, the expected-choiceworthiness approach is not merely mistaken; it is a solution to a problem that dissolves on inspection. I would most like to keep (1) and (6). Which premise do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost the rest of the view? If more than one step is suspicious, tell me how far back the problem goes and what a repaired version of the argument would need to give up.
