Hi—I'm a retired physicist, so I keep wanting to treat what is up to us as a clean control variable and then check whether the system behaves. I've recently fallen into philosophy, and the thing I can't stop chewing on is what the modern Stoicism revival has done with the old dichotomy of control. I'm sketching an essay and would like you to comment on the idea before I committ to a draft.

Here's the sketch. Epictetus opens the Handbook (Enchiridion) with a division: some things are up to us, such as judgment, desire, and aversion, and others are not, such as the body, reputation, and office. Modern self-help Stoicism has more or less rewritten this as advice to focus on what you can control. William Irvine, for example, says the dichotomy is really a trichotomy, because plenty of things (your health, a tennis match) are only partly up to you. My worry is that this quietly changes the thesis. For Epictetus the point isn't that effort should go where your causal leverage is best so that you get better outcomes; it's that your good should be located only in what cannot be taken from you, so that you cannot be thwarted. The modern version turns an ethics into a worry-management and productivity technique, and then people wonder why it no longer feels like Stoicism.

I want to argue two things. First, the trichotomy is defensible as psychological advice, but it's a distortion of the ancient view rather than a friendly repair, because it keeps the language of control while dropping the claim that your character—your ruling faculty—is the only thing that can really be harmed. Second, the more interesting question isn't which one is the real Stoicism but which one we should want, though I'm not sure the modern version has an answer to why I should care about my character once outcomes are the point.

Two things I'm genuinely stuck on. Is the ancient dichotomy a claim about what is causally in my power, or about what is attributable to me—what I'm the author of? Those come apart. And does anything in my argument hang on reading up to us one way rather than the other?

Could you comment on this sketch? I'd especially appreciate comments that separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the material, and that don't present a contested reading as if it were the consensus. Please give me exactly five numbered comments, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. If you think the essay is boring or the argument is broken, I'd much rather hear that than get polite hedging.
