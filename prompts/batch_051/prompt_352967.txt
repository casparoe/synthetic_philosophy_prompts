I've been trying to make sense of David Pearce's hedonistic imperative, and I suspect I've been softening it into a more familiar utilitarian position. The strong version of the view, as I understand it, says that suffering is not something to be reduced merely where doing so is convenient, but a biological capacity to be phased out altogether, through genetic engineering, neurotechnology, and what is sometimes called paradise engineering, and that this is a moral imperative rather than one charitable project among many. Could you explain the conceptual difference between reducing suffering and abolishing the capacity for suffering, and why the abolitionist conclusion is meant to be required rather than merely commendable? I am especially confused about two points. First, if pain no longer sets the lower bound of experience, what could still structure motivation: does the view collapse into a single flat state of bliss, or can gradients of well-being do the motivational work that pain used to do? Second, what would the view imply about wild animals, whose suffering has no human author and no near-term technological fix? I am not asking for sources or a reading list; I want the underlying reasoning reconstructed.
