I have spent a long time reading rationalist and forecasting blogs, and one thing I still cannot square is the way public inquiries and retrospective histories handle bad outcomes. When a financial collapse, an intelligence failure, or an industrial accident is examined afterwards, the report is usually written so that the disaster looks as though it was foreseeable, sometimes almost overdetermined; the central criticism is that people failed to notice what the retrospective narrative makes look obvious. Yet the same institutions that write these reports often insist elsewhere that decisions must be judged only by the information available at the time, and in contexts such as trading desks or clinical audit they treat judging a decision by its outcome as a recognised error. I want someone to reconstruct the implicit philosophical commitments of the practice of writing these reports. Specifically: what view of historical explanation does this practice presuppose — for instance, does it presuppose that later events reveal the probabilities that were there all along, or that an event is only explained once it is made to seem inevitable within a narrative? Which rival views does it quietly rule out, which does it merely fit well with, and where is it genuinely underdetermined between several theories? And what would have to change in how such reports are written, and in who is blamed for what, if the institutions rebuilt the exercise on the principle that outcomes carry no evidential weight about the quality of earlier decisions?
