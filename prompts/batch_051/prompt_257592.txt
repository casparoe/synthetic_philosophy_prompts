I am a PhD student in political philosophy working on informal norms, and I cannot settle the following asymmetry. In cities where table service is tipped, a visitor who leaves a modest but below-customary tip—say 12 percent where 20 is the local floor—is treated as having wronged a particular person: the server took the table on the understanding that tips are most of her wage. Yet the same visitor who crosses a four-lane road mid-block against the signal is treated as having done, at worst, something mildly unlawful, even in a city where jaywalking is a codified infraction justified by third-party safety and even a careful crossing can force a driver into a sudden stop. In practice the informal wage convention is moralized while the formal safety statute is not. Is there a principled justification for that asymmetry? If you defend it, defend it against the hard version in which the street is genuinely empty and the under-tip is one dollar below the local floor; and say whether your rationale also explains why queue-jumping, which wrongs no one in particular, is policed far more than jaywalking. If you cannot defend it, say whether we should de-moralize under-tipping, moralize jaywalking, or accept the gap as an inconsistency we live with, and what each response would cost.
