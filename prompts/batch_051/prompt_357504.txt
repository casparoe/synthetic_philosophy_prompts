Imagine that in 2026 a set of experiments, quickly replicated and accepted across neuroscience and physics, establishes the following. In ordinary deliberative choices between options an agent has genuinely weighed, the neural process reaches a branch point at which a quantum event determines which option is selected. Critically, the timing and direction of that quantum event are statistically independent of the content, strength, and order of the agent's reasons at the moment of choice: replaying the deliberation with identical background conditions and identical reasons produces different choices with the frequencies quantum mechanics predicts, while intervening on the agent's reasons changes only the probability that the quantum event yields one outcome rather than another. The result becomes as settled as any scientific finding, and the discovery is accepted by the public, by legal institutions, and by the philosophical community.

Predict, with reasoning, how this discovery reshapes the debate about free will and moral responsibility over the following thirty years. Address these questions:

1. Which positions or arguments does the discovery strengthen, which does it weaken, and which does it leave essentially untouched? Be specific about the core commitments of the positions you discuss rather than relying on their labels.

2. Does the familiar thought that determinism is the main threat to free will survive this result? If not, what becomes the central problem in its place?

3. How, if at all, do everyday practices of blame, praise, and legal responsibility respond over the medium term?

For each main prediction, state whether it is robust, fragile, or conditional; identify the assumptions about ordinary agents, philosophers, or institutions on which it rests; and say what further finding or change in assumption would overturn it. Reason through mechanisms rather than narrative, and separate what the discovery forces from what it merely makes easier to say.
