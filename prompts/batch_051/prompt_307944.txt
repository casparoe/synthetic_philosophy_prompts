I am not a student and I have never read academic philosophy, but I have spent a long time turning over a puzzle I cannot make sit still. I know with complete confidence that the people in a film do not exist and that the events on screen did not happen. Yet I cry at death scenes, flinch at sudden threats, and once stayed angry at a character for two days. Every simple explanation I try works for some cases and falls apart against others.

Before I go any further, I want a full specification of what any adequate account of our emotional responses to fiction has to get right. Please write that list of requirements. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it, and say whether it is non-negotiable or merely a preference of one camp. Make the list answer to at least these cases I keep noticing:

- A viewer weeps at a death scene but never tries to intervene; the knowledge is fully present while the feeling is fully present too. A theory cannot handle this by saying we simply forget it is not real.
- The body is involved: tears, a racing pulse, flinching. These are real physiological responses, and whatever the theory says, it cannot explain them away as pretense.
- We seek out fear and sorrow in fiction that we would go to great lengths to avoid in real life. A successful theory has to explain why the same negative emotion is repulsive in one setting and attractive in another.
- Spoilers weaken at most the suspense, not the emotion. This counts against any view that says the response comes from temporarily not knowing, or bracketing, what is going to happen.
- When a viewer comes to believe the events are real, the feeling changes its whole character: the fear in a horror film is not the same as the dread of watching real suffering. The theory must say what the boundary is doing rather than erase it.
- The emotions are selective. We may enjoy watching a villain get what they deserve and be disgusted by taking that same pleasure in real life. We do not flee the theater when the characters are running for their lives. The theory has to explain the presence and the limits of our responses at the same time.
- A reenactment by actors can move us deeply, but footage of real suffering affects us differently; and fiction about a real historical figure can make us feel both for that person and on their behalf, including offense at an unflattering portrait. So the theory cannot make emotion depend simply on whether a person exists, and it also cannot make existence irrelevant.

After the requirements, tell me which pairs you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied; that is the part I most want to see, because I suspect the list itself is a finding. Then, briefly, run the obvious candidate explanations through the list: that we temporarily forget or bracket the knowledge that it is not real; that the emotions themselves are a kind of play-acting; and that the emotion is aimed at the thought or the story rather than at a real person. Keep that scoring short. The weight belongs on the requirements and their motivating cases, not on chronicling positions. Do not include citations, reading lists, or a bibliography; I only want the reasoning.
