Here is a passage from Book XI, chapter 20 of Augustine's Confessions, in the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers translation:

“But what now is manifest and clear is, that neither are there future nor past things. Nor is it fitly said, ‘There are three times, past, present and future;’ but perchance it might be fitly said, ‘There are three times; a present of things past, a present of things present, and a present of things future.’ For these three do somehow exist in the soul, and otherwise I see them not: present of things past, memory; present of things present, sight; present of things future, expectation. If of these things we are permitted to speak, I see three times, and I grant there are three. It may also be said, ‘There are three times, past, present and future,’ as usage falsely has it. See, I trouble not, nor gainsay, nor reprove; provided always that which is said may be understood, that neither the future, nor that which is past, now is. For there are but few things which we speak properly, many things improperly; but what we may wish to say is understood.”

I am trying to work out exactly what Augustine is doing here. Earlier in Book XI he has argued that the past no longer exists and the future does not yet exist, and he has also worried that the present has no duration, which makes it hard to see how any stretch of time could be called longer or shorter than another. This chapter looks like his answer, but I cannot tell how radical it is meant to be. Is he recommending that ordinary tensed talk about the past and future be abandoned as literally false, or is he offering a paraphrase that preserves ordinary claims while revealing that they are really about states of the soul? And even if I accept that the past exists in memory and the future in expectation, I do not see how this helps with the measurement problem: a memory of a long-past event need not itself be a long memory, and an expectation of a far-off future event can be a brief expectation. Walk me through how this passage is supposed to solve the problem of measuring time, and say where, if anywhere, the solution depends on treating the mind’s own activity as the thing that is really being measured.
