I've been trying to sort out my actual views on animal ethics by going through concrete cases, and I'm hoping you can extract the principle behind them (or tell me there isnt one). Here's where I land.

- If I swap beef for chicken in my regular diet, I'm pretty confident that's worse: a steer yields a huge number of meals, a chicken yields maybe four, so per meal chicken kills vastly more individual animals.
- But a grass-finished steer that lived a decent cow life and was killed quickly feels worse to me than a factory-farmed chicken that lived in misery and was slaughtered at six weeks. So the quality of the life and death seems to matter, not just the number of animals killed.
- I don't think eating mussels or oysters is a serious moral problem, even though they're animals.
- I think someone who hunts deer for meat is doing something less bad than someone who buys factory-farmed pork, even though the hunter kills a sentient wild animal and the shopper doesn't personally kill anything.
- I get uneasy when vegans hand-wave crop deaths because harvesting wheat and soy kills field mice and insects. But I also don't think that makes a vegan morally equivalent to a beef eater just because both cause some deaths through farming.
- I don't think we currently have an obligation to reduce wild animal suffering by, say, vaccinating wild animals against disease or culling predators, even though I think a wild animal starving or being eaten alive is genuinely bad, and saying it's natural doesn't make it fine.

Is there a single underlying principle that gets all of these, or close? If you had to state it precisely, what would it be, and which of my verdicts would it force me to drop? And what would your principle say about the case I keep flip-flopping on: a lab-grown chicken breast that is biologically identical to chicken but no bird ever lived or died?
