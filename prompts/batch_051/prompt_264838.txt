Okay so me and my friend got into this long argument last night about whether you can ever be obligated to follow a law you think is unjust, and he kept coming back to this passage from Thoreau, which I've been chewing on all afternoon. It's from the part where Thoreau is responding to the idea that in a democracy you should go along because the majority has decided. The passage is:

"Must the citizen ever for a moment, or in the least degree, resign his conscience to the legislator? Why has every man a conscience, then? I think that we should be men first, and subjects afterward. It is not desirable to cultivate a respect for the law, so much as for the right. The only obligation which I have a right to assume, is to do at any time what I think right."

I get the basic idea—he's saying don't outsource your moral judgment to the state. But I keep getting stuck on that last sentence, because on one read it sounds like he's claiming there's literally no obligation except following your own conscience, which would make any stable society impossible. Is he actually saying that, or is there a distinction between what he calls "obligation which I have a right to assume" and some other kind of obligation he'd still admit exists? My friend says the point is that political obligation is always self-assumed and can't override conscience, but I don't see how that answers the worry, because a society where everyone acts only on their private sense of right seems like it would dissolve into everyone just doing whatever. Also, the question "Why has every man a conscience, then?" seems to be doing a lot of work, and I'm not sure what argument he thinks it makes—is it just a rhetorical way of saying conscience would be pointless if we had to surrender it, or is there something stronger going on? What exactly is Thoreau doing with the word "right" here—is it just a synonym for conscience, or is he pointing at some standard that isn't purely subjective?
