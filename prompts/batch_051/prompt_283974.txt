I've been reading Grice's Logic and Conversation for a seminar, and I can't shake a puzzle about an asymmetry in ordinary blame. Grice wants to say that what a speaker means goes well beyond what the sentence conventionally means: if you ask whether a mutual friend is still causing trouble, and I say "he hasn't been arrested recently," I haven't literally asserted he's a troublemaker, but I've clearly communicated it, and Grice treats that as speaker meaning. Now compare two people: one says falsely "he was arrested last week"; the other says truthfully "he hasn't been arrested recently" knowing you'll take it as "he's trouble." If you later discover you were misled, most people (and courts) are much harder on the first, even though both intended to produce the same false belief, and Grice's framework says both are cases of speaker meaning. Is there any principle that justifies treating the liar worse than the misleader? Or does Grice's account of implicature actually undermine our instinct that one is a liar and the other merely tactful? I want a real defense if there is one, and an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept.
