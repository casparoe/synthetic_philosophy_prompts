I've been going in circles on Hofstadter's careenium lately — the one with the little balls and flippers inside a slowly rotating carousel, where the low-level story is just Newtonian collisions but you can set the flipper angles so that the whole thing computes, like a logic circuit or even a simple symbol-manipulating program. The bit I can't settle is whether the symbol-level description (the gates, the tokens, the decision to reverse direction) is doing any actual pushing, or whether it's just a readable shadow of what the balls and flippers were going to do anyway.

Here's the variation I'd like you to work through. Suppose each flipper now has a small mechanical memory: a couple of extra balls and springs inside it so that it's angle depends not just on the current hit but on the pattern of the last three hits, and those patterns are themselves distributed across the whole ring rather than sitting in one place. You can also interact with the system only at the symbolic level — you type a new rule into the little calculator it implements, and the physical parts have to settle into whatever configuration encodes that rule. Does the standard careenium analysis (symbols as epiphenomenal patterns, no top-down push) survive this, or has something shifted because the symbols are now partly temporal and distributed? And what about if the careenium can read its own symbol-level output and use that to adjust the flipper thresholds — does that introduce a loop that makes the top-down story harder to dismiss, or does it just add another layer of the same problem?

Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion, on how the bottom-up and the emergentist/functionalist readings get affected by the memory tweak, the distributed encoding, and the self-reading loop.
