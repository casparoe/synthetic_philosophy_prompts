I have spent the last few years defending a position in decision theory, and I would like an honest map of what would count against it. The position is this. In a sufficiently large world—one containing very many agents facing structurally similar choices—an agent's decision is evidence about what other, similar agents will decide, and this evidential correlation can matter even when the decision exerts no causal influence on those other agents or on the shared outcome. On this view it can be rational to vote, to refrain from a minor act of pollution, to pay a tax one could safely evade, or to contribute to a public good, not because the act causes anything, but because choosing the cooperative act is evidence that one belongs to the better-coordinated set of agents, and one should act so that one's action, considered as evidence, favors the better world. Tell me what would count against this position. I want the arguments, counterexamples, and formal results that should move me, sorted by how decisive they are; I want to know which of these a committed defender would concede as genuine defeaters and which she would dismiss as answering a different question; and I want your honest judgment as to whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether, if it has, that is a problem for a position of this kind.
