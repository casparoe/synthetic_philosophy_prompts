A statute establishes a parenting-licence scheme and provides, in section 9(3): 'The Authority must not grant a licence unless it is satisfied that the applicant will preserve for the child, so far as is practicable, an open future — that is, a reasonable range of life-options among which the child will be able to choose as an adult.'

Now consider an application from parents who practise a deliberate programme of maximal optionality. To ensure that no future path is foreclosed, they move cities every year; they withdraw the child from any sport, instrument, or friendship as soon as it begins to become a settled attachment; they decline to state family values or plans on the ground that these are for the child alone to choose; and they expose the child to a wide range of activities without ever allowing one to develop into a lasting project. The child has encountered many options but has never been able to pursue one far enough to learn whether it fits.

Is section 9(3) satisfied? Work through the plain words, the evident purpose, what the drafters would have said had they anticipated this family, and what a reasonable applicant would take the clause to require. I am less interested in the licensing outcome than in whether any coherent reading of 'an open future' reaches or spares this family, and what that shows about what the clause was protecting. Throughout, separate what is settled about the clause's interpretation from what is genuinely contested about the idea of a child's right to an open future, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
