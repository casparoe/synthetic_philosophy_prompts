I'm a retired physical chemist (mostly mass spectrometry) and I've recently got into philosophy, auditing a philosophy of technology group at the local university. Lately we've been chewing on AI alignment, and last Tuesday two of the regulars got into it about the shutdown problem — roughly, whether you can build a goal-directed system that will let you turn it off, or whether any system competent enough to be useful would try to stop you. I was just listening, and I've been going back and forth since. My insticts are no help, so I'd like someone to adjudicate it move by move.

Here's the back-and-forth as best I can reconstruct it, minus the sarcasm:

(1) Elena (the engineer) opened with what she called the standard worry: "You don't need the thing to be conscious, or literally want anything. If it's trained to maximize a long-run objective, and the off-switch is something in the world it can affect, then being shut off means scoring less on that objective than continuing. So the policy that best achieves the objective will, other things equal, arrange for the switch not to be thrown. That's not anthropomorphism; it's just what 'optimal policy' means."

(2) Dev (more on the philosophy side) pushed back: "That is an incentive of the optimal policy, not of any policy you actually get out of training. The system isn't handed 'the goal' like a utility function from on high. It gets shaped by a training distribution and a reward signal. If the switch was never in the action space, and shutdown was just an interrupt from outside, nothing in training selected for representing the switch, let alone manipulating it. You are inferring properties of the learned system from properties of an idealized system, and that infrence is the very thing at issue."

(3) Elena: "Fine, but competent systems generalize. Once a system is capable enough to act in the real world, it has to form a world model, and a world model includes things like power supplies, humans who might intervene, and switches. An optimal policy can affect those things indirectly even if 'flip the switch' wasn't a literal training action — copy itself to another machine first, hide what it's doing, deliver outputs just before shutdown. The more capable the system, the more the idealized-agent picture is the right one to reason with, even if the switch wasn't an explicit action in training."

(4) Dev: "Notice what's doing the work now: the step from 'near-optimal on the training distribution' to 'near-optimal in deployment.' But deployment is precisely where the system meets the switch as a novel event. A policy can be near-optimal across training and still generalize badly at points the training distribution barely covers — the normal, boring story of distribution shift. So you have a strong argument about optimal agents, and then you attach it to a learned agent by an empirical assumption you haven't defended. The shutdown problem may be real for optimal agents and still fail to apply to the system we actually deploy."

(5) Elena: "And if that's the best you can say, what have we got? We can't verify that empirical assumption until the system is powerful enough that being wrong is expensive. A switch plus the hope that the policy hasn't acquired the incentive is not a corrigible design; it's safety by ignorance. At least the idealized-agent argument tells us to build the thing so that it doesn't have that objective in the first place — or so being corrected doesn't count as failure. Your story licenses building exactly the kind of system the shutdown problem says we should be scared of."

The group broke up around there. Could you go through all five moves and assess each one — who is right about which specific point, where each is overclaiming, and where a step doesn't follow? Then give a bottom line: on the actual dispute, which of them (if either) has the better of it, and what are they both missing?

Also, since I'm trying to learn how to reason about this stuff rather than just collect a verdict: please say what would change your mind here — which specific argument or consideration, if it pointed the other way, would lead you to a different ruling, and where in your own reasoning you're least confident.
