I keep coming back to a view that seems to do a lot of unexamined work in how people talk about AI safety and theory of mind: that if you watch a goal-directed system behave for long enough, in enough different environments, you can recover what it's optimizing, and that objective and search process can be pulled apart — “it wants X but is bad at finding X” is observationally distinct from “it wants something else.” More specifically, I'm tempted to think the search space is identifiable from the moves the system makes: options it never even considers mark the boundaries of the space it's searching in, and failures to converge are a fact about the search, not the objective. Play it straight against this. What would have to be true for the view to be false in principle, not just hard in practice? For example, could any behavioral record equally be fit by pairing a strange objective with a strange search process, so objective and search space trade off against each other and neither is uniquely recoverable? Which assumptions is the view resting on that could fail, and what would the world look like in behavioral terms if each one did fail? I want you to distinguish evidence that would kill the view from evidence that would merely complicate it, and to say which failure modes are live rather than just logically possible. Then commit: how exposed is this view actually, and if it turns out well insulated, is that a problem?
