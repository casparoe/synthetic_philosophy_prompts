I'm a physicist who works on nonlinear dynamics, and I keep encountering a thesis about history that gets summarized by the old proverb about a missing horseshoe nail that costs the shoe, then the horse, then the rider, then the battle, then the kingdom. Call this the nail-thesis: vanishingly small causes can cascade into world-historical outcomes; many large historical outcomes were not robustly determined by the large-scale conditions preceding them; and we retrospectively single out one small cause as decisive. I want you to translate the nail-thesis into the vocabulary I actually trust: phase space, trajectories, sensitive dependence on initial conditions, Lyapunov exponents and Lyapunov time, attractors and basins of attraction, bifurcations, and structural stability. Do this as systematically as you can, the way you would write the interpretive notes for a mathematical model.

Then give me the residue report, and commit rather than hedge: which parts of the nail-thesis map cleanly onto nonlinear dynamics, which parts you can only force across with distortion, and which parts have no counterpart in the formalism at all. In particular, take a definite position on whether the vocabulary of chaos theory lets me treat the nail-thesis as a legitimate historical insight, or whether the formalism captures the easy part and quietly drops what historians mean by contingency. Identify the one piece of residue you judge most important and say whether it is a defect of the dynamical-systems framework or a sign that the nail-thesis was less clear than it looked.

Format: exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, no introduction and no conclusion. Each item should state one component of the thesis, give its dynamical-systems translation, and record whether it survives cleanly, survives only with distortion, or fails to translate; the fifth item should be your overall committed verdict on whether the translation vindicates, partially distorts, or misses the historical claim, and which residue matters most.
