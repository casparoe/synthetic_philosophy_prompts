Design a classroom game for a 90-minute upper-level seminar on practical reason (14–18 students) that makes participants feel the force of Parfit's hitchhiker and "rational irrationality" — the idea that a purely self-interested calculator might do better to already be the kind of person who keeps a promise when keeping it is, at that moment, against his interests. I want a runnable activity, not a lecture: rules and roles with point payoffs that can be exchanged for something small, a timeline, and a debrief. Use a repeated pickup/trust game with drivers and hitchhikers, a public reputation ledger, and sealed moves, but no enforceable contracts, escrow, or externally binding promises — the credibility problem has to stay live. Set the payoffs so paying a promised amount genuinely stings relative to the value of the ride, or the nice students will just pay anyway. I'd like three phases: anonymous one-shot rounds (where the no-ride equilibrium should show up), reputation rounds with a visible ledger, and a final round with a twist that makes the ledger worthless so the unraveling problem bites. The punchline should show up in what happens, not in what the facilitator says: by the end, participants should notice that the hitchhikers who got the most rides were the ones who stopped calculating, or who had a rule about keeping their word, while the pure calculators loose out — and that the cautious drivers only trusted the non-calculators. In the debrief, map each phase to a part of the idea, ask whether the reputation-builders turned themselves into genuine promisers or just faked it, and be honest about one feature the game can't reproduce: the real hitchhiker and driver meet once with no ledger, and the deepest puzzle is whether you can deliberately make yourself irrational for your own good; the facilitator will have to supply that in words. Also include a paragraph on what predictably goes wrong when the activity is run (for instance, everyone pays anyway out of fairness, the last round collapses into no rides, or it devolves into unstructured negotiation) and how the facilitator should handle each.
