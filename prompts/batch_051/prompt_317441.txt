Now the problem.

I find myself stuck between a theory I admire and an intuition I cannot shake.

The theory. I am attracted to a safety or margin-for-error conception of knowledge: a subject knows that a condition obtains only if the subject's belief could not easily have been false—the condition would still have obtained in any subjectively indistinguishable but slightly different case. The theory's systematic virtues impress me: it explains in one move why you cannot know you will lose a fair lottery from the odds alone, why there is no knowable sharp cutoff in a sorites series from, say, feeling warm to feeling cold, why external-world knowledge is bounded in roughly the way skeptical arguments suggest, and why self-knowledge might reasonably be expected to run out at its edges. It also yields a striking result about luminosity. Say a condition is luminous when, whenever it obtains, the subject is in a position to know that it obtains. The safety view entails that no interesting or non-trivial condition is luminous. The argument is familiar: take a long series of inner states that differ stepwise but are pairwise introspectively indiscriminable, running from clearly cold to clearly warm. If feeling cold were luminous and knowledge required a margin for error, then feeling cold would propagate all the way down the series; since it does not, there must be cases where the subject feels cold yet is in no position to know it. The same machinery is standardly used against the KK principle: knowing that p need not put one in a position to know that one knows that p, because the knowing itself is just the kind of condition that can be lost across an indiscriminable boundary.

The intuition. And yet I cannot believe that searing pain is like that. When I am in intense, conscious, attended-to pain, the state seems to reveal itself: there is no gap between the pain and my being in a position to know I am in pain. It strikes me as false that there could be a case, indiscriminable to the subject from this one, in which she still feels exactly this searing pain but cannot know she does. If any condition is luminous, intense pain is; indeed, the denial of luminosity for such states seems to me to misdescribe consciousness, not just to impose a surprising epistemic limit. I hold this intuition firmly, not as a half-disowned prejudice. And I have a second, wobbly cousin of the intuition: for at least some simple, fully transparent knowledge—say, that I have hands right now, or that 2 and 2 are 4—it feels as though if I know it, I can know that I know it merely by reflecting on my knowledge.

The question. Tell me which should yield, and how far. Should the intuition be debunked—explained away as a conflation of being in a state with knowing one is in it, or as an overgeneralization from clear cases in which knowledge is safe? Should the theory be amended or restricted—for instance, by allowing self-presenting phenomenal states as an exception to the margin-for-error principle while keeping it for inexact and external subject matters? Or is there a reading on which the two are compatible—for example, one on which what is luminous is not the exact condition being-in-this-pain but a coarser, boundaryless condition such as being-in-pain-or-indiscriminable-from-it, which the safety view can concede? In your verdict section I want you to commit to one resolution and defend it: say clearly which side yields, or exactly how each is amended, and why the alternatives are worse. Whatever you conclude, state explicitly what your resolution implies for the KK principle.
