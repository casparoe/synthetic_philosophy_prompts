I've been using a rough three-part test to decide wether a liar-like paradox is self-referential, mostly so I can keep Curry-style paradoxes (where a sentence says its own truth implies some arbitrary claim) separate from Yablo-style ones. Here's the test:

1. Direct: the sentence contains a term that picks out the sentence itself, like "this sentence" or "the sentence in box 4."
2. Indirect: the sentence sits in a finite loop of sentences each of which mentions the next one, and the chain eventually comes back around (A says B is false, B says A is true, etc.).
3. Fixed-point: the sentence's truth conditions plug the sentence itself back into what is claimed, so its truth value is a fixed point of the operation its content defines.

Now Yablo's sequence is supposed to be a liar paradox with no self-reference. It's an infinite list S1, S2, S3, ... where each Sn says that every sentence later in the list is untrue. More precisely, Sn is true just in case Sk is not true for every k greater than n. No sentence names itself or any earlier sentence, and there are no finite loops.

(You can check the contradiction: if some Sn is true, then S(n+1) is not true, so some later S_k with k > n+1 is true, contradicting what Sn says; hence none of the sentences is true. But then S1 says exactly that none of the later sentences is true, which seems right, so S1 is true. Contradiction.)

Go through my three criteria and say whether Yablo's sequence counts as self-referential on each. Where a criterion doesn't decide the case, say why it underdetermines rather than giving a clean yes or no. Then give your overall verdict: is Yablo's sequence self-referential, and should I revise the test in light of it—drop a criterion, sharpen one, or give up on self-reference as a single well-defined notion?
