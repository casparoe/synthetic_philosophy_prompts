I have no formal training in linguistics; what I know comes from self-study and from learning several languages on my own. The thing I keep coming back to is a puzzle about grammatical categories. Descriptions of languages are organized around categories such as noun, verb, and adjective; nominative, accusative, and dative; preposition and postposition. These categories do a great deal of descriptive work, and textbooks treat them as though they pick out real groupings. Yet languages also disagree at the edges, and distinctions that look sharp in one language become uncertain in another. I have been trying to decide whether such categories are genuine joints in human language — things one might reasonably call natural kinds of linguistic cognition — or whether they are convenient fictions, imposed on a messier reality because description has to begin somewhere.

I would like you to take the noun/verb distinction as the clearest test case and show me how one could bring evidence to bear on the claim that it is a natural kind rather than a convenient fiction. Please design one or more concrete tests or observations, whether cross-linguistic, experimental, developmental, neurological, computational, or some combination. For each, specify what would be measured, what result would count in favor of the natural-kind reading, and what result would count in favor of the convenient-fiction reading.

I am especially worried about one difficulty, and I would like your design to confront it rather than ignore it. Identifying nouns and verbs in an unfamiliar language by their syntactic positions or their morphology seems circular, because that already assumes the categories one is trying to test. But identifying them by meaning seems to collapse a grammatical distinction into a semantic one. Explain how a careful test could break this circle, or at least seriously weaken it.

Finally, be precise about the residue. Suppose the test came out strongly positive. What exactly would that establish, and what would it fail to settle about whether these categories are natural kinds — for example, whether they are innate, whether they are universal, or whether they are mind-independent rather than stable products of how human beings use language? Please also say briefly whether the same approach could be extended to case categories or to adpositions (prepositions, postpositions, and their kin), and where it would stop being illuminating. The answer should be written in American English.
