Last week my girlfriend asked me why I love her, and I rattled off a list — she's funny, patient with my moods, she gets my sense of humor — and instead of being flattered she got quiet and said "so if somebody funnier and more patient showed up, you'd just love her instead?" I said of course not, but then she said then the list isn't the real reason. I don't have a good answer, and it's made me notice how I react to other people's situations. I want you to take my reactions to these five cases and tell me what principle I'm operating by, because I can't see it myself.

1. My uncle is still completely in love with my aunt after her stroke. Her personality changed a lot: she's irritable, doesn't laugh at the jokes she used to make herself, and has forgotten big chunks of their life together. He says he loves her the same as ever and it isn't just duty. I believe him. I'd call it love, not just a promise he's keeping or momentum.

2. My old roommate Dani broke off an engagement. She said her fiance was kind, smart, good-looking, reliable, wanted the same future — everything on her list — but she just didn't love him. I thought she did the right thing. You can't argue somebody into love from a checklist.

3. My coworker Marco says he loves his wife because she's gorgeous, loyal, hardworking, and a great mom. I had a twinge that this sounded like a job reference, but I couldn't say he was wrong. Those are real things about her, and they seem like real reasons.

4. My friend Priya loves her dog in a way I can only describe as ridiculous, and the dog is objectively ugly, anxious, and destructive. She says the flaws aren't even relevant to whether she loves him. I think that's still love, but its wierd — if a human had that exact list of traits and nothing else going for them, I'm not sure I'd say the same. Though maybe I would if I'd raised them? This one I keep flipping on.

5. A friend stayed for years with a guy who belittled her, and when we finally asked why she said "I just love him, love doesn't need reasons." I thought that was nonsense, and it let her treat a bad situation as romantic. Maybe love doesn't need to answer to a resume, but "no reasons at all" can't be a blank check.

My half-baked rule is this: love starts as a response to valuable qualities in the person, so it can't be totally arbitrary, but once it exists it's no longer conditional on those qualities still being there, so it can survive a stroke and isn't just waiting for a better offer. The reasons are the entry fee, not the membership. But I can already see that case 4 and case 5 pull against it in opposite directions, and I don't know whether the rule needs repair or my verdicts do.

Please don't just tell me the standard philosophical labels; I want the principle made explicit and then tested against the cases. Give me a JSON object with these fields:

- "principle": the principle stated in plain English, 2–4 sentences.
- "clauses": an array; each element has "clause" (one clause of the principle), "job" (what that clause is doing for the principle), and "motivating_case" (which of my five cases forces that clause).
- "case_by_case": an array; each element has "case" (1 through 5), "my_verdict" (restate my verdict), "principle_says" (what the principle implies), and "fit" (one of "fits", "strains", or "contradicts").
- "overruled_verdicts": an array of any of my verdicts the principle can't accommodate, plus a one-sentence explanation each.
- "new_cases": two or three cases I didn't list; each has "case" and "predicted_verdict" under the principle.
- "bottom_line": your honest judgment of whether one principle can capture all five of my reactions, or whether one has to go — and if so, which one you'd overturn and why. Also say whether my half-baked rule survives, and if it does, what needs to be added so it handles cases 4 and 5.
