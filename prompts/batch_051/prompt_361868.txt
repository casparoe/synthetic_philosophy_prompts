I'm in a value theory class and we've been stuck on spectrum arguments about whether 'better than' is transitive — you know, the ones where a bunch of tiny trade-offs add up to something that seems clearly worse overall. I tried to prove the spectrum people are wrong, and my friend says I'm cheating but won't tell me where. Can you go through this step by step and tell me exactly which step, if any, fails, and what the right version of that step would be?

(1) Suppose 'better than' is not transitive. Then there are outcomes A, B, and C with A better than B, B better than C, but A not better than C.

(2) But 'at least as good as' is complete: for any two outcomes, one is at least as good as the other. So if A is not better than C, then C is at least as good as A. So either C is better than A, or C is exactly as good as A.

(3) If a rational person judges one outcome better than another, then, if they're holding the worse one, they'd pay a small amount to switch to the better one. If they judge two outcomes exactly as good, they'd switch for free.

(4) Now start the person holding A. If C is better than A, they pay a cent to swap A for C; then pay a cent to swap C for B, since B is better than C; then pay a cent to swap B for A, since A is better than B. Their back at A and three cents poorer, and you can repeat this until they refuse or go broke. If instead C is exactly as good as A, the same cycle works except the A-to-C swap is free, so they lose two cents per round.

(5) A rational person can't be made to accept a series of trades that just leaves them where they started but poorer. So one of (1), (2), or (3) must be false.

(6) (2) and (3) are the obvious ones, so it has to be (1): 'better than' is transitive, and the spectrum arguments are just wrong about at least one of their little pairwise judgments.

I honestly think this is solid, but maybe I'm smuggling something into (3), or sliding between how things are better and what a person would actually do. Is this argument valid? If not, name the exact step and give the strongest version of it you can, and then say whether the argument still goes through.
