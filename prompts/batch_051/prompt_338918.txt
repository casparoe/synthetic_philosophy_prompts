My friends in a local democracy-reform group have been arguing for weeks about how to stop the same dozen names from cycling through every city board, committee, and council seat, and someone threw out a mechanism I can't stop thinking about. The city would replace its current council elections with a weighted sortition. Every adult on the voter roll gets a number of lottery tickets equal to the number of full years since they last held any municipal office—elected or appointed, paid or unpaid—with a floor of one ticket and a cap of fifteen. If you've never held office, the clock starts at eighteen, so basically every older person who never served gets fifteen tickets; someone who left a school board seat two years ago gets two; a sitting councillor gets one. Winners get a single two-year term on a nine-member council, can't hold any other municipal office while serving, and after their term they go back into the pool at the floor of one.

The surface effects are easy to see: the council skews toward people with no record in city government, and doing a boring low-level stint on, like, the zoning appeals board early in life torches your odds later. What I want to understand is the second-order behavior. Does this push ambitious people to strategically decline unglamorous appointments so they can stay "fresh" for the council lottery? Do parties or interest groups start running clean first-timers as fronts while the actual operators call shots from outside? Does anyone bother to develop real expertise if the system is engineered to punish having served? What happens to institutional memory when almost everyone in the room is new at once? And are any of these failure modes fixable—say with a separate chamber for experienced ex-councillors, or a cap that counts differently—without it collapsing back into a plain lottery or plain term limits? I'm less interested in whether it's fair in the abstract and more in how it would actually behave.
