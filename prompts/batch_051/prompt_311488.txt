While reporting a feature on the paradox of tragedy — the standing puzzle of why people voluntarily seek out artworks that make them weep, dread, and mourn — I found myself in a long conversation with a psychologist who proposed what she called a dissolution argument. The reasoning now seems to me to prove too much, and I want to know exactly where it breaks before I quote her in print. Here is the argument as she laid it out:

(1) If an experience has net negative affective valence, a person will not repeatedly and voluntarily seek it out for its own sake. (2) The overall affective valence of an experience is the sum of the valences of its constituent feeling-episodes. (3) Audiences repeatedly and voluntarily seek out tragic drama for its own sake. (4) Therefore, the experience of tragic drama has net non-negative affective valence. (5) A genuinely sad feeling has negative affective valence; that is part of what makes sadness sadness. (6) In tragedy, the sadness is the dominant constituent of the experience, and the residual pleasures of plot, verse, and performance are too weak to pull the sum back into the non-negative. (7) From (2), (4), (5), and (6), the sadness felt during tragedy cannot be genuinely negative, since a genuinely negative dominant constituent would make the sum negative. (8) But a feeling that is not genuinely negative is not sadness; so audiences are never truly saddened by tragedy, and the paradox dissolves because it rested on a misdescribed state. (9) The problem is that this proves too much: the same reasoning shows that the pain of a voluntarily chosen marathon, the burn of deliberately ordered hot chili, and the fear in a horror film are all non-negative as well, and therefore not really pain, burn, or fear. That conclusion is unacceptable.

I am most reluctant to accept (8), and most reluctant to give up (5) or the observations behind (9): real sadness is aversive, the runner's legs hurt, and the chili does burn. So I expect the correction lies somewhere in the arithmetic, but each of (1), (2), and (6) seems to be doing indispensable work, and I cannot see how to reject any of them without either severing the link between choice and affect or losing the distinction between an experience and its parts. I would like a diagnosis of the chain: which premise or inferential step should be rejected, what exactly does rejecting it cost, and how should a correct principle of affective valence be stated so that it preserves the real phenomenon of tragedy without licensing the marathon-and-chili generalization?
