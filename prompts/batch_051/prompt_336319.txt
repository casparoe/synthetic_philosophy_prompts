I am a novelist writing a book set in a religious studies department, and in the course of developing a character -- a mid-career comparativist -- I have spent several months reading the methodological literature in her field. One dispute has come to feel central to her intellectual life, and I find I can no longer tell whether it is a live argument or a ritual. The question is whether 'religion' is a coherent enough category to organize scholarly inquiry at all. The debate has run for decades without visible movement: the same exemplars recur, the same objections are made in the same order, and I have never come across an account of someone actually changing their mind.

One side holds that 'religion' is not a natural or universal kind but a modern Western artifact. On this view, the term took its present shape under the pressure of Reformation-era Christianity's emphasis on belief and interior conviction, and it sits awkwardly with traditions organized around ritual, lineage, practice, or political order rather than assent to doctrine. When European scholars set out to catalogue the 'world religions,' the objection runs, they squeezed living traditions into a Protestant mold: Buddhism was long presented in the West as a philosophy rather than a religion because it lacked a creator god, and Confucianism was filed under the rubric despite being an ethical, ritual, and administrative tradition that does not map cleanly onto the category. This side also stresses that the category does political work -- deciding which communities get legal recognition, which practices count as legitimate worship, and which are dismissed as superstition -- and that attempts to patch the concept by speaking of 'secular religions' such as nationalism only show that the term has been stretched until it picks out everything and therefore nothing.

The other side does not deny the history. It replies that every analytical category is historically situated -- money, art, kinship -- and that this does not make any of them unusable. On this view, what matters is not where a category came from but whether it can be used with explicit criteria; 'religion' should be kept as a second-order, comparative instrument, not as a claim about what insiders call their own tradition. Family-resemblance or prototype definitions allow scholars to compare phenomena across cultures while acknowledging fuzzy boundaries, and no proposed replacement has actually done the comparative work the term does. Critics, this side adds, are really attacking the folk-category and its uncritical deployment, not the disciplined technical use of the term.

My working hypothesis is that the two sides are mostly not contradicting one another. One is making a genealogical and political claim about the term's origin and its unreflective use; the other is making a pragmatic claim about whether the term can be disciplined for comparison. The fight is kept alive, I suspect, by translating everything into a single question of the form 'does religion actually exist?', which both sides then answer in different senses.

So my question is diagnostic rather than adjudicative. What kind of disagreement is this really: verbal, substantive, or two inquiries conducted under one heading? Do the sides differ about facts, about standards of evidence, about which cases count as data, or about starting intuitions that neither can argue for? Is a hidden question doing the work beneath the surface? And on the basis of your diagnosis, what would count as actual progress -- a distinction, an empirical investigation, a change in the question -- or should we conclude that the field cannot give the category up for institutional reasons and no progress should be expected?
