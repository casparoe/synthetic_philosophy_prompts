Yesterday evening I spent several hours arguing with a colleague about whether the impossibility results in algorithmic fairness should make practitioners give up on formal fairness criteria. Her position was that once one knows a calibrated risk score cannot simultaneously satisfy the standard balance conditions across groups whose base rates differ, there is little left for formal constraints to do: one picks the least bad model case by case and documents the choice. I ended up defending the opposite reading. On my account, the impossibility results are best understood as a taxonomy of which criteria conflict under a single shared decision threshold, and the true lesson is that the single-threshold pipeline does much of the apparent work. If one permits group-specific thresholds, the conflict does not disappear; it migrates, and the sharpest remaining tension is between error-rate balance and a procedural parity norm holding that two people assigned the same score should face the same decision. The value of the impossibility results, I want to argue, is that they force a deployment team to choose, explicitly, which side of that ledger it is willing to be wrong on rather than allowing the scoring pipeline to make the choice by default.

I am presenting this argument next month to an applied machine learning reading group at a company that builds risk-scoring systems. The audience is mostly production-focused engineers who implement de-biasing interventions that legal and product teams then scrutinize; there are also two statisticians who work with causal models and will dislike the word impossibility because the standard trade-off conditions are observational rather than counterfactual.

Prepare my question-and-answer prep sheet: the objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way these people would phrase it, with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is weakest. Tell me which objections I should concede up front, which I should hold, and include the question I am hoping nobody asks. The objection that group-specific thresholds are legally and procedurally harder to defend deserves especially careful handling rather than dismissal.
