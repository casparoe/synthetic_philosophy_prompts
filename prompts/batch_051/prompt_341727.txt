I got into it with a friend who's deep into Hofstadter's idea that the self is a pattern rather than a substance. His move is that when someone knows you very well, a lower-resolution copy of you keeps going in their head after you die, so you literally enough survive in other people. His favorite way to defend this is musical: a melody isn't identical to any single performance or any particular instrument — it's a pattern that can be transcribed for another instrument, played by a different musician, re-orchestrated, and still be "the same piece," even after the original score is lost. So just as a tune can outlive its first performer, a bit of your self can keep being played in someone else's head.

I'm suspicious but I keep failing at saying exactly why, so I want an audit rather than a thumbs up or down. What corresponds to what — the score, the performer, the instrument, the performance, the transcription, the fidelity of a cover band versus a professional orchestra, the fact that every performance is also an interpretation? Where does the mapping break in ways that threaten the survival conclusion, and where does it break only in decorative ways? What does the music analogy quietly smuggle in about selves — that they can be written down in a stable form, repeated without essential change, performed by any competent player, and appreciated from outside by an audience — that a brained, embodied, first-person self doesn't have? Then rule on whether the conclusion, that surviving in other people's heads is more than a warm metaphor, actually survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether we should just say people carry a rough model of you and not a piece of you. If some weaker version does survive, give me a better analogy for that weaker thing. And please seperate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this — don't make a contested take sound like the consensus.
