I am one of five commissioners on a national truth and reconciliation commission established by statute after a negotiated transition that ended two decades of internal armed conflict. Under our enabling Act, the Commission may grant immunity from prosecution for gross human rights violations committed with a political objective during the conflict years, provided the applicant has made full disclosure of all relevant facts known to the applicant. Our legal advisor has advised that the Act is silent on a question that now confronts us: whether the disclosure requirement is to be assessed for each act separately or for the application as a whole, and she regards either reading as defensible.

We must rule on the application of a 63-year-old retired naval captain who ran a military interrogation center for six years. He applies for immunity for the torture and enforced disappearance of nine named detainees. For eight of the nine, he has testified in detail: he acknowledges command responsibility, describes the center's procedures, and has led investigators to two unmarked graves. For the ninth case, a trade union organizer, he maintains that the man died under interrogation in 1991 and was buried at sea. Three witnesses, among them two former orderlies, have told our investigators that the detainee was removed alive and transferred to the military intelligence directorate, and a partial duty log appears to record such a transfer, though the entry is incomplete. Our investigative unit regards his account as probably not full on this point. The dispute matters because tracing the transfer would take the case into the intelligence directorate then commanded by a senior officer now serving as a minister in the unity government, who has never appeared before us.

The applicant's representatives argue that he has given more disclosure than nearly any other applicant; that the disputed transfer is a peripheral discrepancy after thirty years; and that refusing immunity now would deter future applicants and dry up the very truth the Commission exists to recover. They add that to deny the one officer who came forward, while those who gave the orders remain untouched, would be scapegoating. The family of the ninth detainee, supported by the Commission's victims' unit, answers that the Act's bargain is truth in exchange for immunity, and that granting immunity on an account that is incomplete exactly where the political stakes are highest would turn full disclosure into a formality and compound the victims' injury.

Please draft the memorandum I will circulate to my four fellow commissioners before our next sitting. It should open with a concrete bottom line — grant immunity in full, deny it in full, grant it only for specified acts, or defer with conditions and a time frame of your choosing — and it should then give the reasons a skeptical colleague could act on. It should take seriously and answer the strongest argument on the losing side, not a weak version of it.
