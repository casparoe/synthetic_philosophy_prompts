OK here’s one I’ve been chewing on. I’m a retired physicist and I only recently started reading metaphysics seriously, so I dont have a settled view and I’m probably using some of this vocabulary a little loosely. From what I can gather, folks who work on grounding and fundamentality often run with something like these criteria for an entity being fundamental:

1. It is not grounded in, and does not depend on, anything else.
2. It is part of what everything else is ultimately grounded in, or depends on.
3. It is a genuine part of reality, not just a useful representation or a bookkeeping device.

Now take the universal wavefunction. In the working physics I was trained in, the wavefunction is mostly treated as a device for computing probabilities of measurement outcomes; nobody thinks it is a concrete thing sitting out there. But if you take the Schrödinger equation literally for the universe as a whole, the state vector starts looking like the thing from which the behavior of all subsystems is somehow derived, and it does not obviously depend on anything more basic. It is also, strictly speaking, a function on a very high-dimensional configuration space rather than an object sitting in ordinary spacetime, which makes it feel halfway between a mathematical object and a physical constituent.

Does the universal wavefunction come out fundamental on the three criteria above? Go through the criteria one at a time, and be honest about the places where the verdict is unclear. Where it is unclear, say whether that is because the criteria are too coarse, or because the underlying physics and its interpretation are genuinely unsettled. And if you think the criteria themselves are the problem, say so and suggest what a better criterion would have to do.
