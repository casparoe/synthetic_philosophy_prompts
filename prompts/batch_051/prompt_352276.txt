I'm working on a taxonomy of the mechanisms by which a person's information environment can insulate them from corrective evidence, and I suspect my current list already captures the familiar ones: (1) homophilic network formation, in which people primarily associate with others who share their prior views; (2) engagement-optimized algorithmic curation, in which platforms select for content that reinforces existing interests and aversions; (3) selective exposure, in which individuals actively choose outlets and queries likely to confirm rather than challenge their views; (4) information avoidance, in which individuals steer away from topics or evidence they expect to find aversive; (5) geographic and institutional sorting, in which people's residential, educational, and professional settings become ideologically homogeneous; (6) partisan trust heuristics, in which a source's credibility is assessed mainly by whether it is in-group or out-group; and (7) collective discrediting practices, in which a community maintains shared narratives about why outside experts and media outlets are corrupt, compromised, or methodologically illegitimate. I have also been working with a rough distinction between mechanisms of omission, which prevent corrective evidence from arriving at all, and mechanisms of distrust, which ensure that corrective evidence is discounted when it does arrive; I understand the former to be characteristic of epistemic bubbles and the latter characteristic of echo chambers.

I would like you to add one further mechanism to the list: a distinct way in which a person's information environment can insulate them from corrective evidence, one that is not already among the seven above and is not merely a rewording, special case, or combination of them. It does not need to be something you are certain is real; a plausible, clearly specified candidate is fine. Then classify your proposed mechanism: does it operate primarily by omission, primarily by distrust, or does it cut across the two? Say whether it would be better described as sustaining an epistemic bubble, an echo chamber, or both. Finally, assess it honestly: is it a genuine addition to the taxonomy, or could a critic fairly say it reduces to one of the seven existing entries?
