I'm a PhD student in technology governance, and the analogy I keep running into in seminars and policy drafts is that frontier AI systems are relevantly like nuclear weapons. The argument runs roughly as follows. Both are dual-use capabilities whose destructive potential arrives alongside, or even ahead of, the ability to use them safely. Both tend to be produced by a small number of well-resourced actors under competitive pressure, and both are dangerous enough that ordinary product-safety regulation looks inadequate. From this people conclude, quickly, that AI should be governed the way fissile material and warheads are: a nonproliferation-style treaty covering the most capable models, international inspections of large training runs, export controls on the relevant compute, and a strong presumption against the proliferation of capable systems.

I want an audit of that analogy, not a general survey of AI risk. I know the printing press and the Industrial Revolution are sometimes wheeled in for the opposite conclusion, but the nuclear analogy is the one doing most of the regulatory work in what I read, so I want it tested specifically. Start by giving me the correspondence table: what in the nuclear case is supposed to correspond to what in the AI case — the fissile material, the enrichment cascade, the warhead, the test, the inspectorate, the nuclear-weapon states, the near-uselessness of weapons-grade material for legitimate industry, and whatever else carries weight. Then separate the disanalogies that actually matter for the intended conclusion from the ones that are merely superficial differences. I especially want you to identify which features of the source domain the analogy quietly imports that the target may lack: detectable physical signatures, a hard material bottleneck, state-centric actors, technical verifiability, the logic of deterrence and mutual vulnerability, and the fact that the dangerous artifact can be kept out of civilian hands by controlling a substance rather than controlling knowledge or capability. Then take a definite position: once those disanalogies are on the table, does the arms-control conclusion survive, or was the analogy carrying it? If it breaks, say what should replace it — either a better analogy or a direct argument that no longer leans on one. I would rather you commit to a verdict and defend it than lay out the options for me to pick from.
