Here is where I've landed:

(1) 1789 was not basically a bourgeois class revolution. The revisionists are right that the idea of a rising capitalist class overthrowing a feudal aristocracy misdescribes what actually happened.

(2) But I think the revisionists underrate material pressures once we get to 1792–94. The sans-culottes and the subsistence crisis set real limits on what the Convention could do, and you can't explain the radicalisation of the Revolution by discourse alone.

(3) I hold that the Terror was not inevitable from 1789. It came out of a contingent set of events: the war, the invasions, the flight to Varennes, the counter-revolution, the mutual suspicion those produced.

(4) Yet I also believe that the revolutionary discourse of popular sovereignty and virtue had a logic of its own, and that this logic made purges close to natural once the Convention accepted that there were enemies of the people hiding inside the Revolution itself. This is basically the Furet-ish line about the Revolution inventing a new kind of political legitimacy that had to keep finding traitors.

(5) I think the old slogan that revolutions devour their children captures something real, but I don't treat it as a law of revolutions. I think it's a feature of revolutionary situations where the criterion of legitimacy keeps shifting and nobody's revolutionary credentials are safe. So I want to say: the devouring pattern is real but not inevitable.

(6) Finally, I've been rereading Tocqueville's The Old Regime and the Revolution, specifically the part where he argues that the Revolution did not really interrupt the centralizing work of the Bourbon monarchy. On his telling, the revolutionaries demolished the remaining intermediate bodies and pushed administrative centralization further than the kings could have dared. The break was less clean than the revolutionaries believed. I find this long-run continuity thesis compelling, and I think it matters more for explaining what the Revolution actually did to the French state than any class-struggle story does.

My question is whether this package hangs together. In particular, I'm worried but unsure about the tension between (3) and (4): can I say the Terror was contingent while also claiming the discourse had its own logic of purification? And does (5) smuggle in a determinism I've officially rejected in (3)? Also, does Tocqueville's claim in (6) commit me to something about the Revolution's radicalisation that conflicts with (2)?
