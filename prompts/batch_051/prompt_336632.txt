I have been rereading John Maynard Keynes’s essay “Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren” (1930), and a friend built an argument out of one passage that now strikes me as proving too much. Here is the passage:

> We are suffering, not from the rheumatics of old age, but from the growing-pains of over-rapid changes, from the painfulness of readjustment between one economic period and another. The increase of technical efficiency has been taking place faster than we can deal with the problem of labour absorption; the improvement in the standard of life has been a little too quick; the banking and monetary system of the world has been preventing the rate of interest from falling as fast as equilibrium requires.

His argument goes like this:

(1) Keynes is claiming that the unemployment and economic dislocation of his moment are not signs of terminal decline or a structural contradiction in capitalism, but are analogous to growing pains: they are caused by technical efficiency advancing faster than the labour market can absorb displaced workers.

(2) If the pain is genuinely a growing pain, then it is evidence that technical efficiency and capital accumulation are working in the direction Keynes thinks they work—toward solving the economic problem, after which the good life, understood as the life of wise and agreeable activity rather than endless accumulation, becomes possible.

(3) If such changes are moving us toward that goal, then we should not impede them. Policies that slow labour-saving technical change, protect existing forms of employment, or soften the market’s signals to workers to reallocate would be like treating a child’s growing pains by stunting the child’s growth.

(4) Therefore, even severe short-run unemployment caused by automation should be accepted, perhaps welcomed, as part of the mechanism that eventually delivers the good life.

(5) This conclusion is strong enough to condemn not only narrow craft-protectionism but also nearly any transitional protection or compensation for workers displaced by automation, because compensation may dull the incentive to move to new work and thereby slow the transformation.

(6) That seems like it proves too much. It seems to license inflicting concentrated, severe harms on particular people now for diffuse benefits to distant future people, and it makes the severity of the transition morally irrelevant.

I am not sure which step to reject, or what rejecting it costs. If the flaw is in the inference from (1) to (2), then Keynes’s diagnosis may still be right but much less morally significant than it appears. If the flaw is in (5), then Keynes’s optimism may survive only if we add a heavy dose of redistribution and transitional justice that the essay itself does not foreground. Which step do you reject, why did it look plausible, and what does rejecting it cost?
