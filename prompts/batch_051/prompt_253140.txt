In a particular liberal democracy, a legislator may stand on the floor and say, without formal or informal sanction, that she supports a child-poverty bill because her religious tradition commands care for the poor. Yet if the same bill is challenged in court, the government's lawyers defend it with economic evidence, constitutional doctrine, and policy analysis, and do not cite scripture; a judge who invalidated the bill would not offer a private religious experience as a ground of decision. The same state pays chaplains in its prisons and armed forces, and begins each legislative session with a prayer. Reconstruct the view of public justification — of which kinds of reasons may properly support the exercise of political power — that this pattern presupposes. Does it presuppose that coercive laws must be justifiable by reasons that all reasonable citizens can accept, or only that officials must be able to produce a rationale that does not depend on any particular religious tradition in certain forums? Does the prayer-and-chaplaincy side of the practice presuppose a different account of what those activities are doing, and if so, is the overall pattern coherent? Say clearly which parts of the practice strictly presuppose a view, which merely fit well with one, and where the practice is philosophically underdetermined. Then describe what would concretely have to change if the state instead held that religious reasons may be offered in any political forum on equal terms with secular ones. Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on public reason, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
