so in my tech and society seminar, the guy next to me keeps falling back on the same line anytime someone says an artifact has politics: "its just a tool, like a hammer — a hammer doesn't care whether it builds the house or bashes in the wall, only the hand holding it does." From that he concludes roads, apps, and machines are politically neutral, and that calling a bridge racist or an algorithm authoritarian is just a category mistake. the professor pushed back with the Long Island parkway overpasses built too low for buses, the mechanical tomato harvester that only made economic sense at really large scale and pushed out small farms, and a more abstract point that some technologies seem to come with built-in requirements about how the surrounding society has to be organized — like nuclear power needing centralized, hierarchical control. she says the hammer analogy is doing actual argumentative work and none of us have actually audited it. so do that. treat the hammer analogy as an argument for the neutrality conclusion, not just a bit of rhetoric. first give the correspondence table: what maps to the hammer, the hand, the nail, the swing, the choice to pick it up? then go through the disanalogies. which ones genuinely threaten the conclusion that artifacts are politically neutral, and which are just differences that don't matter? what does the analogy quietly import — one user, one-shot use, reversibility, symmetric purposes, no prerequisites for how it must be used — that a parkway or a platform or a reactor lacks? and once those are on the table, does "tools are neutral; only people have politics" survive, or does it shrink into something much narrower? if theres a better comparison, give it and say what it can and can't carry.
