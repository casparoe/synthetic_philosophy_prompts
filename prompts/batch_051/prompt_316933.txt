My professor in an intro Chinese philosophy class keeps framing the Mencius–Xunzi debate as a fight over two metaphors, and then uses the metaphors to make the whole dispute look smaller than it is. On his telling, Mencius thinks human nature is like a sprout: the four beginnings of virtue are already in there, and moral education is just water, sunlight, and weeding. Xunzi thinks human nature is like warped timber: left to itself it's crooked, and you need the steam press and the carpenter's square — ritual, teachers, the accumulated forms the sages left behind — to straighten it out. So, the professor concludes, they aren't really disagreeing about whether human nature is good or bad; they're disagreeing about what kind of moral education we need, one that nurtures versus one that reshapes. He says the old fight evaporates once you put it that way.

I'm suspicious this is a case of the metaphors doing more work then they're licensed to do. I want an audit. Give me the mappings explicitly (a table or list is fine): what does the sprout map onto, what does the watering and weeding map onto, what does the warped wood and the steam press map onto, and so on. Then tell me where each metaphor quietly imports features of the source that may not hold of the target — a sprout has a built-in direction, timber doesn't, that sort of thing — and separate the disanalogies that actually matter for the professor's conclusion from the ones that are just vivid dressing. Finally, commit: does the it's-only-about-education conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or does a real disagreement about human nature survive too? And if the metaphors are misleading, what should he have said instead?
