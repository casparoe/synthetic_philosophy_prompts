I'm a physiotherapist, and I work mostly with people who've had strokes or orthopaedic injuries and are trying to relearn movement. I see patients once a week for about half an hour, eight or nine a day, and whatever I plan in that session shapes what they practise on their own all week, so the decision matters even if it's not life and death. I've noticed this thing over and over: some patients can do a movement perfectly well when it's part of a real task — reaching for a cup, stepping over the doorstep, zipping up a coat — but the same movement falls apart when I ask them to do it on command, as an exercise, out of context. With others it's the reverse. I've been reading a bit of Merleau-Ponty, just the ideas, and the stuff about the lived body and motor intentionality, about the body being oriented towards tasks rather than executing explicit commands, feels like it explains a lot of what I'm seeing. But I can't turn it into a plan for Tuesday morning.

Could you write me a reusable decision procedure for this? Something I can run in five to ten minutes when planning a patient's rehab, that tells me when I should try to rebuild a movement by breaking it down into components and giving step-by-step verbal instructions, and when I should instead set up a whole task or environment and let the patient's body reorganise around it. Give me the questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure stops with a clear recommendation, and where it hands the decision back to clinical judgement because the ideas run out or the situation is ambiguous. Then run it on three cases: a woman who can't straighten her fingers when asked but does it perfectly to put down a cup she's picked up; a man who wants to get back to bowls and can swing the arm fine in the clinic but loses it in an actual game; and a patient who keeps asking me exactly which muscles he should contract and I just want to tell him to stop thinking — I suspect an honest procedure will tell me to do something I don't feel like doing for that last one, and that's okay.
