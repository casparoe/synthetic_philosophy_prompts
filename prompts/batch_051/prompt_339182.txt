I am a novelist, and I am writing a book in which a character becomes convinced that the future is already as real as the present — that the moment of her death is already there, fixed in the same way the far side of a mountain is there before she has walked around to see it. In my research I came across the block universe view and the Rietdijk–Putnam argument for it: because special relativity gives us no frame-independent global "now" — different observers' present moments are differently oriented slices through spacetime, so that one observer's present can be another observer's future — some philosophers conclude that all times exist together and that the felt passage of time is an illusion of perspective. I have also seen the reply that the argument slides from "simultaneous in some frame" to "real," and that what the physics really gives us is only frame-relative simultaneity.

That conclusion would suit my novel, but I do not want to smuggle an unexamined claim into the book. If the block universe is a claim about the world rather than a choice of description, how would one test it? Please propose as concrete a test or observation as you can: what would be measured, what result would count in favor of the block picture, what result would count against it, and — this is where I most need help — exactly what a positive result would and would not settle. Would it establish that future events are fixed, that they are real, or only that temporal relations are frame-dependent? What part of the claim, if any, could no observation reach? Finally, tell me how confident you are in your answer and single out the point you are least sure of.
