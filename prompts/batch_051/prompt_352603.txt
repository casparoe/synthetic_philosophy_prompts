I'm putting together notes for a reading group on persistence through time — the endurantism vs. perdurantism debate (roughly: endurantists say an object is wholly present at each moment it exists, perdurantists say it's a four-dimensional thing spread through time with temporal parts). I've been collecting the standard arguments for perdurantism, and my list so far is:

(1) The temporary-intrinsics argument: a poker that is cold at t1 and hot at t2 is, on endurantism, the same wholy present thing at both times, so it looks like it's both cold and hot simpliciter; the endurantist has to relativize properties to times in a way that feels ad hoc, while the perdurantist just says one temporal part is cold and another is hot.
(2) The coincidence argument: a statue and the lump of clay it's made from are distinct (squash the statue and the lump survives), yet they coincide exactly for a while; perdurantism lets them be distinct things that share temporal parts during the overlap, rather than two whole objects occupying the same region at once.
(3) The fission/overlap argument: when an amoeba splits, both descendants seem continuous with the one original; perdurantism allows two four-dimensional worms to overlap in a shared pre-fission temporal part, like two roads sharing a stretch of pavement.
(4) The special-relativity argument: if simultaneity is frame-relative, there's no absolute now, and the idea of an object being wholly present at one privileged time starts to look hard to make literal, which pushes toward treating objects as extended in spacetime.

I'm fairly sure the perdurantist arsenal has at least one more move that I've seen in passing but can't reconstruct — though I might be misremembering. If you can recover the standard fifth argument, that counts, but I'd be just as happy with a genuinely new argument as long as it has a different structure from all four of them; then tell me honestly whether it's actually any good or whether it quietly collapses into one of the four.
