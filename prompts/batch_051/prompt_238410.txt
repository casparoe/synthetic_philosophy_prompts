I am drafting a novel whose decisive turn rests on an advance directive. Years before the story opens, the protagonist—then a precise, self-possessed woman—signs a directive refusing antibiotics and hospitalization should she ever develop advanced dementia. By the time of the novel she no longer knows her children, but she is gentle and content: she takes pleasure in her food, she keeps her flower beds in order from old habit, and when pneumonia arrives she says plainly that she wants to live. Her daughter, as her designated proxy, must decide whether the signed paper or the woman in the bed is to govern. I do not want the philosophy here to be mere plot machinery. What theory of the self—about whether the signer and the patient are the same person, and about whose authority should prevail—does the practice of enforcing advance directives presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it is merely compatible with, identify where it leaves the choice underdetermined between rival views, and say what would have to change if the presupposition were replaced by the view that the present self has final authority.
