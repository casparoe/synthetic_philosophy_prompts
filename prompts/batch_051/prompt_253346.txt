I am taking a seminar on decision-making under uncertainty, and a pair of cases came up that I cannot get to cohere. In the first, a patient will certainly die unless he receives a drug. The surgeon is 95% confident on the medical evidence that the drug will cure him and 5% confident that it will kill him. Everyone in the room, myself included, agrees that she should give the drug, and that a surgeon who refused because of the 5% chance would be irrational. In the second case, a patient is in an irreversible coma and, if treated, will live for years without recovering. The same surgeon is 95% confident that withdrawing life support is morally permissible and 5% confident that withdrawing it would be murder. Here the room splits: several people think the surgeon should hesitate, consult others, perhaps refuse, and that this hesitation is not a defect but a mark of moral seriousness. I notice that both surgeons act on a 95% credence, and that in both cases what is risked is a death for which the surgeon's act would be causally responsible. Yet we call the first 'following the evidence' and the second 'taking an unacceptable moral risk.'

Act as a philosopher working on moral uncertainty. Brainstorm the strongest candidate justifications for treating the two cases differently: for example, that wrongdoing is a different kind of badness from a merely bad outcome; that moral claims are not the sort of thing that admits of degrees of confidence at all; that there is no common scale on which 'permissible ending of suffering' and 'murder' can be weighed; or that the baseline difference — certain death anyway versus years of life lost — is doing all the work. Then test each candidate against nearby cases: a surgeon who is 95% sure a treatment is in a patient's best interests and 5% sure it violates the patient's explicit prior wishes; a businessperson who is 95% sure a supplier is clean and 5% sure it uses forced labor; a rescuer who is 95% sure a risky maneuver will save a climber and 5% sure it will kill them both. Be honest about which justifications survive contact with the cases.

I am not asking only what the surgeon should do in the second case. I am asking whether the asymmetry between empirical and moral uncertainty is defensible at all, and if it is, what principle underwrites it. Think carefully; do not settle for the first plausible principle. At the very end of your answer, on its own line, write \answer{...} where the inside is exactly one of: keep; modify; give up. Nothing should follow that line.
