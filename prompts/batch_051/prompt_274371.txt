So I'm taking a course on death, autonomy, and the self this semester, and the placement for the course has me shadowing the clinical ethics service at a teaching hospital. The team there says the most common 2 a.m. call is some version of the following: a patient no longer has capacity, there's an advance directive in the chart, and the patient is now saying or doing something that doesn't match the directive. The classic one is a woman with dementia who years ago wrote that she wouldn't want antibiotics or a feeding tube, and now she seems happy, eats whatever the nurses give her, and smiles when her family visits. But they also get directives signed during a depressive episode, a wallet card from a patient raised Jehovah's Witness who hasn't practiced in a decade, and an 18-year-old's care plan that the family insists was only meant to keep their options open. The residents want a one-page flowchart they can actually use on a night shift, not a seminar; the attending has to sign off before anyone overrides a directive, and the stakes are pretty much what you'd expect.

The attending asked me to draft that flowchart as my term project. So here's what I'd like: a decision procedure for deciding when to honor an advance directive over the patient's current wishes or behavior. Make it a sequence of yes/no questions a tired resident can run at the bedside in about five minutes, with each question phrased in plain terms, and mark the points where the procedure can't decide because it's a values call rather than a factual one. Then run the procedure on three cases: the dementia patient above; a currently-competent patient in the ER who refuses a blood transfusion but whose twenty-year-old wallet card directs transfusion; and a patient with bipolar disorder whose directive was written during a manic episode and who is now admitted for something unrelated. I'd also like you to flag any case where the procedure gives an answer that feels wrong, or where you yourself would pause and call the ethics consult instead.
