I have recently had to confront the regress of justification in a setting where it is no longer a textbook puzzle. I sit on a hospital ethics committee, and after one of our recommendations was challenged, the reviewing body asked us to state the ultimate basis for the principle we had relied on. When we offered a deeper reason, they asked for the basis of that, and I realized I could not honestly say what we are doing when we give reasons, or when a reason is good enough to stop. The familiar positions—foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism—seem to offer three exits: some considerations must be basic or self-supporting; our reasons support one another in a web with no unique foundation; or the chain of reasons may extend without end, and this is not a defect. I do not need to settle the philosophical dispute, but I do want to stop gesturing at these options.

I plan to spend roughly a year of serious study on this, not to become a professional epistemologist but to be able to give an honest account of what I am doing when I endorse the committee's reason for a decision. Before I begin, I want to know which unresolved questions in the structure of justification actually deserve that year. I have come across several candidate issues: whether a coherent system of mutually supporting reasons can justify anything unless it is anchored somehow in experience; whether calling a reason "basic" or "self-justifying" names a real epistemic property or merely marks the place where we decline to go further; whether an infinite chain of reasons must be actually available to a finite mind in order to justify, or whether the possibility of continuing suffices; and whether the regress is a genuine problem at all or an artifact of an over-demanding picture of giving reasons.

Please identify what you take to be the main open questions in this area as they would concern someone in my position, and rank the two or three you would put first for a year of non-specialist study with my practical aim in view. Defend the ordering rather than merely presenting it. For each question, say how far upstream it is of the others, how much difference the answer would make to what I actually say and do on the committee, and what would count as real progress on it within a year. Also identify at least one question that is frequently discussed but that you would deprioritize for me, and explain why settling it would leave my practice largely unchanged.
