I'm a PhD student working on practical rationality, and I've been trying to put weakness of will and hyperbolic discounting into a single framework. The framework has led me to an argument I can't accept, because it seems to show that the very thing I wanted to explain is mostly an illusion. Here is the argument as I've reconstructed it.

(1) An agent acts akratically iff she intentionally does ψ while holding, at the time of action, an all-things-considered judgment that an alternative φ is better.

(2) If an agent intentionally does ψ while φ is a live option, then ψ is what she most prefers at that moment; otherwise the action would not be attributable to her as the action it is.

(3) Hyperbolic time preference is a way of valuing delayed rewards in which the near future is overweighted relative to a consistent exponential curve. Its signature consequence is preference reversal: from a distance, the agent most prefers the larger later reward (finishing the paper, remaining a non-smoker), but as the smaller sooner reward becomes imminent (checking messages, one more cigarette), it comes to be most preferred.

(4) What an agent most prefers at a time just is what she judges best at that time, all things considered; if 'prefers' does not mean that in (2) and (3), then preference is doing no explanatory work in the account.

(5) By (3), the person who lights up, or opens the inbox, at the moment of acting most prefers the sooner, smaller reward.

(6) By (4), she therefore judges at that moment that the sooner, smaller reward is better, all things considered.

(7) By (1), her action is not akratic: it agrees with her all-things-considered judgment at the time.

(8) So paradigm cases of weakness of will are simply preference reversals across time, not akratic actions. But that seems to prove too much: the smoker can sincerely say, while lighting up, that she still judges refusing the cigarette to be better, all things considered, and that she is giving in against that judgment.

I'd like to keep the idea that hyperbolic discounting explains real akrasia rather than explaining it away, and I don't want to reject the intentional-action premise outright. So my question is: which step should be rejected, or is the argument valid with a conclusion I should simply accept? If a step is false, explain why it initially looked compulsory and what the best available fix costs for the larger account.
