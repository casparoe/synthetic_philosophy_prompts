For a school assignment I have been reading about Peter Abelard's claim that sin lies in consent, not in the outward act—and, as I understand it, not even in the desire or appetite itself. I have been trying to extend that view with a distinction I have not seen stated in exactly this way, and I would like someone to check it honestly.

I call the two sides act-consent and policy-consent. Act-consent is consent directed at a particular action in its full particularity: consenting to tell this lie, now, to this person, under these circumstances. Policy-consent is consent directed at a standing rule or plan that authorizes a whole class of actions: resolving, for instance, that if my experiment fails I will falsify the data, or that I will lie whenever it would spare me serious embarrassment. The policy by itself performs no action; it just makes a class of future acts available to me as options I have already accepted in advance.

My central claim is that Abelard's criterion may be right about act-consent but too narrow about sin. I suspect policy-consent is itself morally assessable, and sometimes already sinful, before any particular occasion to act has arisen. My reason is that a person never consents to an act in all its brute particularity anyway; one consents to an act under a description, and the description carries the moral content. Consenting to "lie whenever it protects my reputation" seems to me more revealing of character, and more clearly objectionable, than any single lie later executed under that policy—even if no occasion ever arrives.

I also worry that the distinction may collapse. Every act-consent might just be policy-consent with a time and place filled in, and every policy-consent might just be an act-consent I have not yet performed but have already decisively endorsed.

So my questions are these. Is the distinction between act-consent and policy-consent a genuine one, or only verbal? Does it already exist under another name, and if so should that discourage me? And is my substantive claim defensible—that policy-consent can be sinful entirely on its own, apart from any particular act? Please reconstruct my idea in its strongest form, then give me the three strongest objections to it, and tell me whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable.
