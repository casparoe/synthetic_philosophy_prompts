I'm an occupational therapist in an outpatient neuro rehab clinic. Several times a week I have to write an assessment note that decides whether a patient gets rehab, what kind, and whether insurance will cover it, and I keep hitting a wall with patients who can do a movement when they're not thinking about it but can't do it on command — or the reverse. A colleague lent me a book on Merleau-Ponty and I got interested in the idea that the body has its own kind of know-how, motor intentionality, where the world shows up as solicitations to act rather than as neutral objects to think about. I want to actually use that in the clinic. Could you turn it into a one-page checklist I can run in about twenty mintues, something my therapy aides and I can use to sort out whether a patient's problem is mostly motor-intentional — a breakdown of that absorbed, habitual relation to a task — as opposed to strength or coordination, perception, or motivation, and to flag what kind of treatment each points to, with the attending physican signing off on the final category? It should be a decision procedure: questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer, where the procedure stops with a no or hands off to someone else. Then run it on two cases: a stroke patient who can pick up a glass to drink but can't mime picking it up when asked to demonstrate, and a patient with phantom limb pain who says the missing hand is frozen in a cramped position. And be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and where this framework might push me to a conclusion I don't like.
