I have spent the last several years on a hospital ethics committee, and I have arrived at a settled theoretical view: there are no genuine moral dilemmas. I do not mean that hard choices are rare or easy; I mean that in any apparent conflict between two all-things-considered obligations, at least one of them was never all-things-considered binding in the first place. It was either a merely prima facie duty that is outweighed by the other, or it depended on a false factual premise, or it lapses because it cannot be jointly fulfilled. I rest this on the familiar structural arguments: if one genuinely ought to do A and genuinely ought to do B, then one ought to do A-and-B, and if one ought to do something then one can do it. A situation in which A-and-B is impossible therefore cannot contain two real all-things-considered oughts. The regret, grief, or sense of unfinished business after a hard choice I explain as the felt weight of the defeated prima facie duty, not as evidence that the agent did anything wrong.

What I want is a careful map of what would count against this view. I know the stock objections; what I need is an honest assessment of their force, because I use this view when I vote on cases and I am worried about how easy it is to insulate. Sort the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each say what the world, or our moral phenomenology, would have to look like for it to be a genuine counterexample rather than pressure the position can absorb. Consider at least the following: guilt-like remainders that persist after the correct choice and seem to demand apology, compensation, or expiation rather than mere regret; conflicts between requirements drawn from independent and apparently non-comparable sources, where no priority ordering seems available even in principle; the possibility that the no-conflict axiom and agglomeration are the wrong formalization, and that moral conflicts should be admitted in deontic logic while carefully restricting what follows from them; and the suspicion that the position survives only by an unearned appeal to invisible rankings or to a right answer that is in principle unknowable. For each, say which parts a committed no-dilemmas theorist should concede as counting against the view, and which they can reasonably dismiss as re-describable within it. End by saying whether the position has, in your judgment, been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that is a defect for a view of this kind in ethics or a harmless clarification.
