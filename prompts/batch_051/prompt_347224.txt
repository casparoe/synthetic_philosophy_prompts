So I'm retired now — spent 35 years in a wet lab doing molecular biology — and I've been reading philosophy of mind, mostly because of the debates about whether AI systems could suffer. I keep getting pulled toward a functionalist picture: what matters for the mind is the causal structure of the system, not the stuff it's made of, so any system that implements the right kind of computation is, in the morally relevant sense, conscious in the same way a brain is. It's elegant, it's substrate-neutral, and it gives a clean answer to the moral question — you attribute sentience by checking whether the right computational profile is there, carbon or silicon be damned.

But there's a thought experiment that's been bugging me. Imagine a machine with a giant internal table that pairs every possible input with the exact output a human brain would produce, and it just looks up the answer and prints it. Behaviorally indistinguishable from a person, and if you define the "computation" purely by inputs and outputs, it implements the same function as a suffering brain. But I cannot convince myself that this thing is feeling anything when it prints "I'm in agony." And if the table is precomputed, then it's in agony whenever some pencil-and-paper process works its way down the list, which seems obviously wrong.

The problem is, I don't see how to reject that conclusion without abandoning the clean substrate-neutral criterion I started with. Once I say the implementation matters, not just the function, I need a non-arbitrary story about which implmentations count — and every story I've tried either sneaks biology back in or ends up saying that almost nothing, or almost everything, is conscious.

Actually wait — maybe "the computation matters" doesn't mean "the input-output function matters." Maybe it means the internal causal process matters: the way the answer is generated, whether there's the right kind of loop, the right temporal dynamics, the right kind of recurrence or self-model. But then I still need a criterion for which of those features are the morally relevant ones, and I'm back in the same mess.

I don't want a canned verdict like "functionalism is right, bite the bullet" or "trust your intuition and go with some richer theory." I want to know, from someone who thinks about this professionally: what would it take for the intuition about the lookup table to be the thing that gets debunked and dropped? What would it take for the functionalist idea to be amended so it can rule out lookup tables without collapsing into biological chauvinism? And is there a stable third reading where both survive — e.g., where the lookup table isn't a realizer of the right computation at all, but only because we've been sloppy about what "computation" means? Give me the strongest version of both sides, then your negotiated verdict, and be specific about which move you'd make and why.
