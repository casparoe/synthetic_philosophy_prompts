I've been working through the wrong kind of reasons problem, and I hit a variant I can't settle. The stock case is a demon who threatens to harm you unless you admire him. On the face of it you have a reason to admire him — it would save your skin — but the standard view is that this is a state-given reason: a reason to bring it about that you admire him, rather than an object-given reason that counts in favor of his actually being admirable. The threat does not make him admirable. In the same way, being offered money to like a dull film bears on the usefulness of the attitude, not on what the attitude is about. The hard part is drawing that boundary without also ruling out legitimate reasons for attitudes.

Here is the variant. Change the required attitude from admiration to fear, and have the demon threaten to torture you unless you are genuinely afraid of him. The incentive looks structurally identical to the original: fear would avert the harm, so there is a state-given reason to get yourself to fear him. But now the very fact that generates that incentive — his disposition to harm you if, and only if, you do not fear him — seems to make him dangerous, and dangerousness is exactly the kind of feature fear is supposed to answer to. So the move that makes the reason state-given appears to manufacture an object-given reason at the same time. Does the state-given/object-given distinction collapse in this variant? Or do we get two distinct reasons arising from one fact, and if so how exactly do they come apart? Work through each candidate reason, and consider the obvious objection that a purely conditional disposition to harm is not the right sort of dangerousness for fitting fear. Then tell me whether, and in what form, the original problem survives the swap.
