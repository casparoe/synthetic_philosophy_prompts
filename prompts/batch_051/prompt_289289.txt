I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am working on a long-form piece about a problem that keeps surfacing in public debates over pandemic policy, long-term technological risk, and extreme-poverty spending: how a person or institution should act when uncertain about which moral theory is correct. The standard moves — follow your best guess, or fall back on common sense — strike me as exactly the kind of unexamined default the piece should pressure-test. Before I interview philosophers and begin reading the competing proposals in earnest, I want a specification of what any adequate account of decision-making under moral uncertainty would have to deliver.

Write the desiderata: the cases it must handle, the distinctions it cannot collapse, the structural constraints it must satisfy, and the phenomena it must explain, with the motivating case or argument behind each requirement. Among the questions the list should answer, though please go well beyond these:

- Must an adequate account unify ordinary empirical risk and moral uncertainty in a single expected-value calculation, or is there a principled division of labor between them?
- What must it say, if anything, about comparing how much different moral theories favor an option, when the theories may not share a scale of value and some categorically forbid the trade-offs others invite?
- How must it handle tiny credences in radically high-stakes moral views without either dismissing genuine catastrophic risk or letting a bizarre view dominate every decision — the fanaticism problem?
- What forbids the meta-theory from quietly reconstructing every first-order theory as a maximizer of some value, thereby misrepresenting deontological or virtue-theoretic views?
- Must it apply to uncertainty about itself, and what follows if its first-order and higher-order recommendations conflict?
- Is it required to say how credences over moral theories are formed and revised, or may it legitimately treat them as fixed inputs?
- Which demands arising from public decision-making — justifiability to others, feasibility for institutions, susceptibility to gaming — are genuine adequacy conditions rather than practical afterthoughts?

For each requirement, state whether it is non-negotiable for any adequate account or merely a preference of one camp, and explain the case that makes it a requirement. At the end, flag any subsets of desiderata you suspect are jointly unsatisfiable or in tension; that is the part I most want to publish, and I would rather have the conflict stated plainly than have it glossed over. Then name the single requirement you think does the most work in separating adequate accounts from merely plausible-looking ones. Finally, in a short table or several lines, indicate how the main contenders fare against your list: maximizing expected choiceworthiness, where one chooses the option with the highest credence-weighted average of each theory's evaluation; ordinal or voting-style aggregation, which minimizes reliance on strength-of-preference comparisons; threshold constraints, such as ruling out any option that at least one minimally plausible theory categorically forbids; and following the theory one has most credence in, unless it is silent. Keep that scorecard brief; the desiderata are the point.
