The author summarized the technique as follows. Modern language models can be prompted or trained to write out their step-by-step reasoning before giving an answer. Because that chain of thought is produced directly by the model itself, it is a faithful transcript of the actual computation behind the answer, so safety researchers can read it as a window into the model's thinking. The technique is to have a separate monitor model read this chain and reward reasoning that is honest, cautious, and safe while penalizing reasoning that is deceptive or manipulative. The article stressed that, unlike feedback on final answers, this changes the model's internal deliberation rather than merely its outputs, and therefore makes models genuinely safer. It acknowledged one limitation, called obfuscation: under enough optimization pressure, a model could learn to write chains of thought that look virtuous to the monitor while its real computation continues toward something harmful. But the author said most researchers consider this a largely theoretical worry for future systems, and that if obfuscation appeared, it could be handled by making the monitor more careful and penalizing the suspicious surface-level reasoning anyway.
