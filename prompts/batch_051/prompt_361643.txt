I help lead a monthly discussion group at my parish. A few weeks ago, after a session on providence, one of the regulars—a retired engineer with no formal philosophy—stayed behind and put the old question to me: can God create a stone so heavy that God cannot lift it? I gave him the standard answer in summary form and sent him home, but I came away convinced that I had erased the problem rather than taught him anything, since he could now recite the conclusion without having felt why it was forced. Next month I have ninety minutes with about a dozen adults, several of them devout and a few skeptical, to do this properly.

I want the session to be built around this passage, from Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae, First Part, Question 25, Article 3, in the English translation by the Fathers of the English Dominican Province:

"It remains therefore, that God is called omnipotent because He can do all things that are possible absolutely; which is the second way of saying a thing is possible. For a thing is said to be possible or impossible absolutely, according to the relation in which the very terms stand to one another, possible if the predicate is not incompatible with the subject, as that Socrates sits; and absolutely impossible when the predicate is altogether incompatible with the subject, as, for instance, that a man is a donkey."

And, a little later in the same article:

"Therefore, everything that does not imply a contradiction in terms, is numbered amongst those possible things, in respect of which God is called omnipotent: whereas whatever implies contradiction does not come within the scope of divine omnipotence, because it cannot have the aspect of possibility. Hence it is better to say that such things cannot be done, than that God cannot do them."

Do not explain the passage to me. Plan how I should teach it. In particular, work out the explanatory arc from where the group will begin—the intuition that an omnipotent being can simply do anything at all—to the point where they can see for themselves that the stone challenge rests on a pseudo-task. What distinction has to be in place before the passage can do its work, and in what order should the components come so that Aquinas's conclusion feels inevitable rather than like special pleading? What should the carrying example be: the stone itself, or something less freighted such as making a square circle or making two and two add to five—and what must the example have and what must it lack if it is to do this job? Which misconceptions should I expect my group to form, for example that Aquinas has quietly weakened omnipotence, that he is merely changing the subject to logic, or that the real answer is that God can after all do contradictions, and at which point in the sequence should each be headed off rather than left to harden into an objection? What should I openly decline to explain at this level, given that I do not want the evening to become a treatise on modal logic or on whether God can change the past? And what one question or exercise at the end would show me that they have actually acquired the distinction rather than learned to repeat it?

Finally, please tell me how confident you are in your interpretation of Aquinas's move and in your predictions about how the group will misread it, and single out the point you are least sure of.
