Imagine a nuclear-armed state adopts the following mechanism. It publicly registers a set of red lines, each attached to a response lottery fixed in advance. If another state crosses a red line—say, invades a non-nuclear ally the first state has sworn to defend—the first state does not deliberate about whether the line was crossed or what the response should be. Instead, within twenty-four hours it runs a tamper-evident random draw with three possible outcomes: with probability 0.70 it makes no military response; with probability 0.25 it launches a week of conventional air and missile strikes against the forces that crossed the line; and with probability 0.05 it detonates a single low-yield nuclear weapon at high altitude over an uninhabited stretch of the adversary's territory as a demonstration. The probabilities, the trigger conditions, and the response packages are published in advance; the draw is overseen by neutral inspectors and broadcast afterward; and the state's constitution purports to make the draw binding on both the executive and the military command.

Treat this strictly as a mechanism to be analyzed, not as a proposal you are being asked to endorse or condemn. I want to know how it would behave, not whether it is a good idea. What would it incentivize among potential adversaries, allies, and the state's own political and military leaders? How would each of those actors try to game or adapt to it? How would its deterrent effect and its credibility evolve over repeated crises and near-misses? What are the most serious failure modes, and which of them could be reduced by changing the probabilities, the trigger conditions, the timing or transparency of the draw, or the character of the responses? In particular, trace the second-order effects on crisis bargaining and on the norm against nuclear use. As you answer, state how confident you are in your main claims and identify the single point you are least sure about.
