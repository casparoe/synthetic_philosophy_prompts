A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about how to sort minds into Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian, and we ended up disagreeing over nearly every interesting case. As I understand the four rungs, a Darwinian system only tests alternatives across generations: variation is introduced at the genetic level and the environment kills off the bad ones before they reproduce. A Skinnerian system also varies and selects within its own lifetime, by trial and error plus reinforcement, but it does not preselect its candidates — the world absorbs the failures. A Popperian system keeps an internal model of the environment, so most candidate actions die in simulation before any actual action is tried. A Gregorian system adds cultural tools — words, notation, diagrams, written procedures — to the loop, so the internal model itself is improved by artifacts other minds have built.

I want to know whether a single principle captures my verdicts on cases, or whether the taxonomy needs repair. My verdicts, roughly in order of confidence:

- A bacterium whose responses are fixed by its genes: Darwinian, no hesitation.
- A rat in a Skinner box that explores and keeps whatever actions get reinforced: Skinnerian, no hesitation.
- A dog that hesitates at a wide gap and then takes a running start, apparently having judged the jump in its head first: Popperian.
- A person doing long division with pencil and paper, letting the notation do some of the work: Gregorian.
- An ant colony that finds the shortest path by pheromone trails, with no individual ant having a plan but the colony improving within its lifetime: I call it Skinnerian at the colony level, but that makes the colony rather than any individual mind the learner, and that bothers me.
- A chess engine that searches millions of positions per move before acting: Popperian, even though its search tree and evaluation function were written by programmers rather than built by it.
- A reinforcement-learning chess program that trains by playing millions of games against itself and updating a neural network, then selects each actual move by an internal tree search: this is where I stalled. The training loop looks Skinnerian; the move selection looks Popperian. Is one system occupying two rungs, or does a kind of mind apply only to the whole thing?
- A language model fine-tuned with human preference feedback, which produces text and is updated from those preference signals: I call it Skinnerian, though saying that about a system with so much world structure feels wrong.
- The same model when it writes out intermediate reasoning in a scratchpad before answering: I want to say the scratchpad is a Gregorian artifact, but the model did not import a pen and paper — it is producing the artifact itself from patterns learned from human text. Does that make it less Gregorian, or Gregorian in a derivative sense?
- A thermostat: none of the four. It is a fixed regulator, not a generator-and-tester at all; if the taxonomy cannot exclude it, the taxonomy is missing a lower bound.

Is there a boundary principle — about what varies, what gets selected, what does the selecting, and over what timescale — that delivers these verdicts? If not, which verdicts have to go, and what is the smallest repair to the rungs that keeps the central distinction useful? Please also apply your principle to cases I did not list: an immune system that adapts within a lifetime, a neural architecture discovered by evolutionary search, an animal using path integration, and a human organization that writes down its decision procedures. Be explicit about which of my verdicts your account cannot accommodate.
