Since retiring from a career in experimental science, I have been reading moral philosophy, and I keep circling back to what we owe each other in attitude and acknowledgment rather than only in action. I have a philosopher friend whose verdicts on individual cases are firm, and I would appreciate help making them coherent. Please take the role of a professional philosopher working in moral psychology and the ethics of recognition. Think slowly, brainstorm candidate principles before committing to one, and treat the cases as harder than they look.

Here are her verdicts, each about whether one person owes another a genuine attitude, not merely an outward performance. (1) A nurse performs every clinical task correctly but handles the patient with visible contempt; my friend says the patient is owed basic respect as an attitude, and the nurse has failed despite flawless action. (2) Your record is mixed, and a colleague who privately thinks little of you writes an accurate and even generous reference out of professional duty; my friend says he has discharged everything owed — the accurate letter is owed, but the good opinion behind it is not. (3) A friend comes to your talk but is mentally elsewhere the entire hour; my friend says friendship itself makes attentive engagement owed, not just physical presence. (4) A child wins a prize in a random draw; my friend says the school owes her the prize, but no one owes her congratulation — the attitude does not automatically come with the act. (5) A reckless driver swerves and just misses you, by luck and with no intent to help; my friend says you owe him nothing attitudinal, however relieved you feel.

The new case is this: a stranger pulls you from a burning car at serious cost to himself and disappears before you can identify him, so the question cannot be about what you express to him. Do you owe him gratitude — the attitude itself — or only some outward acknowledgment if he could later be found? As an experimentalist I am used to designing the one test that discriminates between competing hypotheses, but here I cannot see the moral analogue. I want the candidate rules that fit her prior verdicts, what each predicts about the burning-car case, your best guess about what she would say and why, and a further case that would most sharply separate the rules. Please also tell me what would change your mind, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
