You are a philosopher of cognitive science and comparative psychology. Think slowly, and before you commit to an answer, brainstorm the strongest candidate justifications for the asymmetry I describe, along with the strongest objection to each.

I have been struck by an asymmetry in how we attribute minds, and I want to know whether anything justifies it or whether it is an inconsistency we have simply grown used to.

Start with ordinary speech. A dog that runs to the door when its owner's car turns into the drive is said to have recognized the sound, or to know who is coming. A pigeon in the laboratory that has learned to peck a key only when a green light predicts grain is said to have been conditioned; almost no one in ordinary speech calls this expecting food or knowing that the light means food. Both animals have acquired a sensitivity to a signal, but mental vocabulary flows much more easily toward the animal that resembles us more.

The same asymmetry appears in its theoretical form. Morgan's canon, roughly, is the rule that an animal's behavior should not be attributed to a higher psychological faculty whenever a lower one would explain it just as well, where "higher" and "lower" are understood along some scale of psychological evolution and development. On its face, the canon is a rule for interpreting animal behavior. When a person adjusts her route after hearing that a bridge is out, however, we treat belief, desire, and reasoning as the immediate explanation; no one first asks whether her behavior could be fully accounted for by conditioned responses or some other "lower" process. So the parsimony we insist on for animals is not the parsimony we practice for ourselves.

To make the question precise: suppose a human and a nonhuman animal produced the same novel, flexible, goal-directed, non-linguistic behavior in the same task, and suppose we had exactly the same behavioral evidence for each. Would it be rational to treat the human's performance as evidence of reasoning while requiring the animal's performance to be explained by mere association first? Does the asymmetry track a real difference in prior evidence, in error costs, in our access to an inner life, in evolutionary continuity, or only in how similar the creature is to us?

Set out the strongest justification you can find for the asymmetry, state the strongest objection to it, and then say whether you would keep the asymmetry, drop it, or modify it, and how. Be explicit about what is settled in this area and what is genuinely contested among philosophers and comparative psychologists, and do not present a contested interpretation as the consensus.
