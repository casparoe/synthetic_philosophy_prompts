I have spent the last two years revising a historical novel, and a chance remark from a friend who teaches historiography has left me unsure whether I have discovered something real or only turned over a pretty metaphor. My friend read a chapter in which an elderly protagonist looks back over his career and called the narration Whiggish. I recognized the term: the habit of writing the past as a progress toward the present, so that earlier events matter only insofar as they produced what came later. But what struck me is how precisely that describes the ordinary craft of shaping a novel. In revision I select and reorder incidents, plant small details early that will return at the end, adjust emphasis, and cut whatever does not lead somewhere. The goal is an ending that feels, in retrospect, inevitable: everything before it seems to have been tending toward it all along. Yet I know how much of that inevitability is manufactured by selection, foreshortening, and hindsight. The novelist admits the ending was designed; the Whig historian, supposedly, does not. Is that a difference in kind, or only in candor?

Is this a genuine identity of structure between fictional plotting and Whig narration, or am I pattern-matching on the word narrative? I would like you to state the mapping as precisely as you can, say what would have to be true on both sides for anything to transfer in either direction, and then try to name at least one thing the parallel seems to predict that turns out false. I suspect that is the surest way to tell a real structural homology from an attractive analogy. If the parallel is real, I want to know whether the novelist's practical knowledge of how retrospective inevitability is manufactured could serve as a diagnostic for Whig history, and conversely whether the critique of Whig history implies that certain narrative forms, the well-made plot or the bildungsroman, falsify a life in some identifiable way. If it is not real, I want to know exactly where the two practices come apart: does historical narration make claims about evidence and causation that fictional plotting withholds, or does the difference lie elsewhere?
