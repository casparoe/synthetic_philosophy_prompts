I follow a policy whenever someone proposes a trade or bet whose payoff depends on one of us being wrong. Before I accept, I force myself to identify a reason they could rationally be on the other side that has nothing to do with my being mistaken: they need liquidity, they are hedging a risk I do not bear, they get consumption value from the thing, or they enjoy the gamble. If I cannot identify such a reason, I update substantially in their direction and refuse. Last week this led me to decline a colleague's even-money bet on a policy outcome I thought I understood well, because I couldn't see a non-informational motive for his offer. A friend says the rule is self-defeating: if everyone reasoned this way, a pure difference of information would produce a standoff, and almost no one would ever trade or bet. I answer that markets seem to work on this very principle, since a price move is informative only because someone was willing to trade at the moved price. Is my rule defensible? Under what conditions does an offer to trade reveal information rather than preferences or circumstances, and is there a version of the rule that keeps the epistemic benefit without freezing exchange on information altogether?
