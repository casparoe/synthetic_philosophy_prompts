I am a PhD student in early modern philosophy, and I am rewriting Locke's memory criterion in graded form to see whether Reid's brave officer objection still goes through. Take the usual case: the boy is flogged at school; the officer later recalls the flogging vividly; the general later recalls his taking of a standard as a young officer but does not, spontaneously, recall the flogging. Now replace "remembers" with a continuous memory strength and adopt the criterion that a later stage is the same person as an earlier stage just in case it retains an above-threshold recollection of at least one of that earlier stage's experiences. Add two complications. First, the general has a sub-threshold trace of the flogging, and an afternoon in the schoolroom temporarily raises that trace above threshold for about an hour. Second, the officer's recollection of the flogging is only marginally above threshold and slowly fades as he ages. Work through what this version does to the transitivity objection: does the Lockean get pushed into saying that the general is identical with the boy only during that hour in the schoolroom, identical with the officer only while the trace is strong enough, or neither? Where exactly must the criterion be fixed, in the threshold, the time of assessment, the notion of recollection, or the transitivity of identity, in order for it to remain a criterion of identity rather than a criterion of apparent memory?
