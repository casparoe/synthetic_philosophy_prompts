I retired a few years ago from a long career in the natural sciences and have been reading my way through older philosophy of religion; having spent decades around causal explanation, I am drawn to arguments that work like scientific hypotheses. One that keeps occupying me is Freud's argument in The Future of an Illusion. As I understand it, he holds that religious doctrines are illusions in a precise sense: not just false beliefs but beliefs produced and sustained by wish. Confronted with a dangerous universe and the helplessness that lingers from childhood, human beings project a protecting father onto the cosmos; the resulting ideas satisfy emotional needs, and that is why they grip people as firmly as they do. The debunking force of the argument, I take it, is this: if a belief can be fully explained by the psychological work it performs for the believer, then its apparent grip on reality gives us no reason to think it tracks reality.

A later body of work, though, seems to pull the ground from under that story. Over the past several decades, cognitive scientists of religion have argued that religious concepts spread and endure because they fit ordinary human cognition: people over-detect agency in ambiguous stimuli, a small violation of intuitive expectations makes a concept especially memorable and transmissible, and ritual seems to recruit evolved systems for handling contamination and danger. On this account, belief in unseen agents is largely a byproduct of how normal minds are built, not primarily a response to fear or longing. Some of the findings look particularly awkward for Freud, since widely successful religious ideas are often terrifying, costly, or only weakly consoling.

What I want to know is whether Freud's argument survives this development, needs repair, or is dead.
