I keep coming back to a claim about value that I cannot decide is an empirical hypothesis, a conceptual point, or just a mood. The claim: many of the things we find worth doing now—a long-running research program, a half-written novel, a commitment to a political cause—get their point from a background assumption that the human enterprise will continue after our own deaths. If I could be made certain that humanity would be annihilated thirty days after my death, the thought goes, those activities would immediately lose much of their point for me now, even though the extinction would harm no one I care about while I am alive. Call this the collective-afterlife conjecture.

I want to know whether anything observable bears on it. Design the strongest test you can construct. Be concrete: the condition to manipulate, the comparison conditions, the outcome to measure, and the pattern of results that would count for the conjecture versus against it. Then mark the boundary of what the test settles. In particular, say whether a positive result could be dismissed as a mere mood effect of imagining extinction; whether a negative result could be dismissed as an artifact of asking people to react to a hypothetical rather than to live in a genuine expectation of the end; and what, if anything, remains untestable even in principle—for example, whether the future's existence is really a condition on value or merely something we happen to care about.
