I'm in a course on markets and morality, and last week we talked about moral trade: the idea that you can pay somebody else to discharge a duty on your behalf. The standard cases are paying a substitute to serve in a draft instead of you, or buying carbon offsets so someone else makes the lifestyle changes you'd rather not make. I understand why those are philosophically interesting, but the discussion surfaced an asymmetry I can't shake. If I pay my flatmate to do my share of the cleaning, nobody treats me as if I've dodged anything: the floor gets mopped, he's fairly paid, and the duty is just gone. But if I pay a random student down the hall to visit my grandmother in hospital on a weekend I can't make it, or pay someone to stand in for me at a protest I had agreed to attend, people act like I've dodged something rather than fulfilled it. The obligation that should have been discharged feels like it's still sitting on me, and the paying itself seems mildly shameful.

What I want is the strongest principle that justifies that asymmetry, or an honest verdict that there isn't one. Is there some property of a duty—whether the person owed it cares about who performs it, whether it's tied to a role only I occupy, whether my doing it expresses something the hired person's doing can't—that cleanly seperates paying to outsource a chore from paying to outsource a duty? Pressure-test your principle agains nearby cases: paying someone to write my exam, paying someone to take my shift at the soup kitchen, paying someone to deliver an apology to my partner. Then tell me whether the line ordinary people draw is one we should keep, redraw, or give up.
