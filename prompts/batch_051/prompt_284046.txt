I am a PhD candidate in philosophy of law, working through the Hart–Fuller debate on legal positivism and natural law. In our graduate seminar one participant repeatedly ends discussion of Hart's separation of law and morality by saying that an unjust law is not a law — sometimes in the Latin, lex iniusta non est lex — as though this single maxim disposed of legal positivism. I want to pin down what the maxim could actually be asserting. Please set out the distinct precise theses it might express: for example, a claim about validity (that grossly unjust enactments cannot count as law at all), a claim about obedience (that unjust enactments create no moral obligation to comply), a claim about adjudication (that judges should decline to apply or give effect to unjust enactments), and any further reading you think is live in the Hart–Fuller exchange, including something like Fuller's view that a legal order which systematically violates his procedural desiderata fails in a way that may amount to not being a legal system. For each reading, say whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and what it would commit its user to. Then identify which reading or readings the maxim must bear if it is to put real pressure on Hart's positivist separation of law and morality, and which readings a careful positivist can accept without cost. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — which family of readings does the argumentative work and whether the maxim, so read, actually lands a blow against positivism — then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
