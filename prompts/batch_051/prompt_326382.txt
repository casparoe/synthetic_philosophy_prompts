Had a long argument with a friend last night about the stone paradox — you know, can God create a stone so heavy he can't lift it — and by hour two I'd worked up a little theory I want checked, because I can't tell if its real philosophy or just me being tired and stubborn.

The passage that started me down this road was Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologica, First Part, Question 25, Article 3 ("Whether God is omnipotent?"), in the "I answer that":

"Therefore, everything that does not imply a contradiction in terms, is numbered amongst those possible things, in respect of which God is called omnipotent: whereas whatever implies contradiction does not come within the scope of divine omnipotence, because it cannot have the aspect of possibility. Hence it is better to say that such things cannot be done, than that God cannot do them."

My friend's version was the usual: if God can do anything he can make such a stone; if he can't make it, there's something he can't do; if he can make it but can't lift it, there's something he can't do. I kept pushing Aquinas's point that "a stone God can't lift" is not a coherent object because it includes its own relation to God's power, and he kept saying the two acts are specified independently: making is one ability, lifting is another, and omnipotence should cover both.

So here's my theory, which I've been calling the sliding-specification account (or maybe task-indexation, I can't decide). The idea is that ability claims are never about an agent plus a fully independent outcome; the outcome is specified relative to a baseline of the agent's powers. "Too heavy for God to lift" only stays the same specification relative to the current, maximally powerful God. Put the stone in a world where God has the power to lift it and it's not too heavy for God; put it in a world where he lacks that power and it is. So across the "first create it, then lift it" story there is no single stone the phrase picks out; the act-description slides with the world. The paradox pretends there is. Omnipotence, on my view, isn't the power to tick off every task on a fixed list; it's world-openness. For every consistent world in which God remains omnipotent, God can actualize it; and a world containing a stone too heavy for the being who is God is consistent only if God doesn't remain omnipotent in it.

My friend's comeback was: fine, can an omnipotent God create some plain ordinary stone and then voluntarily give up the power to lift it? If yes, the end state is God existing and a stone he can't lift — so the paradox comes back, just delayed. If no, then omnipotence doesn't include the power to abandon power, which sure sounds like a limit. I think my view has to answer yes, but then I worry I've turned omnipotence into a temporary historical property and made the original paradox escapeable by saying "he just stops being omnipotent whenever it would bite," which my friend called cheating.

I'd like an honest assessment, not a pep talk. Please act like a philosopher of religion who's comfortable with medieval modality and analytic metaphysics; think carefully and maybe brainstorm a little before you answer. Then tell me: (1) is the sliding-specification account coherent once it's picked apart, or is it just Aquinas with a newer metaphor? (2) is my friend's "plain stone then abdicate" case genuinely different from the original paradox, and what should my view say about it? (3) what are the three strongest objections to my account, and is any of them fatal rather than just uncomfortable?
