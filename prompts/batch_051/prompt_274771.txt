A friend and I recently spent an evening arguing about capital punishment, and he pressed what I take to be the strongest version of the abolitionist case, the one I associate with Camus. The argument is not fundamentally about deterrence, and it does not depend on denying that some offenders have done horrifying things. It is that the state is fallible, that any penal practice must therefore remain reversible, and that execution is uniquely irreversible. From those premises the conclusion follows cleanly: whatever one thinks of the person on trial, the state may not inflict a punishment it can never take back on the strength of a verdict that no procedure can make certain. I find the austerity of this argument compelling. It yields one exceptionless rule, never execute, from premises I cannot see how to deny, and it avoids having to settle messy empirical disputes about whether the practice deters.

Yet I cannot shake the opposite conviction in certain cases. Consider a person who has committed, with full knowledge and deliberation, an atrocity so complete that speaking of judicial error, moral change, or rehabilitation seems like a confusion about what is being judged. My reaction is not that executing him would advance our interests or deter others. It is that his continued existence is itself a standing wrong, and that a community which keeps such a person alive has failed to take the measure of what he did. Death looks less like a fallible instrument than like the only response that matches the gravity of the act. My friend says this is an outrage heuristic dressed up as philosophy, a failure of scope sensitivity rather than a perception of a moral fact. I am not sure he is right, and I am not sure he is wrong.

I am not asking which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the theory to be the thing that goes, and whether there is a reading on which they are compatible: perhaps an abolitionism about the state's routine machinery that leaves room for a distinct judgment about what such offenders deserve, or a retributivism that can accept irreversibility as a tragic feature rather than a refutation. Present both sides at their strongest, and tell me which you would give up, or how the apparent contradiction dissolves.
