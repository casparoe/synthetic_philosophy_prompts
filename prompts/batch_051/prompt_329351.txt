Novelist here, so fair warning that I think in last chapters. I've been trying to get Fukuyama's end-of-history thesis straight in my head, and I keep having the feeling that he's pulling the same move I pull when I plot a book. In a novel the ending is supposed to throw light backwards: a detail that looked decorative in chapter two turns out to be load-bearing, and on reread the whole book seems as if it could only have ended this way. I know better than anyone that this is partly an artifact—pick a different cutoff, or write a sequel, and the so-called inevitable ending becomes just another scene among scenes. Fukuyama seems to me to do exactly this with history: he stops at the collapse of communism, takes that as the last chapter, and reads backward so that everything, even the disasters, becomes a chapter in the long fight over recognition, with liberal democracy as the form that was always going to satisfy it. The endpoint does the work of making the earlier struggle meaningful.

My question is whether this parallel between narrative closure and end-of-history thinking is deep or whether I'm just pattern-matching from my own job. If it's deep, which parts of the novelist's toolkit should a political philosopher take seriously—the sequel problem, the difference between an earned ending and a forced one, the fact that where you stop is chosen not discovered—and which parts dont carry over? If it's superficial, where exactly does it snap? And specifically: what does the analogy predict about either fiction or history that comes out false? Give me the verdict as \answer{deep}, \answer{superficial}, or \answer{mixed} at the very end.
