I should like to pin down and systematise my own intuitions about the following question: which truths are such that they could, in principle, come to be known? I have assembled a short list of my verdicts on particular cases, and I want to know what principle underwrites them — or, if no principled account underwrites all of them, exactly which verdicts must be revised and what the least damaging repair would be.

Let me fix the key term. By "knowable" I mean: a true proposition is knowable if it is possible that someone, at some time or other, comes to know that it is true. Being knowable is therefore compatible with never actually being known.

My verdicts are as follows.

1. An ordinary, determinate proposition, such as that the number of stars in the Andromeda Galaxy at a given moment is even. If it is true, I take it to be knowable. Nothing in principle bars a suitably placed knower from arriving at the fact, even if no one ever does.

2. A proposition about a particular now-forgotten dream: that a certain person dreamed a certain content on a certain night. I again say knowable, provided it is true. It is a determinate fact about the past; the obstacles are the loss of evidence and nothing deeper.

3. The proposition that some truths are never known by anyone. I regard this proposition as true, and also as knowable. It seems the sort of thing one could in principle come to appreciate by reasoning about the scale of the world and the fragility of records.

4. Now consider a particular conjunctive proposition: "The number of stars in the Andromeda Galaxy at this moment is even, and no one will ever know that it is even." I want to say that this one is not knowable — not merely that it will never be known, but that it could not be known. For suppose someone knew the conjunction. Then they would know each conjunct. If they knew the first conjunct, then someone would know that the number is even, and the second conjunct would be false. A false proposition cannot be known. So this proposition cannot be known, even in a case where it happens to be true.

5. Yet I am also firmly drawn to the general principle that every truth is knowable in the sense just fixed. This feels to me like a minimal anti-realist commitment: truth cannot be the kind of thing that is in principle hidden from every possible knower.

I cannot at present see how to hold verdicts 4 and 5 together, given verdict 3. I should like you to do three things. First, state precisely the principle, or small set of candidate principles, that best captures verdicts 1 through 4, and say how it relates to the general principle in 5. Second, say what each candidate principle predicts about two further cases I have not listed: (a) the proposition that some particular unrecorded fact about an ancient battle will never be known by anyone; and (b) the proposition "The number of stars in the Andromeda Galaxy at this moment is even, and I will never know that it is even." Third, identify which of my verdicts, if any, must be surrendered, and describe the least costly coherent repair of my overall position. If the most attractive repair amounts to restricting the principle in 5 to a subclass of truths, I want the restriction stated as precisely as possible, together with the cases that force each of its clauses.
