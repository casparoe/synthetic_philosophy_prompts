I've been going round and round on that old question about why we like sad art. Like, I'll cross the street to avoid someone crying, but I'll pay to watch a movie that makes me sob, and I've never felt that was wierd until now. The usual answers I hear are that it's safe because it's not real, or that watching from the outside turns the pain into something beautiful or meaningful, or that we're in control because we can turn it off. But lately I've been playing a bunch of those choice-based story games, where the game reacts to you and you can reload. In those I'm not outside the sad thing at all: I'm the one picking the dialogue option that gets a character killed, or deciding who gets left behind, and the sad thing actually happens because I did it. Not in real life, obviously, but in the story I'm the cause, not just a witness. That feels like it breaks the "safe distance" part of the usual explanation in a way that just watching a movie doesn't.

So the question I ended up with is: does the old explanation for why we like sad art survive this, or does it need to be rebuilt? Which part of it is actually doing the work, and is there a weaker version that still makes sense of why I reload and pick the sad ending even when a happy one is sitting right there?
