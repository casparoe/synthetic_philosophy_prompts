I keep a running list of my own verdicts on advance directives, and I'm not sure wether they hang together. Could you help me figure out the principle I'm implicitly applying, or which of my verdicts has to go? I'd rather end up with the right principle than keep all the verdicts, but I genuinely don't know which one I'd drop.

1. A philosophy professor in his early sixties is diagnosed with early-onset Alzheimer's. While fully lucid he writes a directive saying: if I reach the point where I no longer recognize my family and can't eat on my own, I don't want a feeding tube. Three years later he's exactly there. He doesn't recognize his wife, but he isn't distressed; he claps when the staff plays music, shuffles around the ward smiling, and opens his mouth for the spoon. Then he aspirates and can't swallow safely. The team says a feeding tube would probably win him several more comfortable months. I think the tube should not be placed. The person who wrote the document is gone, and I'd give him the last word over the contented man in the bed.

2. A 24-year-old signs a Jehovah's Witness advance directive at a church retreat, refusing blood transfusions. At 45 she has left the faith and is a loud atheist; she's told friends she'd obviously take blood, but she never sent a revocation. After a bad car accident she's brought in unconscious and needs blood to live. I think the doctors should transfuse. The document is stale, and everyone who knows her knows it describes somebody who no longer exists.

3. A 30-year-old with bipolar disorder writes a Ulysses contract while stable: if I later have a severe manic episode and demand to stop my mood stabilizer, and my doctor judges I'm manic, treat me over my objection. Two years later, floridly manic, she tells the doctors the stable person was a different person with no right to run her life, and that she never agreed to any of this. I think the contract should win, and they should keep medicating her over her objection. The person in front of me has values, but I set them aside as untrustworthy.

4. A woman in her forties writes a living will saying she doesn't want to be kept alive in a minimally conscious state. After a catastrophic brain injury she is minimally conscious: she can't speak or follow commands, but she tracks her daughter around the room, calms to a particular song, and squeezes hands when people talk to her. The family is split, and as the team is about to withdraw the ventilator per her document, she seems to grip her sister's hand hard. I still think they should withdraw. Those reactions don't add up to a self that can revoke.

5. A man in his late fifties writes a do-not-resuscitate order two months after his wife dies. He's competent but sunk in grief, and says his life without her isn't worth prolonging. Two months later the depression has lifted, he's making plans, and he tells his doctor he wants everything done. Before the form is changed, he arrests on the ward; the DNR is still valid on paper. I think they should code him. The later self has shown up, and the paperwork lag shouldn't kill him.

When I put it that way, I can see a rough principle trying to emerge: the earlier self gets to rule when the later self can't form or revise considered values (the dementia case, the minimally conscious case), and the later self rules when it has (the ex-Witness, the recovering widower). The Ulysses case is the weird one: the manic woman has values coming out of her ears, but I disqualify them. And I'm not sure what makes an expression of values "considered" enough to count. Is there a coherent principle that gets all five, or close? If not, which of my verdicts does it force me to drop, and is that a loss I should accept?

Then tell me what the principle says about the case I keep going back and forth on. A man has a directive on file from years ago saying never amputate my legs, I'd rather die. He now has gangrene in one leg, is awake and oriented but badly sedated and in severe pain, and tells the surgeon to cut it off, that he was wrong about himself. Does the earlier refusal count for anything in that moment, or does his current voice wipe it out? I lean toward saying his current voice wins, but then I feel a creeping asymmetry between deciding in advance to sacrifice someone else's leg and deciding in the moment to save his life — and I don't know if that asymmetry is real or just squeamishness.
