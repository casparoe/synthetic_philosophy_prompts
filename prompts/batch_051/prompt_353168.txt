I've come around to prioritarianism as the best way to think about distributive questions — roughly, the worse off someone is, the more a given benefit to them counts, but equality itself isn't an independent good, and you just add up those weighted benefits across people. I want you to map out what would count against this view, and I want you to be honest about how exposed it actually is.

The leveling-down objection is the obvious first target, but don't just gesture at it. Actually work through what I'd have to find intuitive for leveling down to register as a real loss for the view: if a change makes the better off worse off while making no one better off, and prioritarianism says that's bad, is it bad only because some person ends up worse off, or is there something lost that the view can't name — and would that unnamed thing be equality, or something else? I'd like you to distinguish the version of the objection that just leans on "leveling down is bad" from the version that leans on "there is a reason against inequality that your view is blind to," since those point at different parts of the theory.

But leveling down isn't the main reason I'm asking. I want the fuller set of defeaters, not just the standard one. Which possible cases should worry a prioritarian — for instance, giving a very large benefit to someone already well off over a barely noticeable benefit to someone worse off, cases where the welfare numbers are barely known, cases involving risk and how you aggregate chances before versus after outcomes, cases about future people, or cases where the "worse off" category itself gets vague? Which formal or structural commitments does the view have that could turn unacceptable — the concavity of the weighting function, the additive separation between persons, the fact that it's silent about how inequality arose, or something about time and duration of disadvantage?
