In Nozick's tracking account of knowledge, a true belief counts as knowledge only if the belief tracks the truth: roughly, in the nearby worlds where p is false, S does not believe p by the same method, and in the nearby worlds where p is true, S still believes p by that method. This account is commonly used to deny that knowledge is closed under known entailment. The canonical illustration is Dretske's zebra. A zoo visitor sees a zebra-like animal in an enclosure marked "zebra"; the animal is in fact a zebra. The visitor believes (Z) that the animal is a zebra, sees that Z entails (M) that the animal is not a mule cleverly painted to look like a zebra, and thereby believes M. On the standard Nozickian treatment, the visitor knows Z but not M: the visual method tracks Z, but it fails to track M because in the relevant not-M world—the animal is a painted mule—the visitor would still believe Z, and therefore M, on the same visual basis.

Now vary the case. The zoo has posted a true notice at the enclosure: "One animal in every two hundred here is a mule painted to be visually indistinguishable from a zebra." The visitor reads and accepts the notice, looks at a particular animal, and—on the strength of its zebra-like appearance together with the stated base rate—forms the outright belief Z and infers M. Call this the posted-odds zebra.

What should a tracking theorist say about the posted-odds zebra? Specifically:

1. Does the visitor know Z? Does she know M?
2. Has the notice changed the visitor's method, and if so, what is the relevant method-type for evaluating the tracking conditions? Does introducing a known statistical deception rate alter which worlds are the nearby worlds against which sensitivity is tested?
3. The original case derives its interest from an asymmetry: Z is known while M is not, so closure fails. Does the posted-odds zebra preserve that asymmetry, or does it collapse it—for instance, by making neither belief knowledge? If it collapses, is the tracking theorist forced to say that adding a true statistical warning destroys knowledge?
4. Now vary the posted rate: 1 in 1,000,000; 1 in 200; 1 in 20; 1 in 2. Do the tracking verdicts flip at some point? Is there a principled reason for a sharp threshold, or does the series expose pressure on the tracking account's reliance on method individuation and an ordering of nearby worlds?

Throughout, please separate what is fixed by the standard statements of the tracking account from what is genuinely contested—such as how methods are individuated, how nearby worlds are ranked, and whether knowledge requires outright belief rather than graded confidence—and do not present a contested reading as the consensus.
