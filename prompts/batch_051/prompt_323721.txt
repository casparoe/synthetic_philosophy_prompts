I've recently been trying to think through whether my own tradition's claim to possess an uncreated revelation can be defended, and a professor of Indian philosophy pointed me to Mīmāṃsā. He summarized the school for me as follows. Because the Veda is authorless (apauruṣeya), never composed by any person, human or divine, it cannot be false: error and deception arise only from the defects and motives of an author, so a text with no author has its truth guaranteed by its authorlessness alone. On Mīmāṃsā's principle of intrinsic validity (svataḥ prāmāṇya), all cognitions, including testimony, are to be accepted as true unless some specific defect is identified in their source; applied to the Veda, this means that no external objection can get a grip on it, because there is no author whose competence or honesty one could question. He then concluded that Mīmāṃsā holds that any tradition conflicting with the Veda is false simply in virtue of having a human author, and that the Veda is exempt from rational scrutiny because its source cannot be checked. I'd like to know how fair this summary is. Please work through its claims one by one, distinguishing what a careful Mīmāṃsā defender would accept, what they would qualify, and what they would reject, and then tell me whether the overall picture the summary gives survives once the qualified claims are corrected.
