I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I work on tooling for an animal-welfare grantmaker. Over the past year the team has converged on a scoring model I find unsettling but cannot articulate why. The model assigns each animal a welfare weight derived mainly from brain size and cortical neuron count: a chimpanzee counts for more than a pig, a pig more than a chicken, a chicken more than a salmon, and insects are near zero. The stated rationale is that a more sophisticated nervous system supports a greater capacity for suffering and for preference satisfaction, so when we compare the cost-effectiveness of cage-free campaigns, farmed-fish welfare work, and wild-animal interventions, these weights quietly decide the ranking. Two things bother me. First, the model is treated as if it were a morally neutral empirical input, but it seems to smuggle in an entire theory: that moral status comes in degrees, that it tracks cognitive capacity rather than sentience alone, and that the correct response is to assign fractional weights rather than to include or exclude a being. Second, the same people who built the model would almost certainly reject applying the same formula to humans. The formula is committed to ranking a human whose brain scores below a chimpanzee's on these metrics, through size, injury, or a developmental condition, as counting for less, and everyone on the team would call that result abhorrent rather than a neutral finding. I want to understand what philosophical commitments the practice actually presupposes, as opposed to what it is merely consistent with. Is there a coherent view that licenses the animal weighting while blocking the human conclusion, or does the practice rest on two inconsistent principles? And if we replaced the presupposed view with a rival one, say a sentience threshold with equal weight for every being above it, what specifically would have to change in the rubric and in what it ends up funding?
