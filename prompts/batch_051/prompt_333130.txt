You are a professor of aesthetics grading an upper-level undergraduate exam in the philosophy of music. You grade carefully: you award credit for what an answer gets right, you do not let smooth prose compensate for argumentative slips, and you catch errors that a hasty grader would miss. Grade the following exam question and student answer. Before settling on a grade, reconstruct the student's argument in your own words and identify the precise points where it silently changes position or draws a conclusion that does not follow. Then assign a letter grade on the standard A–F scale and justify it with point-by-point comments tied to specific sentences of the answer. Your comments should be specific enough to be usable by a TA who is new to this material: they should say what earns credit, what is defensible but needs qualification, what is wrong, and how seriously each error affects the overall answer. Do not write general impressions; the student will see these notes.

Exam question:

In the philosophy of music, a familiar distinction is drawn between music's being expressive of an emotion, such as the music's being sad, and music's arousing that emotion in a listener, such as the listener's being made sad. Explain this distinction and give at least one example in which the two come apart. Then explain the resemblance, or contour, account of musical expressiveness, state the main objection an arousal theorist would raise against it, and explain how a contour theorist could reply to that objection while still preserving the original distinction between expressiveness and arousal.

Student answer:

Music that is expressive of sadness has the sadness as an audible property of the music itself, whereas music that merely arouses sadness causes the listener to feel sad. The two can come apart in both directions: a slow, descending dirge can remain sad even when a listener is in a cheerful mood and feels no sadness at all, and a cheerful jingle associated with a painful memory can make a listener sad without the jingle itself being sad. The resemblance account explains expressiveness in terms of the audible structure of the music: slow tempo, falling melodic contours, soft dynamics, and a drooping phrase shape resemble the way sadness appears in human voice and bodily movement, and that is why the word "sad" is appropriate rather than merely arbitrary. The arousal theorist's main objection is that resemblance underdetermines expressiveness: weeping willows and drooping clouds can resemble sad postures without themselves being sad, so resemblance alone cannot be what makes the music sad. The contour theorist replies that what it is for music to be sad, in the end, is its disposition to move suitably situated listeners to sadness; the music's resemblance to sad human behavior is merely the mechanism by which it produces that sadness. This reply has the further advantage of explaining why we value sad music: we seek out sad music because we value the sadness it affords, and valuing that sadness requires the music actually to produce sadness in us.
