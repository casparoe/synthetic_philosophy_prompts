For a seminar on well-being and practical reasoning, I have to circulate a short statement of where I actually stand on what makes a life go well and how it is best pursued, and what makes the statement hard to write is the familiar paradox of hedonism sitting inside an otherwise maximizing outlook. Before I show it to anyone, I want an audit of whether the following package hangs together.

(1) Prudential hedonism: the only thing that ultimately makes a life go better or worse for the person living it is the balance of pleasant over unpleasant experience; achievement, friendship, knowledge, and the like have no final value, only the value they derive from that balance over time.

(2) The byproduct claim: deliberately aiming at one's own happiness, especially in the moment of choice, tends to make one less happy; happiness is largely a byproduct of wholehearted care for activities and people pursued for their own sake.

(3) Deliberative maximizing: because pleasure is the only ultimate good, a person should decide among important options by estimating which option yields the greatest expected net pleasant experience over the remainder of their life, properly calculated.

(4) The absorption claim: the pleasures characteristic of a good life, such as absorption, intimacy, and mastery, are fragile under self-monitoring; pausing to check whether one is enjoying an activity tends to reduce the enjoyment, and constantly recalculating choices tends to weaken the attachments that would have made those choices rewarding.

(5) The commitment claim: much of a good life is built through durable commitments and cultivated tastes, which means treating some earlier decisions as binding and declining to reopen them case by case.

(6) The policy claim: it is nevertheless possible and sensible to improve one's life by arranging, at the level of policy, for more pleasant experiences, such as planning richer social contact, structuring one's days around absorbing work, or deliberately altering habits, as long as the arranging happens at the policy level rather than through moment-to-moment monitoring of the experience itself.

Which of these six actually pull against one another, and which only seem to? I am not asking for a summary of each; I want an ordered audit. For each pair in genuine tension, say how severe the tension is and what the least costly revision would be, and for each apparent tension, say why it dissolves. Please rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equal.
