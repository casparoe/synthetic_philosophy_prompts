Write a dialogue between two philosophers who disagree about the moral of Lewis Carroll's story of Achilles and the Tortoise. One holds that the story shows that accepting an inference is never the same as accepting a further proposition: the step from the premises "if P then Q" and "P" to the conclusion "Q" is licensed by a rule, and treating that rule as an extra premise simply gives the Tortoise another foothold for the same delaying question. The other holds that this is evasion: the Tortoise's questions are perfectly intelligible, and if no proposition can state what licenses the step, then the inference's claim to be rationally compelling rests on habit, convention, or blind practice rather than on anything reason can acknowledge. Have the two characters press each other on the hard cases — whether the regress reappears at the level of rules about how to apply rules, whether "grasping a rule" explains anything more than the original disposition, what distinguishes a reasoner who cannot follow the inference from one who will not, and whether ordinary reasoners can be systematically mistaken about what their own inferential practice commits them to. Each character should be competent and intellectually honest: neither should dismiss the other's best version of the problem.
