At a dinner last month I listened to two friends of mine, Zara and Ivan, argue about something a third friend, Leila, had done, and I still do not know how to referee them. Leila sits on the five-person committee that awards a small, publicly funded artist residency in our city. This year the committee had two finalists. One was a former collaborator of Leila's; the other had no connection to any committee member and had scored slightly higher on the committee's written rubric. Leila argued that her first-hand knowledge of her former collaborator's reliability under deadline should outweigh the small scoring gap. The committee followed Leila's recommendation, and the other finalist was turned down.

Zara and Ivan's exchange went roughly as follows.

(1) Zara: "What Leila did was favoritism, not judgment. The rubric exists precisely to stop personal history from deciding who gets a public good, and on the rubric the other finalist won. Once you set the friendship aside, the case for the stranger was stronger; the only thing that closed the gap was that Leila had an attachment. That is exactly what a bias is: a systematic departure from the standard you would apply to someone you did not know."

(2) Ivan: "That presupposes the rubric tracks what matters. Choosing a resident is not scoring a math contest; a residency succeeds or fails on traits like reliability, candor, and what a person does when a project comes apart, and those are often observable only from inside a working relationship. Leila's history is not a contaminant; it is a source of evidence the rubric cannot contain. And notice what your standard demands. A person who gave a friend's crisis and a stranger's crisis exactly equal claim on her attention would not be perfectly fair—she would be failing as a friend. So the 'bias' you want her to overcome is partly constitutive of having relationships at all."

(3) Zara: "The two cases are not parallel. When Leila consoles a friend on a difficult night, nobody is excluded; when she casts a committee vote, a flesh-and-blood applicant loses a public opportunity. That applicant is owed an answer to 'why her and not me?', and the answer cannot be 'because Leila has known her longer.' If she cannot state a ground that would count as a reason for the other committee members, rather than merely as an explanation of her own attachment, then it is not functioning as the institution's reason—it is a private preference wearing the costume of a credential. There is also an epistemic problem. Leila cannot tell from the inside whether her confidence in her friend is genuine knowledge or the friendship doing the belief-forming for her. That opacity is the signature of bias: a virtue can be owned in the first person without undermining itself, whereas a bias typically does its work unnoticed."

(4) Ivan: "Fairness can be just as opaque—the self-congratulating neutralist may not notice that his 'impartiality' is really disengagement or a fear of defending anyone—so the transparency test cannot separate virtue from bias. And Leila can state a public reason: 'I have worked with this person for four years, and I know she does not disappear when a deadline collapses; a portfolio cannot tell you that.' The rejected applicant may dislike that answer, but disliking it does not make it private or arbitrary. What Leila should do is disclose the relationship and let the other committee members discount her advocacy—that is the discipline friendship requires in a shared institution. But requiring her to treat the relationship as itself a point against her friend, as though history were a contaminant to be subtracted, is not impartiality; it is a bias in the opposite direction."

I honestly cannot tell which of them is more right, so I would like an adjudication. Please assess each of the four moves in order, saying how far it falls between clearly mistaken and clearly right and briefly justifying each assessment. Then state which specific points each speaker gets right, and what, if anything, both of them are missing. Finally, give an overall verdict on who is more right overall. At the very end of your response, as the last line, write \answer{...} where the enclosed text is a short phrasal verdict (no more than eight words) summarizing that overall judgment.
