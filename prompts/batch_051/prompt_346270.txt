I am putting together a short essay for an undergraduate philosophy-of-mathematics course, and I want a second opinion on one student's answer. Here is the question the student was set:

"Explain what Hilbert's Hotel is supposed to show about infinite collections, how Cantor's diagonal argument qualifies that result, and whether a finitist could consistently accept the first result while rejecting the second. Conclude with a short assessment of the claim that the potential infinite can do all the mathematical work of the actual infinite. (Maximum 500 words.)"

The student wrote:

"Hilbert's Hotel is an imaginary hotel with as many rooms as there are natural numbers, all of them occupied. When a new guest arrives, the manager moves the occupant of room 1 to room 2, the occupant of room 2 to room 3, and so on, leaving room 1 empty. The lesson is that an infinite collection can be put into one-to-one correspondence with a proper part of itself — the whole hotel matches the part consisting of rooms 2, 3, 4, and so on — so the principle that the whole is larger than any of its parts fails for infinite totalities. That is one standard way of distinguishing the infinite from the finite. The same trick can be adapted, by sending n to 2n, to free all the odd-numbered rooms at once; since there are countably many of these, any countable collection of new arrivals can be fitted in.

Cantor's diagonal argument then warns us not to overstate the point. Suppose the real numbers between 0 and 1 could be written as a list r1, r2, r3, and so on. Construct a real whose first decimal digit differs from that of r1, whose second digit differs from that of r2, and so on. This number differs from every number on the list, so the list is incomplete; hence there is no list of all the reals, and the reals form a strictly larger infinity than the naturals. This is the standard content of the continuum hypothesis.

Finitism is the view that completed infinite totalities are illegitimate, and that mathematics should make do with the potential infinite — an unbounded process that is never finished. But the finitist need not reject either of the above results, because both are constructive: the diagonal argument in particular gives an explicit procedure for producing the missing real from any proposed list, so it never assumes a completed collection. And since ordinary analysis only ever uses the potential infinite — an epsilon-delta limit says 'for every positive ε there exists an N', not that a sequence has been completed — the finitist restriction costs mathematics nothing."
