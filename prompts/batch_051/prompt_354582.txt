I have been offered two research fellowships, and I would be genuinely happy to take either. Over the past several weeks I have compared them along every dimension I can think of — location, salary, teaching load, prestige of the host institution, quality of colleagues, freedom to pursue my own projects — and after repeated, painstaking reflection I keep arriving at the same result: they are not merely close, but evenly balanced. I am not indifferent to them in the sense of lacking enthusiasm; I badly want to accept one of them, and I have no desire to wait for a third possibility. Yet in my all-things-considered assessment the two options come out as exact equals, and this is precisely what stops me. Any reason I could give for selecting one over the other would, by my own lights, be arbitrary, and I keep hoping that some further fact will emerge to break the tie. A friend insists that I am being irrational: if I genuinely judge the options equal, then there is nothing left to deliberate about, and I should simply pick one — say, by tossing a coin — since further delay is just a refusal to accept the situation. This strikes me as wrong. A rational agent, I want to say, ought to be able to give reasons for what she does, and the outcome of a coin toss is not a reason; it is an abdication of judgement.

I would like to know: is my friend right that a fully rational person with complete and transitive preferences would simply pick either option when the two are tied, and that my paralysis is therefore a failure of rationality? Or is there a defensible account of rational choice on which remaining undecided, or searching for a non-arbitrary tie-breaker, can be reasonable even when the options are perfectly balanced? And what does this kind of impasse reveal about the demands we place on rational preference — for example, the assumption that preferences must be complete, or the idea that a rational agent must always be able to justify choosing one alternative over its rivals?
