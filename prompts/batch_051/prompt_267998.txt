I've been going in circles trying to figure out what I actually think about purely statistical evidence, and I'd like someone to extract the principle from my own verdicts. Here are the cases with my gut reactions. (1) The blue bus case: I'm sideswiped by a bus and cant identify it. Blue Bus Company runs 80% of the buses on that route, and I have nothing else. Intellectually it's more likely than not that it was theirs, but I don't want to make them pay. (2) Same accident, except now an eyewitness says it was a Blue Bus and I regard the witness as about 80% reliable. That sounds like it gives me roughly the same chance of being right as in (1), yet here I'd be fine finding against the company. (3) The gatecrasher: 1,000 people attended a rodeo, 499 paid and 501 jumped the fence. The organizer sues one attendee selected at random. I wouldn't make that randomly chosen person pay, even though the chance they crashed is 50.1%, above a civil standard. (4) A DNA match from a crime scene with a random-match probability of one in a billion: I'd convict without much hesitation. (5) A prison yard where 999 of 1,000 prisoners beat a guard, and one prisoner is picked at random and charged. I'd acquit, even though the probability of guilt is 99.9%, which is way above any numeric reading of beyond a reasonable doubt.

Is there one coherent principle behind all five verdicts? It isn't just "decide for the plaintiff or prosecution when the probability clears the relevant threshold," because (1) and (3) clear the civil threshold and (5) clears any criminal threshold, yet I balk. And it isn't just "no probabilities in the courtroom," because (4) is full of probability talk and I'm fine with it. I keep reaching for something like statistical evidence being about groups while individualized evidence is about this particular defendant, but then (2) is also a group statistic about how often the witness is right, so that line blurs. What's the best statement of the principle I'm groping toward? And what does it commit me to in cases I haven't listed, like a traffic camera that reads license plates with a 1% error rate, or an expert who says a tire track "matches" with a 1-in-50 chance of random coincidence? If no clean principle gets all five, which of my verdicts should I reconsider?
