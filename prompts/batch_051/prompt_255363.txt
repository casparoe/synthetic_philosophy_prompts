Two people in my department got into an argument after a seminar on concept learning, and neither was willing to concede. The speaker had projected a Bongard problem — a two-panel display in which six simple line drawings on the left all obey a rule and six on the right all violate it — and had asked whether a system that induces the rule from those twelve examples is doing what a human does when the human just gets the concept. (1) One of them said the whole task is underdetermined: any finite collection of positive and negative examples is consistent with indefinitely many rules, so there is no fact of the matter about which rule is the rule apart from what the puzzle-maker happened to intend, and that intention is not in the drawings. (2) The other replied that human solvers nevertheless converge on the same rule far more often than chance, and the best explanation of that convergence is a shared preference for simple rules; without some such preference, no induction from examples would get off the ground. (3) The first answered that simplicity is language-relative: a rule is simple only relative to a vocabulary, and for any gerrymandered rule one can choose a vocabulary in which that rule comes out simplest. (4) The second said that is exactly why you fix a universal description language in advance and measure simplicity by description length; the shortest description then gives a non-arbitrary candidate. (5) The first replied that the choice of description language is itself a prior, since different universal languages can yield different shortest descriptions when the descriptions are short, and twelve drawings leave too many candidate rules for the shortest description to be unique in any deep sense. (6) The second said the solver's job was never to recover the puzzle-maker's intention; it is to find a rule that generalizes to new drawings, and the shortest-description rule is the best available bet at doing that. (7) The first answered that calling it the best available bet is an empirical claim rather than a conceptual point, and there are Bongard problems whose intended rule is not the shortest description, yet human solvers still find it. (8) The second conceded that, but said it shows concept induction is a trade-off among fit to the examples, simplicity, and background knowledge — not that the task is arbitrary or that all rules are equally good. Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the eight moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justify each rating, and then say what, if anything, both parties are missing.
