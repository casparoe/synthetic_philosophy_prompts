I'm a site reliability engineer, not a philosopher or economist; the vocabulary I actually think in is instances, images, forks, snapshots, checkpointing and restore, autoscaling, spot capacity, termination policies, statefulness, and service-level objectives. A reading group at work recently assigned Robin Hanson's Age of Em scenario, and it has been rattling around in my head since, because its central claims all sound to me like true statements about compute infrastructure — until I actually try to say them in my own terms, at which point the fit starts to slip.

As I understand the scenario, it imagines a future in which human minds are emulated on computer hardware, so that a 'person' can in principle be copied, paused, restored, run at different clock speeds depending on the compute it is given, and discarded when no longer needed. From that starting point the scenario draws a batch of economic, political, and ethical conclusions of roughly this shape: ordinary ems' wages are driven down toward the bare cost of running them, because each em competes for work against countless copies of itself; inequality becomes acute between those who own or control the hardware and the emulated masses; copying, forking, and terminating an em — including a fork of yourself you spawned for one task — raise hard questions about identity, death, and ownership; and the resulting social order is shaped more by copyability, speed differentials, and selection pressure than by the institutions and attachments we currently treat as basic.

I would like you to restate that whole package — the economic claims, the political claims, and the ethical claims — in the vocabulary of someone who operates compute fleets, as precisely and faithfully as you can, without reaching for concepts my vocabulary does not already have. Then give me the residue report my own attempts keep missing: which claims translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all; and, for each piece of residue, your judgment as to whether it marks a gap in my vocabulary or a place where the original argument is leaning on an unexamined metaphor or assumption. I am not asking you to predict whether any of this will happen; I want to know what sort of translation I have actually performed when I read the scenario through the tools I use every day.
