I cover the science-and-ideas beat for a general-interest magazine, and I have spent much of the past year reporting on a methodological quarrel that keeps resurfacing in public scientific controversies, from early-stopped drug trials to election forecasts that attach a probability to a one-off event. I have persuaded two people on opposite sides of the quarrel to take part in an adversarial collaboration. One is a committed frequentist: probability, for her, is an objective long-run relative frequency in a suitably defined sequence of repetitions, and the purpose of statistical inference is to guarantee how often false conclusions would be drawn under repetition. The other is a committed subjectivist, or personalist Bayesian: probability, for him, is the degree of belief of an agent, constrained by coherence and revised by conditioning on evidence, and any proposition at all—a scientific hypothesis, a single patient's prognosis—can legitimately bear one.

Write the joint statement these two could both sign at the end of the collaboration. I am not looking for a survey of positions or a peace-and-reconciliation editorial; I want the document itself. Give me a numbered agreed-claims section whose entries are substantive—neither side would later disavow them—followed by a disagreements table whose rows set out each remaining difference in the wording its own side accepts. Then identify the cruxes: the sub-questions whose settlement would actually move at least one of them. For each crux, specify the argument, case, or simulation both sides would accept as informative, and record each side's prediction in advance, including what outcome would count against its own view.

The statement should at least confront: whether a single event or a hypothesis can have a probability; what the reference-class problem does to frequency talk—when an oncologist says a patient's recurrence risk is 40%, what makes some set of "similar patients" the right one; where priors come from and what disciplines them; whether optional stopping is a methodological sin or an evidential irrelevance; and whether the propensity interpretation is a third live option or a label for the gap each of them leaves open. The measure of success is that both would actually put their names to the agreed section and recognize the formulations of their own disagreements as precise rather than slanted.
