Ive ended up defending a view about cultural preservation that I'm not sure I can get back out of. I was talking with a friend who studies economics about whether we should fund the revitalization of a nearly extinct language, and I found myself saying that even if no future person would ever be made worse off by its disappearance—no one grieves it, no one is deprived of an identity they wanted, no downstream art or knowledge is lost—the disappearance would still be bad. The view, I guess, is that at least some cultural things like languages, musical traditions, ceremonial practices, and craft techniques have a kind of intrinsic value, value over and above their value to the people who hold them. The universe is just better with them in it, the way I'd say it's better with species or great artworks in it, and not only because someone gets something out of them.

What I want is a map of what would count against that. Not just the standard objection that if know one is around to care, nothing can be bad; I want to know which considerations would be decisive and which merely awkward. For example, would it count against me if communities often drop traditions cheerfully when they find something that serves them better, and describe it as gain rather than loss? Would it count against me if the value of a language always turned out to be traceable to things that are themselves instrumentally valuable—poetry, kinship obligations, a sense of home—so that once you've accounted for those, the language itself adds nothing? Are there slippery cases, like a minor dialect, an arbitrary custom, or a family's inside joke, where my view overextends, and is that a reason to reject the view or just a reason to restrict the class of things that get intrinsic value? I'd also like an honest answer to whether the view can be stated without becoming unfalsifiable. If I keep saying the value is there even when nobody would miss it and all the downstream goods are already accounted for, have I insulated the claim so completely that nothing could count against it? And if I have, is that a decisive problem for a claim about intrinsic value, or just what it is to claim intrinsic value? The thing that would probably shift me most is a case where a community themselves judge a tradition worthless and are glad it's gone, and any attempt on my part to mourn it starts to look paternalistic.
