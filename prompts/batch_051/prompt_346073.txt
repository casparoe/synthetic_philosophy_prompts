I am drafting a short essay for a general philosophical audience on the afterlife of Aristotle's four causes, provisionally titled Four Causes Without Four Entities. Rather than a full draft, I am sending the argumentative core below, and I would be grateful for comments of the kind listed at the end.

The essay begins from the familiar story that modern science winnowed the four causes down to efficient causation and expelled formal and final causes as occult. I want to argue that this story is instructively false. The explanatory work Aristotle assigned to formal and final causes did not vanish; it was redistributed and renamed. Natural selection gave biology a restricted, non-intentional notion of function; variational principles and thermodynamics gave physics explanations that look strikingly like explanations by optimality or form; the social sciences have continued to rely on goal-state and equilibrium explanations. Each idiom is locally respectable, but there is no common framework for saying what they share.

My central claim is that the four-cause scheme is best read not as an ontology of four kinds of thing in the world, but as a taxonomy of explanatory why-questions that survives mechanism: what a thing is made of, what organization or form is doing the work, what brought it about, and what end or state the explanation is organized around. On this reading, the fate of teleological explanation is not elimination but fragmentation: the practice persists while the vocabulary has been lost.

The risky move is a closing proposal: we can recover a deflationary notion of final cause without backward causation or cosmic design, meaning simply the end for the sake of which an explanation is organized — the function, optimum, or equilibrium that makes the explanation explanatory. The attraction is that it reintroduces no designer; the worry is that it is too thin to be interesting and collapses into ordinary causal explanation.

I would appreciate comments on four points: (1) whether the opening historical framing is fair, or whether it overstates how much formal and final explanation survived; (2) which of the three redistributed idioms is weakest and should be cut or replaced; (3) whether the deflationary final cause is worth defending or merely relabels ordinary causal explanation; and (4) where a historian of philosophy or a philosopher of biology would most plausibly attack the argument.
