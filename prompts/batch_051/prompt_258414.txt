I am a retired geologist who has recently begun working through philosophy of language, and I now sit on the advisory board of a small foundation that funds the documentation of endangered and previously undescribed languages. The linguists we support often begin with no bilingual interpreter, no grammar, and no shared language between themselves and the speakers. In the first weeks of fieldwork they must choose among competing interpretations of a speaker's utterance — whether a word should be glossed as a kind term, a predicate, or a whole sentence, for example — and an early mistake, once entered in the field notes, tends to propagate into the permanent record. These decisions recur dozens of times a day, often have to be made between utterances and with a speaker whose patience is finite, and they are mostly made by linguists with no philosophical training. I have been reading Davidson on radical interpretation, the principle of charity, and triangulation, and it seems to me that the account contains the bones of a field procedure, but Davidson is doing the epistemology of interpretation, not giving instructions.

Please write a reusable decision procedure that our grantees could actually run during early elicitation sessions, grounded in Davidson's account without pretending that his position is a step-by-step method. Include: (a) the questions to ask, in order, when comparing rival glosses of an utterance; (b) what counts as an adequate answer to each question given the evidence actually available in the field — assent, dissent, pointing, repetition, correction, and the surrounding circumstances; (c) where the procedure stops with a definite conclusion that a proposed gloss is not warranted; and (d) where it must hand the decision to the linguist as an explicit judgment call, and why no procedure can eliminate that judgment. Then apply the procedure to three cases: an ostensive case in which a speaker says something while indicating a river and the linguist is torn between a kind-term gloss, a predicate gloss, and a sentential gloss; a case in which the principle of charity seems to push toward attributing a true belief where the fieldworker has strong independent grounds to think the speaker holds a false one; and a case of the sort Quine and Davidson discuss in which two incompatible glosses both fit all the available evidence so far. Be explicit about what the procedure cannot determine and what further evidence would be needed to narrow the indeterminacy.

Reply format: begin with the procedure itself, with no preface and no restatement of the task; follow it with the three worked applications; then close with a section on the points where the procedure cannot eliminate judgment and what additional evidence would reduce the remaining underdetermination.
