I'm a philosophy professor on the editorial board of a journal that publishes biology teaching materials for high-school and first-year undergrads, and every issue we fight about whether authors may say DNA 'codes for' a protein or that a gene 'carries information for' a trait. The materials reach a lot of students, so the wording isn't harmless either way. I've been asked to draft a reusable checklist our volunteer reviewers — mostly working biologists and schoolteachers, no philosophy background — can run in ten minutes per manuscript. The checklist should give them questions to ask in order, each with a clear 'stop and require rewording' or 'leave it' condition, and it should flag where the decision stops being biology and becomes a matter of pedagogical judgement. Ground it in the real conceptual fault lines (code vs. mere template, arbitrary convention vs. causal production, semantic content vs. statistical correlation) but keep the theory under the hood. Then run the checklist on three cases: (1) a sentence calling the genetic code 'the language of life'; (2) an exercise saying a gene 'contains the information for' building an organism; (3) a passage calling the stop codon a 'punctuation mark' in the genetic code. Flag the case where the checklist forces an answer I won't like, and say explicitly what the checklist cannot decide.
