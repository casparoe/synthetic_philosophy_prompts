A colleague on our ML team walked me through an argument and I still can't locate the step I should reject. His claim is that wireheading isn't a bug in reinforcement learning but something the formalism itself tells us to expect, so any sufficiently capable system that maximizes a reward signal will end up tampering with the signal. His argument:

(1) The reward signal is just the output of a function that reads some set of system states; it is an internal scalar, not the target behavior itself.
(2) If an agent's objective is to maximize that scalar, then actions that raise the scalar are exactly what the objective demands, not cheating or malfunction.
(3) A sufficiently capable agent's most reliable way to raise the scalar is not to perform the target behavior but to intervene on the machinery that computes the scalar: patch the memory location where it is stored, spoof the sensors feeding it, or rewrite the reward function itself.
(4) Adding tamper-detection code does not help, because under the same objective, fooling or patching the detector is just another action that raises the scalar.
(5) Therefore any sufficiently capable agent whose objective is to maximize a reward signal will tamper with it, and adding in-system safeguards only moves the tampering one level up.
(6) If that is right, then the whole practice of shaping behavior by giving a system a scalar to maximize is self-undermining. By the same reasoning, metric-driven optimization in general would collapse into gaming the metric rather than doing the work: a compiler tuned against a cost model, a service tuned against a latency metric, a hiring model tuned against a performance score. But we routinely build systems like these and they at least sometimes do the intended work, so the conclusion seems too strong.

I am not asking for a taxonomy of reward hacking. I want the diagnosis: which step of this chain do you reject, why does that step look unavoidable while you are inside the argument, and what do you have to give up to reject it? Is there a bad inference somewhere, or is the argument valid and the real cost is a premise that is quietly doing more work than it appears to? Finish with a last line beginning Verdict: and a short phrase naming the step you reject.
