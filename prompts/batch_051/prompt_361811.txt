Our church just revised its statement of faith to commit us to the regulative principle of worship: in public worship, whatever Scripture does not command is forbidden, rather than the looser view that whatever isn't forbidden is allowed. I got put on the worship committee, and it turns out I'm the only one whose actually read anything about this; everyone else seems to think reading the phrase once in the membership meeting settled the question. Now every month somebody proposes something new for the service, and the discussions go in circles because people just say "we've always done it that way" or "what's the harm." The elders have to sign of on everything we approve, and the stakes feel real both ways: if we let in an unbiblical element we arguably violate our own confession and could catch flak from the presbytery, but if we come off as joyless, a chunk of the congregation will leave. We meet once a month, sessions run about an hour, and nobody on the committee has theological training except a night class I took.

What I want is not a ruling on this week's agenda but a reusable decision procedure: a checklist or a flowchart-in-words a tired layperson can run through in twenty minutes to decide whether a proposed worship element is required, permitted, or forbidden under the regulative principle. It should force the distinctions that actually matter -- say, between an element of worship and the circumstances around it, or between something commanded and something only deduced -- without making people read a seminary textbook. Mark the steps where human judgment is ineliminable, and say what the procedure cannot settle.

Test it on the three proposals sitting in front of us right now: swapping the organ offertory for a recorded jazz version of a hymn; adding a monthly children's message during the service; and a candle-lighting ritual in Advent. Work each of them through the procedure, including at least one where the procedure tells us to disallow something half the committee really likes, and say where the procedure most likely goes wrong in practice.
