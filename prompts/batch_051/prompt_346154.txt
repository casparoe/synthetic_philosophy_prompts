I'm applying for a small undergraduate research grant for next term and have to hand in a one-page proposal, and I want it to be about the Parfit teletransporter stuff but not the usual versions. My half-formed idea is this: everyone in the personal identity debate treats the teletransporter as an all-or-nothing case—you step in, the machine scans the exact state of your brain and body, the original is destroyed, and a perfect copy wakes up on Mars. Parfit then uses it to argue that what matters in survival isn't identity itself but psychological continuity and connectedness, and the fission case is supposed to show that identity can't be what matters because identity can't branch while your prudential concern for your futures can. But I think the clean binary setup is doing a lot of silent work. The interesting cases are the partial ones: a lossy transporter that preserves only 60% of the psychological connections, a copy that has your episodic memories but not your cares or projects, a branchline case where one copy is much more strongly connected to pre-scan you than the other. I suspect "what matters in survival" only gets a determinate answer once you specify how much and which kind of psychological continuity is preserved, and that forcing all of these into "I survive / I don't survive" misses whatever is actually doing the normative work.

So the question I want the proposal built around is something like: does the relation that prudentially matters in teletransporter cases have a graded, multi-dimensional structure, and if it does, does treating it as all-or-nothing identity distort the standard Parfitian argument? Turn that into a tight one-pager: state the question so it has a determinate answer, say why the existing positions don't settle it (they mostly assume the clean case), say what I'd actually do over the semester—what cases I'd construct, what principles or intuitions I'd test them against—and be honest about what would count as the project failing or getting too big.
