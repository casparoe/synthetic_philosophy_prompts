I’m a PhD student in moral philsophy, specifically early modern stuff, and I’ve been reading Hume on promising for a seminar. I keep turing over this passage from A Treatise of Human Nature, Book 3, Part 2, Section 5:

“I say, first, that a promise is not intelligible naturally, nor antecedent to human conventions; and that a man, unacquainted with society, could never enter into any engagements with another, even tho’ they could perceive each other’s thoughts by intuition.”

Hume’s line is that promising isn’t a natural mental act, it’s a convention. I want to see if that actually illuminates the ethics of small promises, like saying “I’ll be there at 7” or “I’ll send the file by Tuesday.” If promises are artificial all the way down, then punctuality norms are just a set of brittle social technologies, not expressions of sincerity or respect. But I also suspect we treat them as something deeper — being late feels like more than breaking a convention.

Could you write a satirical “community FAQ” or “user guide” for a fictional app/startup called something like Tymely (or whatever) that treats punctuality as a rigorous ethical system, maybe with a karma ledger for small promises. Make it deadpan, corporate-policy voice, around 600 words. The satire should land the tension in Hume’s view: once you conventionalize promising, small promises start functioning like debts or contracts, and people can game them, moralize them, or hide behind them, without ever touching whether they actually intended to be reliable. Don’t state the tension outright; let the form do it.
