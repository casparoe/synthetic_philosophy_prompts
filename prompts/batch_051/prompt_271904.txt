I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've been rereading John Stuart Mill's *The Subjection of Women* for a piece on how often "it's just nature" shows up as a trump card in political arguments. This bit seems like the real engine under the whole essay: "So long as an opinion is strongly rooted in the feelings, it gains rather than loses in stability by having a preponderating weight of argument against it. For if it were accepted as a result of argument, the refutation of the argument might shake the solidity of the conviction; but when it rests solely on feeling, the worse it fares in argumentative contest, the more persuaded its adherents are that their feeling must have some deeper ground, which the arguments do not reach; and while the feeling remains, it is always throwing up fresh intrenchments of argument to repair any breach made in the old." Now, a colleague and I are stuck on how far this goes. She thinks Mill's point is deep but too cynical — sometimes when people say a sex difference is natural, they're doing real biology or anthropology, not just scrambling to protect a feeling. I think Mill would say the word "natural" gets invoked exactly because the feeling is doing the work. Could you write a dialogue between the two of us, both informed and arguing in good faith, that works through that passage and tries to settle whether appeals to nature are usually post hoc intrenchments or whether Mill's diagosis needs a limiting condition? I don't need a winner declared, just a real back-and-forth where we respond to each other's actual points.
