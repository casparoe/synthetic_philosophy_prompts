I'm a law clerk for a state supreme court justice, and next term I need to prep for a case. She's got a reputation as a textualist, but honestly her track record is messier then that, and I want you to figure out what interpretive rule she's actually running — textualist, purposivist, or something else — before I write the bench memo. You're an expert on statutory interpretation and jurisprudence; take your time, and maybe start by brainstorming the different rules that could fit her votes before you settle on one.

Heres the track record:

- Sales tax case: a state sales tax statute exempted fruit but not vegetables. She voted that a tomato is a vegetable, because that's how ordinary shoppers use the word, even though botanically it's a fruit.

- Stolen airplane case: a state statute made it a crime to transport a stolen motor vehicle across county lines. A man flew a stolen plane across county lines. She joined an opinion holding that a plane is not a motor vehicle, because ordinary usage means land vehicles, even though the statute's broad purpose of stopping vehicle theft might be served by covering planes.

- Church organist case: an old state statute barred bringing workers into the state under contract to perform labor or service of any kind. A church hired an organist from a neighboring state. She voted the statute doesn't apply, saying the legislature obviously meant manual labor, and that reading the phrase literally would produce a result it never intended.

- Attorney's fees case: a state civil-rights statute lets a prevailing party recover fees. A plaintiff won a preliminary injunction, the defendant then changed the offending policy, and the case was dismissed as moot before any final judgment. She held the plaintiff wasn't a prevailing party, because that term in ordinary legal usage requires a final judgment on the merits, even though the suit achieved its goal.

- Sex discrimination case: a state employment statute makes it unlawful to fire someone because of sex. She joined an opinion holding it covers firing a male employee for crying at work, on the ground that the employer was enforcing a sex stereotype and sex was a but-for cause of the firing. She said the text alone gets there; she didn't touch the legislative history.

The new case: our state clean-water statute requires a permit before discharging any pollutant into navigable waters. A factory discharges wastewater into a desert arroyo that is dry most of the year. After heavy rain, the water in the arroyo flows into a navigable river about 40 miles away. The question is whether that arroyo counts as navigable waters for the permit requirement.

Predict how she'll vote. Which prior case is the closest analog, and which interpretive rule or small set of rules best explains the whole track record? If her precedents genuinely underdetermine the new case, say what principle she'd be adopting with each possible vote, and give me your best guess with the reasons for it. Also name a future case, not on our docket, that would most cleanly tell the competing rules apart.

At the very end, on its own line, put \answer{Yes} if you predict she votes that the arroyo counts as navigable waters, or \answer{No} if you predict she votes it doesn't.
