I'm on the acquisitions committee of a small city historical society, and we've been offered the complete personal and business papers of the Calloway family, which owned and ran the town's textile mill from the 1880s through the 1940s. It's around forty boxes: ledgers, correspondence, payroll books, photographs, even family diaries. The family heir wants us to take the whole thing, process it, and mount an exibit about the town's industrial past. The catch is that the collection is nearly silent about the people who actually worked the mill — mostly Black farmhands and later Appalachian migrants — except when they show up as names in a wage book or as troublemakers in a floor manager's memo. A local descendants' group has asked us either to decline the donation or to accept it only on the condition that we first build a parallel collection of oral histories and other materials documenting the workers and their families, before anything goes on display. One of our board members, though, says that's not an archive's job: our job is to preserve what survives, and holding the Calloway papers hostage to a collecting project we may never complete would just keep real historical material out of public view. I have to circulate a short written opinion before our meeting next week. Should we accept the donation as offered, decline it, or accept with conditions — and if conditions, which ones, and on what timeline? Please write it as a decision memo the board could actually vote on, with a clear recommendation and a fair treatment of the strongest reason on the losing side.

The deeper issue for us is how to think about the silences in a source like this: is the payroll ledger evidence of the workers' lives or evidence of their erasure, and does accepting the collection amount to endorsing that erasure? I'd rather the memo not just recite our collections policy but actually reason through that question. Please write in American English. And at the very end, in a short note addressed to me rather than part of the memo, say how confident you are in the recommendation and single out the point you're least sure about.
