Near the start of Chapter XXV of The Prince, Machiavelli writes: "Nevertheless, not to extinguish our free will, I hold it to be true that Fortune is the arbiter of one-half of our actions, but that she still leaves us to direct the other half, or perhaps a little less." He goes on to picture Fortune as a flooding river against which wise men build dikes and channels while the weather is fair.

Now consider a case he could not have pictured. A democratic government is deciding whether to replace parole hearings with an actuarial risk score. The score is trained on the outcomes of millions of past cases; its makers show that it predicts re-offending more accurately than the parole board does, and that the board, when shown the score, almost always defers to it. One official defends replacing the board by invoking Machiavelli's own principle: if Fortune governs half of what happens to a defendant—his parents, his neighborhood, the economy he enters after release—then a government should measure that half as precisely as it can. A critic replies that the score does not observe Fortune from the outside; it alters the half Machiavelli says is left to us. A defendant stamped high-risk is then watched more closely and denied opportunities, so his own efforts matter less, while one stamped low-risk is left alone, so his own efforts matter more. The prediction, the critic says, eats into the second half.

Apply Machiavelli's maxim to this case. What does it require the government to do? Argue the question from the words of the maxim, from its purpose, from what Machiavelli would probably say if shown a validated statistical risk model, and from what a reasonable modern reader should take the passage to mean. Then commit to one approach and set out what it demands: adopt the score as a sharper map of Fortune, reject it as a usurpation of the part left to virtù, or impose conditions that would make it compatible with the maxim—and say what those conditions would be.
