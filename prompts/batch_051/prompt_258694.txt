Bernard Williams argued that a person has a reason to do something only if that action could be reached by sound practical deliberation from the person's existing motivations; reasons that are supposed to bind a person independently of what they already care about are, on his view, suspect. He died before the self-described effective-altruism movement took shape; that movement argues that nearly anyone in a rich country has decisive reason to give a large portion of their income to the most cost-effective global-health charities, even if concern for distant strangers plays no role in their current motivations. Predict his assessment: if Williams were alive today and pressed to give an honest verdict on this movement's central argument, where exactly would he agree, where would he resist, and on what grounds? Answer in exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion; the five points together should constitute a complete verdict.
