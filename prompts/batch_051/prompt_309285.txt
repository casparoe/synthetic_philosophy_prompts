Suppose that, beginning with the next general election, a stable democracy with first-past-the-post districts and voluntary voting introduces the following reform. One week before election day, every registered voter receives from a trusted, nonpartisan electoral commission a personalized, costless estimate of the probability that their ballot will be decisive. Decisive is reported at two levels. The first number is the probability that, among all other ballots in the voter's district, the margin will be zero or one, so that this single ballot would break a tie or force one. The second is the probability that the district result itself will decide or tie which party controls the national legislature. The commission also reports the product of the two. The numbers come from a public statistical model of the distribution of all other ballots; the model is updated daily, and its estimates are accepted across the major parties as unbiased. For most voters the district-level number will be effectively zero to several decimal places; only in races the model sees as nearly tied, and especially in small districts, will it become appreciable—at most on the order of a percent—while the national-level number will almost always be many orders of magnitude smaller still.

Predict, with the chain of reasoning made explicit, how this reform changes voting and electoral politics over the following three election cycles. Cover at least: overall turnout and the demographic composition of the electorate; where campaigns spend money and target mobilization, and whether that changes the platforms candidates run on; what voters say and reveal about why they vote, and the standing of duty-based and expressive explanations of turnout; and how the perceived legitimacy of elections and of mandates shifts once these numbers are public.

Do not settle for a single overall verdict. For each major prediction, separate what follows robustly from the mere availability of the information from what depends on contestable assumptions. Among the assumptions to consider: how voters process extremely small probabilities, whether instrumental considerations are the main reason people vote or merely a rationalization added to duty and expressive motives, how campaigns exploit the new numbers, and whether the numbers can remain accurate once voters and campaigns begin responding to them. State which predictions you are most and least confident in, and identify at least three empirical assumptions that, if they turned out false, would overturn a prediction you make.
