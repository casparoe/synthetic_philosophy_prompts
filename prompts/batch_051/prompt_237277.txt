I'm a philosophy PhD student working on formal decision theory and distributive justice, and through some weird co-teaching arrangement I'm giving a theory seminar in the econ department next month. The paper I'm presenting is basically a defense of the claim that the Rawls/Harsanyi fight over the original position is, at bottom, a fight about decision rules under ignorance, and that Harsanyi's veil-of-ignorance argument for average utilitarianism is only as strong as the uniform prior it smuggles in. My line is that behind the veil you don't have objective probabilities over which citizen you'll be; you have a decision problem under ignorance, and there are at least three real contenders — maximin, minimax regret, and expected utility with equal probabilities (the principle of insufficient reason) — and none of them is ruled out by the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms alone, because those axioms concern risk with known probabilities, not ignorance. So Rawls's maximin isn't the obviously irrational bugbear economists tend to think it is, even though I don't want to defend maximin as universally correct. My argument has two parts: first I formalize the veil as a decision problem under ignorance with the possible identities as states, and show Harsanyi's conclusion only follows once you add a uniform prior; second I compare the three rules on a few simple cases and argue they encode genuinely different normative attitudes toward the worst-off.

The audience is a micro theory seminar: mostly microeconomic theorists, two people who work on ambiguity and decision theory, a welfare economist or two, plus one political philosopher who knows Rawls well and is already suspicious of economists. They'll think Rawls just doesn't get expected utility, and they'll care a lot about whether my formalization satisfies their standard axioms. I'm much more nervous about the questions then the talk. What I need is a Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this audience, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way an econ theorist would put it, with a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is soft. Tell me which objections have real force and what I should concede up front, and include the one question I'm hoping nobody asks.
