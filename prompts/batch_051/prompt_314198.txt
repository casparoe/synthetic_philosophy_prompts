I recently became a parent, and the question of how much weight to give to the far future has stopped being abstract for me; it now affects how I think about my savings, my work, and what I owe to the future. But I keep running into an objection I cannot settle.

The argument for far-future fanaticism seems to me almost arithmetically forced. If there is a non-trivial chance that the future contains an astronomically large number of lives—across our galaxy and beyond, over astronomical timescales—then even a very small reduction in the probability of permanent extinction has expected value that dwarfs all near-term good one could do. That would make the far future dominate any serious decision about where to direct resources.

However, the simulation argument looks as if it attacks the part I was taking for granted. In its standard trilemma form, if posthuman civilizations would run many ancestor simulations, then the typical mind like ours is simulated, and we should think we probably are in one. A newer twist I have come across sharpens this against the fanatic: a simulation needs only to be run for the historical window its creators are interested in, so there is no reason for our simulated civilization to be permitted to reach a galaxy-spanning future; letting it do so and allowing recursive simulations of simulations would consume unbounded computing. On that picture, if we are simulated, the far future the fanatic counts on is probably truncated, and the base-level future is one our actions may not touch.

So my question is: does the fanatic argument survive this development? Point to the exact premise it bears on, and say whether the fanatic can repair the argument without giving up its conclusion.
