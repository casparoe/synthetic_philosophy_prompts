You are a professional philosopher working at the intersection of decision theory, personal identity, and the philosophy of mind; I am a journalist who covers science and ideas and have been reporting on the small industry that cryopreserves legally dead people in the hope of future revival. I want your help thinking through a piece about what the practice presupposes, not about whether revival will actually happen.

Here is the practice as I will describe it to readers. A customer, while alive, funds a cryonics contract, usually through a life insurance policy made payable to a preservation organization. After legal death is pronounced, a standby team begins cooling and, where possible, circulatory support; the person is then vitrified and stored in liquid nitrogen at a temperature low enough to halt almost all chemical change. The organization holds the body or brain in that state indefinitely and manages a fund meant to keep the storage going, on the expectation that some future technology—molecular repair, whole-brain emulation, or something not yet specifiable—will restore the person. The contract is an explicitly long-shot bet: the signer pays premiums for a small probability of an enormous future payoff, and the payoff, if it arrives, will be collected by the revived person, not by the signer alive today.

What philosophical commitments does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory of personal identity, death, probability, and rational choice under uncertainty that makes the contract a coherent bet rather than a category mistake or the purchase of a comforting ritual. Be strict about three levels: what the practice strictly presupposes, what it merely fits well with, and where it is underdetermined such that several rival theories would each license the same arrangement. Then say what would have to change in the practice—the contract language, the marketing, the trust, the storage decisions, the revival protocols—if one of the presupposed views were replaced by a serious rival.

I would also like you to address a tension I keep noticing in my reporting. Preservationists go to great lengths to retain biological detail, and the underlying technology is justified by the idea that future medicine will repair the specific body. Yet many of the same people say, when asked, that what matters about them is the pattern of memories, dispositions, and capacities, which could survive in a very different substrate. Does the practice secretly side with one of these views of identity over the other, or is it designed to be agnostic between them, and is that agnosticism coherent?

Please work in stages. First brainstorm the candidate commitments the practice could be taken to endorse, without yet deciding which are strict. Then sort them into the three levels and defend each placement with a concrete feature of the practice. Then trace the consequences of at least two rival replacements. Think carefully, and close by identifying which commitment, if abandoned, would do the most to make the practice look irrational, and which could be abandoned while leaving the practice more or less intact.
