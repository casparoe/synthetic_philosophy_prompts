Consider how private law handles two adjacent claims in many common-law systems. When a failed sterilisation or a negligent prenatal test deprives a woman of the chance to avoid or end a pregnancy, she can generally recover the extra costs of raising the unexpected child — what lawyers call a wrongful birth claim. Yet when the child herself sues, claiming she has been harmed by being brought into existence with a disability, the claim — a wrongful life claim — is almost always rejected. A recurring judicial explanation runs: the clinic did not cause the disability; at most it caused this child to exist rather than not to exist, and no comparison is possible between an impaired existence and no existence at all.

Reconstruct the philosophical commitments this pattern of rulings presupposes about personal identity and origin. Does the practice ultimately rest on origin essentialism — the thesis that a person could not have come from a different sperm and egg, so that a possible healthy child from the same parents would be a different person rather than a better-off version of this one? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, identify where rival theories of harm and identity would license the same rulings, and say what, if anything, would have to change in the law if origin essentialism were rejected.
