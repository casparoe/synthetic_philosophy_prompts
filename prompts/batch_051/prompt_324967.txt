The examination question was:

Does the ticking time bomb scenario show that the moral prohibition on torture should not be held absolute? In your answer, clearly distinguish between (i) an all-things-considered judgment about what the interrogator ought to do in the scenario as described, and (ii) an institutional judgment about how a legal system ought to regulate torture under real-world conditions.

The student wrote:

The ticking time bomb scenario stipulates that an interrogator is certain that a detainee knows the location of a bomb, that torture will make him reveal it, and that many lives will be lost if he does not act. Under those stipulations it is hard to insist that the interrogator should simply stand by; he would at least be excused for torturing, and most likely he does the right thing. But if he does the right thing, then the moral prohibition on torture cannot be absolute, because a rule that contains a justified exception is by definition overrideable. This is the core of the lesser-evil justification.

The institutional question is separate. The legal system should retain a formal, categorical ban: torture should remain a criminal offence, and no list of permitted interrogation methods should be written into the law. In the rare genuine case, however, an official who tortured could still be convicted and then have the sentence commuted or be pardoned, so that he is not treated as an ordinary sadist. This arrangement preserves the absolute legal ban while acknowledging the exceptional moral situation. The result is that the prohibition is morally overridable but legally absolute.
