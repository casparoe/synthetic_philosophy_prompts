I've been thinking about how concepts get induced from a handful of examples, and I keep noticing a structural echo between Bongard problems and the way much of analytic philosophy proceeds by cases. A Bongard problem presents twelve panels of simple line drawings, six on the left and six on the right; the solver must state the rule that all the left figures satisfy and all the right figures violate. The striking thing is that the twelve panels never uniquely determine the rule: indefinitely many predicates separate the two groups, including gerrymandered disjunctive ones, so finding some separating rule or other is not yet solving the problem. What the solver is really after is the rule the constructor had in mind — in practice, the rule that cuts at the salient, natural joints, the one another human being would find. Yet nothing in the panels themselves marks one separating rule as the intended one.

The same structure seems to be at work when a philosophical analysis is built from paradigm cases and stress-tested with counterexamples. A theory is fitted to a small stock of settled verdicts — about knowledge, intentional action, or moral permissibility, say — and a counterexample acts like a newly added panel that forces the theory to be refitted. The cases underdetermine the theory just as the panels underdetermine the rule; what does the extra work is a shared sense of which features are projectible and which are gerrymandered, together with an implicit story about what the analysis is supposed to track. It looks to me as though having an adequate analysis is like having solved the Bongard problem, and a disputed case is like a Bongard problem on which different solvers favor different rules that all fit the panels.

Is this a genuine identity of structure, a loose analogy, or a confusion? If it is genuine, does anything transfer from the study of Bongard problems to philosophical methodology — for instance, the result that a Bongard problem is soluble only because the constructor's intended rule supplies a fact of the matter that the panels themselves leave open? That forces the sore question: does philosophical analysis have any real analogue of the constructor's intent (linguistic practice? the structure of the world? reflective equilibrium?), and if not, does the analogy break exactly where it would be interesting? My own suspicion is that the analogy overpredicts: it seems to predict that, given a fixed stock of paradigm cases, there is a fact of the matter about which analysis is the intended one, and I doubt that there is — but I may be wrong, and I invite you to say so. Please take the role of someone expert both in cognitive-science accounts of concept learning and in philosophical methodology. First state the strongest, most charitable version of my analogy, then check carefully where the structures match and where they come apart. Name at least one prediction the analogy would make that you think turns out false. One meta-request: if the parallel rests on a confusion — about Bongard problems, about the method of cases, or about underdetermination — tell me plainly rather than politely grading the analogy; I would rather learn that the setup is flawed than get an agreeable answer.
