Since retiring from a long career in the natural sciences, I have been reading philosophy of history, and one question has begun to trouble me. The habit of mind I brought away from the laboratory is the controlled comparison: to find out whether something made a difference, hold the rest of the system fixed, remove or alter that one factor, and observe whether the outcome changes. That habit has left me strongly drawn to a counterfactual account of causation in history. On this view, to call the assassination at Sarajevo a cause of the First World War is to say something like this: had the assassination not occurred, or had it occurred differently in the right way, the war would not have broken out as and when it did, or would not have broken out in the same manner. The view has real systematic virtues. It is parsimonious; it replaces the sprawling lists of factors historians produce with a disciplined test; and it draws a clean line between genuine causes and mere background conditions.

But I find I cannot actually live with the view. So many of the outcomes historians care about are overdetermined, or nearly so: the war was very likely coming even without Sarajevo, the panic would have occurred even without that particular failure, the discovery would have been made within a few years by someone else. Yet when a historian says the assassination was among the causes of the war, I do not hear that as an error. The intuition is firm, not a prejudice I am ready to set aside: some events that were not difference-makers were nonetheless causes. So I am left with a compelling theory and a recalcitrant intuition, and I want to know which should yield.

I am not asking for a verdict on who is right. I want the strongest version of each side. First, the case for the counterfactual theory: that it captures what causation itself is, and that the historian's attribution in an overdetermined case is either strictly false or is better reinterpreted as a claim about something other than causing — salience, explanatory emphasis, moral or political responsibility, or what a good narrative foregrounds. Second, the case for the intuition: that it is tracking a feature of historical causation the theory leaves out, so the theory must be amended — perhaps with an INUS analysis (a cause as an insufficient but necessary part of an unnecessary but sufficient condition), with degrees of necessity rather than an all-or-nothing test, with contrastive explanation that fixes a different contrast class, or with a distinction between causing and explaining that lets difference-making govern one and not the other.

Then I want a negotiated verdict. What would justify each of the three moves: debunking and dropping the intuition as an artifact of how we narrate the past; revising or abandoning the counterfactual theory; or finding a reading on which both survive? In particular, I would like to know whether the apparent conflict turns on the difference between "a cause" and "the cause," on how finely or coarsely we describe the outcome, or on the difference between singular causation in a controlled system and multi-causal explanation in history — and what it would take for each side to be the one that gives ground.
