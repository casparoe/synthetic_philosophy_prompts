A friend has an argument that appears to prove that escalation of commitment is not a bias, and I am trying to steelman it before I decide where it overreaches. I want a diagnosis rather than a survey.

The argument:

(1) In the standard two-player dollar auction, a one-dollar bill is sold in ascending minimum increments of ε (say 5¢); the highest bidder pays their bid and wins the dollar; the second-highest bidder also pays their bid and receives nothing. Suppose you are the second-highest bidder at b, and the other player holds the standing high bid at b+ε, so your least raise is to b+2ε. If you stop, you pay b, for payoff −b. If you raise and the other player then stops, you win the dollar at price b+2ε, for payoff 1−(b+2ε), which exceeds −b whenever 2ε < 1. So the next bid, conditional on its ending the auction, always improves your payoff, no matter how high b has climbed.

(2) At every decision point, the current second-highest bidder faces this same conditional arithmetic. If the next bid does not end the auction, the identical situation merely reappears one increment higher with the roles reversed. Therefore the local arithmetic never tells the current player that stopping is better than bidding, at any bid level.

(3) So the escalation is stepwise rational from inside the auction. No bid level can be singled out as the point where reason switches from continuing to quitting; the only ex ante rational move is never to enter.

(4) Ordinary escalation of commitment has the same structure: abandoning a failing project realizes the accumulated loss, while one more unit of money, effort, or time buys another chance to avoid that loss; if the project does not end, the same choice recurs one unit later. The next increment is always small compared with the loss it might avoid.

(5) Therefore, either escalation of commitment in general is also stepwise rational—in which case “cut your losses” cannot be a rational principle, only a patch for finite budgets, willpower, or agency problems—or the argument proves too much and one of (1)–(4) is false.

Which numbered step is the first false one, and what exactly does rejecting it cost? In particular, if the flaw lies within the dollar-auction reasoning itself, is there a repaired version that still forces an ideal agent to keep bidding once entered, or does the repair kill the escalatory conclusion? If the flaw lies only in the generalization from the auction to ordinary projects, what precise feature of the auction’s conditional losses distinguishes them from genuinely sunk costs in real commitments? I would most like to keep “cut your losses” as a rational principle, but I do not want to keep it by simply declaring the auction a weird game and leaving the transfer question unanswered.
