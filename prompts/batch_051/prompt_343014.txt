I keep returning to a parallel between the principle of subsidiarity in federal systems and error handling in layered software, and I cannot decide whether it is a real structural identity or just a shared picture of layers deferring to one another.

The mapping, as I see it, runs like this. In both cases an event that exceeds the smooth running of affairs is handled at the lowest layer that can deal with it; higher layers are expected to intervene only when the lower layer cannot cope, and not to take over work the lower layer can do, even if they could do it a little better. The subsidiarity formula is very close to an exception-handling discipline: handle the exception at the level that can fix it, propagate it upward only when that level cannot, and keep the caller from redoing the callee's job.

The similarity seems to extend to the standard pathologies. In software, exceptions get swallowed when a lower layer claims to have handled a problem it has actually mishandled, and exceptions get escalated too eagerly when lower layers punt everything upward while doing none of the real work. I see analogues in federations: constituent units invoke subsidiarity to shield underprovision, and central authorities use crises to expand their remit and never give it back, as in the recurring argument over whether the EU or the member states should be acting on a given issue. Both cases feature a boundary specified in advance but renegotiated in practice, and both arguments are largely about information: whether the higher level can know enough about local conditions to intervene without destroying the local knowledge that justified the division of labor in the first place.

Is this parallel deep or superficial? Please give me your honest assessment. In particular:
(1) Identify the nearest things to a genuine isomorphism between the two, and the exact point where the analogy comes apart.
(2) Say what would have to be true of both sides for results from software engineering — about error swallowing, cascading failures, or leaky abstractions — to transfer to federations.
(3) Name at least one prediction the parallel suggests that is likely false or misleading in politics, or, if you think none is, give the strongest surviving reason to doubt the parallel.

Begin with a one-sentence bottom line (deep, superficial, or mixed), then defend it in no more than 400 words.
