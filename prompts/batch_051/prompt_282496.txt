I have been working for some time on a distinction in the epistemology of deepfakes, and I should be glad to have it scrutinised by someone with a more systematic eye than mine. I shall set out the distinction, the central claim built on it, and an example; I should then be grateful for an honest assessment of whether the apparatus is coherent, whether it is new or merely a restatement of something familiar, and where it breaks.

I call the first notion a *terminating backstop*. In ordinary evidential practice, some classes of item—documentary photographs, mobile-phone footage, the records of a well-run institution—function not merely as further support on one side of a dispute, but as the point at which a factual disagreement properly ends. If two parties disagree about what a public official said at a meeting, and a complete, unedited recording appears, both sides normally treat the matter as settled. To go on disputing the words would be to change the subject from the official's utterance to the authenticity of the recording, and that new question would itself require some independent backstop. A terminating backstop, as I understand it, is an evidential item whose distinctive role is to halt the regress of challenges, not to add one more piece of evidence to one party's case.

The claim I should like examined is that the primary epistemic cost of deepfakes is not that they introduce falsehoods into our evidence, but that they demote every documentary item from a terminating backstop to what I have been calling a *partisan exhibit*. A partisan exhibit can always be met by a further query that the item itself cannot settle: and why should I suppose this particular recording is real? In a sufficiently mature deepfake environment, that query is no longer frivolous. The consequence is that contested recordings cease to terminate disputes and instead prolong them, because the genuineness of each recording becomes one further object of dispute rather than a shared platform beneath it.

I also want to claim that this demotion can occur without any particular deepfake being circulated, believed, or even produced. I call this second notion a *counterfactual forgery*: the deepfake that does not exist but that the parties know could exist. My suggestion is that robust awareness of the capacity is enough to strip a recording of its terminating role, since any defence of the recording will eventually appeal to a further recording or technical report, which is itself open to the same doubt. If that is right, deepfakes damage our evidential practices less by what they show than by what their bare possibility licenses us to say.

The example that motivates me is this. A credible allegation of misconduct in a public institution is supported by a bystander's video. In an environment in which such videos can be convincingly synthesised, the accused party need not produce a rival video. They need only observe, correctly, that the capacity exists. The dispute then shifts from what happened to whether the video is authentic, and the authenticity question cannot be closed by anything the video itself contains. The video has been, as I would put it, demoted from backstop to exhibit.

I should value an assessment on three points. First, is the distinction between a terminating backstop and a partisan exhibit coherent, and does it mark a real difference in evidential role rather than merely a difference in our confidence or manners? Second, does the notion of a counterfactual forgery do genuine explanatory work, or does it collapse into familiar thoughts about undercutting defeaters, higher-order evidence, or the longstanding point that documentary evidence has always been forgeable and that deepfakes merely widen an existing capacity? Third, where does the account break: are there cases in which a recording continues to terminate disputes even within a mature deepfake environment, and what would such cases show about whether the harm is to a particular medium or to the very practice of treating anything as a backstop at all? I should be obliged if your assessment were written in British English.
