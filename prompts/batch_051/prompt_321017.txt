I'm a PhD student working on the history of self-sufficency ideals—think autarky, the Stoic sage, the self-sufficient individual, and how that ideal gets applied to whole political communities. Lately I've noticed I've smuggled something like it into my own intellectual life, and I want to know whether it's defensible or just a strange case of my research colonizing my epistemology.

Here's the policy I've been following. If I can't reconstruct the argument for a view cold—without appealing to what the literature says, without saying "I remember reading a persuasive treatment," and certainly without invoking the fact that someone I respect holds it—then I treat myself as not entitled to the view. This is strongest when I'm writing: I won't put a claim in a dissertation chapter unless I could defend it solo against a sharp-but-hostile questioner, using only material I've already digested. And in discussion, if someone asks why I believe P, I won't fall back on "so-and-so argues that somewhere"; I either give the argument myself or retract P from the set of things I'll publicly stand behind. My advisor says this is epistemic vanity—that nearly everything we know comes from testimony and the division of cognitive labor, and that pretending my creedence in a claim should be self-generated is either impossible or a good way to become confidently wrong in private. That stung, because I use roughly the same move to criticize national autarky arguments in my own field: countries don't need to produce everything they consume, so why should a person need to produce every justification they rely on?

So: is this rule defensible, or is it exactly the bias I study in my day job? If the unqualified version is indefensible, is there a restricted version that keeps the good effects—forcing me to actually understand things, catching fake comprehension, not laundering deference into a false sense of ownership—without the absurd demand that I be an epistemic hermit?
