I work as a journalist covering health policy and the life sciences, and I keep running into the same difficulty in debates about triage. Before I read another paper or interview another bioethicist, I want the specification sheet: what must any adequate theory of allocating scarce, lifesaving medical resources get right? By theory I mean a principled rule or family of rules for deciding who gets treated when not everyone can be — QALY maximization, save-the-most-lives, first-come first-served, a lottery with priority classes, or something else. Fix the purpose as follows: the theory has to be usable under real triage conditions, meaning time pressure, imperfect information about prognosis and long-term quality of life, and decision-makers who are not philosophers; and it has to say something principled about the usual fault lines — aggregating many small benefits against one large one, treating an identifiable patient now against reducing a merely statistical risk, discriminating on the basis of age or disability or refusing to, and whether the right unit is lives, life-years, or something like well-being-adjusted life-years.

Write the desiderata. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that makes it a requirement, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are only the preferences of one camp. I especially want to know which single requirement excludes the most candidates, and whether any pair of requirements cannot be jointly satisfied — for example, whether the considerations that push toward QALY maximization and the considerations that push toward never valuing some lives less than others can be met by any single rule.

Since I will use this list to judge theories, I also want it to expose itself to correction: tell me where the list is most likely to fail, which requirement you would drop or amend under pressure from a hard case or a real feasibility constraint, and what argument or empirical finding would lead you to revise the list itself. Please write in American English.
