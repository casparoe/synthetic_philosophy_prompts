In my political theory seminar we’ve been reading Mill’s late essays, and I keep getting stuck on this passage from his unfinished *Socialism*, which he drafted around 1869 but was published only in 1879:

“But they are not engaged (we may call it bribed) by any peculiar interest of their own, to the support of property as it is, least of all to the support of inequalities of property. So far as their power reaches, or may hereafter reach, the laws of property have to depend for support upon considerations of a public nature, upon the estimate made of their conduciveness to the general welfare, and not upon motives of a mere personal character operating on the minds of those who have control over the Government.”

The discussion in class turned into a disagreement about what Mill would think of something he couldn’t have seen coming: mass retirement accounts. A friend argued that Mill’s worry is now partly outdated, because most wage earners in rich democracies own capital indirectly through pensions, 401(k)s, and index funds. She said that gives workers a genuine stake in the system, so the laws of property no longer rest merely on the class interest of the propertied. Another student said that completely misses Mill’s point. His point wasn’t that each worker needs to own a tiny diversified slice of capital, but that the rules of property have to be justified on public grounds once working people can act together politically. Owning a few thousand dollars in an index fund, he argued, doesn’t give you the kind of power Mill is talking about, and it might even function as a bribe in Mill’s sense—giving people just enough of a stake to keep them from asking whether the system as a whole is justified.

I’m less interested in who’s right about Mill scholarship and more in working through the counterfactual carefully. Would Mill have accepted mass retirement accounts as satisfying the demand he makes in this passage, or would he have treated them as a politically relevant new kind of property holding that still leaves the basic challenge intact? Then push it one step further: what would he have thought about platform cooperatives—say, Uber drivers collectively owning the app they drive for—versus ordinary gig workers holding some company stock? I’d like a prediction of his view that stays close to the passage and doesn’t just collapse into “he liked worker ownership” or “he opposed markets.”
