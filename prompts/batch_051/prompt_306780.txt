As a PhD student working on the moral psychology of engagement with fiction, I keep encountering a particular conversational move. In a recent reading group on the ethics of games, I asked whether a player who spends an evening having her character methodically humiliate and injure a non-player character — carefully, repeatedly, for no in-game goal beyond seeing how much the simulation permits — is doing something morally blameworthy: either because the character is the kind of thing that can be wronged, or because the act expresses or cultivates something vicious in the player. A colleague cut the discussion short by saying "it's just a game," and most of the room nodded. I doubt that settles the question, but I also suspect the sentence is being asked to do several different pieces of work at once.

I would like you to distinguish the precise claims that "it's just a game" could be asserting in this context. The candidates might be claims about the ontology of fictional characters, about the moral significance of voluntary rule-bounded make-believe, about what in-game behavior reveals about a player's real dispositions, about the absence of real-world victims, or about the norms that should govern criticism of players. For each candidate reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible position, or false; then identify which reading or readings would actually license my colleague's dismissal of the question, and which would not. Present the readings as a menu I could bring back to the reading group.
