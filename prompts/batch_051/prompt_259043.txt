I've been teaching the extended mind debate for years and I still can't settle when an external tool is part of my cognition and when it's just, you know, a tool. Here are the verdicts my gut keeps giving, and I'd like you to help me find the principle behind them, if their is one.

A man with a serious memory impairment keeps a notebook with his addresses and appointments. He carries it everywhere, reaches for it automatically, and trusts it the way I trust my biological memory. I'm comfortable saying his memory is at least partly constituted by the notebook.

A woman with no memory impairment uses her phone for every trip, including routes she's driven a hundred times; if she leaves the phone at home she cannot get anywhere. I'm much less comfortable saying the phone is part of her memory. It feels like she's using a tool badly, not like the tool has become part of her mind.

A chess player can't hold complex positions in her head and works by sliding the pieces around the board, trying moves, and seeing what results. While she plays I want to say the board is part of her reasoning process, but I'd stop short of saying it's part of her cognitive system. I'm not sure why.

A professional relies on their spouse to remember all their social obligations: birthdays, thank-you notes, the works. The spouse is as reliable and as automatically accessed as the notebook in the first case, but I don't think the spouse's memory is part of the professional's mind, even though I'd say the notebook is. That looks inconsistent to me.

An engineer does all her arithmetic with a calculator and hasn't done mental arithmetic in years. I have no temptation to call the calculator part of her mind, even though it's doing operations she used to do in her head.

So: is there a single principle that gets all of these, or at least all but one? It would have to explain why the notebook is special, why the phone and calculator aren't, why the board is half-way, and whether the spouse case should flip my first verdict. Then test it on a case I keep going back and forth on: a writer who dictates ideas into a recorder while driving, then transcribes them later; while the recorder is on, she solves problems she can't solve sitting still. Is the recorder part of her cognition, and does your principle say I'm wrong about any of the cases above?
