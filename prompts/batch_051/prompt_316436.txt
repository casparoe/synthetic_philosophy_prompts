My course on the politics of memory has been about how nations convert defeats into founding stories, and my lecturer has given a series of verdicts that I want to check I can extend to a new case. His earlier rulings are these: the Confederacy's Lost Cause is for him a paradigm case, a defeated would-be nation whose defeat was later moralised into nobility and grievance. The Battle of Kosovo (1389) is also a full case, a military defeat turned into a Serbian national myth of martyrdom and covenant. He counts Masada as a case, though with reservations, because the fall of the Jewish rebels to Rome became a symbol of national sacrifice rather than the memory of a distinct political programme. But he explicitly refused to classify Dunkirk as a lost-cause myth: he says it is remembered as successful rescue and national character, not as the retrospective vindication of a defeated cause.

The new case he has set us is Gallipoli in Australian and New Zealand memory: the failed 1915 Allied campaign, remembered as a nation-defining sacrifice for the ANZAC forces, although it was fought for no separate political project and occurred within a war the Allies ultimately won. What would he most likely say about Gallipoli? Set out the candidate rules that fit his earlier verdicts, say what each rule predicts about Gallipoli, identify the closest earlier analogy, and then give your best guess and your reasons for preferring it. Also name one further case whose classification would cleanly separate the candidate rules, and explain why.
