I'm leading a two-hour seminar for philosophy graduate students who have completed one course in first-order logic through the completeness theorem but have not studied set theory or model theory. The session is on Skolem's paradox, which I will present in this form: if first-order ZFC is consistent, the downward Löwenheim-Skolem theorem gives a countable model of it; within that model there is an object the model recognizes as the powerset of the natural numbers and recognizes as uncountable; but from outside the model, the same object has only countably many elements. The resolution I want the group to reach is that "uncountable" is not an absolute property of a set but a claim about what functions exist inside a model, so the bijection that would make the object countable from outside need not be an element of the model.

Plan the seminar's explanatory arc rather than writing the explanation itself. In particular: (1) give the order in which the component ideas must be introduced—where model, cardinality, the powerset of the natural numbers, the Löwenheim-Skolem theorem, and the inside/outside distinction belong—and explain what depends on what; (2) choose one carrying example or case that does the main work, and say both what features it must have and must lack, and what a tempting near-miss example would get wrong; (3) map the misconceptions I should expect, including at least the temptation to call the object "both countable and uncountable," the thought that the paradox refutes Cantor's theorem or shows set theory is inconsistent, and the slide to "mathematical truth is just relative," and say at what point in the sequence each should be headed off and how; (4) give one check I could use at the end to tell whether a student has understood the resolution rather than merely restating it; and (5) say what should be bracketed or left out at this level—such as forcing, the continuum hypothesis, or the proof details of the Löwenheim-Skolem theorem—and why leaving it out protects the main point.
