As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I have watched a debate resurface repeatedly under new headlines while its underlying structure never changes: oil in the 1970s, carrying capacity and biodiversity in the 1990s, planetary boundaries and water today. On one side, Malthusians argue that a growing population and rising consumption must eventually collide with a finite resource base. On the other, their critics reply that human institutions, technology, substitution, and the demographic transition have repeatedly moved the threshold, and that the predicted collision keeps receding because the original model was misspecified from the start. The question lurking beneath each flare-up is whether Malthus was wrong, or simply early.

What puzzles me is that neither side seems to treat the other's evidence as a genuine test of its own position. Malthusians treat the fact that the predicted collision has not arrived on schedule not as falsification but as postponement: the underlying tendency remains, they say, only delayed by a sequence of escapes—new land, fossil energy, fertilizer, the demographic transition—each of which shifts the constraint rather than removes it. Anti-Malthusians treat each failed date for catastrophe as decisive, and regard those very delays as evidence that the original mechanism was badly specified. The result is a ritual in which past projections are re-scored but few participants revise their views.

I would like a diagnosis of the impasse rather than a verdict on Malthus. Is this a substantive factual disagreement about the historical record—for example, whether the Green Revolution counts as a refutation of diminishing returns or as an example of pushing a limit outward without escaping it? Is it a disagreement about standards of evidence—about what could ever falsify a long-run tendency claim with no fixed deadline, and about when an auxiliary delay is a legitimate refinement rather than an ad hoc rescue? Is it partly verbal, with the term Malthusian used for a demographic mechanism, a model of wages, and a broader worldview about scarcity all at once? Or is it a clash of bedrock intuitions about human ingenuity and the hard limits of nature that no empirical data can settle?

Which diagnosis best explains why two centuries of evidence have moved so few people, and what would count as progress under that diagnosis—a distinction that partitions the claim, a test or formal result that could discriminate the underlying mechanism from the delays, a change of question that would make the dispute tractable, or an argument that the question as usually posed is underdetermined and should be replaced? Please ground the diagnosis in what the two sides actually say about the evidence, rather than simply adjudicating the underlying disagreement.
