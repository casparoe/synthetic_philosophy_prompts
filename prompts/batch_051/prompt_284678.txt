I'm on my company's people-ops commitee and we recieved a complaint that I have to turn into a short decision memo by Thursday. We have an office in Amsterdam and a small team in Tokyo. A Dutch team lead gave a Japanese analyst her quarterly review in front of five colleagues, and told her the recent client report was sloppy, full of avoidable errors, and something a junior person should have caught. She complained that she was humiliated. She says she does not object to hearing that the work fell short, but in every previous job she has had, criticism arrived in private and through hints, so a person could take the point without losing face. The team lead is not contesting the facts. He says he was being honest, and that making him soften the message would be asking him to lie. There is no company policy against giving feedback in front of others, and his team routinely reviews work out in the open. Half the committee wants to discipline him or make him apologize; the other half wants to adopt a policy encouraging direct, no-sugarcoating feedback, because they think softness protects weak work. I keep noticing he attacked the report, not her character, but she says doing it in front of everyone made it a character attack anyway. Can you draft the memo I would circulate? It should land on a concrete course of action: whether to discipline him, and whether we should set any companywide rule about how feedback gets delivered or leave it to individual teams. It should also take seriously the strongest point of the side you do not choose, especially the idea that bluntness is a way of respecting people and the idea that indirectness is not the same as lying. Just the memo, no further-reading list.
