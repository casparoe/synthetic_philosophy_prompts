Counterfactual: Suppose that in 2040 a safe, inexpensive, and permanent neurotechnological procedure becomes widely available. It allows any adult to select a finite list of their current terminal values — their deepest moral commitments, life-priorities, and standards of what matters — and have those values frozen. A frozen value cannot be weakened, reversed, or displaced by later experience, argument, relationships, or social pressure, though the person continues to update factual beliefs and may acquire further values that are compatible with the frozen core. Adoption is voluntary and cheap enough to be globally affordable. By 2050, roughly forty percent of adults have undergone the procedure; the remaining sixty percent decline. Freezers are older and wealthier on average and overrepresented in aging wealthy democracies, but every society contains a non-trivial minority of them.

My question is what this does to the long-run distribution of human values, and whether it makes the risk of a far future populated by values we would now regard as bad more or less likely. By value drift I mean the process by which an individual's or civilization's priorities change so much that earlier selves or earlier generations would come to disown them; the scenario effectively lets some people, but not others, turn drift off. I am less interested in a one-line prediction than in the argument. Trace the dominant mechanisms: which values get frozen and by whom, how frozen parents transmit values to children who are not frozen, how institutions, firms, and states adapt to a partially frozen public, whether cultural evolution now runs mainly through the unfrozen minority or through conflict between the two groups, and whether the presence of frozen contemporaries makes later drift more or less detectable and correctable. For each link, say whether you regard it as robust across plausible socio-economic specifications or as a guess, and state what would have to be different for your overall verdict to flip.
