So I have this habit for thinking about the Church–Turing thesis—the claim that anything computable by an effective procedure (the informal "you could do it on paper with finite, unambiguous steps" idea) is computable by a Turing machine. I know it can't be proved in the ordinary way, since it hooks an informal notion to a formal one, so my policy is: treat it as settled, because every serious attempt to make "effective procedure" precise in the 1930s—Turing machines, Church's lambda calculus, Gödel's recursive functions, the other formalisms—landed on the exact same class of functions. If someone says there might be a computable-but-not-Turing-computable function out there, I basically shrug; the convergence is enough for me. My friend says I'm double-counting one witness: those formalisms were all built by a small group of people in the same decade who already shared the assumption that computation is finite, discrete, step-by-step, and mechanical, so of course they agree. He says it's like polling my own family and acting like that's a national survey. Is my policy defensible? If its not, can it be fixed into something weaker that still does the work I want? And whatever your verdict, tell me what would actually change your mind.
