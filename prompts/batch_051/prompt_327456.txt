I'm a journalist who covers artificial intelligence and the cognitive sciences for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a piece about what happens to a benchmark's argumentative force when the systems being tested are no longer the systems the benchmark was designed for. The case I keep returning to is the Bongard problem: a pair of panels, six positive examples on the left and six negative examples on the right, with the task being to infer the rule that separates them—left examples contain triangles inside circles, right examples contain circles inside triangles, or some relationship more elusive than that. The standard argument, as I understand it, is that success across many Bongard problems is evidence of a fluid capacity to form a new concept from a handful of cases, precisely because the solver cannot have memorized the classes in advance: with only twelve frames to go on, it must construct the concept rather than retrieve it.

That argument, though, contains a silent assumption about the solver—that the solver's task-relevant experience more or less begins when the twelve frames appear. A contemporary multimodal system arrives at the puzzle having been trained on billions of image–text pairs, along with a large share of the world's diagrams, captions, puzzle books, and visual-analogy examples. For such a system, the twelve frames are less the raw material from which a concept is built than a cue into an enormous prior; the system may be selecting among concepts it has already learned to model, not inducing a new one on the spot. So I'm trying to work out whether the Bongard argument survives that change. Does a high score on these puzzles still license the inference that a system is doing concept induction from few examples, or has internet-scale pretraining quietly replaced the thing the benchmark was supposed to measure—turning it into a test of prior fluency, or of similarity to concepts already represented in training data? Which premise of the original argument exactly is under pressure, and is there a repaired version of the argument—for example, using Bongard problems built around deliberately novel, held-out visual relationships—that can restore some of the original claim, or should the original claim be given up?

A secondary issue I'd like the answer to address: if a model not only solves a Bongard problem but also states the separating rule in words, is that verbalization evidence that it possesses the concept, or is it too weak a signal to carry that weight? I'm less interested in a survey of datasets than in the structure of the argument: what it needed to be true, whether it is still true, and what remains salvageable.
