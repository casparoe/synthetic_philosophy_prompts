The Copycat model and its descendants work in a deliberately small domain—letter-string analogy—and the phenomenon they were built to exhibit is the following. Given abc→abd, nearly everyone maps ijk to ijl, following the transparent rule of replacing the final letter by its successor. But given the same source pair with target xyz, where z has no successor, people divide between completions such as xya (wrapping the alphabet) and xzz (moving the successor operation one position left). The standard description is that the concept successor itself slips: under the pressure of a blocked application, it deforms rather than being discarded. I find it hard to tell whether that is a substantive claim or merely a redescription of the data. Design the strongest test or experiment you can to distinguish the claim that the concept itself deforms in context from the claim that people simply abandon one fixed rule and adopt a different fixed rule. Say which result would count for each reading, and then say what a decisive result would still leave open about whether conceptual slippage is a mechanism of the mind or only a convenient summary of choices.
