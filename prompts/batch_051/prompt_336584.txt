Lately I've been putting Michel Foucault's Discipline and Punish next to current arguments about large language models and training data, and the overlap feels less clean than most people in my circle want it to be. In the section on the examination, Foucault lays out how disciplinary power produces a documentary apparatus around individuals—records, files, case notes, comparative tables—that turns each person into a case measurable against a norm. His point is not that this is neutral information gathering, but that it individualizes and normalizes in the same move. I keep coming back to that passage while reading claims that a language model trained on a snapshot of digitized text is a new kind of archive. I suspect Foucault would have resisted that identification, but I don't trust my own reading enough to say exactly why.

What I'd like from you is a careful counterfactual: if Foucault had been shown a contemporary large language model trained on a large slice of digitized text, what would he have said about its relation to the documentary power he describes? Would he treat the training corpus as an archive in his sense, or as something importantly different because it is not assembled as case files for institutional supervision? More interestingly to me: would he think such a model reinforces the archival silences he spent much of his later work trying to notice, or would it produce a new kind of silence? I want the answer to use his actual analytical vocabulary—examination, normalization, the historical a priori, subjugated knowledges—rather than a generic Foucauldian-sounding critique, and I want you to flag the places where his framework would have to stretch to accommodate what a stochastic model trained on text actually does.
