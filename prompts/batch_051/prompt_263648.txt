I got into a drawn-out argument last week about whether a human language could get by with no pronouns, and it went nowhere becuase we never pinned down what a pronoun is. So let me state my working criteria and hand you a borderline case, and you tell me where the case lands. My criteria for a genuine pronoun (or an indexical like I, here, now) are: (i) its referent is fixed by the utterance situation itself — who is speaking, who is addressed, where and when — rather than by a descriptive concept that picks out some individual; (ii) its standing meaning is just a rule that maps any utterance situation onto a referent, which is why the same word shifts systematically from speaker to speaker; and (iii) it forms a small closed class of dedicated function words, instead of being ordinary vocabulary pressed into service. Now imagine a language with no dedicated first- or second-person forms. Speakers refer to themselves and others with kin and status nouns: a woman says something we would translate as 'older sister will go' even when the person she speaks to is a stranger, and a subordinate says 'servant has finished' meaning that he himself has finished. The same nouns keep ordinary descriptive uses with fixed social content — an actual older sister, an actual servant. Is this a language without pronouns, or are these kin and status nouns functioning as pronouns in those self-reference and address uses? Go criterion by criterion, say honestly where my criteria underdetermine the verdict, and say whether the case shows the criteria themselves need to be redrawn. And if you end up saying these nouns effectively are pronouns, does that mean any language that can talk about the current speaker and addressee will end up with indexicals somewhere, even if it has no dedicated pronoun forms — or could a language genuinely lack them altogether?
