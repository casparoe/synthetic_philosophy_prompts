I am defending a paper before my department's decision theory workshop next month. The paper's thesis is that counterfactual mugging and a variant of blackmail I call XOR blackmail are not two separate anomalies but one structural problem for act-based decision theories, and that the only satisfactory response is to move to a policy-based or updateless view in which the agent's decision algorithm, rather than the particular act it outputs, is the object of rational evaluation. The argument proceeds by laying out my version of counterfactual mugging: a superior predictor flips a fair coin; if the coin lands tails it pays the agent $10,000 if and only if it predicted the agent would have paid $100 had the coin landed heads, while if it lands heads it simply asks for the $100. I compare it with my version of XOR blackmail: a predictor sends a blackmail letter in which the allegation is true if and only if the agent will not pay the demanded sum, so that paying is strong evidence the letter is harmless without causally affecting the letter's contents. I argue that causal decision theory gives the wrong verdict in both cases, and that evidential decision theory, though it reaches the right verdict in these two, does so only by relying on a conditional probability whose origin it cannot explain without borrowing the policy-level picture it officially rejects.

The audience is the department's formal group: two Bayesian decision theorists who treat what I call policy-based reasoning as merely an honorific for precommitment strategies already available inside standard expected utility theory, a game theorist who will want to recast both cases as reputation-building in a larger repeated game, and a philosopher of probability who is sympathetic to evidential decision theory and will press me to just accept it. No one in the room has read the LessWrong or MIRI literature, and if I use that vocabulary they will dismiss the whole position as cult jargon. Give me the Q&A prep sheet: the objections I will actually get, in roughly the order of likelihood and damage, phrased as these people would phrase them, with what I should concede, what I should hold, where each reply is weakest, and the question I most do not want fielded.
