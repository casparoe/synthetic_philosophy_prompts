Hey—I'm a PhD student in philosophy of biology, but I've drifted into writing about evolutionary arguments for AI takeover. The core move I keep coming back to: if you take the replicator/gene's-eye view seriously, then once you get artificial systems that can be copied quickly, cheaply, and with high enough fidelity, selection should start favoring them over humans, and a lot of our "we can just turn them off" confidence starts to smell like human exceptionalism. I've got about three years left, and my background is philosophy plus enough population genetics to follow Price and replicator dynamics, but I don't do ML.

I dont want a reading list. What I want is a defended agenda: if a small funder could support three or four sub-questions in this area over the next few years, which should they be and why? I especially care about which questions are upstream of the others—where work on B, C, and D is built on sand until A gets sorted out—and which are actually tractable for someone with my kind of background rather than for ML people or empirical biologists. For each pick, I want the reasoning: why it deserves the years, what you'd count as progress, and what new evidence would knock it down the list.

One thing I'm already suspicious of is that a lot of this stuff smuggles in "fitness" and "reproduction" from population genetics without checking whether they really apply to digital artifacts. A model doesn't have a generation time in the usual sense; copying fidelity is weird; the "environment" an AI faces is partly designed by us and partly an economy that we keep reshaping. Is that conceptual cleanup the right first move, or is it the kind of captivating detour that would eat my whole dissertation while the empirical situation races ahead? Be blunt.
