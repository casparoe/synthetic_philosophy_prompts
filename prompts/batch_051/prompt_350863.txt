I'm a journalist who covers ideas and have been reading Hobbes for a piece, and a political theorist friend backed me into a corner. His argument runs like this:

(1) Hobbes says a covenant in the state of nature is void whenever one side can reasonably suspect the other side will not perform later, because without a common power to enforce it, words alone are just words.
(2) The covenant that sets up a sovereign is made in the state of nature, since by definition there is no sovereign yet to enforce it.
(3) So, by Hobbes's own principle, the covenant that sets up a sovereign is void.
(4) But Hobbes also says the only way out of the state of nature is for people to covenant with one another to set up a sovereign.
(5) Therefore the escape from the state of nature is impossible: it requires an agreement that, on Hobbes's own standard, is no agreement at all.
(6) The only consistent Hobbesian move, my friend says, is to admit sovereignty gets started by force or fear, not agreement—which turns the social contract into a story we tell after the fact.

I want to keep (2) and (4) because they feel like the whole engine of Leviathan. But I also don't want to give up on the thought that agreement can actually do some work. Which numbered step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the problem is that I'm treating the sovereign-making contract as the wrong kind of covenant, say that plainly rather than humoring the argument.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion.
