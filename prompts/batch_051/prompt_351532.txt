I spent my working life as a theoretical physicist and have only recently begun reading formal epistemology. Next month I am to lead a ninety-minute session of my retirement reading group on Fitch's paradox of knowability — the result that, from the principle that every truth is knowable together with little more than the distribution of knowledge over conjunction and the factivity of knowledge, one can derive that every truth is known. The group is a dozen retired scientists and one former classicist. They are comfortable with propositional logic and truth-tables, but none has worked with modal operators or possible-worlds semantics. Do not explain the paradox to me; plan how I should explain it to them. I want the order of ideas and what depends on what: where the knowability principle has to be fixed, where the Moorean proposition (p and it is not known that p) must do its work, and the exact point at which the modal inference has to be made explicit rather than left implicit in the symbols. Choose one worked example or sentence to carry the whole explanation, and say what it must have, and what it must not have, if the collapse is to feel forced rather than like a trick of notation.

Then give me the misconception map: I already suspect they will read 'knowable' as 'will eventually be known' and will confuse a proposition's being unknown with its being unknowable; identify the other predictable errors and where in the sequence each should be headed off. End with a single question I can put to the room to test whether they have grasped the paradox rather than merely followed the derivation. Finally, tell me where your own plan is most likely to fail: which kind of confusion in the room would make you reorder the sequence, replace the carrying example, or decide the audience needed a different piece of preparation before any of this.
