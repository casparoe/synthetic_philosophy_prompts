I'm a PhD student in formal epistemology/decision theory, and I've spent the last year defending a Buchak-style risk-weighted expected utility (RWEU) account of rational preference. The core claim is that preferences are a function of three things, not two: probabilities, utilities, and a distinct risk attitude captured by a risk function r that weights probabilities nonlinearly, with expected utility theory as the special case r(p)=p. On my view the Allais preferences aren't a reasoning error to be corrected; they're evidence that the independence axiom was a bad norm all along, and a rational agent can be sensitive to how outcomes are distributed across states in a way that no utility function alone can capture. I've heard the obvious objections—r is ad hoc, it's just rank-dependent utility or cumulative prospect theory with a new coat of paint, the Allais pattern is a framing effect or driven by regret—and I have the standard replies, but I havent forced myself to ask what would actually move me off the view.

So treat the position as the target. What would have to be true for RWEU to fail as a normative theory of rational preference under risk? I want the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to separate the ones a committed defender would concede as real costs from the ones they'd dismiss. Things I'm especially worried about: whether the risk function is actually identifiable from choice behavior, or whether adding it makes the theory curve-fit almost anything; whether the theory can be distinguished from rank-dependent utility in a way that matters normatively; whether there are formal results—dynamic inconsistency, violations of betweenness or separability, Rabin-style calibration concerns—that leave a mark even after the standard replies; and whether the view has been qualified (e.g., by making r free or by going sophisticated about dynamic choice) to the point where it no longer risks anything. I'm most likely to be persuaded by the charge that the theory is empty unless r is constrained independently, so I'd like a serious treatment of that rather than just a list.
