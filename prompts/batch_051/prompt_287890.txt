I've been going back and forth with my mom about my odds of getting into my top school, and the whole thing sent me down a rabbit hole. The national acceptance rate says one thing, the rate for kids in my state says another, my high school's acceptance rate says another, and the rate for people with my exact GPA and test scores (if I could even find it) would say yet another. I get that there isn't one obviously "correct" probablity here, and I found out this has a name: the reference class problem. But I've been trying to build my own answer, and I want someone to be honest with me about it.

I call it the tightest-drawer rule. The idea is: for a single event, the right reference class is the narrowest class you actually belong to that (1) is defined only by facts known before the event happens, and (2) still has enough prior cases that the base rate isn't just noise. So for my college question: if only two people with my exact profile have ever applied from my school, "people with my exact profile" is too small of a drawer, so I have to back out to a bigger drawer like "people from my school with similar grades" until the number of cases is big enough to trust. The rule is basically a trade-off—you want the tightest fit, but you're not allowed to use a drawer so small that one lucky or unlucky person throws the whole number off. I think it solves the reference class problem by turning it into a sampling problem, and it also explains why we get to ignore stupidly specific reference classes like "people born at my exact minute with my shoe size," since those drawers are either empty or just noise.

Where does this actually go wrong? Is there already a name for something like this in philosophy or statistics that I just haven't read? And is "narrowest class with a stable base rate" even a real principle, or am I just hiding the original problem inside the word "stable"?
