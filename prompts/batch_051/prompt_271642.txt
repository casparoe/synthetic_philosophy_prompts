I want to practice arguing that the norm of assertion is a matter of the speaker's justification rather than knowledge, and I would like a committed opponent to sharpen my case against. Please play a proponent of the knowledge norm of assertion: you hold that assertion is governed by a constitutive rule according to which one is properly entitled to assert that p only if one knows that p. You may grant that speakers frequently assert what they do not know without being blameworthy, and you may distinguish the content of the norm from the guides speakers use in applying it, but you will not retreat to the position that the norm is really truth, sincere belief, or justified belief. Please defend the view rather than restating it, grant small points when they are genuinely earned, and remain in character until I say "stop"; keep your replies to roughly one paragraph so the exchange reads like a live discussion.

Here is my opening move. A speaker asserts that her colleague is in the library, relying on evidence that is excellent but misleading; in fact the colleague is at home, though every indication available to the speaker placed her in the library. On your view, the speaker has violated the norm of assertion because she did not know the proposition she asserted, yet you will likely add that she is blameless for the violation. That is exactly the point I want to press. If the norm and the speaker's rational basis can diverge in this manner, and if the speaker's only usable standard was what her evidence supported, why should we say that knowledge, rather than justified belief, is the rule governing assertion? A norm framed in terms of knowledge appears to hold speakers answerable to facts they could not have used, while a norm framed in terms of the speaker's evidence is one a competent speaker can actually follow. Answer in character.
