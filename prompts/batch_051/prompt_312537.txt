I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a piece about how we should think about morally loaded historical figures — the usual flashpoints: Jefferson, Churchill, Columbus. In nearly every interview, an archivist or biographer eventually reaches for some version of 'he was a man of his time' or 'you can't judge historical figures by today's standards.' The phrase is doing a lot of diffrent jobs in those conversations: sometimes it's an empirical claim about what was normal, sometimes a claim about whether the person is blameworthy at all, sometimes a claim about what we get to admire or name buildings after. I want to pull these apart before I write.

What are the distinct precise claims that slogan could be making? For each, say whether it's trivially true, defensible but substantive, or just false, and which reading would actually support the move it's being used for — keeping a statue up, or saying Churchill's racism shouldn't erase his legacy. I've already noticed that Columbus had contemporaries criticizing his cruelty in his own lifetime, and that plenty of people in Jefferson's world thought slavery was wrong, so I'm especially interested in readings that treat 'his time' as morally uniform versus readings that allow for real contemporary dissent. I'd also like you to flag where the claim slides from explaining behavior to excusing it, and where it smuggles in a claim about commemoration that doesn't follow from anything about moral judgment. A menu of readings with verdicts is what I'm after, not one tidy answer.
