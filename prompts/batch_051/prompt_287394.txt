Last night I had a long, increasingly technical argument with a friend who works on the governance of transformative AI. I was defending the seemingly modest claim that if, by our current values, one far-future path looks clearly better than another, then that is at least a reason to steer toward it. He spent the evening insisting that this is the one move that value drift will not permit: by the time the future we set in motion actually arrives, the beings who live in it will evaluate it by standards shaped by technological, institutional, and selection pressures so different from ours that our present judgments, however confident, simply fail to reach the outcome that matters. When I pressed him to make the argument explicit, he produced the following numbered chain and told me to reject a premise if I could. Each line, read on its own, strikes me as intuitive, yet the conclusion seems to me to prove too much; I suspect a suppressed premise is hiding somewhere around step four, but I cannot state it without sounding as if I am claiming that my present values are uniquely authoritative. Here is the argument as he gave it:

(1) An action aimed at shaping the far future can be justified now only if we can reasonably expect it to make the far future better, rather than worse, according to our present values.
(2) The far future will not simply be the world we set in motion; whatever institutions, norms, or structures we establish now will be evaluated, maintained, revised, and steered by future agents whose values have drifted in ways we cannot anticipate.
(3) Because we cannot anticipate the content of those future values, we cannot now predict which features of the world we set in motion will be preserved and which will be dismantled once those later standards are in force.
(4) If we cannot predict which features of the world we set in motion will be preserved and which dismantled by later standards, then we cannot reasonably expect that our action will make the far future better even by our own present values, since the features we now regard as making it better may be among the first to be undone.
(5) Therefore, no action aimed at shaping the far future is justified.

I would like a precise analysis of this argument. Is it valid as written? If not, which step fails, and what suppressed principle would have to be added between which steps to make it go through? In particular, I want to know what the weakest reading of reasonably expect is on which step four is credible, and whether on that reading the conclusion retains its force, or whether it shrinks into something far less damaging that a defender of long-term action can simply accept.
