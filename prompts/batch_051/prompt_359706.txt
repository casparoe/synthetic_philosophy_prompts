In my German Idealism course we spent a day on the part of the Phenomenology of Spirit where Hegel tries to show that a self-consciousness only becomes certain of itself through recognition by another. My professor paraphrased the opening this way: each self-consciousness demands to be recognized by the other but does not at first want to extend recognition itself. My friend recontructed an argument from that material and now I think it proves too much, but I can't find the bad step.

(1) My freedom can only be confirmed by the recognition of someone who is themselves free; if the recognizer is subordinate to me, their recognition doesn't confer anything.
(2) By the same logic, if I recognize another person as free, my recognition can confirm their freedom only if I am also free.
(3) So to get the recognition that actually does something for me, I need to seek recognition from someone I regard as free.
(4) But seeking recognition from them already assumes they are free; so I have, in effect, recognized them first, without any guarantee that they will recognize me back.
(5) If they do the same, we are stuck in a standoff where each has already recognized the other but neither has received the recognition they demanded.
(6) The only way to break the standoff is for one party to force the other into a subordinate position. But by (1), a subordinate's recognition is worthless.
(7) Therefore recognition either does not happen, or it happens asymmetrically and is worthless to the master. Mutual recognition between equals seems impossible.

That conclusion looks way too strong and I doubt Hegel would accept it, especially since I gather the rest of the chapter is supposed to lead somewhere else. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Or is the argument actually valid and I should accept that the lord-and-bondsman section has no exit?
