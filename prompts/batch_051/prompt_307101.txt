I spent most of my career as an ecologist building population models, and in retirement I've gotten into the philosophy of action — plans, intentions, this whole is-planning-overrated debate. A parallel has been nagging me and I can't tell if it's deep or just pattern-matching. In my old field we borrowed a distinction from engineering: open-loop versus closed-loop control. Open-loop is when you compute the whole trajectory in advance and then execute it. Closed-loop is when you keep measuring and correcting as you go: compare where you are to where you meant to be, adjust, repeat. Everyone treated open-loop as a toy, because models are always wrong and the world is noisy; close the loop or crash.

Now I read philosophers defending planning, and I can't shake the thought that a plan is just open-loop control with nicer prose: a precomputed path through future state-space. It works only while the actual state tracks the predicted one, and the moment it drifts the plan is dead weight. Improvisation looks exactly like closed-loop control — sensitivity to feedback, updating on error. The engineering result is that closed-loop beats open-loop under uncertainty, often by a lot. So my question: is this a genuine structural identity, or am I just seeing the same shape in two things I happen to know? If the mapping is real, should we conclude that planning really is overrated almost everywhere and only pays off in near-deterministic corners of life? Or does a plan do something a controller can't — create commitment, coordinate other people, let you rehearse a response in advance — so the analogy breaks just where the philsophical interest is?
