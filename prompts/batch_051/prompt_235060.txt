Here's an argument I constructed for my philosophy club's discussion of self-knowledge, and I now find it suspicious because its conclusion is plainly false. I cannot find the faulty step.

Imagine you are in a room while the temperature rises from 50°F to 90°F in steps so small that you cannot distinguish any one second's feeling of warmth from the previous one. You are attending to your sensations the entire time. At t1 you feel cold; at tn you are hot and do not feel cold.

(1) Whenever you feel cold, you are in a position to know that you feel cold. (This is the claim some call luminosity: the state reveals itself to you.)
(2) Because you are attending throughout, whenever you are in a position to know that you feel cold, you know that you feel cold.
(3) If you know at ti that you feel cold, then you feel cold at t(i+1). Justification: if you felt cold at ti but not at t(i+1), then at ti your feeling was so close to a case of not feeling cold that your belief could easily have been wrong, and a belief that could easily have been wrong is not knowledge. Knowledge requires a margin for error.

Now, by (1) and (2), you know at t1 that you feel cold; by (3), you feel cold at t2; repeating the steps gives that you feel cold at every second, including tn. But the setup says you do not feel cold at tn. So at least one of (1), (2), or (3) must be rejected, or the induction must fail in some way I do not see.

I would most like to keep (1), since it seems undeniable that a person is in a position to know their own current sensations, and I also do not see how to reject (3) without letting beliefs that could easily have been false count as knowledge. Yet together they seem to force either that one can never gradually stop feeling cold, or that there is no sharp point at which one stops, which itself seems strange. Where does the argument go wrong? If a step is equivocating or invalid, identify it; if the argument is valid, tell me which premise is least costly to give up and what exactly I would be committed to in giving it up. I would also like to know whether rejecting (1) forces me to reject the KK principle (if you know p, then you know that you know p), or whether that principle is a separate matter.
