I keep noticing that arguments about nature versus nurture fail at the same step: everyone shares an estimate of heritability, and then everyone slides from that number to a conclusion it doesn't support. Before I read another twin or GWAS result, I want the specification I should be holding such results to. Write the desiderata for an adequate account of what a heritability estimate means: the cases it must get right, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the inferences it must explicitly license or forbid.

At minimum, make it handle: number of fingers in humans, where genetic causation is total but heritability is near zero; height, where within-population heritability is high and average height has also shifted with nutrition and other environmental changes; phenylketonuria (PKU), a single-gene metabolic condition whose neurological effects are largely preventable by diet; two seed stocks planted across enriched and depleted plots, with the rank order of yields reversing by soil; and the equal-environments assumption behind twin estimates. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it, and mark which requirements are non-negotiable versus which are preferences of a particular methodological camp.

Then tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude the standard loose readings—such as that heritability measures how genetic a trait is, how immutable it is, or how much of a group difference is genetic. I also want to know whether the demand for a clean additive partition of variance and the demand to handle gene–environment correlation are in tension, and if so, how an honest account should present the tension.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating of the question.
