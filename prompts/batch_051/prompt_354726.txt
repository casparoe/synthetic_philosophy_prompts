Here is a working definition of a full natural language, stated so as not to privilege speech over sign. A communication system is a full natural language if:

1. It has a conventionalized lexicon: a stable, shared inventory of form–meaning pairings whose meanings cannot be recovered on the spot from pantomime alone.
2. It has combinatorial grammar: discrete units combine according to shared, rule-governed patterns, and users recognize systematic violations as errors.
3. It is productive and allows displacement: users can produce and interpret novel expressions, and can talk about past and future events, absent people, hypotheticals, questions, and negations.
4. It is natively acquired: children can and do acquire it as a first language without explicit teaching.
5. It is maintained by a community across generations, not confined to a single individual or household.

Now consider a large village with an unusually high rate of congenital deafness. The oldest deaf residents grew up with no shared sign language. Each one developed an idiosyncratic gestural system: the gestures are reasonably stable for a given person, and close relatives can interpret them, but there is no shared lexicon across households, and utterances are produced roughly in the order of the events described, with heavy use of pointing and pantomime. Younger deaf residents — and hearing children raised in households where these gestures are used — have regularized the system into something common. In this second-generation system, there is a shared vocabulary of several hundred signs; a fixed sign order marks who did what to whom; and users tell stories about past events, ask questions, and discuss absent people. But the second-generation system has no subordinate clauses, no grammatical tense or aspect, and no systematic way to express counterfactuals such as, "If it had rained, the crop would not have failed." In the third generation, children have begun to add conventional devices for marking hypotheticals and locating events in time.

Is the second-generation system a full natural language by these five criteria? Is the third-generation system? Apply the criteria to each separately, and where a criterion is indecisive, say precisely why. Then answer the broader question: does this case show that any of the five criteria is doing too much or too little work — in particular, that child-driven regularization, rather than the presence of subordination or tense, is what separates a full language from a precursor system? Please answer in American English.
