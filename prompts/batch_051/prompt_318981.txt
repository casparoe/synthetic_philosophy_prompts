In much of ordinary social and working life, we grade failures of punctuality and reliability in a way that does not track their material costs. A person who is reliably fifteen minutes late but always shows up is usually tolerated and scheduled around; a person who cancels an hour ahead is mildly resented; a person who simply does not appear is resented much more sharply; and a habitually flaky person is treated as having a character flaw rather than as having committed many small promissory wrongs. Yet the aggregate time and disruption caused by these different people can be identical, and the canceller may cost someone the same wasted hour as the no-show. Reconstruct the implicit theory of what makes breaking a small promise wrong that this pattern of blame, resentment, and forgiveness presupposes. In particular, is the wrong located in the harm done, in the disrespect communicated, in the reliance disappointed, in the information the failure gives about future reliability, or in the agent's character or dispositions? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with and is in fact compatible with several rival theories. Then say what would have to change in our reactive attitudes and in how we schedule, blame, and forgive if we replaced the implicit view with (a) a straightforward harm-based account, on which identical wasted time is an identical wrong, and (b) a Kantian account, on which the wrong lies wholly in the intention or maxim and not in the outcome or the pattern. End your reply with a one-sentence summary of the reconstructed view as the final line.
