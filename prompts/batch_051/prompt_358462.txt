Consequentialists sometimes distinguish between being merely uncertain about the long-run consequences of an action and being clueless about them: in the latter case, small changes in initial conditions or modeling choices are said to flip the sign of the net effect, so no feasible inference reliably beats a flat prior over outcomes. How could one test whether a given class of ordinary actions is genuinely clueless in this sense, rather than uncertain in a way that more data or better models could reduce? Propose a concrete study—using historical microdata, a multi-agent simulation, or a forecasting tournament—that would separate the two hypotheses. Specify which result pattern would count for cluelessness and which for reducible uncertainty, and then state what a positive result on either side would and would not settle about consequentialism as a usable decision procedure.
