I'm a journalist covering science and ideas, working on a feature about the governance of autonomous weapons, and I need a rigorously engineered case to anchor a section on meaningful human control. Construct a near-future thought experiment in which an autonomous weapon system meets three common operational safeguards — a human authorizes each specific engagement before release, a human monitors the system throughout the engagement, and a human can abort up to the moment of release — yet the human fails to exercise meaningful human control, where meaningful control is understood substantively as requiring a positive role: the human must be able to shape, select among, or at least modify the options the system pursues, rather than merely accept or reject a single machine-generated option. Constraints: the technology should be credible within the next two decades rather than science-fictional; the operator is competent, well-intentioned, unhurried, and given full and accurate information about the options the system presents; and the absence of meaningful control must stem from the structure of the human–machine relationship, not from negligence, deception, coercion, or time pressure. After presenting the scenario, run the checks: show how each of the three safeguards is satisfied, explain why the substantive account nevertheless denies meaningful human control, identify the single design feature doing the main work, and say what the verdict would be if that feature were removed. Then give the strongest reply available to a defender of the safeguards-only checklist.
