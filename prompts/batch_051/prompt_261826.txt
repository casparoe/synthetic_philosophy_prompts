I'm the staff analyst for a state health professions board that disciplines nurses, pharmacists, and respiratory therapists. We meet monthly and each case gets maybe 40 minutes of discussion. Board members are mostly practicing clinicians, not lawyers or philosophers, and they have to write an order that can survive an appeal and a newspaper headline. The thing we keep fighting about is this: two licensees can make the identical error—same lapse of attention, same skipped check, same policy violation—but in one case the patient dies and in the other a colleague catches it or the patient happens to be fine. Right now we punish the bad-outcome case much harder, and some board members think that's indefensible, we're basically punishing people for luck. Others say no, the outcome is part of what the person did, its why we're all sitting in the room, and the public would be outraged if we treated a fatal error the same as a near miss.

I want you to act as the philosopher-consultant here, someone who knows the moral luck stuff deeply and can translate it for non-philosophers. Write us a reusable decision procedure, grounded in that work (resultant luck, circumstantial luck, the control condition, all that), that our board members could actually run in 40 minutes per case. It should tell us: what questions to ask, in what order; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; when the outcome of the error should change the sanction and when it should'nt; where the procedure stops with "this can't be settled by rules" and a human has to make the call; and what the procedure is doing, not just blaming—because a board is also protecting the public. Please flag the places where judgment is being dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure on three cases:
(1) A nurse forgot to recheck a lab value before hanging a medication, and the patient had a fatal bleed. Normal shift, normal staffing, no prior discipline.
(2) A pharmacist made the same kind of lapse—got interrupted, dispensed the wrong dose—but the patient noticed the pills looked different and wasn't hurt.
(3) A nurse made the same lapse as the first nurse, and the patient died, but she'd been on her fourteenth straight hour after two call-offs, on a unit she'd never worked before.

I want the worked applications to include at least one place where the procedure gives an answer the board won't like. And don't give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations—just use the ideas.

One last thing, and it matters to me: after you give me the procedure, tell me where your reasoning is most likely to fail, and what argument would make you change the procedure—for example, an argument that a licensing board is really in the business of blame and retribution, or that public protection genuinely requires weighting outcomes. Please think it through carefully before you commit, and maybe sketch the main considerations first.
