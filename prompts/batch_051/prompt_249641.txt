You are a professional philosopher specializing in social ontology and the philosophy of the social sciences. I am preparing a graduate seminar on whether generational categories — Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z, and the rest — pick out real kinds or are better understood as convenient, reified labels for statistical aggregates. I want you to produce the specification I will circulate in class: a list of desiderata that any adequate theory of social kinds must meet if it is to adjudicate, for a proposed cohort category, whether that category counts as a real kind rather than a merely nominal grouping.

Before you write, spend some time brainstorming: what candidate criteria might one impose, what cases would the theory have to classify correctly, what work the term "generation" is made to do in social explanation, and where the main theoretical fault lines lie. Then present the polished list.

The purpose is fixed: the theory should be adequate for deciding when generational talk earns its place in serious social explanation — when "Gen Z" or "Boomers" tracks something real enough to carry explanatory weight — and when it is merely a stereotype with demographic window dressing. For each requirement, give the consideration or case that motivates it. Then mark each as either non-negotiable — a theory that failed it would be a non-starter — or as a preference of a particular camp, such as those who think real kinds require shared causal mechanisms versus those who think stable property clustering suffices.

Your list must engage at least the following pressure points. (i) The age–period–cohort problem: an adequate account should say whether cohort effects must be distinguishable from age effects and period effects before anything can be attributed to a generation. (ii) Mechanism versus clustering: whether real-kind status requires a common generative mechanism (shared formative events, institutions, material conditions), shared properties, or only robust statistical clustering, and what each option would imply for generational kinds. (iii) Fuzzy and retrospective boundaries: how the account handles the fact that generational boundaries are contested, drawn differently in different countries, and sometimes fixed only in hindsight, given that many uncontroversial kinds also have vague boundaries. (iv) Within-cohort heterogeneity: whether the account must respect the familiar pattern that variation within a cohort usually exceeds variation between cohorts, and whether that pattern by itself rules out kindhood. (v) Multiple kindhoods: whether a generation could be real as a demographic cohort — a group unified by shared exposure to events — while failing to be real as a cultural kind around which people organize their identities, and whether "real kind" should therefore split into more than one notion. (vi) Joint satisfiability: whether the desiderata, taken together, are jointly satisfiable, or whether some subset pulls against another — for example, demanding both a common mechanism and stable property clustering may be too strong, or demanding sharp boundaries may conflict with the actual phenomena. If some subset is jointly unsatisfiable, say which and why; that is the part I most want to see.

Finally, keep any scoring of existing approaches — essentialist, homeostatic-property-cluster, nominalist, purely demographic — brief, because the weight belongs on the criteria and their motivation, not on the scorecard.
