I find myself caught between two ideas. On one side, I keep returning to Kant’s essay On a Supposed Right to Lie from Philanthropy (1797). As I understand it, Kant argues there that truthfulness is an unconditional duty, and that even a lie told to benefit another person remains forbidden, because lying, even in a particular case, strikes at trust in general, and the liar may be held accountable for whatever harm results. On the other side, I have friends from the Netherlands and Japan. The Dutch friend says it is respectful to tell someone exactly what you think of their bad idea; the Japanese friend says that bluntness is cruel and indirectness is a mark of care. Each seems to treat their norm not as a strategic choice but as basic courtesy.

I want your actual credence that telling a white lie is wrong in the following case: someone asks a close friend for an honest opinion on creative work that is sincerely terrible, and the close friend says it is good in order to avoid hurting them, while knowing the person will later be embarrassed in front of others. Please decompose the probability: for example, how much rides on whether a Kantian no-exceptions view of lying is correct, whether this particular white lie falls under the same kind as the murderer-at-door case, whether culturally embedded forms of indirectness change the act’s meaning, and whether the action’s predictable benefits outweigh its harms. For each term give a rough probability and one line of justification, then combine them. Finally, tell me which term you are least confident in, what evidence would shift it, and whether the disagreement between my Dutch and Japanese friends is most likely about the descriptive facts, the moral principle, or the meaning of the act.
