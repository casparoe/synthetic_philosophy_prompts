I've been going round in circles on the foundations of set theory and I can't settle a stand-off between a principle and my own conviction. The naive comprehension principle seems nearly self-evident to me: for any well-defined condition, there's a set of exactly the things satisfying it. That's what a set is, as far as I can see; once you start putting restrictions on it, it feels like gerrymandering to avoid an unwelcome result. But I also can't shake the conviction that 'x is not a member of itself' is as well-defined a condition as any, so the Russell set R is a perfectly definite collection, and the question whether R is a member of itself has to have a definite answer, yes or no. The standard arguement then shows each answer flips into the other, given the principle. So I'm stuck between a principle I find self-evident and an instance of it I find just as self-evident.

I don't want 'just accept ZFC and move on' as the verdict. Please give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion, covering: what it would cost to give up the principle; what it would cost to give up the conviction that R is a definite collection with a determinate membership; whether a classes-versus-sets distinction lets both survive as stated or only by quietly redefining one of them; whether the right move is to write the conviction off as an artefact of natural-language talk about 'conditions'; and which I should actually give up.
