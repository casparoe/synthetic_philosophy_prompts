I've been going around in circles on epiphenomenalism, and specifically on the paradox of phenomenal judgment. The argument for it feels basically airtight: the physical world is causally closed, so either mental events do no causal work, or every intentional action is overdetermined by both neural and mental causes, which seems insane. So consciousness has to be an inert byproduct. But then I notice I'm writing this sentence precisely because my headache is distracting me. My judgment that my head hurts is (or is realized in) a physical event, and it's about my headache. If the headache is causally inert, how did my brain produce a judgment that matches it? The standard reply is that a single neural cause produces both the headache and the judgment, so the match isn't a coincidence. But that still makes my judgment causally blind to the very thing it's about—like a caption bot that writes accurate captions only because the image and the caption share a cause, not because it ever saw the image. I can't tell whether I should give up the causal closure argument (and if so, which premise exactly should break), or debunk the intuition that my pain caused my report, or accept the shared-cause story as fully satisfactory, or whether there's a fourth option where the intuition and the theory are compatible after all. Walk me through what each move commits me to, and which one you'd actually make.
