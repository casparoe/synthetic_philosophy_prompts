I work in computational cognitive science, with a working vocabulary built around generative models, precision-weighted prediction error, active inference, and learned reward functions. I keep returning to Adam Smith's account in The Theory of Moral Sentiments, where he says that when a person judges his own conduct, he divides himself into two persons: an examiner and judge—a spectator whose sentiments he tries to enter into—and the agent whose conduct is being judged. Smith also says this internal spectator, which he sometimes calls the man within the breast, is formed by internalizing the perspective of an actual, impartial spectator; we imagine how our conduct would look to someone who has no partial feeling either for or against us. My request: restate Smith's mechanism of conscience as faithfully as possible in the vocabulary of generative models and reinforcement learning—an internal evaluator, a learned model of how an impartial observer would judge, an inference process that weighs the agent's self-regarding account against the spectator's account, and so on. Then give me the residue report: which parts of Smith's view translate cleanly, which parts you had to fudge, and which parts have no counterpart in this vocabulary. In particular, I want to know whether Smith's distinction between being praised and being praiseworthy—between merely receiving approval and deserving it—can be expressed in terms of learned reward versus something like value in a distributionally shifted or counterfactual environment, or whether it marks a limit of the computational vocabulary. Don't offer a bibliography; I want the translation assessed on its own.
