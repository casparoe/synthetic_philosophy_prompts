In my former career as a physical scientist, I spent decades building models in which a few conserved quantities did most of the explanatory work, and I came to moral philosophy only recently. That may be why I find it so odd that the two main candidate designs for an artificial moral reasoner are so rarely forced to translate into one another. I am not asking for a verdict. I am asking for the document that a good-faith adversarial collaboration would produce between two camps. The aggregationist camp holds that a morally competent AI should reason chiefly through an impartial, welfare-based value function: identify the relevant outcomes, discount appropriately, and maximize expected value, with prohibitions treated as derived heuristics or as especially steep penalties. The constraint camp holds that a morally competent AI must represent certain act-types as side constraints—prohibited or required independently of the aggregate balance of value—so that the system refuses to do certain things even when the expected sum says otherwise. Please draft the joint statement both camps could sign.

It should open with a numbered agreed-claims section: claims about what each architecture commits its proponents to, about where simple formal devices (a lexicographic ordering, an arbitrarily large penalty) make the two converge or fail to converge on benchmark behavior, and about what would and would not settle the difference. Then provide a disagreements table; for each residual disagreement, give a neutral statement of the issue, the aggregationists' wording of their own position, the constraint camp's wording of their own position, and a note on whether either side considers that particular dispute partly verbal. Then give the cruxes—the sub-questions such that an answer would move at least one side. For each crux, specify a case or argument both camps would accept as probative and record each camp's prediction in advance. I need no references or bibliography.

Make the statement engage explicitly with at least these pressure points: (1) whether a side constraint can be encoded as a lexicographic priority or a near-infinite disutility without the position collapsing into aggregationism; (2) whether the separateness-of-persons objection undermines interpersonal aggregation as such or only certain ways of distributing burdens and benefits among persons; (3) how each architecture handles moral uncertainty, and whether the aggregationist's expected-choice-worthiness framing simply reintroduces a maximizing meta-criterion the constraint camp is entitled to reject; (4) whether the fact that a single action can be described as, say, a false utterance or as a lie makes act-type constraints hopelessly sensitive to initial description. Do not resolve the debate; the deliverable is the residual, exactly stated disagreement itself.
