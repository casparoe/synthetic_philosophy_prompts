I'm a software engineer who mostly does backend work but recently got put on a project that involves a lot of model selection, and I've gone down a bit of a rabbit hole reading about the no-free-lunch theorems on the side. My shaky understanding is that, averaged over all possible target functions, every learning algorithm performs exactly as well as every other one, so any edge an algorithm actually gets has to come from the fact that the real world is not a random draw from the space of all possible functions. That sounds a lot like the problem of induction to me: induction only works if we already assume nature is regular or smooth or compressible or something, at which point the original problem just moved one level down. But we do still pick models and they do work, so the right conclusion clearly is not that learning is impossible.

What I'd like from you is a list of requirements a theory of induction has to satsify before it counts as adequate in a world where the no-free-lunch results hold. For each requirement, give the concrete case or consideration that motivates it, and mark whether it is non-negotiable or whether it is really just one camp's preference (some people will want the theory to give practical model-selection advice, others will be content if it only explains why induction is rational at all). For example, the theory should probably explain why a model that just memorizes the training set is less trustworthy than a simpler one, and why inferring that all emeralds are green is a better bet than inferring that all emeralds are green before 2030 and blue after, even though both fit the data equally well. The part I most want to see is whether two or three of these requirements are jointly unsatisfiable: maybe you cannot both respect the full force of the no-free-lunch results and still say our ordinary inductive successes are justified, or maybe you cannot demand that the theory tell us which real-world problems are learnable without sneaking in the very bias the theory was supposed to justify. Say which requirement is doing the most work — the one that, if you dropped it, would let all the other criteria be satisfied at once. I am not after a bibliography or a history of the debate, just the criteria and where they pull against each other.
