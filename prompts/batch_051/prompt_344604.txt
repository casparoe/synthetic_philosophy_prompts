I'm a PhD student in cognitive neuroscience of consciousness, and I keep hitting the same impasse in the split-brain literature. Everyone agrees that after complete forebrain commissurotomy the hemispheres can be driven to produce conflicting responses that the speaking hemisphere then disowns or rationalizes; what is contested is whether the patient has two centers of consciousness in one skull, or one subject whose experience and control are partially disunified by lost interhemispheric access. I want to know whether that dispute is empirically resolvable or merely verbal. Design the strongest feasible experiment you can think of—behavioral, physiological, or a combination—that would discriminate between the two-subject and one-disunified-subject hypotheses. State exactly which outcome would favor each reading, explain why the two readings are so hard to separate, and then say what the experiment would still leave open, including whether the leftover ambiguity is a mere limitation of method or a sign that the concept of a single subject of experience is underdetermined by third-person evidence.
