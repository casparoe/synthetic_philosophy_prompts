I have been thinking about a specific argument that used to be common in discussions of online games and virtual worlds, and I want help determining whether it still holds. The argument I have in mind runs roughly as follows: when a player in a virtual world steals another player's virtual sword, wrecks their virtual house, or embarrasses their avatar, no one has been genuinely wronged in the same way as in ordinary life. The stolen thing is only pixels and code, the victim could simply walk away from the game, and the whole interaction takes place inside a fictional, make-believe space whose rules were entered voluntarily. On this view, treating such losses as real harms is like saying someone genuinely injured you by capturing your rook in chess.

The development that troubles me is the rise of persistent social virtual worlds in which people now work, sell goods, manage stores, and depend on virtual property for real income. In some of these worlds, a shop can be the owner's actual livelihood, an account can be worth a large amount of money, and a demolition or fraud can erase months of real labor. That seems importantly different from losing a rook in chess, because the person is no longer simply leaving a self-contained game behind. My question is whether the older argument survives this change. Specifically, which premise of the "it's just a game" argument is really under pressure, and is there a repaired version that still separates genuinely real virtual harms from merely fictional ones? If you think the argument survives, show exactly why the new cases do not damage it; if you think it fails, say what the better criterion should be. Please answer in American English.
