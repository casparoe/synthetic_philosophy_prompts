You are an expert in political economy, the philosophy of markets, and the study of repugnant transactions. Approach the following as a careful mechanism-design analysis, not a policy recommendation. Before drawing conclusions, brainstorm at least four distinct ways the proposed rule could be gamed or produce unintended second-order effects; then analyze the rule’s behavior systematically, marking where each effect comes from.

In Book IV, Chapter V of The Wealth of Nations, Adam Smith defends a claim that his contemporaries found morally repellent: he argues that the private interest of the inland corn dealer and the interest of the public at large coincide, even during periods of severe scarcity. On his account, a dealer who paces his sales does not create the dearth but smooths it; a price kept artificially low during shortage would only accelerate consumption and sharpen the late-season distress; and the very fact that a merchant fears holding stock past the market’s peak tends to moderate his profits. Popular hostility toward corn merchants in a famine, Smith suggests, is not much better grounded than popular belief in witchcraft.

Now imagine a modern jurisdiction during a declared food emergency adopts the following substitute for anti-price-gouging and anti-hoarding laws. Every licensed wholesale dealer in essential staple foods must post daily public inventories at warehouse level. Dealers may sell at whatever price they choose. But each dealer must pay a sharply progressive scarcity-profits levy: for each unit sold in a given week, the levy is 80% of the margin by which the sale price exceeds the average wholesale price for that item in the same week over the previous five years. The proceeds are distributed equally to all resident adults as a monthly cash rebate.

Analyze how this mechanism would actually play out. What does it incentivize on the supply side, given that dealers retain pricing freedom but face the levy? What does it incentivize on the demand side, given the per-capita rebate? How does the combination affect hoarding, smuggling, vertical integration, quality degradation, misreporting, or collusion among dealers? Does Smith’s argument about the dealer’s interest tracking the public interest survive the addition of the levy, or does the levy break the alignment he identifies? And how does the transparency requirement interact with the moral repugnance of profiting from hunger: does making inventories public discipline the dealers, intensify public outrage, or transform the practice into something more or less legitimate? Identify the probable failure modes and say which, if any, can be fixed without gutting the scheme.
