I'm writing a paper for a value-theory seminar and I want to put G. E. Moore and *The Great Gatsby* in the same room. Moore argues in *Principia Ethica* (I'm paraphrasing, not quoting him) that the most valuable states of the world are certain states of consciousness — the enjoyment of beautiful objects and affection between persons — and that wholes containing them are worth more than the sum of their parts. Read the novel through that lens. Where would a Moorean say the real value in the book is: Gatsby's aesthetic idealization of Daisy, Nick's friendship with Gatsby, the beauty of the parties? And where does the novel push back? My hunch is that Fitzgerald treats beauty as something that is almost always bought, displayed, or performed, and friendship as entanglement in other people's deceptions, so the book might be a better critique of Moore than Moore is a reading of the book. But I want you to be fairer than that: say what Moore's categories genuinely illuminate in the novel, and say what the novel adds to Moore's picture of beauty and friendship that argument alone misses.
