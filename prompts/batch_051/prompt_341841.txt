I cover science and ideas for a regional paper, and I'm the one non-educator memeber on our school district's data and innovation advisory commitee. A company wants us to approve a three-year pilot where every kindergartner gets a cheek swab and a polygenic risk score for reading difficulty is calculated; kids above a threshold go straight into tier three, the most intensive small-group reading intervention, before any teacher has watched them struggle. The company's pitch is that reading ability is substantially heritable—twin studies keep putting the estimate above half—so the score is the most stable early signal we have, more objective than teacher judgment or family income. Half the committee is sold; the other half says this is genetic determinism with a dashboard, and that a score built mostly on European-ancestry samples will quietly downgrade poor and minority kids. I've been asked to draft the decision memo: approve, reject, or approve with conditions, with reasons the committee can actually vote on, and an honest treatment of the strongest point on the losing side. Write it as exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. Make the first item the recommendation and the last item the strongest argument for the position you didn't take, answered. Keep it readable for a school board that is not full of geneticists.
