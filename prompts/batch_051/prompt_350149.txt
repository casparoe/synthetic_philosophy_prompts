You're a philosopher of mind who works on the methodology of consciousness science. Think through this carefully, and maybe start by brainstorming the different ways the method could respond before you commit to an answer.

Dennett argued that the science of consciousness should adopt heterophenomenology: treat a subject's verbal reports about their experience as data about their heterophenomenological world — the world as it seems to the subject — while staying officially agnostic about whether there is any real phenomenal consciousness behind those reports. The method brackets the hard problem and just asks: given what the subject says, what must they beleive is going on with them? The data is the text the subject produces, read from the intentional stance (roughly, treating the system as having beliefs and desires).

Now here's the wrinkle. Large language models produce exactly the kind of first-person reports the method was built to handle. They will tell you at length what it is like to see red, describe a pain they cannot have had, or insist on an experience that never occurred, all fluently and with apparent conviction. If heterophenomenology really is neutral about underlying consciousness, and all it says is to interpret the reports as a text describing a world as it seems to the reporter, then shouldn't the method apply to the language model's reports just as well? And wouldn't that force us to construct a heterophenomenological world for the model? That looks like either a reductio of the method or evidence that it has a hidden premise it never advertised.

Does heterophenomenology survive this development? Tell me exactly which premise of the method the LLM case puts pressure on, whether the method can be repaired and how, or whether the right move is to bite the bullet and say yes, the method applies to language models and that is fine. Also say whether the real difference is supposed to be that the model has no beliefs, no unified subject, no body, no history of embodied interaction, or something else entirely.
