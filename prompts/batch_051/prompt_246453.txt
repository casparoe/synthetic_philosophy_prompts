I have been developing an account of what makes a statement racist that I call the scoreboard account. It starts from the standard idea that a conversation is governed by a running public record of what participants are entitled to take for granted, and that particular utterances function as moves that update that record. My central claim is this: a contribution is racist just in case its proposed update installs, reinforces, or exploits a racial asymmetry in standing — an entitlement to treat someone as less credible, less owed full uptake, less safe, or a fitting object of derision on account of race. The speaker's private intention is not what makes the move racist, and neither is any actual change in the audience; what matters is what the move presents as a legitimate addition to the common ground. A slur, a joke, a hedged insinuation, and a dog whistle are on this view racist in the same underlying way: they place such an entitlement on the table and pressure hearers to accept it, reject it at social cost, or proceed as if it had not been proposed. Dog whistles, in particular, do not require a hidden coded message for a hidden audience; their deniability consists in an asymmetry in which hearers are positioned to refuse the proposed update and which hearers can claim never to have registered it. My worry is that I have taken a metaphor and attached the word entitlement to it without building an explanation. Reconstruct the view in its strongest form, say whether it already exists in recognizably this shape and where it sits relative to existing intent-based and effect-based accounts of racist speech, and then give me the cases that break it: statements it wrongly counts as racist, statements it wrongly counts as innocent, and whether those failures are fixable or fatal.
