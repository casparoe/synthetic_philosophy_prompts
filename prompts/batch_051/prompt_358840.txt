I teach a small graduate seminar on judgement under uncertainty, and last term a student challenged me to state, not the positions I assign for discussion, but the commitments that actually govern my own planning and forecasting. I wrote the following list over the break. Each item seems right to me in isolation, and I can more or less live with the whole set, but I have a persistent sense that the position is less coherent than it looks, and I cannot locate where the strain is. I would like an audit rather than a lecture: take the list as given, identify which pairs of commitments genuinely pull against one another and which only appear to be in tension and dissolve on inspection, and rank the genuine tensions by severity. For each genuine tension, say what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would be; then tell me which single revision would do the most to restore coherence, and what it would cost me.

Here are the commitments:

1. The outside view should be the default for planning and forecasting: begin with the distribution of outcomes across a reference class of broadly similar projects and let that base rate carry most of the weight. The inside view — the detailed, case-specific model of the project — should move the estimate only modestly, and only on the strength of identifiable, well-supported differences.

2. Case-specific knowledge is a genuine source of forecasting skill rather than merely a bias to be suppressed. A forecaster who refuses to use what they know about the particular system, team, or design is not being admirably modest; they are discarding relevant evidence.

3. There is no mechanical, interest-independent procedure for selecting the reference class. Which past projects count as relevantly similar depends on a substantive judgement about which dimensions of similarity matter, and that judgement can itself be skewed by the same optimism that distorts one's direct forecasts.

4. Epistemic modesty requires a standing suspicion of first-person exceptionalism. The felt conviction that one's own project is importantly unlike the available comparison class is the standard signature of the planning fallacy; a chain of reasoning that ends in 'the base rate does not apply to my case' is a warning sign, not a settled conclusion.

5. Some undertakings are genuinely novel in ways that make the closest available reference classes systematically misleading, and an experienced judge can sometimes recognise this before the fact. It must be possible, at least sometimes, for the inside view to override an unfavourable base rate without this being mere self-deception; otherwise whole categories of high-value work would be irrational to attempt.
