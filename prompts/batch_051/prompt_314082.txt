Consider the following argument about the St. Petersburg game. A fair coin is tossed until it lands heads for the first time; the bank pays £2 if the first heads is on toss 1, £4 if on toss 2, and in general £2^n if the first heads is on toss n.

(1) The fair price of any gamble is its expected monetary value.
(2) The probability that the first heads occurs on the nth toss is (1/2)^n.
(3) The payoff, if the first heads occurs on the nth toss, is £2^n.
(4) Therefore the contribution of the nth possible outcome to the game's expected value is (1/2)^n × 2^n = 1, for every positive integer n.
(5) Therefore the expected value is the sum of infinitely many 1s, which is infinite.
(6) Therefore the fair price for this game is infinite.
(7) Yet no reasonable person would pay more than a modest finite sum for it, so some step must be wrong.

Is the argument valid? If not, state precisely which step fails and whether the failure is a false premise, an invalid inference, or an equivocation between two readings of a phrase. If you think it is valid, say which premise a reasonable person should reject, what replacement would be defensible, and whether that replacement fully dissolves the paradox or only moves it.
