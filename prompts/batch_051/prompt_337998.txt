I am a high school student writing a term paper for an introductory philosophy course. I am defending a measured version of Bernard Williams's claim in his essay on the Makropulos case: that for human beings as we know them, an endless life would sooner or later be marked by a kind of terminal boredom, and that this gives us reason to doubt that endless life would be desirable. I am not arguing that death is good, only that accounts of heaven or utopian immortality have to explain what would stop the slide into tedium.

Below are six objections to Williams's line of argument that I have gathered from classmates, my teacher, and philosophy discussion forums. Please sort them into a ranking from most damaging to least damaging, and assign each a rough percentage of the total critical force the six objections have together, so that the six percentages add to 100. After each objection, give one sentence explaining why you placed it where you did.

1. The analogy with ordinary long marriages or careers does not work: boredom in finite activities is produced by repetition of one role, but an infinite life could contain infinitely many distinct projects, friendships, and skills.

2. Williams assumes that the person's character stays fixed; if personality and desires can change enough, each stretch of life may feel as urgent as the last.

3. Heaven is usually described as involving a perfected or glorified nature, so objections based on ordinary human psychology may simply not apply.

4. Even if a particular person would get bored, that is not a necessary truth about every possible immortal; some temperaments might be endlessly fascinated by a single pursuit.

5. Boredom is not an all-or-nothing state; a life could be punctuated by long stretches of calm or routine without the person wishing for nonexistence.

6. The argument proves too much: if our deepest projects and commitments are needed to make sense of continuing, then many ordinary finite lives would also become boring or pointless after their main projects are complete, yet we do not usually think those people should wish to die.

After the ranking, I would like you to be self-critical: tell me which of your placements you are least confident about, where your reasoning is most likely to fail, and what specific argument, example, or consideration would most likely cause you to change your top-ranked objection.
