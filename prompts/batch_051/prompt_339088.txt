I am a high school student in a philosophy club, and we are going to discuss theories of emotion. For a handout, I want to create a decision tree—something like a “which theory of emotion do you actually hold?” flowchart—that sorts people into positions based on how they answer particular questions about cases, not on which label they prefer. I have been especially struck by William James’s article “What Is an Emotion?” (1884). As I understand him, he reverses the common-sense sequence: we ordinarily think we meet a bear, feel fear, and therefore run; James says we meet a bear, run and undergo other bodily changes, and the feeling of those bodily changes is the fear. I want the first branch of my tree to test whether someone actually accepts that reversal or only finds it plausible in some cases.

The tree should have about eight questions and end with leaves for at least these positions: (a) emotions are perceptions of bodily changes, (b) emotions are cognitive appraisals or judgments, (c) emotions are sui generis feelings not reducible to body or judgment, and (d) emotions are compound states with both bodily and judgment components. It should also identify any logically possible combinations of answers that do not correspond to a live position and briefly say why each is unoccupied—is it incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored?

Please phrase each question as a concrete case or a yes/no claim about a situation, for example a person whose body shows the signature of fear but who sincerely denies being afraid, or someone who judges that they have been wronged but feels no anger, so a reader can answer without knowing the theory names. End by telling me which positions the tree cannot separate and where the Jamesian reversal does the most diagnostic work.
