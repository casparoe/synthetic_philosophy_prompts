I'm an undergraduate taking a seminar this term on the self and personal identity. We have been using a working set of criteria for when a self persists over time, and I want to test those criteria against a hard case that came up in a lecture on Hofstadter's idea that a self is a pattern and can be partially instantiated in more than one brain.

The working criteria are as follows. A self persists from an earlier time t1 to a later time t2 just in case:

(a) a single subject of experience is continuous from t1 to t2 — there is one stream of consciousness and one first-person point of view across the interval;
(b) the later stages stand to the earlier stages in overlapping chains of psychological connectedness and continuity — autobiographical memory, belief, intention, and character — carried forward in the right kind of causal way;
(c) the later stages are carried by the same living body, or at least by the same brain;
(d) the later person retains a first-personal sense that the earlier person's experiences are their own, remembering them from the inside rather than as someone else's episodes.

Now the hard case. Emil and Rafael are friends. Over the course of a year, Emil uses an experimental neural interface that reads the high-level structure of his mental life — episodic memories, verbal habits, emotional dispositions, characteristic trains of thought, the style of his inner monologue — and writes a copy of those structures into Rafael's brain. Emil loses nothing in the process; his own memories and dispositions remain fully intact throughout. By the end of the year, Rafael contains something close to a complete duplicate of Emil's autobiographical memories and mental dispositions. He sometimes finds himself thinking in Emil's voice, anticipating Emil's reactions, and remembering episodes from Emil's life with a strange sense of ownership. Emil and the now Emil-like Rafael live side by side for six months. Then Emil dies suddenly in an accident.

The lecture framed this as a challenge to the working criteria. Rafael plainly satisfies condition (b) to a high degree, but he appears to fail or only partly satisfy (a), (c), and possibly (d). A Hofstadter-style view would suggest that a large part of Emil's self persists in Rafael, because the self-referential pattern of memory, disposition, and thought that constituted Emil is now running in substantial form in another brain. A more conventional view would say that Emil's self died with Emil, and what survives in Rafael is at most a copy or a trace.

Please evaluate the case against the working criteria. Structure your response in the following five sections, in this order and with these approximate lengths:

1. Working criteria restated (about 120 words): restate (a) through (d) and flag any ambiguities you will rely on, especially what counts as the right kind of causal way in (b).
2. Criterion-by-criterion assessment (about 450 words): take (a), (b), (c), and (d) one at a time, say whether Rafael satisfies each, and explain why.
3. Where the criteria underdetermine the verdict (about 180 words): identify the points at which the criteria genuinely do not settle the question, and say whether that is a fault of the criteria or of the case.
4. Verdict (about 180 words): say whether, on balance, Emil's self persists in Rafael — wholly, partly, or not at all — and distinguish the persistence of the Emil-pattern from the persistence of Emil's first-person self.
5. Do the criteria need revising? (about 180 words): say whether this case should push us toward a degreed or pattern-based account of the persistence of a self, and what such a revision would look like.
