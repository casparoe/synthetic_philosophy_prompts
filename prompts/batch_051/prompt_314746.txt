I am trying to sharpen my use of "path dependence" and would like my criteria tested against a borderline case. My working criteria are: (1) the outcome was not foreordained — small differences in early events, choices, or chance could have put the process on a different trajectory; (2) the trajectory became self-reinforcing, because coordination costs, sunk costs, or network effects made it progressively more costly to leave; (3) the outcome is sticky, so reversing it would be expensive and disruptive; and (4) the realized outcome is worse, by some relevant standard, than at least one alternative that was genuinely available early on.

Now consider left-hand versus right-hand road traffic. The original choice of side was mostly contingent, with surviving variation shaped by local custom, edict, and empire; and once a country has settled on one side, vehicle design, road markings, junctions, and driver habit make switching very costly, so almost no country switches. Conditions (1)–(3) look satisfied. But condition (4) is doubtful: neither side is intrinsically better; what matters is that drivers on the same network use the same side. A left-hand-traffic country does not appear to be locked into anything worse than a right-hand-traffic country — just different.

Is left-hand traffic genuinely path-dependent, or only contingently chosen and then persistent? If it fails my criteria, does that show that condition (4) should be weakened or dropped — and if I drop it, what then distinguishes path dependence from mere inertia? If it passes, what is condition (4) contributing, given that no inefficiency is involved? I care less about the yes/no verdict than about the best formulation of the criteria and where the boundary should run.
