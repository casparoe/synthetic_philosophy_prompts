I am a PhD student in moral and political philosophy, and next month I am presenting a paper to a workshop on moral status. My thesis is a qualified defense of a Nozickian two-tier view, reconstructed from the discussion in Chapter 3 of Anarchy, State, and Utopia usually summarized as utilitarianism for animals and Kantianism for people. On the view I defend, our treatment of nonhuman animals is governed by a broadly utilitarian calculus, while our treatment of human beings is governed by Kantian side constraints that forbid sacrificing persons for aggregate gains. The paper grants the familiar grounds for unease that Nozick's own discussion raises: the view seems to owe us an account of why infants, the temporarily nonrational, and the severely cognitively disabled are protected by side constraints even though they are not occurrent rational agents; I also add that the human/nonhuman divide can look arbitrary given evolutionary continuity. I argue the divide is defensible if the status-conferring property is not occurrent rational agency but membership in a species whose normal mature members are rational agents, so that marginal humans are covered without collapsing into utilitarianism for animals.

The audience is not a general one. It includes a classical utilitarian who works on animal welfare and population ethics and is broadly in the Singer tradition; a Kantian constructivist who thinks the Kantian tradition can ground direct duties to animals and who will regard my appeal to side constraints as a misreading of Kant; a rights theorist in the Regan tradition who takes many animals to be subjects of a life with inherent value; and a Nozick specialist who will care less about whether the two-tier view is right than about whether it is Nozick's. There will also be several agnostic philosophers of biology who are skeptical of species-membership criteria in ethics.

Draft me a Q&A preparation sheet. I want the objections I will actually get from this audience, not the objections I would get from a generic applied-ethics crowd. Order or otherwise flag them by how likely and how damaging they are, and phrase each the way the person would put it. For each objection, tell me which part has real force and what I should concede up front, which part I should hold, and give me a short reply suitable for a two-minute floor response, with a note on where that reply is softest. Include the question I should most hope nobody asks, and say why. Finally, say how confident you are in this read of the audience and single out the point you are least sure of.
