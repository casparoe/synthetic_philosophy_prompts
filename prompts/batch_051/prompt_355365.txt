I'm a grad student in moral philosophy and I keep going back and forth on Williams's integrity objection to utilitarianism. The way people summarize it — utilitarianism sometimes requires you to violate your deepest commitments when the numbers come out that way, and that's bad because it undermines your integrity — honestly sounds to me like the standard demandingness objection with better branding. Every moral theory requires you to sometimes do things that cut against your projects: pacifists drafted into wars, parents who can't favor their kids in triage, whatever. So why does the fact that a moral theory conflicts with someone's 'ground projects' count as an objection to the theory, rather than just being the ordinary price of not being a selfish person? If integrity is valuable, why can't the utilitarian just learn to value it like any other good and weigh it in the calculus along with everything else?

And then the wife-rescue case. I can't figure out why the guy can't just quicky run the consequentialist calculation, conclude that saving his wife is the best thing available, and then go save her. The thought "she's my wife" and the thought "saving her has the best consequences here" can both be true, and I don't see why the second thought contaminates the first. Williams seems to be saying that entertaining the second thought at all is already one thought too many, but that just sounds like he's romanticizing unreflective action. What am I actually missing? If the objection isn't just the demandingness objection dressed up in talk about character, what exactly is the thing Williams thinks utilitarianism can't represent, and is there a version of consequentialism that actually fixes it rather than just biting the bullet?
