I've been having a recurring argument with two friends who both work in end-of-life care, and I've realized the thing they disagree about is really a much wider asymmetry in how we judge people who feel other people's pain. Outside work, everyone in this group agrees that a person who sees a news story about a refugee family, spends the evening in tears, and wires them money is a beautiful, big-hearted person. But at work, the same two friends split completely: one says a nurse who comes home crying about her patients is the only kind of person who should be doing the job, while the other says that nurse will burn out in six months and that a good nurse keeps some distance, treats the patient's pain as a problem to manage, and leaves it at the door. So we seem to have one and the same trait -- being strongly moved by the suffering of strangers -- treated as admirable in the donor and as a warning sign, a "boundaries" problem, in the nurse. Why?

I've read enough to know this is basically where Schopenhauer and Nietzsche split. Schopenhauer made compassion (Mitleid) the one genuinely moral incentive, so that being moved by another's suffering just is what morality is; Nietzsche thought pity was nowhere near as clean and selfless as we pretend, that it spreads the suffering around rather than reducing it, and that it often disguises a little jolt of superiority over the pitied. These look like two rival theories of exactly where the line between the donor and the nurse should go. Act as a professional philosopher who knows both thinkers well, and think carefully. Start by brainstorming the candidate principles that might justify the asymmetry -- maybe it's about whether the feeling leads to effective action, about consent to be exposed, about chronic versus acute exposure, about professional role, whatever seems strongest. Then put Schopenhauer and Nietzsche to work: where would each actually draw the boundary between valuable compassion and pathological pity, and wether their lines track the ordinary donor/nurse distinction or cut across it in uncomfortable ways. Finally give your honest verdict: is the asymmetry something a principle can justify, or is it mostly a messy social compromise -- we praise the people whose tears cost us nothing and pathologize the ones whose tears cost us care or labor? If the asymmetry should be revised, say how.
