Me and a couple of friends got into an argument last night about what actually makes something matter morally — what has to be true of a system before it can be wronged or harmed, and not merely broken or damaged. We ended up with three criteria we were all roughly willing to sign onto: (1) the system has valenced states, states that feel positive or negative to the system itself rather than just encoding 'good' or 'bad' from an outside designer's point of view; (2) those states belong to a single subject, a unified point of view that undergoes the pain or pleasure rather than some aggregate of feelings spread across loose parts; and (3) the states get tokened by the right kind of processing — the system has to actually be doing the feeling in its causal structure, not just reading out canned responses that happen to match what a feeling would do.

Then somebody brought up a case none of us could settle. Call it the Net. A billion people are given radios, identical rulebooks, and shift schedules, and none of them has any idea what the whole arrangement is for. On each shift, a person recieves a short string of symbols, looks up a rule, and radios new strings to other shifts. Staggered and routed the right way, the Net as a whole implements, step by step, exactly the computation an ordinary human nervous system performs when it's in pain after touching a hot stove. The Net controls a robot body that jerks back, says 'ow,' and later avoids the stove, and the engineers confirm the computation is neuron-for-neuron identical to the pain computation in a normal human. But no person in the Net feels anything; each of them is just passing symbols.

Does the Net feel pain, and does it matter morally the way a burned person does? Go criterion by criterion and tell me where each one gives a clear verdict, where it goes quiet, and whether the case shows the criteria need fixing or the Net just counts.
