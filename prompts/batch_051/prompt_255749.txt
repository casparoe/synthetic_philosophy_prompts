A city government, citing Mill's view that a society discovers which ways of life suit human beings only by giving individuals room to conduct varied, uncoerced experiments in how to live, proposes the following policy. Each year every adult resident must file a life-plan declaration covering five domains: occupation, household structure, religious or ethical practice, leisure, and consumption style. Within each domain, each declaration is compared with all declarations filed that year by residents in the same ten-year age band; a choice is classified as common if it is among the three most frequently declared choices in that domain and band, and otherwise as uncommon. Residents pay a small surcharge for each common choice and receive a credit for each uncommon choice; the credits are funded by the surcharges so that the scheme is revenue-neutral, and all credits and surcharges are capped at a modest fraction of income. The announced rationale is that custom already subsidizes conformity through social approval and ease of imitation, so an explicit counterweight is needed to keep the social repertoire of ways of life from narrowing.

Treat this as a mechanism, not as a moral verdict on the policy. Analyze how it would actually behave in operation. What does it incentivize among residents, among employers and marketers who shape the available options, and among the officials who write the categories and audit the declarations? How would people game the declaration without changing how they actually live? What second-order effects would it have on the social meaning of nonconformity, on the sincerity of declarations, on genuinely exploratory ways of living, and on people whose well-suited choices happen to be common? Does the scheme produce the Millian good it is designed to subsidize, or does pricing and administering experiments in living alter the very thing it aims to support? Finally, distinguish the failure modes that could be fixed by amending the scheme from those that are intrinsic to it.
