I've just come from a long argument with a friend who clerks for a state supreme court justice, and I'd like your help with the part we couldn't settle. We were arguing about the death penalty—retribution, deterrence, and the risk of executing an innocent person—and my friend claimed that her justice runs a single consistent principle through her capital proportionality review, in which the justice reviews each death sentence and either lets it stand or reduces it to life without parole. I wasn't so sure the principle is as single as my friend thinks.

The justice's recent docket includes these cases. In one, the defendant slowly poisoned his business partner with antifreeze over several months in a planned buyout dispute; the evidence included purchase records, incriminating emails, and ethylene glycol in the victim's preserved blood. She let the death sentence stand. In another, the defendant shot a prosecution witness two days before the witness was to testify in a drug case; two independent eyewitnesses identified him and cell-location data placed him at the scene. She let that sentence stand. In a third, a prisoner already serving life for murder killed a correctional officer during an escape attempt, and the killing was captured on video and witnessed by several officers. She let that sentence stand as well. But in a fourth case, a woman with a long-documented history of abuse shot her husband during an assault and immediately called 911 and confessed; the justice reduced her death sentence to life without parole. In a fifth, the getaway driver in a robbery during which the gunman killed the store clerk—his involvement was established by security video and he had no prior record—also had his sentence reduced. And in a sixth, the defendant was convicted of a fatal parking-lot shooting that followed a heated argument, but the state's case depended mainly on a jailhouse informant and a cell-tower match placing him in the same part of town; there was no weapon and no confession. She reduced that sentence too.

The new case before her is this. The defendant was convicted of murdering her neighbor by adding antifreeze to the neighbor's drinks in small amounts over several weeks, apparently because of a property-line dispute. There was no confession and no eyewitness. The prosecution relied on her purchase of antifreeze, records showing she had searched for information about the symptoms of antifreeze poisoning, and a single toxicologist who testified that the victim's symptoms and later kidney failure were consistent with ethylene glycol poisoning; the defense expert disputed the toxicologist's methodology, and no tissue samples were preserved for retesting. The justice must now let the death sentence stand or reduce it to life without parole. What decision best coheres with her own precedents, what are the candidate principles that fit her record, and what does each predict in this new case? Identify the closest analog from her docket on each side of the decision, give your best prediction of what she will do along with the reasons for preferring that reading, and say explicitly which principle she would be adopting if she affirms and which she would be adopting if she reduces. If her record underdetermines the result, describe one further case—different in its facts from this one—that would do the most to reveal which principle she has been applying all along.
