At a workshop on the psychology of laughter, a colleague kept compressing the field to a single picture: humor is tickling without touch, and tickling is humor with touch. On her telling, both are what she calls benign violations — a small threat or intrusion that the subject simultaneously reads as safe — and they share the same involuntary staccato laugh, the same dependence on a playful frame, the same failure under threat or self-production, and the same social function of testing signals of trust. From this she drew a strong conclusion: the benign violation theory is not one candidate among four; it is the correct account, and the superiority, incongruity, and relief theories are just special cases of an underlying tickling mechanism — superiority being the tickler's enjoyment of a safe dominance, incongruity being the cognitive version of an unexpected poke, and relief being the after-the-threat safety signal.

I want you to audit this analogy rather than just endorse or dismiss it. First, build the correspondence table as carefully as possible: what corresponds to the tickler, the ticklish spot, the protective frame that makes the touch safe, the involuntary laugh, the difficulty of tickling oneself, and the developmental or evolutionary function of tickle-play. Then work through the places where the mapping strains or breaks — for example, humor is verbal and conceptual while tickling is bodily; tickling requires a concrete other body and a contact event, whereas most humor has no assailant; tickle laughter is shorter-latency and less dependent on a stored body of shared knowledge. For each disanalogy, say whether it is fatal to the intended conclusion that benign violation subsumes the other three theories, or merely a wrinkle. Finally, tell me how much of the colleague's conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether some better analogy — or none — should replace the tickling picture.
