I write about brains and decision-making for a living, and I keep tripping over Gregory Kavka's old toxin puzzle. The setup: a billionaire with a flawless intention-scanner offers you a million dollars tomorrow morning if, at midnight tonight, you genuinely intend to drink a nasty but harmless toxin tomorrow afternoon. You don't have to actually drink it to collect — the machine just reads your brain at midnight. You also know that tomorrow afternoon, after the money clears, you'll have zero reason to drink the stuff and you won't. So at midnight it seems impossible to honestly form the intention: you can't intend to do what you know you won't do. Yet if you can't manage it, you end up a million dollars poorer for want of a mental state.

Here's the snag. I'm deeply attracted to the clean consequentialist picture: a rational agent at each moment does whatever has the best expected consequences, and intentions are just an internal prediction or summary of what you expect to do — their rational standing is entirely borrowed from the actions they point toward. On that view, intending to drink the toxin tomorrow would be irrational because drinking it would be irrational; the fact that an intention would win you the money just means you'd like to have an irrational mental state, not that you can rationally have one, and rationality was never supposed to hand you powers you lack. But I also can't shake the intuition that the rational agent gets rich. It feels like a real fact about agency — not a heuristic glitch — that you should be able to cash in on a commitment, or at least that a theory which says a perfectly reliable brain scan can make a million-dollar difference but rational choice can't reach across it is missing something central about deciding and self-binding.

So I don't want to be told who wins. I want a negotiated verdict. If the consequentialist picture is right, what exactly is the intuition doing — is it a scope error, a confusion about whether you can change your own incentives, a sloppy everday use of the word 'intend'? What would it cost to drop the intuition, and what do I get for that price? If the intuition is right and commitment has independent rational force, what does the theory have to look like: do we allow rational intentions aimed at irrational actions, do we need a richer story about self-binding and resolve, and do we thereby license a kind of rational self-deception your not comfortable with? Or is there a reading on which both survive — say, the winning move is to change your future incentives until drinking becomes rational, which keeps the tidy theory and the get-rich intuition compatible? Walk me through what each move costs and which part of my picture of agency gives way under each.
