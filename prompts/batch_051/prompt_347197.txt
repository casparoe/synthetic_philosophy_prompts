Benjamin Franklin's attempt at moral perfection was an engineered practice rather than a vague aspiration: a list of thirteen virtues deliberately ordered, a ruled notebook with rows for each virtue and columns for each day, a nightly examination that recorded every fault, and a rotating program in which he concentrated on one virtue per week while still logging lapses in all the others. Suppose Franklin could examine a contemporary habit-tracking application of the quantified-self sort—software that lets users log behaviors, maintain streaks, designate a weekly focus, review analytics of lapses and trends, receive automated nudges, and optionally broadcast progress on social leaderboards—and suppose he were invited to produce a formal appraisal of it as an instrument of moral improvement, not merely as a convenience.

Write his likely assessment, grounded in the particulars of his method and psychology: the reasoning behind the order of the virtues; the fact that Humility was added last, and only after a friend told him he was proud; his insistence that he never achieved a clean ledger and that the disciplined effort itself, rather than a flawless end state, made him better and happier; and his sensitivity to pride and to how virtue presents itself to others. Avoid a one-to-one mapping between the app's features and his notebook. Identify at least two features he would endorse or adapt, and at least two he would reject or warn against, with each judgment traced to commitments internal to his moral project rather than to stock present-day techno-skepticism. Then propose one concrete redesign of the app that Franklin might demand from its developers, and explain why it follows from his actual commitments. Aim for roughly 700 words.
