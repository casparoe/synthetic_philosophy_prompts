I'm drafting a paper on information cascades and want to test whether the position I've been sketching actually hangs together before I build the argument around it. My current commitments are these:

(1) In the standard sequential binary-choice setting with private signals, a rational agent can be justified in ignoring her own signal and following the actions of predecessors; doing so is ordinary Bayesian updating, not conformity bias or groupthink.

(2) One of the model's best-known implications is fragility: once a cascade is underway, later actions carry no further information, so a single sufficiently informative public disclosure or an unusually well-informed dissenter can collapse the herd.

(3) I nevertheless want to use this same model to explain the long-term entrenchment of false consensus — a medical practice, a policy, an investment — without appealing to sanctions, preference falsification, or groupthink. I take the persistence to be the rational herding story's doing, not a separate conformism story.

(4) I also regard reputation-based herding (managers or committee members who follow a consensus because deviating when the consensus turns out right is penalized more than following when it turns out wrong) as complementary to informational cascading: reputation makes real-world herds more robust than information alone would.

(5) When a cascade goes wrong, I don't think any individual who followed the herd merits epistemic criticism; each updated correctly on the available evidence. But I do want to say the outcome is a collective epistemic failure that well-designed institutions could have prevented.

(6) In practice, I treat the longevity and stability of a consensus as evidence of its likely truth: the longer a market or profession has converged without collapse, the more reluctant I am to second-guess it.

Are these views jointly tenable? I'm not sure where the strain is, and I don't have a favored revision in mind. Please rank any tensions by severity and, for each, identify which commitments conflict, whether the conflict is genuine or only apparent, and what would have to be given up to remove it — flagging any tension that dissolves on closer inspection so I don't revise away a commitment unnecessarily.
