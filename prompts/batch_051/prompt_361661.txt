I'm on the committee that runs the annual rememberance service for my congregation, and there's a fight at the moment that I need help thinking through. Our constitution says: 'The Congregation shall preserve the memory of those of its members who perished in the Persecution, and shall read their names aloud at the service of remembrance each year, for as long as the Congregation shall exist.' Last year the family of one of the murdered members found a letter in her handwriting, written just before she was taken, saying she did not want to be turned into a ceremony; she wanted her name to rest with God, and anyone who wished to honor her should do a day of unpaid work for a living person. The committee is split: one side says the rule exists precisely so that no individual preference, even the dead person's own, can erode the community's duty to remember; the other side says the whole point of remembering is faithfulness to her, and she has released us from the rite. I'm less interested in the vote than in how the rule should be read. Walk me through what the plain words give, what the purpose gives, and what the founders would have said if this letter had surfaced in their lifetimes, and tell me which reading should win and why. Keep the two sides recognizable as a Margalit-ish duty-to-remember view and a Rieff-ish case-for-forgetting view, but don't turn it into a bibliography.
