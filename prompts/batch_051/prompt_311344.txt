I spent most of my career in the physical sciences, and since retiring I have been working through philosophy more systematically than I ever could while employed. The area that has stayed with me longest is epistemic injustice, in particular the familiar distinction between testimonial injustice, in which a speaker receives less credibility than they deserve because of a prejudicial stereotype about their social identity, and hermeneutical injustice, in which a gap in the collective interpretive repertoire leaves someone poorly equipped to make sense of, or convey to others, an experience. I understand the basic taxonomy; I am less interested in further refinements of it for their own sake than in knowing which of the field's live questions are worth a serious person's limited time.

I would like you to produce a defended agenda, not a survey and not a reading list: an ordering of what you take to be the three most important open questions in epistemic injustice today, with your reasons for that ordering made explicit. The agenda should be fitted to me as the relevant audience — a retired natural scientist, working alone, able to devote perhaps ten to fifteen hours a week for the next two years, with a quantitative and mechanistic cast of mind and a strong interest in institutional design: courts, expert advisory bodies, medical encounters, peer review. Please weigh the candidate questions along something like the following criteria: how much would settling the question change institutional practice; how many other open questions in the area depend on it, and therefore which questions are upstream of which; how tractable it is for someone with my scientific background rather than one trained only in conceptual analysis; and how neglected it is. Defend the ordering rather than merely asserting it, say explicitly which questions you are deprioritizing and why, and indicate what evidence or developments would most change your ranking.

Where I especially want your judgment is a tension I keep noticing between extending the concept and making it empirically usable. I can see respectable arguments that the field should spend more energy on credibility excess rather than deficit alone, on structural and institutional forms rather than one-to-one encounters, and on turning its claims into testable, operational hypotheses. I can also see arguments that each of those moves risks draining the concept of its distinctive moral content. Which of those directions — or which questions cutting across them — would you place at the top, and why? Please answer in American English.
