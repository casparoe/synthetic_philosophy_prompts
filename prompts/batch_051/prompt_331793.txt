Last week at a bar two friends of mine, Dana and Marco, got into it about Darwin and Turing. I was mostly just drinking my beer trying to keep up, and by the end I honestly couldn't say who'd won. Here's their arguemnt as best I can reconstruct it.

Dana said: Darwin and Turing made the same weird move. Darwin showed you can get design without a designer, and Turing showed you can get intelligent output without an intelligent operator. The moral is that comprehension isn't an ingredient. A system can do the job without grasping anything, so when something acts smart the default shouldn't be that it understands.

Marco said: That's backwards. The inversions don't eliminate comprehension; they explain how it gets there. A Turing machine doesn't understand arithmetic, but a whole system -- like a person working out a bill -- can. Being made of non-comprehending parts doesn't mean the whole lacks comprehension, any more than being made of dry molecules means the whole can't be wet.

Dana: But "wet" doesn't point at anything; it's a loose label. Comprehension is about something. If a symbol-shuffling machine can be indistinguishable from someone doing arithmetic, then the shuffling is doing the work in both cases, and "he really understands" is just a bit of stage magic.

Marco: It's not magic, it's a set of observable capacities. The person can explain the algorithm, notice where it breaks, connect it to other things, use it in new cases. If you demand some private glow, of course you won't find it. Turing's point was that the private glow was never needed.

Dana: But each of those capacities is just another competence an uncomprehending system could in principle simulate. Every time I point that out, you add another competence. You never point to comprehension itself, just a bigger pile of competences.

Marco: That's the water case exactly. No single molecule is wet, but the pile is. If you rule out patterns at the level of the whole, you've ruled out every emergent property. Darwin's inversion took away the designer-skyhook, but designs showed up afterwards; Turing's inversion took away the ghost, and understanding might still show up afterwards.

Dana: Except water can't be wrong about being wet. Comprehension is normative: it can be mistaken, it can aim at things that aren't there. Wetness is just a pattern of molecules, so saying it emerges is cheap. Intentionality is exactly what's at issue, so you can't just say "emergent" and be done.

Marco: Agreed, that's the open question. But I don't need the full story to keep my point: the inversions rule out the miracle, so an emergence story is the only game left. That's a reason to build the bridge from competence to content, not to deny the content exists.

Dana: "The only game left" only works if you've already assumed comprehension happens. A machine that acts smart without understanding shows that the inference from smart behavior to understanding can always be resisted. Maybe the thing you're trying to bridge to is a folk category we should retire.

I walked away not knowing who had the better of it. Can you go through their exchange point by point and say who is right about which specific points, where each of them is missing something, and give an overall verdict on who's more right? Keep your answer under 400 words.
