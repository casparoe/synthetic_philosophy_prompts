I've been rereading Jerrold Levinson's 1980 paper "What a Musical Work Is," and two moves in it seem to pull in opposite directions. On one side, he says any account has to respect the plain fact that composers create their works: a piece like the Hammerklavier Sonata came into being when Beethoven composed it and didn't exist before that, and its not just a loose way of talking. On the other side, he's clear that a musical work isn't a score, isn't any particular performance, and isn't the set of all its performances either — it's an abstract type that performances instantiate, something he approaches by first considering a work as a sound structure and then tinkering with that idea.

But abstract objects are the sort of thing you'd normally say exist timelessly and get discovered, not brought into existence. So can the two claims be reconciled without draining creation into mere first-noticing, or demoting one of them to a façon de parler? I want an answer that runs through the plausible reconciliations quickly and then commits: pick the one you'd defend, say why the others amount to special pleading, and name the bullet the best reading still leaves you biting.
