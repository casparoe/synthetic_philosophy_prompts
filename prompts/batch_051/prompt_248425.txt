I have spent enough time around mathematicians to notice an asymmetry in the way the community treats two kinds of proof that are both, in any literal sense, unsurveyable. The classification of the finite simple groups is accepted as one of the field's great achievements, even though its proof is spread across hundreds of papers by dozens of authors and, on any honest account, no single mathematician has ever read and checked the whole thing from end to end. Yet the computer-assisted proof of the four-colour theorem—and, more recently, the proof of the Kepler conjecture, whose final step rests on a computer check of many cases that no person can follow—still makes many of the same people uneasy, and the first objection they raise is precisely the one they do not raise against the classification: that the proof cannot be surveyed by a human being.

I want to know whether this asymmetry can be defended or whether it is simply an inconsistency the discipline lives with. Please do not just rehearse the usual complaints about computer proofs. Identify the strongest candidate justifications for treating a large distributed human proof differently from a large machine-checked proof—consider, for example, the fallibility of code against the fallibility of referees, the value of surveyability against the value of certainty, and the thought that a proof should explain rather than merely certify—and test each of them against nearby cases: a three-hundred-page human proof that no one has yet checked, a computer proof that emits a formally verifiable certificate, a proof formalised in a proof assistant and checked down to its axioms. Then say whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
