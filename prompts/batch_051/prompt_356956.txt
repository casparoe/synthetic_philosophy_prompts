I am taking a course in the philosophy of probability, and a classmate and I have been unable to resolve a disagreement about the following problem. A factory produces solid metal cubes, and the only information supplied is that a cube's edge length lies somewhere between 0 and 1 meter. My classmate argues that, because no edge length in that interval is any more likely than any other, the probability that the edge length is less than 0.5 meters is one-half. That seemed right to me. However, when I applied the same style of reasoning to the area of one face, I obtained a different answer: because no face area between 0 and 1 square meters is any more likely than any other, the probability that the face area is less than 0.25 square meters should be one-quarter. But a cube has a face area below 0.25 square meters exactly when its edge length is below 0.5 meters, so the two probabilities seem to be assigned to the same event. If I reason about volume instead, the same event receives probability one-eighth. One of us concludes that the other has made a mistake somewhere; the other concludes that the original problem is under-specified. Which conclusion is correct, and what should we take away about arguments that start from the idea that equal ignorance ought to yield equal probabilities?
