I have been working out a theory about how the first evil will could arise in a creature God created wholly good, and I want it tested against the account in Augustine's City of God, Book XII, chapter 7. Augustine there denies that the evil will has an efficient cause: he says its cause is not something that produces it but a deficiency, a falling away from the supreme good when a created will turns toward itself. I accept that, but it seems to me it needs a mechanism if it is to explain the devil's fall rather than just redescribe it. My proposal is this: the angelic will has a natural orientation toward justice, but it also has a second-order capacity to treat its own finite perspective as if it were the standard of good. The first evil act is not the choice of a positive end; it is the will's unforced failure to will the higher good, a kind of self-tilting rather than an acquisition. I call this 'first-order defection.' Does this theory actually make sense of Augustine's denial, or is it just a fancier way of saying the same thing while still leaving the fall unexplained? Is the idea of an unforced failure coherent, or does it smuggle in the very autonomy Augustine might reject? And what is the strongest objection that would make a defender of my theory admit it fails?
