I am a high school student, and after a social studies unit on crowd behavior, I tried to reconstruct the reasoning behind why otherwise sensible people end up herding. The argument I produced unsettles me because its conclusion is absurdly strong, yet I cannot find the moment where it fails.

Here is the argument as I wrote it out. (1) When someone must choose between two alternatives under uncertainty, it is rational to choose whichever alternative is more likely to be correct given all the available evidence. (2) If other people have each looked into the matter and chosen alternative A, their choices constitute evidence that A is correct. (3) The larger the number of independent, information-based choices for A, the stronger that evidence becomes; past some size it outweighs any single person's private impression pointing to B. (4) Therefore, once enough such choices for A have accumulated, a newcomer is rational to choose A even while setting aside private information that favors B. (5) The same holds for every later newcomer, since each later person sees an even larger crowd already choosing A. (6) Hence, once a crowd of sufficient size has formed, everyone who joins it is individually rational, even in a case where the members of the group privately hold enough evidence, taken together, to show that B was the better choice. (7) So a sufficiently large crowd is always authoritative, and I should defer to it even if I suspect it merely grew by people imitating an accidental early queue. That last step cannot be right.

I would most prefer to keep (1), since it seems to me only to restate what it means to follow one's best evidence; I am prepared to surrender whichever later step is doing the real work, but steps (2) through (4) each look unavoidable when I read them separately. Please structure your answer in titled sections, in this order. 'The diagnosis' (about 150 words): state exactly which step or premise should be rejected and how the inference breaks. 'Why it looked right' (about 150 words): explain what makes that step tempting and why a careful reasoner might accept it. 'What rejecting it costs' (about 150 words): give the minimal claim that should replace it and say what that replacement gives up or forces us to accept.
