Two friends of mine, Maya (a philosophy PhD student) and Dan (a physicist), got in an argument a while back and I've been mulling it over since. I realized later they were basically acting out Kant's first antinomy without knowing it. You are a professional philosopher who works on Kant. Please think carefully, brainstorm the moves on each side first, and then give me a detailed verdict.

It went roughly like this:

Maya: If time had no beginning, then before this present moment an infinite number of moments has already elapsed. But an infinite series can't be completed by successive addition — you'd never finish running through it to reach now. So time must have begun.

Dan: That's backwards. If time began, then before the first moment there was empty time, but a moment can't arise in empty time because no point of empty time gives it a reason to start rather than any other. 'A first moment' is just incoherent.

Maya: Fine, then the past is an actual infinite, and actual infinities can't exist in reality. An infinite regress of causes also explains nothing — every state would be explained by an earlier one forever, so nothing ever got the whole thing going.

Dan: But your alternative is a first moment with no predecessor. What explains that, nothing? You've just moved the missing explanation to a magic boundary. And the past doesn't have to be a completed totality; it's just unbounded — for every earlier time there's an earlier one, but no one has to collect the whole series together.

Maya: The whole past before now is a completed succession — it literally got completed by my sitting here. So the series was completed, which an infinite series can't be.

Dan: The 'whole past' isn't a thing physics needs. General relativity only needs local conditions at each moment; the sequence as a whole doesn't have to be completeable. You're reifying 'the entire past' into an object and then complaining it's impossible.

Then the conversation got interrupted.

Adjudicate this step by step. Where does each of them make a decent point, where does each overreach, and how do their moves line up with the thesis and antithesis of Kant's first antinomy?
