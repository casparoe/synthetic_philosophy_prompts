Here's my four-part test for whether something is literally a code, as opposed to a physical process that's useful to describe in code language. A code needs (i) a mapping from symbols to meanings where the link is arbitrary — nothing about the symbol makes it the right one, (ii) a sender who produces symbols under that mapping and a receiver who recovers meanings from them, (iii) the receiver gets there by interpreting the symbols, not just by being causally triggered, and (iv) the system can be wrong: a symbol can misrepresent its object, not merely fail to have an effect. Now run DNA through it. The codon table looks arbitrary in the right way — there are variant codon tables, so the mapping isn't chemically forced. But is there a sender? And does a ribosome interpret a codon, or just bind and react? Go through all four conditions and be straight with me about which are genuinely ambiguous rather than simply failed. Then take the virus case: a virus gets its genome into a host and the host start making viral proteins. Is the virus a sender, and its genome a message, in a way an ordinary cell's own DNA isn't? Or is that still chemistry with extra steps? Finally, tell me whether condition (iv) is doing most of the philosophical work, and whether the genetic code should be read as a technical term or as a metaphor that has frozen and made itself at home.
