Yesterday in my Chinese philosophy seminar I caught myself saying that wu-wei is just not forcing to a student who was asking whether it means you should stop trying to improve at things, and ten minutes later I was saying something like, no, it is more like trying not to try. I noticed afterward that wu-wei gets made to do very different jobs around the department. I have heard it used as (1) a defense of not micromanaging grad students, (2) a claim about why a pianist's best playing happens when she stops monitoring herself, (3) a claim that the sage acts rightly without deliberating, and (4) a joke about someone who is just being lazy. I want to untangle the one-liner trying not to try and its near variants: effortless action, not forcing, going with the flow, acting without acting. Lay out the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making. I can see at least these candidates: a psychological claim about what causes skilled performance; a phenomenological claim about how the activity feels from the inside; a normative claim about what makes an action good or virtuous; a metaphysical claim about aligning with the way things are; and a narrowly descriptive claim about not deliberately interfering. For each candidate, tell me whether it is platitudinous, substantive and defensible, or substantive and probably false, and seperate which readings actually support which of the four uses above. Then answer the paradox directly: if trying not to try is self-defeating, which readings survive, and does the slogan end up as a piece of advice that can only be followed once you stop treating it as advice?
