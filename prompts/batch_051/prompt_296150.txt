Most people hold both of the following. The grandchild of a person who committed a serious wrong is under no obligation to apologize for it; if a grandchild were asked to do so we would find the demand misplaced, and a voluntary apology would read as a gesture of personal regret rather than as the grandchild's acceptance of guilt. Yet when a state is asked to apologize for a serious wrong committed before any of its current citizens or officials was born — colonial violence, enslavement, a massacre, the seizure of land — the demand is widely treated as live and legitimate: a refusal by the present government is judged a present moral failing, and an apology by its head is received as the state's own assumption of responsibility. A state, though, is not a person over and above the people and institutions that compose it, and today's officials stand to the original wrongdoers in something like the relation the grandchild stands to their grandfather. What, if anything, justifies the asymmetry? Give the strongest principle you can find that generates both judgments — or argue that none can — and test it against nearby cases: a corporation that purchased a company built on stolen assets, a university whose endowment traces to enslavement, a family that has prospered from an ancestor's crime, a state that has since been dissolved, and a wrong old enough that no living person's interests reach back to it. Then say whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or abandoned, and what a head of state who accepts your conclusion should do at a commemoration.
