I have been going back and forth with a colleague about what, if anything, first-person reports of experience tell us about the causal powers of phenomenal consciousness. The epiphenomenalist position, as I understand it, is that although there is something it is like for me to be in pain, the pain itself does no causal work: the neural processes that accompany it produce my grimacing, my avoidance behaviour, and my saying "I am in pain." My colleague replies that this makes the fit between my reports and my experience a massive coincidence. Why should my mouth reliably say "pain" just when pain is present, if the pain never contributes to the saying? He takes that fit as straightforward evidence that experience is causally efficacious. I suspect the issue is not so easily settled, but I want to know whether any observation could actually move us.

I would like you to design the strongest empirical test of the claim that first-person reports are driven by the phenomenal character of a state rather than merely by the underlying physical or functional state of which it is the possibly inert accompaniment. Specify the manipulation or measurement in enough detail that it could be put to an experimenter; say which result would favour the interactionist and which would favour the epiphenomenalist; and be explicit about what the test would not settle even if it came out cleanly. In particular, I want to know which part of this dispute is genuinely empirical and which part is a standing metaphysical commitment that no experimental outcome can touch. Please write your answer in British English.
