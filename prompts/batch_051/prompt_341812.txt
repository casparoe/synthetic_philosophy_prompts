In a study group for my philosophy of religion course, two of my classmates got into an extended argument about petitionary prayer. Both were taking as common ground that God is omniscient and strictly immutable — that is, God can undergo no real change, including in His will or purposes. Cal argued that, given those premises, petitionary prayer cannot coherently be understood as making a difference: if God has always known exactly what He will do and has always willed it, then the outcome would be the same whether we prayed or not. Mira replied that this treats God as a time-bound agent who deliberates after the fact; on the classical view, God's single eternal act can include both our prayers and their results from the outset, so a prayer could genuinely figure in God's reasons for the state of affairs without God ever changing. Cal answered that "genuinely figure" was doing no work — if the same eternal will includes the prayer and the outcome, the prayer is just another item in the world, not something that influences God. Mira countered that influencing need not require changing: God could will from eternity to grant the good conditionally on our asking, so that if we had not asked He would not have granted, which gives the prayer counterfactual bite while God remains unchanged. Cal replied that such a conditional will still does not let the prayer move or incline God; it makes God's will a rule fixed in advance, and the prayer's role reduces to a condition built into a predetermined plan. Mira then said that even if petitionary prayer does not change God, its point may be to align the petitioner with God's will, to express dependence, and to allow goods to be received as gifts rather than as coincidences. Cal responded that this may preserve prayer as a devotional practice, but it surrenders the ordinary meaning of petition: "asking God to do something" would really be asking to be changed, and any outcome could with equal plausibility be described as an answer whether we had asked or not. Could you go through this exchange and say who is right about which specific claims — and what, if anything, both of them are missing?
