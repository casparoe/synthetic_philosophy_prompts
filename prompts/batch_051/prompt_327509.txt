So I got into a long argument with a friend last night about Kant, Hare, Singer and the whole "what if everyone did that?" move, and we ended up trying to turn it into an actual moderation system for our co-op's message board. I'd like someone to walk through how it would actually behave in practice.

The rule we sketched: when a post gets reported, the author can appeal by writing a maxim stating the act as a universal permission — roughly, anyone in situation S may do action A. Then the moderators must keep the post up if (i) the maxim can be conceived as a universal law without self-contradiction, and (ii) the author can sincerly will the maxim as a universal law. Consequences of this particular post don't count. To stop people dodging the test with hyper-specific maxims, other members can propose a more general maxim covering the same act, and if any generalization fails, the post comes down — though the author can add back detail they can show is morally relevent.

What does this actually incentivize? Walk through what posts survive, how people learn to write maxims to get things through, where the system gets gamed first, whether moderators end up smuggling consequences back in, and whether the failure modes are fixable. Does the real work get done by the universalizing logic, or by the choice of description?
