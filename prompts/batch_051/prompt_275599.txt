Here are the criteria I have been using to decide whether a general term for a material has its meaning fixed by the world rather than by the beliefs in the speaker's head—whether the term is semantically externalist. (1) Initial kinding: the term's use descends from an act in which it was attached to actual samples of the stuff rather than from a stipulated definition. (2) Deference to investigation: the community treats the term's extension as answerable to what the stuff turns out to be, so that a discovery about its hidden nature can correct their applications of the term. (3) Twin cases: two speakers who are physical and psychological duplicates—same brain, same beliefs—but who have been causally connected to different local stuffs would mean different things by the same term. (4) Same-kind independence: there is a fact about whether two samples are the same kind that does not depend on how similar the samples look to the speakers.

Now consider a community in a mountain valley that has, for centuries, collected a pale green, fine-grained stone from one riverbed for carving, and has used a single term for it that functions as a natural-kind term. Traders bring in a visually almost indistinguishable green carving stone from over the mountains; the community calls it by the same term, buys it by the same look and feel, and pre-theoretically takes it to be more of the same material. A chemist then tells them that the local stone and the imported stone are two different minerals, nephrite and jadeite, with different compositions and structures—no more the same kind, the chemist says, than either is to a third green stone the carvers have always rejected. The community accepts the chemist's report but its buying and carving practices continue to treat the two stones as one material.

Is the community's term externally individuated in the way the four criteria spell out? Go through the criteria one by one, saying clearly where the case meets or fails each, and noting where the question is genuinely open rather than settled. In your fifth item, say what the case shows about whether the criteria themselves need changing—for example, whether a term can be anchored to the world by its initial kinding while failing to be answerable to hidden nature.
