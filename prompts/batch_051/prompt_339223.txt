Since being elected to my town's council last spring, I have had to confront a 1962 sign ordinance that has not been enforced in at least forty years and that nearly every business on Main Street violates openly. The town attorney says it is still law; some council members say it is a dead letter; I have been trying to work out a principled account rather than just a vote. My current theory is this.

Dormant statutes come in two kinds. A sleeping law is one that has gone unenforced because the conditions for its application have rarely or never arisen, so no one could reasonably have arranged their affairs around its absence. A 1954 curfew that applies only while the levee is failing, and that has never been invoked because the levee has never failed, is sleeping law: it remains fully in force and can be invoked the day the levee fails. A dead law, by contrast, is one that has been openly and repeatedly violated with official knowledge, for long enough that reasonable people have ordered their affairs on the assumption that the conduct is permitted. The sign ordinance is dead in this sense: businesses have paid for signs in plain view of the town, year after year, without enforcement or even a warning.

The distinction, I want to say, is neither age nor legislative intent but reliance. A rule can be centuries old and still sleeping if its triggering conditions never arose; a rule can die quickly after a clear and public policy of non-enforcement. What turns a sleeping law into a dead one is a public history of non-enforcement that breeds legitimate expectations. Enforcing after that history disappoints legitimate reliance, and that is exactly the rule-of-law wrong. So the test would be: would enforcement now punish conduct that a reasonable person, knowing the local history, would have taken to be permitted?

I suspect this is too tidy. Is the sleeping/dead distinction coherent, or is it really a continuum? Does it already exist under a name I should know? What does the account get right, and where does it break first? In particular, is reliance the right boundary condition, or does it smuggle in a moral judgment—about which expectations are legitimate—that the theory pretends to avoid?
