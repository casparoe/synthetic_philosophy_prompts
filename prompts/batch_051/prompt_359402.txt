I'm a doctoral student in philosophy of mathematics, and I've been reading Lakatos's Proofs and Refutations alongside a seminar on the epistemology of computer-assisted proof. As I understand Lakatos, his central claim is that mathematical knowledge grows quasi-empirically: informal mathematics advances through conjectures, proofs that function as thought-experiments, and refutations by counterexample, with concepts and definitions continuously stretched, restricted, or reconstructed in response—monster-barring, exception-barring, lemma-incorporation, and the like. On this picture, the content of mathematics is not fixed by a frozen set of axioms; what counts as a legitimate object, definition, or proof is revisable under the pressure of counterexamples.

The development I want to bring to bear is the last two decades of interactive theorem proving. Substantial results, including some long-accepted theorems, have now been fully formalized and machine-checked in systems such as Coq, Isabelle, and Lean. Inside a proof assistant, every inference is checked against fixed definitions and a small trusted kernel; a completed formalization is a certificate that the theorem follows deductively, and the definitions are effectively frozen in code. That looks like the realization, at least locally, of the Euclidean ideal Lakatos argued was a distortion of mathematical practice—mathematics as infallible deduction from fixed axioms, with no role for refutation or concept-stretching.

I'm trying to determine whether the quasi-empirical thesis should be rejected in light of this, whether it can be repaired, or whether it still goes through once its claims are located correctly. Which premise is really doing the work here: the claim that informal, revisable mathematics is where knowledge grows, the claim that formal deduction cannot capture mathematical practice, or some background assumption about what a refutation is? Is there a defensible Lakatosian account on which the formalization process itself becomes the newest arena of proofs-and-refutations, with the checker's verdicts playing the role of empirical data? I'd like a careful treatment that identifies the premise under pressure and states the strongest weakened version of the thesis that remains true.
