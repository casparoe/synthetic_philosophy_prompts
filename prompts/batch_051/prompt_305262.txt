I am a PhD student working on Hume, and I keep getting pulled between two strands of the first Enquiry. In the sections on scepticism about the understanding, Hume speaks freely of the powers and forces that drive the course of nature, says they are entirely concealed from us, and adds that nature has kept us at a great distance from all her secrets, leaving us with knowledge of only a few superficial qualities of bodies. But in the section where he actually sets out his account of necessary connection, he locates the origin of that idea in an internal impression, a felt determination of the mind to pass from a cause to its customary effect, and he defines a cause first in terms of constant conjunction and then in terms of the customary transition it produces in the understanding. The first strand sounds like a modest realism about real though unknowable powers; the second sounds like a deflationary reduction of necessity to regularity plus habit. Since these two strands sit close together within a single work, a change-of-mind story across books cannot do the needed work. Can the two be reconciled without treating one strand as mere metaphor or loose talk? Give the strongest candidate reconciliations, say what each one costs, and then commit to the reading of Hume you think is most faithful to what he actually says, explaining why the alternatives amount to special pleading. Please write in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, and take a definite position rather than settling for a survey of options.
