I teach a graduate seminar on normativity, and next month we're on the wrong kind of reasons problem—the one where a demon threatens to make your life miserable unless you admire him, everyone agrees you now have a reason to admire him, but it feels like the wrong kind of reason because it does nothing to make him admirable. I keep wanting to think about this through my first career, which was applied statistics: I spent years doing model fitting and estimator selection before moving into philosophy, and I still reach for that vocabulary whenever a philosophical debate gets fuzzy. So here's what I'd like: a systematic translation of the object-given/state-given reasons distinction into the vocabulary of statistical estimation and model selection—estimators, loss functions, risk, likelihood, bias-variance tradeoff, regularization, choosing a model because the data support it versus because it minimizes expected loss. I expect the doxastic case, belief and credence, to carry most of the weight, since the statistician's separation of fit-to-data from decision-theoretic loss looks tailor-made for the evidential-versus-pragmatic reasons fight. But the real test are the non-doxastic attitudes—fear, admiration, respect, desire—where fittingness conditions are less obviously statistical. For each, tell me what translates cleanly, what only translates with distortion, and what has no expression in the statistical vocabulary at all. Then produce the residue report: which strands of the wrong-kind-of-reasons debate reduce to the familiar point that an estimator which fits best and an estimator which minimizes loss for the decision at hand are answering two different questions, and which strands don't survive translation—and say whether the non-surviving residue is a blind spot of the statistical framework or a sign that those bits of the philosophical problem were doing less work than they looked.

Act as an expert on both the wrong kind of reasons literature and statistical decision theory. Before drafting, brainstorm briefly, for yourself, what the core commitments are for a few standard positions—the evidentialist about reasons for belief, the pragmatist, the fitting-attitudes theorist who analyzes value in terms of reasons to value—and what their nearest statistical analogues might be. Then write the final translation from that scaffolding, but don't show me the scaffolding. Be candid about fudges: don't quietly import statistical assumptions and call the result a translation. Format: open with a one-sentence bottom line about whether, and in what form, the problem survives translation; then go attitude-by-attitude; then end with the residue report. Hard cap: 800 words.
