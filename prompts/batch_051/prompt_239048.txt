A friend and I have been having the same argument for months, and last night it stalled again in a way I found instructive. He defends the claim that historians should treat pathogens, climate shifts, and major volcanic eruptions as historical actors: not as inert background conditions, but as processes with their own dynamics that reshape the space of human possibilities. He points to the demographic collapse in the Americas after 1492 or to the harvest failures and unrest of the Little Ice Age, and insists that an account that treats disease or weather only as scenery will systematically miss why events took the course they did. I resist this. My worry is that the moment one writes the pathogen or the volcano into the story as an actor, one is on a slope toward the old environmental determinism that made climate, soil, and disease into fate and dissolved human contingency, culture, and politics. The frustrating part is that we agree on nearly all of the facts, and we agree that both a purely human-centred story and a crude geographic determinism are wrong; we just cannot agree on what it means to occupy the space between them.

What I want is not a verdict on who is right, but a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. Is this a substantive dispute about causation and counterfactuals—for instance, about whether we can say what would have happened absent the epidemic or the eruption without smuggling in geographical destiny? Is it a conceptual dispute about whether attributing causal weight to a non-human process is the same as attributing agency to it, and whether treating something as an agent commits one to determinism at all? Or is the real engine of the impasse a disagreement about the moral and political stakes of environmental history—a fear that strong climatic or microbial explanations let states, empires, and elites off the hook for decisions they took? I would like you to diagnose what kind of disagreement this is, where exactly the two positions come apart, and what would count as genuine progress: a distinction, a way of framing the causal claims, a kind of evidence, or perhaps an argument that the question is ill-posed and should be replaced. Please write your answer in British English.
