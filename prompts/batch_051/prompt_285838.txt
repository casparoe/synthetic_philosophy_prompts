I'm a journalist working on an essay about whether legal authority ever amounts to more than power with a nicer name, and a philosopher friend pointed me to Blaise Pascal's Pensees. The stretch I keep chewing on, in my rough paraphrase, is this: justice without force is powerless, force without justice is tyrannical, and since we are unable to make justice strong, we have made force just. Its the kind of line that sounds like a diagnosis one minute and an endorsement the next. I keep swinging between two readings. One says Pascal is basically an early legal realist: people obey law because it is law, custom passes for equity only because it is accepted, and all the high talk about justice is a post-hoc rationalization of whatever the sovereign can enforce. The other says the line about force without justice being tyrannical shows he is not endorsing that state of affairs at all — he is diagnosing a fall, and his point is that legitimate order requires force and justice to be fused, so that what is strong should be just and what is just should be strong. Write an essay weighing these two readings of Pascal on justice and force and come down on one side, defended with reasons. I do not want a fence-sitting synthesis that says both readings are partial and leaves it there. Also tell me what would change your mind: which argument, passage, or historical consideration would push you toward the other interpretation, or where your case is shakiest. British English, please.
