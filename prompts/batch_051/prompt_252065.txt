I am currently taking an ethics course centred on universalizability, and the one intervention that appears in every seminar is the question "What if everyone did that?" It gets deployed against quite different cases: a student cheats on a take-home examination because the invigilation is weak; a commuter evades a small fare; someone underreports income by an amount too modest to matter to the tax authority; someone keeps a library book long past its due date; someone breaks a promise that has become inconvenient. The professor treats the question as though it settles the matter, but it cannot be doing the same work in all of these cases, and it is not obvious that a single principle would support all of these uses.

What are the distinct precise claims that the slogan "What if everyone did that?" could be expressing? I would like the readings laid out as a menu, from the trivial to the radical, rather than a single settled interpretation. The candidates I can already see pulling apart include Kant's test of whether a maxim could be willed as a universal law, Hare-style universal prescriptivism, Singer-style generalization arguments, rule-utilitarian assessment of the corresponding rule, a threshold or critical-mass reading on which the question is really about how many people could act that way before harm appears, and a reading on which the question is a test of whether the agent's policy could survive being made a public rule that others also act on. For each reading, indicate whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or actually false. Then identify which reading or readings would actually support using the question to condemn the individual free-rider cases I listed, and which readings would fail to do so.
