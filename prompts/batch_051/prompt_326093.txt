Last night I got into a long argument with a friend about Darwin's and Turing's strange inversions of reasoning. He insisted they are fundamentally different moves. Darwin's inversion, on his view, is about origins: apparent design explained by blind variation and selection, with no mind anywhere in the causal story. Turing's inversion is about performance: a machine following rules can display intelligent behavior without grasping what the symbols or the task mean. I found myself wanting a single idea that covers both, and after we talked I wrote one down.

I have been calling it the Guarantor Criterion. The claim is that competence without comprehension is never a property of the competent system alone; it is competence whose success is guaranteed from outside. The guarantee might sit in the system's evolutionary history, as in the bird whose nest-building works without the bird knowing why the materials hold; it might sit in the programmer's goals and the training signal, as in a game-playing system that beats humans without understanding what winning is for; or it might sit in the environment's stability, as in a reflex that works until the world changes. Comprehension, on this account, is competence for which the system itself is at least a partial guarantor of its own success: it can identify something that its success depends on, say what would threaten that dependency, and revise its behavior when the dependency is challenged. So a fluent speaker answers for her own grammatical competence in a way a nest-building bird cannot, and the difference is not in the surface behavior but in where the explanation for the behavior's success can be found. My friend pushed back hard: he thinks the criterion is circular, because identifying what success depends on is itself just another competence that might be uncomprehending; he also pointed out that a thermostat's feedback loop meets my test for a minimal guarantor, while a brilliant but inarticulate carpenter might fail it.

I would like an honest assessment, not encouragement. Is the Guarantor Criterion a genuine distinction or just the intentional stance with a term of art bolted on? Does it already exist in some recognizable form in discussions of competence without comprehension? Give me the three strongest objections you can think of, and tell me whether any of them is fatal rather than merely uncomfortable. Please also say how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you are least sure of.
