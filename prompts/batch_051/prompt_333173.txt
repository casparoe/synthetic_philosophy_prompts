In a seminar on early modern political economy, I have been working through David Hume’s essay *Of Money*, first published in his *Political Discourses* (1752), and I want to compare two claims in it that students often run together. Near the beginning of the essay, Hume asks us to imagine that the whole money of a country is suddenly multiplied in one night. His point is that this abrupt increase would not make the country richer in real terms: prices of labour and commodities would rise, and in foreign trade the country would become uncompetitive until money flowed out and things returned to their former level. That is the neutrality claim. But elsewhere in the same essay he clearly allows that when money enters a country gradually, through expanded commerce, the increase is not immediately absorbed by wage and price rises. In the interval before prices adjust, the new money passes through many hands and rouses the activity of merchants, manufacturers, and farmers in a way that can enlarge the real stock of industry. So the essay seems to contain both a long-run neutrality thesis and a short-run real-effects thesis.

I would like you to compare these two theses on their own terms, working out how far they are compatible. Does the short-run stimulus depend only on a lag, on imperfect expectations, or on something deeper in Hume’s account of how commerce develops? And if Hume really allows that a timely increase in money can permanently improve a nation’s industry, how would he answer a mercantilist who takes that as proof that accumulating money is the route to national wealth? In doing so, please separate what Hume explicitly commits himself to from what is a genuinely contested extension or reconstruction of his view, and do not present a contestable reading as the settled consensus.
