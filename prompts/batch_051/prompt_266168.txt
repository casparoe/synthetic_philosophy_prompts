I have been reading Berkeley's economic writings, and I find that my own verdicts on monetary cases are close to what I take to be his position, namely that money is not itself riches but a device for setting industry in motion. But when I test that thought against particular cases, I am not sure one principle captures all of my reactions. I would like you to say what principle, if any, captures the pattern, and which of my verdicts it forces me to revise.

Here are the cases.

1. A poor island has fertile land, adequate labour, and no gold or silver. Its inhabitants begin using paper notes issued by a public bank to buy and sell goods. My verdict: the island has become genuinely wealthier, even though its stock of precious metal is unchanged.

2. A king halves the silver in each coin by mixing in base metal, keeps the nominal value the same, and spends the withheld silver on useful tools for the kingdom. My verdict: the kingdom has not become wealthier, even though it has the same nominal money stock and more tools than before.

3. A merchant issues transferable IOUs that local traders accept among themselves, and these circulate alongside the kingdom's coin. My verdict: the community's trade is improved and it is better off, even if its stock of coin is unchanged.

4. A government finances a year of unproductive court salaries by printing paper money. Prices rise, and no extra goods are produced. My verdict: the country is not wealthier; it has merely re-labelled its goods in a larger number of units.

5. A country requires its official paper money to be accepted in payment of taxes, but private parties often refuse it in ordinary trades. My verdict: this can still function as money in a thin sense, but it does little to increase wealth until it circulates willingly.

6. A public bank replaces silver coin with paper notes, sends the silver abroad, and uses the proceeds to buy tools. My verdict: the kingdom is wealthier, not only because tools were imported but because the paper now circulates more readily.

I am especially puzzled by the pairing of cases 2 and 6. In both, silver is removed from the coinage and used to purchase tools, yet my instincts differ sharply. Can a distinction between debasement and replacing coin with paper carry that much weight, or should one of my verdicts go?

Please give me the principle or small set of candidate principles you think best fits these verdicts, state plainly which verdicts it cannot accommodate, and say what it predicts about a case I have not settled: a government introduces paper money, declares it legal tender for taxes, and requires the issuing bank to lend only against pledged land, with the notes cancellable by tax payments. Does this create wealth, or does it only re-label existing wealth?
