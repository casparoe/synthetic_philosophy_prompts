I am preparing a decision-tree handout for an advanced undergraduate seminar at the intersection of philosophy of action and AI safety. The handout should classify positions on a single question: whether the demand that an AI system avoid side effects, or act with low impact, can be defined without first fully specifying what is valuable. Please write the classification instrument in American English.

Build the tree from logical space rather than from a pre-established list of named positions in the literature. For my purposes, organize the space around three distinctions.

First, whether a side effect is identified relative to the agent's objective—so that recognizing side effects requires at least a partial specification of the intended outcome—or relative to objective, structural features of the world or of the agent's behavior, so that side effects could in principle be identified without knowing what anyone values.

Second, what an impact constraint ultimately targets: the state of the world as compared with a counterfactual baseline; the set of future possibilities left open, such as the agent's ability to bring about many alternative future goals; or the agent's own means and permitted actions rather than the goodness of its outcomes.

Third, whether a complete specification of value is required, or whether a partial specification can suffice—for example, a coarse distribution over plausible values or a task-specific demarcation of which variables are relevant.

Use the logical combinations of answers to these distinctions to generate the branches, and locate the following six positions as leaves, renaming them if a more descriptive label helps.

a. A value-first position: any workable notion of a side effect presupposes a full specification of what is to be achieved or preserved, so without full values the demand for low impact is either indeterminate or a disguised partial value function.

b. A baseline-divergence position: low impact consists in minimizing some measure of divergence between the actual world and a counterfactual baseline in which the agent did not act, with explicit acknowledgment that the choice of divergence metric itself encodes evaluative commitments.

c. An options-preservation position: low impact is best defined as preserving the agent's ability to achieve a wide range of possible future goals, deliberately avoiding commitment to any single value function.

d. A means-side position: side effects are prevented not by scoring outcomes but by constraining what the agent may do or use—limited effectors, reversible operations, restricted channels of influence, minimal intervention—so that low impact is a property of the agent's means rather than of its consequences.

e. A task-demarcation position: side effects are changes causally downstream of the agent that are not necessary for the designated task, so a well-drawn task boundary can do the needed work without a full specification of value.

f. A deflationary position: the notion of a side effect is a folk-psychological one that resists formalization; at best one gets context-specific engineering controls rather than a general definition of low impact.

When writing the branch questions, use the following three cases as diagnostic probes, so that readers are sorted by what they say about cases rather than by which label appeals to them.

Case A: a home robot is asked to fetch a book from a desk on which a vase sits near the edge, while a cat sleeps on the floor beside the desk. The robot can choose among several routes and maneuvers. Ask which of the resulting changes—the vase, the cat, the robot's path, the moved book—should count when assessing the robot's impact, and why.

Case B: an organizing agent is asked to reduce clutter in one room. It throws away old letters, relabels tax documents, and repaints a wall. Ask what baseline or task boundary determines which of these changes are side effects.

Case C: a toy gridworld agent is rewarded only for collecting apples. It can build a bridge, dismantle a wall, or consume a scarce resource to collect more apples. Ask how one should judge whether its non-apple-related changes are side effects, and what would be lost under each alternative.

At each leaf, include one sentence stating the position's most awkward commitment or the main objection it must answer. For any combination of answers in the logical space that corresponds to no defended leaf, note the empty spot and say in one clause whether it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or apparently unexplored. If any two of the six leaves cannot be separated by the three distinctions, identify them and explain why the tree should flag that limitation. If a leaf rejects the framing of the question itself rather than occupying a branch, give it a root-level exit and say so.

Keep the tree to no more than four decisions deep. Do not include a bibliography or recommendations for further reading.
