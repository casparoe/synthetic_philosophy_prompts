I built this argument and it feels like it proves too much, but I can't find the exact line that's wrong. Take it apart and give me a definite verdict — not a menu of possibilities.

Say a spin measurement has state 0.8|up> + 0.6|down> before I look. MWI says the universal wave function just keeps going, and decoherence splits it into an up branch where I see up and a down branch where I see down.

(1) Both branches are equally real after the split; neither one is the 'actual' outcome.

(2) So before the measurement, it's not true that the up outcome has a 64% chance of happening in the sense of which branch comes to exist: both branches will exist with certainty, and their both equally real.

(3) The thing that differs between the branches is their amplitude. So if the Born rule says 64% and 36%, it has to be a rule about how I, a rational observer, should bet or set my credence about which branch I'll find myself in.

(4) But the pure physics of MWI — the Schrödinger equation plus decoherence plus the claim that all branches are real — doesn't say anything about how a rational agent should weight branches. Weighting by |amplitude|² is one rule; weighting by |amplitude|⁴ or just counting branches are others. Nothing in the physics marks any of them as wrong.

(5) Therefore the Born rule has to be added to MWI as an extra principle (or smuggled in as a norm of rational weighting that doesn't come from the physics).

(6) And that means the selling point of MWI — that it gets all the predictions from just the wave function evolving, no collapse, no extra postulates — is false. It quietly imports the Born rule through the back door.

I half-suspect step (4) is the sneaky one, but I can't say why without hand-waving. Which step actually fails, if any? If step (4) fails, give me the strongest version of the decision-theory-style reply that makes it fail, and check whether the rest of the argument still goes through.
