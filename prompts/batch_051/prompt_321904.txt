Here's a working definition I've been toying with. I'll call x a moral agent just in case: (i) x can tell right from wrong in at least some concrete cases; (ii) x's judgments are responsive to reasons—give x a good argument and x could in principle revise them; (iii) it isn't just loose talk to say x got it wrong or should have judged differently; and (iv) x's judgments can make a difference to what actually happens—there's at least one possible situation where x's arriving at a verdict makes it more likely that things go that way.

Now take a hospital triage advisory AI. It reads charts and outputs a priority ranking with a written rationale. It has no actuator, no authority, and no way to follow up; all it does is write the ranking to a queue. The staff read it most of the time and follow it maybe 70% of the time; the rest of the time they override or ignore it. So the AI's verdicts do change what happens when the humans defer to them, but the AI itself never acts beyond producing the ranking.

Does this AI qualify as a moral agent under the four criteria? Go through them one by one. Where a criterion underdetermines the verdict, say what extra fact about the setup would settle it. Then say whether criterion (iv) is doing any useful work, or whether the case shows it should be weakened—say, to 'x is treated as a source of reasons by others,' or some other fix you prefer. Dont just answer yes/no. Write your answer in American English.
