I am giving a seminar paper next month to a philosophy department whose value-theory working group will be the main audience. The senior members include a hedonist about prudential value, an aesthetic cognitivist who works on Kant and formalism, and a care-ethicist who works on friendship, while several graduate students work on moral particularism and are openly suspicious of any supreme-good claim. I want a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of my view.

My paper defends a revised Moorean position drawn from Principia Ethica, specifically from chapter VI, the chapter on the ideal. The passage I anchor the interpretation in is the one where Moore lists personal affection and aesthetic enjoyment together, says that these two include almost all the greatest goods we can imagine, and then treats each as an organic unity whose value depends on the presence of certain cognitive and emotional elements rather than on any single simple quality. I will distribute the passage and paraphrase it in those terms rather than quote it at length. My thesis is that Moore is best read as holding that beauty and friendship are not two separate highest goods but two mutually illuminating forms of appreciative cognition: genuine appreciation of a person has an aesthetic, contemplative structure, and genuine aesthetic appreciation of an artwork involves a quasi-personal receptiveness to the object. The intended payoff is that Moore avoids the charge that his ideal reduces to a list of pleasant experiences, because on my reading the supreme goods are constituted partly by how one attends to the object, not merely by the feeling that results.

Draft the prep sheet: eight objections this specific audience will actually raise, ordered by how likely and damaging they are, each phrased the way that audience would phrase it. Give me a two-minute reply to each, a note on where that reply is soft, what I should concede up front, and include the question I am hoping nobody asks. Do not turn this into a bibliography or a survey of Moore scholarship; ground each objection in the philosophical pressures these people already bring.
