A well-known argument says deontological constraints are paradoxical: if killing an innocent person is so bad that you may never do it, then five such killings are worse than one, so refusing to kill one to prevent five seems to make the constraint self-defeating. Now suppose we shift to a high-transparency society of the sort cheap logging and reputation systems are making possible, where every decision to obey or break a norm leaves a durable public record and people condition their own compliance on others' histories of following the norm. Does the original paradox survive that change? Identify which premise of the argument the transparency/reputation setting is meant to undercut, and say whether it repairs the argument or merely changes the empirical example.
