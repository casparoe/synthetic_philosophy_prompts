I am a high school student preparing a talk for my school's student-run ethics colloquium. The thesis I will defend is this: the familiar choice between guilt and shame is badly framed, and a community such as a school can, within limits, choose to cultivate a non-humiliating form of shame as the public moral emotion that carries its shared norms. My argument, briefly, is that the standard contrast loads the dice by defining guilt as a response to the act and shame as a response to the self; once shame is defined as global self-condemnation, its bad reputation follows trivially. I distinguish what I call situational shame: a brief and specific sense that a particular act has lowered one's standing before others, without any conclusion that one is worthless. I then argue that public norms need socially legible signals—recognition, endorsement, reinstatement—and that private guilt cannot provide this public coordination, whatever its personal value. Finally, since the school already tries to shape emotional habits such as gratitude and empathy through modeling and ritual, there is nothing mysterious about deliberately foregrounding this shame rather than treating it as a residue of harsh discipline.

The audience is about twenty-five upper-year students and two moderating teachers. Most of the students are debaters or run the school's peer-mediation program; they are fluent in restorative-practice language and generally take it as settled that shame is damaging to adolescents, that guilt is the mature moral emotion, and that any defense of shame is a coded defense of humiliation or stricter discipline. The teachers are less likely to attack my motives and more likely to press me on what, concretely, the school would have to change and on the risk of singling students out.

Prepare my question-period prep sheet. Give me the objections in the order they are likely to occur and in the words this specific audience would use, not generic objections from a textbook discussion. State which objections have real force, what I should concede up front, and what I should hold. For each, offer a reply suited to a two-minute response and note where that reply is weakest. End with the objection I am hoping nobody asks, phrased as a student in the room would phrase it, and explain why it is the dangerous one.
