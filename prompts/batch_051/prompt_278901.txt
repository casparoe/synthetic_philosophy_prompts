I'm a software engineer on the product side of an AI lab, I got pulled into our internal working group that advises leadership on competitive dynamics with other labs. We meet next week to decide whether to approve a proposal from someone on the policy team: we would approach one of our main competitors about a mutual transparency and verification arrangement before either of us gets close to AGI. The idea is not to stop the race just to slow it down and reduce the chance it blows up, things like registering large training runs in advance, shared compute thresholds, maybe a third party that can inspect compliance. It would cost real money and engineering time and it would give the other lab some signal about our internal gating and timelines. Half the group thinks its obvious: both labs would be better off with a slower race, so a deal should be possible if we can verify it. The other half thinks it is naive: the incentive to defect at the last minute is too strong, and even the information sharing is a security risk. The argument has gone in circles.

I have been reading Fearon's article Rationalist Explanations for War, not my usual thing, and there is a part I keep coming back to. He is trying to explain why two rational states that would both prefer a negotiated settlement still end up at war. He says it is not enough for both sides to prefer the deal beforehand; after the deal the situation can shift so one side has a motive to break it, and because it cannot credibly promise not to break it later, the other side will not accept the deal even if the deal would leave both better off. He calls this a commitment problem. There is a passage where he gives the example of a state in relative decline that attacks a rising rival even though both would prefer a peaceful bargain, because the declining state cannot trust the rising one to honor the terms once it gets stronger. That is basically our situation: any mutual slowdown only works if each lab can credibly commit not to use the extra time to pull ahead, and no internal policy or contract can really bind a lab's future decisions when the stakes get existential.

So the memo I need is supposed to take a clear position: approve, reject, or approve with conditions, with reasons people on both sides can actually respond to. My question is whether the commitment problem is the main blocker, and if so is the whole proposal doomed, or is there a version of the arrangement (staged info sharing, third-party escrow, automatic triggers, whatever) that actually solves the credibility problem rather than just moving it around. I also want the memo to engage honestly with the strongest argument on the losing side, because I will have to defend this against people who have been in the field longer than me.
