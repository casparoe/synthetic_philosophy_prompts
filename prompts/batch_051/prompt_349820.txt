I write about science and ideas, and lately I’ve been poking around Hofstadter’s old Letter Spirit project. For anyone not familiar: the idea was to model how humans see all those wildly different shapes as the letter “a” while also recognizing an individual style, by separating the “letter spirit” from the “letter style” and building gridfonts with roles and constraints. The thing I can’t get out of my head is this: today’s generative models do something similiar statistically. A class is a fuzzy region in latent space, and style is roughly a direction or subspace in that region. So we now have two very different ways of saying what makes every “a” an “a”: the Letter Spirit way, where the category is a kind of structural/grid role with allowable variations, and the latent-space way, where the category is a learned distribution with graded membership. Is this a deep structural convergence, or am I just pattern-matching on the word “style”? I’m less interested in history than in the ideas: do the two approaches make different predictions about what a weird-but-recognizable “a” is, or about the boundary between a bad “a” and an “a” in a very unusual style? Could the latent-space model capture the normative part—that some changes preserve the letter and others break it—or is that the spot where the analogy comes apart? And if it does come apart, what exactly does Letter Spirit have that a statistical model lacks? Please also say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would push you the other way on whether this is a real parallel or mostly superficial, and where your reasoning is shakiest.
