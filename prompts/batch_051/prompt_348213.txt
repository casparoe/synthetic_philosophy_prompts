In Plato's Euthyphro, Socrates confronts Euthyphro with a dilemma about whether the gods love what is pious because it is pious, or whether an act becomes pious because the gods love it. Consider, against that background, the actual practice in many modern liberal legal systems of adjudicating claims for religious exemptions and accommodations: the court does not ask whether the claimant's religious premise is true or whether the claimed duty really would be imposed by the divine being in question; it asks whether the claimant sincerely holds the belief, whether the law substantially burdens the claimant's ability to follow it, and whether the state's interest is compelling enough to refuse the accommodation. The same legal systems may also allow conscientious objection to military service, and some of their officials use language suggesting that rights or duties have a divine source and cannot be overridden by human law. What metaethical view of the relation between divine will and moral obligation does this practice presuppose? Does it quietly commit the practice to the first horn of the Euthyphro dilemma, the second horn, neither, or does it intentionally sidestep the dilemma? Reconstruct the implicit view, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it is merely compatible with, assess whether its parts presuppose consistent things, and say what would have to change in the practice if one adopted the other horn of the dilemma.
