I was arguing with a friend who does law about statutes of limitations, and we landed on an asymmetry I can't get straight. Where I live (England, though I doubt the detail matters much), so far as I understand the rules, a person who killed someone can be prosecuted thirty years later — there's no expiry on the state's claim against them, and almost everyone feels that's right. But the family of the person killed, if they want compensation for the loss, have to bring their own claim within a few years or it is lost. Same act, same evidence, same underlying wrong: the public claim survives indefinitely, the private claim evaporates quickly. I can't work out weather that's justifiable or just an accident of how criminal and civil claims developed separately.

It isn't really about evidence, because you can stipulate a case where a diary and bank records settle both the guilt and the size of the loss just as well in year thirty. And it's not obviously about the family's grief fading, since the state's interest in punishing fades too — the killer may have lived blamelessly for decades and be a different person at trial, and we still punish. The best principle I can come up with is that punishment answers a wrong against everyone, so the community's claim never dies, while compensation answers a private loss and the family is expected to get on with life. But that makes the family's claim matter less than a stranger's, which sits badly with the idea that they're the ones actually harmed. What is the strongest justification for this asymmetry, and does it survive the hard cases — a family that never moved on, a killer who has wholly reformed, a case with perfect evidence and a thirty-year delay? Tell me whether we should keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or give it up, and say which argument would change your mind or where your reasoning is most likely to fail. British English, please.
