I'm a backend engineer who's ended up as the token 'AI risk person' at my company, and next month I'm giving a talk to an open-source ML conference defending the view that frontier labs should replace the open/closed binary with a staged release ladder. The audience will be mostly open-source ML people — maintainers of fine-tuning and inference libraries, people who run local models, a few from an open-science nonprofit, and at least one person who helped release a Llama-derived model. They aren't policy people or alignment researchers; they genuinely care about reproducibility, decentralisation, and small labs being able to compete, and they're suspicious of anything that smells like regulatory capture.

My thesis, roughly: above a capability threshold (I'm thinking evals showing meaningful uplift in cyber-offence or bio work), a lab should keep the weights closed by default for a fixed window — say three years after the model is no longer frontier — but publish evals, red-team findings, training-data summaries, and a 'defensive adaptation' score immediately. After the window, weights get released unless that score shows downstream safety tooling hasn't caught up. My bet is that most of what open source actually gives researchers (scrutiny, reproducibility, fine-tuning on smaller models, understanding failure modes) comes from the early transparency layer, and the thing you lose by waiting is mainly the ability to strip safeguards off a frontier model on day one.
