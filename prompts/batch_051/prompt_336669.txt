I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I've been trying to pin down what philosophers mean by a "category mistake," because it looks suspiciously like a type error but I can't tell how far the analogy really goes. My working hunch is that a category mistake is what happens when you apply a term or a question to the wrong kind of thing in principle, as opposed to merely getting the facts wrong. I'd like a definition that handles the following cases: 1–3 strike me as clearly category mistakes, 4–6 clearly aren't, and 7–9 I can't sort out.

1. A visitor is shown all the colleges, the library, and the laboratories, then asks, "But where is the university?"
2. "What color is the number seven?"
3. "Is Tuesday heavier than Wednesday?"
4. "The capital of California is Los Angeles."
5. A toddler who believes the moon is following the car.
6. A novelist writes that the wind sighed through the pines.
7. A strongly typed language's compiler rejects the expression `"hello" + 5`. Is this the same kind of error or merely a formal cousin?
8. In a serious argument about whether corporations can be morally responsible, someone asks whether a corporation can feel remorse.
9. Having read that the average taxpayer paid $8,000 in federal income tax last year, someone asks, "And where does this average taxpayer live?"

Please structure your answer in five sections, in this order: (1) Proposed definition — one paragraph. (2) Case-by-case run — one or two sentences on each of the nine cases, with an explicit verdict. (3) The type-error analogy — one paragraph on whether a category mistake just is a natural-language type error. (4) Revisions or defense — one paragraph saying where the definition misfires and whether you revised it or accepted the surprising result. (5) Least secure verdict — one or two sentences naming the case you're least confident about and why.
