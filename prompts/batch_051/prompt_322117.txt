I retired six years ago after a career in physics, mostly relativity with some thermodynamics at the edges, and I've come to philosophy late — a friend dragged me into a Buddhist philosophy reading group at the university and now I can't leave. We just spent a month on Dōgen's being-time, uji, and I gather every newcomer gets him somewhat wrong, but here's the version I've settled on. Dōgen seems to be denying that time is a container or a track which things and events move through. Instead each particular being — this mountain, this moment of sitting — is itself time. The picture of moments flowing past a stationary 'now' is treated as a habit we impose on things, not the way they really are; each moment is complete as itself rather than a carriage on a pre-laid rail.

The thing that's been naging at me is that special relativity, in the reading I used to teach, seems to have pushed in a strikingly similar direction. There is no frame-independent now; simultaneity is relative to an observer's state of motion, so two observers slice the world into different pasts, presents, and futures. The standard old-school interpretation of this is a block universe, in which things are extended in time rather than moving through it, and no one frame's 'now' is privileged. To my physicist's ear that sounds like the same denial: no single shared now against which all events are dated, but each perspective, or each dharma, carrying its own time. So my question is whether this is a genuine structural convergence — two independent routes to the same picture — or whether I'm just projecting physics onto an old Buddhist text because both of them use the word time in an odd way. I'd like a committed verdict: deep or superficial, with the strongest case for the other side named and answered rather than a survey of options. And please just write it as continuous prose, like an email — no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables.
