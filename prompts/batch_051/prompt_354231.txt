I keep going back and forth on what two-factor accounts of delusion commit one to regarding the status of delusions as beliefs. A colleague pressed the following argument on me:

(1) On a two-factor account, a monothematic delusion is the product of two factors: (a) a neuropsychological anomaly that yields an anomalous experience, and (b) a second bias or deficit in the evaluation of evidence that prevents the subject from rejecting the content of that experience.
(2) If a state is the product of an anomalous experience together with a biased or deficient evaluation of evidence, then its formation violates the norms governing good belief formation.
(3) If a state's formation violates the norms governing good belief formation, then the state is not a belief.
(4) Therefore, two-factor accounts imply that delusions are not beliefs.

The conclusion strikes me as stronger than the two-factor framework warrants, but I am not sure whether the trouble lies in the logic or in the premises. Is the argument valid? If it is invalid, identify the exact step that fails and the reading under which it fails. If it is valid, identify the premise a defender of a doxastic view of delusions should reject, and say what weakened form of that premise would still preserve a recognizably two-factor account.
