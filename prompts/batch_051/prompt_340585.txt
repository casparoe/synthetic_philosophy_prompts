Recently a family argument ended with someone quoting at me the slogan that the sin is in the consent, not in the act. I had made a remark at a dinner that, without any cruel intention on my part and without my having in any settled way consented to the harm, nonetheless humiliated a relative; my defense was that my intentions had been clean, and the reply was that this is exactly irrelevant, because, as the saying has it, what matters is not the act but the consent. Since then I have noticed the same one-liner being used to draw almost opposite conclusions — once to excuse someone who meant well but botched the execution, once to insist that an outwardly polite person was morally worse than a rude one because of what they had inwardly consented to. I gather the saying descends from Abelard's ethics, but I do not need a historical or textual account of him.

What I want is a disambiguation. Take the formula as it circulates — that the sin lies in the consent and not in the act — and lay out the distinct precise claims it could be used to assert. I can already see that it might be a claim about God's judgment as opposed to human courts, about blameworthiness as opposed to punishability, about completed wrongs versus bare attempts, about occurrent choice versus settled disposition, and about consequences versus bodily movements; but I cannot confidently separate the readings that are genuinely different from ones that merely rearrange those distinctions. For each candidate reading, say whether it is a triviality, a substantive and defensible thesis, or an overstatement or falsehood. Then tell me which of these readings my relative's retort actually needs in order to refute my defense, and whether that reading survives scrutiny.

Please organize the response as follows:
1. Candidate readings — a numbered menu of at least five distinct theses the slogan could be expressing, in one or two sentences each, with the contrasts that make each one different from the others.
2. Verdicts — for each reading, two to four sentences on whether it is trivial, defensible, or false, and why.
3. What the retort needs — two or three sentences identifying which candidate has to be true for the retort to show that my clean intentions do not settle the matter.
4. Bottom line — one or two sentences on the most defensible claim the slogan can make, and the most important thing it cannot establish.
