I'm trying to settle an argument I keep half-having about whether DNA is really a code, or whether calling it one is a metaphor that got out of hand and is now doing more work than it can support. The phrase "genetic code" gets used for at least two different things: the codon-to-amino-acid mapping itself, and the whole buisness of a gene sequence specifying a protein. I want a working definition of "code" — not the loose "it's information" sense, but a definition strict enough to correctly classify some clear cases, and to give a real answer on the DNA question.

Here are the cases I want the definition to handle:

1. Morse code: a human sender turns a message into dots and dashes, a human receiver turns it back.
2. The codon table: the mapping from 64 RNA triplets to amino acids or stop signals during translation.
3. The full path from a gene's base sequence to the amino-acid sequence of a protein, including the fact that splicing lets one gene produce more than one protein.
4. A smoke alarm that closes a circuit when it detects smoke particles, causing a bell at a monitoring station to ring.
5. A cake recipe a baker follows and gets a cake.
6. A computer program: source code compiled to machine code, then run.
7. A bird alarm call that, played back on a speaker, makes other birds of the same species dive for cover.
8. Enzyme "recognition": a substrate fits an active site by shape and charge, and the enzyme converts it to a product.
9. Methylation: a cell adds methyl groups to a gene's regulatory region, and that gene gets transcribed less.

Some of these feel clearly like code, some clearly not, and the DNA cases are exactly where I lose track of what the word is doing. If a single definition gets all of them right it's probably rigged, so say so. Propose the definition—or two definitions if the cases force a split—then run it through all nine, tell me which single verdict you're least sure about, and revise once if you changed your mind partway.

After that, give me a table, one row per case, with these columns: (1) the case, (2) verdict under the first definition, (3) verdict under the second definition if you end up with one, (4) a one-line reason for the DNA-related cases. And keep the prose in American English.

Also, my suspicion is that "code" does two jobs—one about rule-governed translation between two alphabets, and one about an intentional sender encoding a message—and that the DNA argument keeps sliding between them. If you agree, just say so straight and build the two definitions around that.
