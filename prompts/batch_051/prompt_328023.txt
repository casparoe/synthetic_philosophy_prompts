Here is an argument meant to show that Gödel's first incompleteness theorem has consequences for the attempt to model human mathematical thought formally. I want to know whether it goes through, and if not, exactly where it fails.

Consider a particular human mathematician, H.

(1) Suppose, for reductio, that there is a formal system F, equipped with a recursive proof-checking procedure, that proves exactly the arithmetical sentences H can in principle recognize as true.

(2) F is consistent and contains Peano arithmetic; otherwise it would not even capture H's minimal arithmetical competence.

(3) By Gödel's first incompleteness theorem, since F is consistent, recursive, and contains Peano arithmetic, there is an arithmetical sentence G that is true under the standard interpretation but not provable in F. Under the standard encoding, G says of itself that it is not provable in F. Moreover, the reasoning behind this step also establishes the metatheorem: if F is consistent, then G is true.

(4) H can recognize that G is true. H derives from step (3) the conditional if F is consistent, then G is true, and H can recognize that F is consistent: after all, F proves exactly the arithmetical truths H accepts, and H can see that the arithmetical truths he accepts are consistent.

(5) Therefore H recognizes an arithmetical truth, G, that F does not prove, contradicting (1).

So no formal system with a recursive proof procedure proves exactly the arithmetical truths H can recognize. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and why? If the argument can be repaired by strengthening one of its premises or adding a further one, identify that repair and explain, with reference to the second incompleteness theorem, whether the repair is compatible with the assumption in step (1).
