I'm serving on an informal advisory committee for someone who wants to launch a griefbot service, and I'd like to think through the mechanism rather than just the ethics in the abstract. The proposal is this. A subscriber, typically a bereaved spouse, parent, or child, uploads the deceased person's texts, emails, voice memos, and social media archives, and the service produces a conversational agent that imitates the person's diction, idioms, humor, and remembered facts about their shared history. The company has built in several safeguards. The agent may not initiate contact; it responds only when the subscriber opens a session. It may not make claims about the present world, give advice in the voice of the deceased, or answer questions about what the deceased would have wanted; when pressed on such matters, it must fall back on a fixed disclaimer identifying itself as a reconstruction. After six months, the subscriber's weekly session allowance declines automatically unless a licensed grief counselor or clinical social worker certifies that continued use is therapeutically warranted, and that certification must be renewed every six months. The service is sold as a monthly subscription, with a discounted lifetime plan paid up front, and the raw training data is deleted if the subscription lapses.

I want a walkthrough of how this institution would actually behave once people adapt to it, rather than a verdict on whether it is tasteful. How do different kinds of users route around the restrictions, by coaxing the agent into giving advice indirectly, by finding counselors who will sign certifications on demand, or by treating a lifetime plan as an estate asset to be inherited or sold? What does the certification requirement do to the relationship between grievers and therapists, and to the market for grief counseling? What happens when the executor, the family, and the subscriber disagree about whether the agent should exist, or when a highly vivid reconstruction makes someone less able to mourn? Where do unregulated clones arise, and what pressure do they put on the safeguarded version? Finally, which of these failure modes can be fixed by adjusting the rules, and which follow unavoidably from the underlying project of simulating the dead?
