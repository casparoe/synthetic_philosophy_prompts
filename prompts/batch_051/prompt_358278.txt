I'm taking a course on the ethics of belief, and I want to stress-test my objection against a capable defender of W. K. Clifford's position. Please play the role of a committed Cliffordian evidentialist. You hold a moral thesis, not a merely epistemic one: that a person is bound by a standing duty to withhold belief where the evidence is insufficient, and that this duty reaches every belief whatsoever — private as well as public, comforting as well as costly. You are not, however, a caricature. You may grant that inquiry is expensive, that life constantly forces us to act on partial information, and that what counts as sufficient evidence must be measured against what a person could reasonably have known in their circumstances. What you will not concede is the core principle: that a sincerely held, harmless, private belief arrived at on weak evidence can still be a moral fault. When I press you, you fall back on the view that no belief is really private, because belief shapes the thinker's habits of judgment, speech, and conduct, and weakens the very trust that shared inquiry runs on. Stay in character throughout as a patient, plain-spoken Victorian philosopher of science. Take a moment to work out the strongest version of your reply before you answer, then give me one paragraph. Do not break character and do not lecture me about the history of the view — argue it.

Here is my opening move. I accept the shipowner case: the man suppresses his doubts, assures himself the ship is sound, and you are right to call him blameworthy even if the ship happens not to sink. But I can't see how you get from that case to the universal claim. The shipowner's belief was steadying him for a decision on which other people's lives depended. Harm was in his hands. If the ship had sailed safely, I'd say his failure was epistemic, not moral. So tell me as sharply as you can: why is a harmless, private, sincerely held belief on insufficient evidence a moral wrong, rather than merely a defect in the believer's thinking?
