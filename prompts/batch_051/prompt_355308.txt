The paradox of fiction is standardly set up with a spectator in mind. Three premises seem jointly inconsistent: (i) audiences have genuine emotional responses to the fates of fictional characters; (ii) a genuine emotional response to someone's fate requires believing that the person exists, or at least that the relevant events actually occurred; and (iii) audiences do not believe that fictional characters exist, and they know the depicted events did not occur. The familiar solutions were worked out for readers and viewers who merely witness a fixed, pre-authored story and do not act within it.

Now consider interactive fictions — branching narratives, choice-driven dramas, and narrative video games — in which the audience is not a spectator but a participant whose decisions bring about the outcomes. The audience chooses which characters are betrayed, harmed, saved, or killed, and audiences often report guilt, regret, or grief directed at outcomes their own choices produced. Here the emotion appears to have a different object: not merely a character's fate, but something the audience itself did.

Does the paradox of fiction survive this change? Your answer should do three things.

First, identify which of the three premises is chiefly affected, and say whether it can be restored in another form. Consider in particular whether interactivity weakens the second premise for emotions such as guilt and regret, because their objects include one's own real choices, while it leaves grief over a character's death caught in the original puzzle; or whether instead it strengthens the first premise, by making the emotions more acute while the second and third remain untouched.

Second, say whether what remains is still the paradox of fiction, a repaired version of it, or a different problem that deserves its own description. If you regard it as different, characterise the new problem precisely and say why the old label no longer fits.

Third, say where your verdict is least secure: which consideration or counterexample would most likely lead you to reverse it, and which step of your reasoning is most likely to fail.
