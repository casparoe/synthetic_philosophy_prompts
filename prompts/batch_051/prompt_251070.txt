You are a philosopher of epistemology who writes scrupulously fair, rigorous dialogues and is known for steelmanning positions you do not yourself hold. Before writing, take a few moments to think through the strongest version of each position below and the most damaging objection each must answer. Then write a philosophical dialogue of roughly 1,000 words between two equally competent epistemologists. The dialogue should be a genuine back-and-forth: each character should respond to the other's specific claims, challenge the other's formulations, and revise or sharpen their own view at least once. Neither may be a strawman, and they should not talk past one another. Do not cite or footnote the literature; the characters must argue directly from the case and from first principles.

The two positions:

Alex is an epistemic contextualist about the word "knows." Alex holds that "knows" is context-sensitive in something like the way "tall" or "flat" is: the epistemic standards for correctly applying the word shift with the conversational context of the person making the knowledge ascription. Thus one and the same sentence, "Maya knows that p," can be true when uttered in a low-stakes context and false when uttered in a high-stakes context, even with Maya, her evidence, and her situation held fixed.

Robin is a subject-sensitive invariantist, a defender of pragmatic encroachment. Robin holds that the truth conditions of "knows" do not shift with the attributor's context; whether a subject knows that p is an invariant matter. But, Robin insists, whether the subject knows can depend on the subject's own practical situation: whether her evidence is good enough, given what is at stake for her, for her rationally to act on p. High stakes for the subject can make the difference between knowing and not knowing; high stakes for a spectator who is merely evaluating the subject cannot.

The dialogue should center on this case, which the characters should use to expose the exact point at which their views come apart:

Maya is a paramedic who has just performed a rapid but reliable field assessment and told the patient's spouse that the patient is having a myocardial infarction. Maya's own stakes are low: she is about to transfer care to the hospital team, and an error would cost her little. The spouse's stakes are extremely high: the spouse must decide immediately whether to consent to an invasive procedure with serious risks, where a false positive would be catastrophic and a false negative would be lethal. The spouse turns to a bystander and asks, "Does Maya know that the patient is having a heart attack?"

The dialogue should, at minimum, force the following points into open confrontation. Robin should press Alex on the oddity of Maya's literally knowing when assessed from one conversational context and literally not knowing when assessed from another, although nothing about Maya, her evidence, or her duties has changed; Robin should argue that this makes knowledge look idle for the knower's own practical reasoning, because whether Maya may appropriately act or assert is then not settled by the fact that she knows. Alex should press Robin on why, on Robin's view, the spouse's assessment of Maya — "Maya doesn't know" — comes out simply false, even though it seems a perfectly appropriate thing for the spouse to say in that situation. Alex should charge that invariantism therefore has to reinterpret away ordinary high-stakes knowledge denials about people like Maya, while contextualism can take those denials at face value. The two should also argue over which account better explains the connection between knowledge and assertion, and over which account does less violence to our ordinary practice of attributing and denying knowledge. They may end in sharp disagreement, but the disagreement should be precise.
