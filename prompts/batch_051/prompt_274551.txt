I have been arguing with a friend who defends karma as a fully naturalistic theory of moral desert, and he keeps returning to one analogy. On his account, karma is not a cosmic judge or a mechanism of punishment; it is a law of nature in the moral domain, much as gravity is a law of nature in physics. Just as gravity requires no enforcer and no intention to pull a stone downward, karma needs no god, no administrator, and no supernatural bookkeeper to connect an action with its fitting result: the proportionality between what one does and what one undergoes is, he says, built into the structure of things. From this he concludes that one can reject all supernatural machinery and still hold that people eventually get the suffering or flourishing they deserve.

I am uneasy with this, but I cannot tell how much of his conclusion is coming from the analogy rather than from the doctrine itself. I would like you to audit the analogy properly. First, give a correspondence table: what in the karmic picture is supposed to play the role of the force, the mass, the law of nature, the conserved quantity, and the absence of an enforcing agent — and say explicitly which of these roles have no natural counterpart. Then identify where the mapping breaks. I am especially interested in whether moral properties such as intention, wrongdoing, and desert can do the same kind of causal work that physical magnitudes do; whether the delay across lifetimes introduces a bookkeeping problem that gravity does not face; and whether a blind law can track desert without that tracking collapsing into mere coincidence. Finally, tell me whether his conclusion — that karma can function as a naturalistic theory of moral desert — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and offer a better analogy if you think there is one. Please write your answer in American English.
