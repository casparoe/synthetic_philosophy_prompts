I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am in the middle of drafting a long-form piece about why the concept of qualia has survived decades of philosophical criticism. The section I am least confident about deals with Dennett's strategy of quining qualia — his attempt to show that the concept is an unstable posit rather than a name for something we observe directly — and specifically with his paired coffee-taster cases, Chase and Sanborn. My working notes for that section are below, and I would be grateful for critical comments before I revise them.

The cases run as follows. Each of two practised tasters reports that something about his experience of the same coffee has altered. Chase maintains that the coffee tastes exactly as it has always tasted, but that he no longer likes that taste. Sanborn maintains that he likes the taste as much as he ever did, but that the coffee no longer tastes the way it used to. Dennett's diagnostic question is whose qualia changed and whose mere preferences changed. His point, as I read him, is that introspection cannot force an answer: both reports are in good order, and either report can be redescribed in the other's terms by moving the boundary between what is brutely present in experience and what is one's response to it. If the notion of qualia cannot settle a difference that should, on its own terms, be obvious from the first-person point of view, qualia start to look less like introspectable items and more like an idle boundary drawn after the fact.

Here is my difficulty. I want to press a stronger conclusion than mere underdetermination: that the very idea of a quale as something that can remain identical while one's attitudes towards it change, or change while one's attitudes remain identical, requires an identity condition that introspection cannot supply and that nothing else provides. But I can already hear the realist's objection: underdetermination is not incoherence, since we are underdetermined about many perfectly real things, from memory traces to other minds. I therefore worry that the section will read merely as the weak claim that introspective reports do not decide the matter. I would welcome comments on three points: first, whether the stronger reading is genuinely available and, if so, how to state it without overreaching; second, whether I have put the realist's best reply fairly, or whether a careful realist would frame the issue differently; and third, which parts of the argument would most likely fail for a reader who finds qualia obvious and has never encountered Dennett, and how those parts might be shored up. Point-by-point comments would be more useful than a rewritten section.
