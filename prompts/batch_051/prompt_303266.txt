Before I read any more theories about what "left" and "right" actually pick out in politics, I want the desiderata rather than a theory: the list of jobs an adequate theory of the left/right distinction has to do, with the case or example behind each job, and a call on which jobs are negotiable and which aren't. I mean a theory good enough for understanding political alignment — predicting coalitions, explaining realignments, making sense of cross-national variation — not just a dictionary definition.

Some jobs I think belong on the list (push back if any of these is a prejudice rather than a genuine requirement):

1. The inferential-ticket job. If you tell me someone is left-wing, I can predict better than chance how they'll lean on inheritance tax, policing, immigration, climate — and vice versa, with the content flipped. None of those follows logically from the label. A theory has to explain what makes the label carry that predictive information: either something real holds the bundle together, or the prediction is a trick that needs debunking. Either way the theory owes us that account.

2. The crossover cases. The anti-immigration leftist, the pro-single-payer right-winger, the economically radical and socially conservative Catholic. A theory that just says "those people are confused" is dodging unless it can say exactly what principle they're confused about, and why the label still stings from both sides.

3. The seat-history problem. The terms come from people sitting to the left and right of the king in the French assembly, which is a seating accident. But the content attached to each side has drifted so far since then that the words should have died, and instead they keep getting re-used across countries and generations. So the theory can't be pure essentialism — the distinction is not just written in nature — and it can't be pure nominalism either, because the accident has stayed useful in a way accidents normally dont.

4. The not-just-any-axis problem. If left/right were only team jerseys, any labels should work as well — up/down, mountain/coast. They don't. So a theory should say why this particular axis got the job and keeps it, and what a fake axis would do differently.

5. The one-axis-or-two problem. The standard move is to split left/right into an economic axis (equality vs hierarchy) and a social axis (openness vs tradition). But then the main thing left unexplained is why those two axes have correlated as hard as they do in most countries — and why the correlation now seems to be weakening in rich countries, producing people who are economically left and culturally conservative, or the reverse. If you dissolve the one axis into two, you inherit the correlation as a fresh anomaly.

6. The realignment problem. Positions migrate across the line without anyone deciding the words stopped making sense. Free trade was a progressive and liberal reform cause in the nineteenth century, and in a lot of the rich world today it reads as right- or establishment-coded, with the left split between defending trade deals for growth and attacking them for labor, and the right likewise split. A theory has to say what, if anything, stayed constant across that migration.

The part I actually care about: I suspect some of these jobs cannot be jointly satisfied. If one deep principle is doing the work (jobs 1, 4, 5), then realignment and seat-history (3, 6) look like failures of the principle. If it's mostly historical coalition-building and self-labeling, then the predictive punch in job 1 looks like luck. So (a) which pairs conflict, (b) which requirement does the most to exclude candidate theories, and (c) which one would you relax first if you had to. Then, briefly — two or three sentences each, no more — say how the main families of theory fare against your list: a single-principle equality-vs-hierarchy view, two-axis models, coalitional or self-labeling accounts, and the "there's no real distinction" eliminativist view. Keep the scoring short; the list is the point.
