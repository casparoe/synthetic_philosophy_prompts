Question (from the exam): "Distinguish conciliationism from steadfastness in the epistemology of peer disagreement, explain the role of the independence principle, and give one objection to conciliationism."

Student answer: "Conciliationism is the view that when you learn that an epistemic peer disagrees with you about p, you should revise your belief in p, at least somewhat. The best-developed version is Elga's Equal Weight View, which says you should give your own opinion and your peer's opinion equal weight; so if you are at .8 and she is at .2, you should move to .5. This is supported by the independence principle, which says that when you are deciding how much weight to give the peer's disagreement, you should bracket the reasoning that is itself under dispute, since otherwise you'd be assuming the very thing at issue. Conciliationism is basically the uniqueness thesis applied to social evidence: a body of evidence can support only one rational attitude toward a proposition, so two peers with the same evidence cannot both be rational, and each should split the difference. Steadfastness says discovering that a peer disagrees does not by itself require you to revise, because your own first-order evidence is what matters and the mere fact that someone else sees things differently is not itself evidence about p. Kelly's total evidence view is the main steadfast position, but it also accepts the independence principle because it says you should set aside your own disputed reasoning when deciding how much to trust the peer. The strongest objection to conciliationism is that it overgenerates: if a flat-earther seems just as smart and well-informed as you, conciliationism says you must suspend judgment about the shape of the earth, which is absurd."
