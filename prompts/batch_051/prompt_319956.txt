I sit on the amnesty committee of the truth and reconciliation commission set up after our country's transition out of a decades-long dirty conflict. We vote Thursday and I need to circulate a short memo beforhand. The applicant was a junior officer in the old security forces. In 1988 he supervised a squad that abducted a student activist who was never seen again. He's now come forward, and by our records his account checks out: who was in the squad, who gave the order, where the body was buried. He's the only reason the family finally has a grave to visit. He says the abduction was part of a counterinsurgency operation and the student was on a watchlist for distributing opposition pamphlets. The family's lawyer says the student's name does not appear on any surviving list, that he'd withdrawn from politics months earlier, and that the real reason he was taken is that he was dating the applicant's sister. If that's right, the killing was personal, and our statute only permits amnesty for acts associated with a political objective, on full disclosure, and where the act wasn't grossly disproportionate. The committee is genuinely split: some say full disclosure is exactly what the commission exists to get, and we should grant; others say granting here would gut the political-objective test. Draft the memo I'd circulate as my recommendation: a clear bottom line, the reasons a divided committee could act on, and an honest treatment of the strongest argument on the side you reject. Don't punt by default—if a conditional grant is right, defend it.
