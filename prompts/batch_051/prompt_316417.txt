I am trying to figure out what I actually think about the value of effort — in particular, whether struggle has value in itself, or only because of what it produces, reveals, or does to the person who undergoes it. Here are my gut verdicts on a set of cases.

Two climbers take the same route to the same summit. Zara is a professional mountaineer and strolls it in a relaxed afternoon. Matt, a year into recovering from a serious injury, takes twelve painful hours. I want to say Matt's is the greater achievement, and that the struggle is not an irrelevant add-on but part of why I value it. Yet in mathematics I have the opposite reaction. Jonas spends three months wandering through dead ends and produces a baroque, forty-page proof of a theorem; Marta sees the structure in an afternoon and writes a three-page proof of the same theorem. I judge Marta's proof simply better as mathematics. The months of dead ends neither improve nor degrade the result, even though I do admire Jonas's persistence more.

Some cases push me further against the intrinsic value of effort. An engineer spends a decade designing a bridge that collapses because of a basic error he could have caught in the first month; the ten years of struggle do not, in my judgment, redeem the project or add anything of value to it. A person carries heavy stones up a hill and back down each day for no purpose, with no audience, no product, and no training effect; I regard this as pure waste, not as an activity made valuable by the effort it takes. If Nina paints an excellent painting in an afternoon and Omar produces a comparably excellent painting through three years of labor, I judge the paintings equal in value as paintings, even though Omar's persistence is the more admirable trait.

But other verdicts pull the other way. Priya finds honesty easy; Dan is a habitual exaggerator who must catch himself constantly, and both end up equally honest. I think Dan's honesty reflects better on him than Priya's does, and I doubt this is only an instrumental judgment about future reliability. And when a hiker takes the steep ridge instead of the gondola for no benefit anyone else will know about, simply to have done it the hard way, I cannot bring myself to say the effort adds nothing.

I am tempted by a single, clean principle: effort never has value in itself; its value is entirely a matter of what it produces, what it teaches, and how it changes the agent — and my climber and moral-credit verdicts are the ones I should probably abandon. But I want that principle tested against the full set before I sign on. Please give me a committed answer. State the best principle you can find that captures these verdicts, formulated precisely, with the case or clause that handles each one; say plainly which of my verdicts, if any, it forces me to give up and why; and then apply the principle to two further cases: a person who spends a lifetime failing at something that never comes to anything, and a school that makes students work through problems the hard way when quicker methods are already known.
