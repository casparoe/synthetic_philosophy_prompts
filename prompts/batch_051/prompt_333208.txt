I am a retired forest ecologist. For most of my career I worked with observational data in which the aggregate pattern and the pattern within each stratum could point in opposite directions, and since retiring I have been drawn into philosophy of science. I now lead a small seminar on inference for advanced undergraduates and am grading a take-home exercise on Simpson's paradox. I would like your help calibrating my grading.

Please act as a careful examiner. Assign a mark out of 20, and then give point-by-point comments that justify the mark. The comments should be tied to the student's specific claims: they should credit what is right and explain precisely what, if anything, is subtly wrong, overstated, or does not follow. A general impression will not help me, and I have deliberately not indicated my own view of the answer because I want an independent assessment.

Question:

An observational study compared two forest-management regimes, A and B, across a landscape divided into plots. At the outset, each plot was classified as either high-stress or low-stress. Ten years later, each plot was recorded as a success or a failure against a fixed regeneration threshold. The table gives the results.

| Regime | Site class | Plots | Successes | Success rate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | high-stress | 100 | 25 | 25.0% |
| A | low-stress | 500 | 400 | 80.0% |
| A | all plots | 600 | 425 | 70.8% |
| B | high-stress | 400 | 120 | 30.0% |
| B | low-stress | 100 | 85 | 85.0% |
| B | all plots | 500 | 205 | 41.0% |

(a) Compare the two regimes overall and within each site class.

(b) The pattern in the table is an instance of a well-known statistical phenomenon. Name it and explain, in your own words, why it occurs in this table.

(c) A member of the research team argues that regime A is the more successful regime because its overall success rate is higher. Another member argues that regime B is more successful because it performs better in both site classes. Which conclusion, if either, follows from the table alone? What further information would be needed before recommending one regime over the other, and what is being asked when one regime is called more successful than the other?

Student answer:

The table displays a reversal. Regime A wins overall, with 425/600 ≈ 70.8% against regime B's 205/500 = 41.0%, but regime B wins within both site classes: 30.0% versus 25.0% in the high-stress class, and 85.0% versus 80.0% in the low-stress class. This is Simpson's paradox. It is produced here by the unequal distribution of site classes between the two regimes. A's results are dominated by low-stress plots, where regeneration succeeds often, while B's results are dominated by high-stress plots, where it succeeds rarely; the overall percentages therefore weight very different mixtures of easy and hard plots. Because the two overall percentages blend a historic mixture in with the comparison of interest, the aggregate difference is a spurious artifact of the unbalanced design and should be set aside. The stratified comparisons hold site stress fixed and are the fine-grained comparisons; for that reason they are the correct measure of each regime's effectiveness. B is therefore the genuinely more effective regime, and the colleague who looked within the site classes, not the one who looked only at the totals, has the right conclusion. Before making a corresponding recommendation, the only additional fact that needs to be confirmed is that site stress was measured before the regime was applied, so that it cannot be an effect of the regime. Once that is established, the stratified comparison can be acted on.

Please keep in mind that this is a philosophy of science seminar, so conceptual precision matters at least as much as arithmetic.
