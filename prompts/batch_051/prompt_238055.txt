I'm a retired control-systems engineer and I've been reading around the free will stuff. Frankfurt-style cases keep bothering me. Here's my own version. A pilot initiates a bombing run on her own initiative; her plane, unkown to her, has a backup override that would have forced the same run if she had hesitated at the decision point. The override never fires. My engineering self says the override is causally inert in the actual run—just an unused if-then capability, like a safety interlock—so of course shes responsible. But another part of me says she couldn't have done otherwise, and if she couldn't, blame seems drained of its normal meaning. It gets worse: if she'd known the override was there, would it still be a Frankfurt case, or would the knowledge change what's fair to say about her? I'm not asking for a verdict. I want to know what it costs to give up the principle of alternative possibilities, what it costs to keep it by insisting there's always some little alternative left, and whether 'could have done otherwise' is ambiguous between the whole system (with its override) and the pilot's own decision loop. If it is ambiguous, is that a real reconciliation or a pun?
