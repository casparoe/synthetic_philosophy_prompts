I'm a philosophy professor who works on anthropic reasoning, and this is where I stall. The self-sampling assumption is the only view I know that gives me a workable rule for indexical uncertainty: conditional on a reference class, reason as if you were randomly sampled from the observers in it. It's simple, unified, and actually fixes the probabilities instead of leaving them up to vibes. But it seems to drag the Doomsday argument along with it. If vastly more humans are coming than have lived so far, then under SSA I should be shocked to find myself born this early, and update toward a shorter future for the species. My intuition isn't just that the conclusion is grim; it's that a rule for locating myself among observers shouldn't reach out and settle something this substantive about the future of my species at all. Yet the obvious alternative, self-indication, has its own embarrassment: it makes a theory more probable merely because it posits more observers, which is how you get the presumptuous philosopher concluding there are zillions of unobserved observers for no empirical reason.

So I don't want a side. I want to know what it costs to keep SSA and debunk the intuition as an indexical artifact, what it costs to amend the theory (self-indication, no reference class, something else), and whether there's a reading on which SSA doesn't actually commit me to the Doomsday conclusion — maybe the reference class is doing the hidden work, or 'randomly sampled from my reference class' is being asked to do more than it can. What exactly would have to give for each move?
