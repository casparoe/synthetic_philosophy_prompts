My stats study group walked into an argument last week that seems to prove optional stopping is completely fine, and by extension that the replication crisis is kind of overblown. None of us can find the exit, and we've been going around in circles. Here's the chain we keep reconstructing:

(1) The likelihood principle says the evidence in an experiment depends only on the likelihood function of the data you actually observed—two experiments with proportional likelihood functions carry the same information about the parameter, so the stopping rule is irrelevant to what the data say.

(2) Bayesian updating satisfies the likelihood principle: the posterior is proportional to the prior times the likelihood of the observed data, and the stopping probabilities never appear.

(3) Optional stopping—peek at the data and stop once p drops below .05—changes the p-value and the long-run false positive rate, but it doesn't change the likelihood function of the data you actually ended up with.

(4) So from (1) and (2), optional stopping doesn't change the evidence, and a Bayesian needn't care whether you stopped because the sample size was fixed in advance or because you peeked.

(5) But if stopping rules don't matter for evidence, then the whole preregister-your-stopping-rule-and-fix-your-sample-size apparatus is pointless, and a p-hacked result from sampling until significance is no worse evidenced than one from a clean fixed design.

(6) And by the same reasoning, the replication crisis should be massively overstated: all those p-hacked findings are, on this view, just as good as the clean ones.

(7) Yet the replication crisis clearly is a crisis. Routinely p-hacked findings fail to replicate at rates the clean ones don't.

(8) So either the likelihood principle is false, or Bayes' rule is the wrong way to think about evidence, or our sense of the replication crisis is confused.

Where does the argument break, if it does? If the steps are valid, tell me which premise is least costly to give up, and what giving it up costs for the usual positions in the stats wars—a strict frequentist, a Bayesian, and a likelihoodist if you think that's a distinct third camp. I'd like to keep (2) because it looks like a theorem, and (7) because the replication data are the replication data, but I need to know what keeping both costs.
