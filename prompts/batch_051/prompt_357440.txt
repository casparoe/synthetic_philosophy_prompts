I am running a forty-five-minute session of an interdisciplinary philosophy of technology reading group – philosophers, engineers, and policy people, none of them new to the field – on the question whether artefacts can themselves have politics. I would like you to build a decision tree that sorts the positions in the debate, constructed from the logical space rather than from a list of familiar labels, and usable by the participants to locate their own views.

The angle I want the tree to take is this. The ordinary phrasing of the question seems to stitch together two claims that are usually run together and should be separated: (i) the determinism claim, that technologies develop according to an internal logic and substantially fix social and political arrangements; and (ii) the politics claim, that a technical arrangement can itself embody or enforce a political relationship before, or independently of, any particular use or intention. Build the tree so that the four combinations of yes and no on these two are visible, rather than forcing the positions onto a single spectrum from tools-are-neutral to technology-rules-us. Add further splits only where the tree genuinely needs them – for instance, a split between intended and unintended political content, or between politics located in the material form and politics located in the surrounding system. Any added split should be tested by at least one case in which the two candidate answers pull apart.

Each internal node should be a question a participant can answer without already knowing which position it routes to, and each should be anchored in a concrete case. Good candidate cases include: an underpass built too low for a bus to reach a public beach; a speed bump; a production line that can run only under a strict chain of command; and a file-sharing protocol that behaves identically under liberal and authoritarian governments. Rotate the cases so that no single one does all the classificatory work. For each split, give one sentence on why it earns its place – which positions it separates and what the tree would lose without it.

The tree should have between eight and twelve terminal nodes. These positions must appear somewhere, under plain descriptive labels: the neutral-tool or instrumentalist view; social constructivism about technology; the view that some technical systems are inherently political in the sense that they require, or strongly favour, a particular pattern of authority; and a position in the family of technological momentum or soft determinism, on which technological change has its own direction but the politics lies in the emergent properties of the whole system rather than in any single artefact. Add further leaves – for instance a self-enforcing-design view, a hard autonomous-technology view, or an eliminativist view that power cannot reside in artefacts at all – only if the tree's own questions demand them.

For each terminal node, give: a short name, or the word unnamed if the position is a real combination that nobody actually defends; one sentence saying what the position commits a person to; and one sentence on the position's most awkward commitment, the implication its defenders are least comfortable owning. Where a combination of answers is unoccupied, say explicitly whether it is empty because it is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or merely because nobody has got round to defending it. Flag any two positions the tree cannot really separate; where a further concrete case would separate them, give it, and where none would, say so.

Do not include a reading list, bibliography, or citations, and do not rest anything on specialist recall of proper names; where a label carries content, use it, but otherwise prefer descriptive names.

One final request. If you think the two axes I have asked you to keep separate actually collapse into one – that determinism and artefact-politics cannot be prised apart in the way I have assumed – or that a decision tree is the wrong instrument for this material, tell me so plainly and build the nearest instrument that actually fits, rather than forcing the material into a tree that distorts it.
