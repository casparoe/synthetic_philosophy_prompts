I direct the annual violence report for a small nongovernmental organization that tracks conflict and political violence worldwide. Each spring we publish a trend line—violence up, down, or flat—and a ranking of the most affected countries, and each year our analysts fight about what belongs in the count: battle deaths in international and civil wars, homicides, state killings of unarmed protesters, deaths from sanctions, starvation, or disease plausibly attributable to a conflict, violence against migrants, nonfatal sexual violence, threats, and property destruction. They also fight about whether to report absolute numbers or per-capita rates, and about whether a claim such as that violence has declined since the 1990s is something our data can settle at all. Write a reusable decision procedure that our analysts, who have no philosophy background, can follow to decide (a) whether an incident counts as violence for our report and (b) how to present the trend, including when the honest answer is that there is no single fact of the matter and we should say so. The procedure should be informed by the best available conceptual thinking about definition and measurement but written as an internal working document, not a literature review; it must be auditable by our board and usable by junior analysts on a one-week coding deadline, and it must include a small set of test incidents with a worked classification for each. Include at least one case where the procedure produces an answer our board or a funder will not like, and flag explicitly the points where the procedure cannot eliminate judgment.
