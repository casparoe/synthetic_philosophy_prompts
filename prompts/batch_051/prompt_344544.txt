I'm teaching the surprise exam paradox in an epistemology seminar next week, and I want to spend the most time on the diagnoses that do real explanatory work rather than just making the puzzle disappear by redefining "surprise." Rank the following five candidate diagnoses by that standard—how much of the original phenomenon they explain, including why the student is still genuinely surprised when the exam comes on Wednesday, rather then how neatly they dissolve the contradiction—and give one sentence for each saying what it handles and what it leaves hanging. Use a tier list (S/A/B/C) if you think some are roughly tied. The five: (1) the announcement is true but unknowable, so the student's backwards induction only shows he can't know it, not that it won't happen; (2) the announcement is strictly unfulfillable—you can't announce in advance an exam that is in principle unpredictable; (3) "surprise" here is psychological or indexical, so there's no epistemic contradiction, just an overconfident proof; (4) the induction breaks because knowledge fails the KK principle, so the student can know there'll be an exam without knowing that he knows it; (5) the rational move is decision-theoretic: the student shouldn't be certain enough of the teacher's announcement for the induction to get going at all.
