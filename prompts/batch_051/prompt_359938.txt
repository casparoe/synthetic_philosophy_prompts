For our unit on the Epicurean argument that death is not bad for the one who dies, I have been working with three criteria for when an event is bad for a particular person. First, temporal location: the event must be bad for the person at some time. Second, the subject condition: at that time there must exist a subject for whom it is bad; the badness must attach to the person, not merely to other people, to a reputation, or to the state of the world. Third, the difference condition: the event must make the person's life as a whole go worse than it would have gone otherwise; I intend this to include the fate of what the person spent her life making, not only the sequence of her experiences. The three criteria seem independently plausible, but I suspect they cannot all be kept.

Consider the posthumous manuscript. A composer completes the one major work of her career and dies that night. A week later, the only score is destroyed in a fire. The fire will leave her life's work without the reception it would otherwise have had, but at the moment of the fire and afterward there is no composer who exists to suffer the loss.

Work through the three criteria one at a time, and say exactly where each underdetermines the verdict or begs the question against the others. Then give me your committed answer, not a survey of options: on balance, is the destruction of the manuscript bad for the composer? Use that verdict to take a definite position on the Epicurean claim that death is not bad for the one who dies — specifically, identify which of my three criteria should be revised or abandoned, and defend that choice. Do not end by saying it is a matter of interpretation.
