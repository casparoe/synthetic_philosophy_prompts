So my book club read Montaigne's essay "Of Cannibals," and two people got into a long argument about it that I wanna get sorted out. They both know the essay pretty well, and neither is a scholar, but they came armed. I'll reconstruct it as close as I can.

Priya said the essay is, at bottom, a piece of cultural relativism. Her claim was that the whole structure of it is: you think the Brazilian Tupinambá are barbarous for roasting and eating their dead enemies, but that's only because you're using your own customs as the standard. Montaigne opens by saying we call barbarous whatever falls outside our own practices, and that custom is the only thing we ever really appeal to when we call something true or reasonable. Then he describes the Tupinambá's warfare, their prisoners, their songs, their polygamy, and shows that from inside their way of life these practices have a logic: the prisoner is fed and housed for months and given a chance to speak back; the eating isn't for nourishment, it's a kind of grim ritual of revenge and courage. So there isn't some neutral vantage point from which we can say their way is worse. At most he's showing that our horror is parochial.

Dan pushed back. He said no, Montaigne's actual argument is pointed and comparative, not equalizing. The essay doesn't say "both practices are equally fine." It says the Europeans are more barbarous, and it gives a specific reason: the Tupinambá kill a man in war and eat him after he's dead, whereas Europeans roast a man alive, stretch him on the rack, let dogs and pigs tear at his still-feeling body — and do it in the middle of their religious civil wars, supposedly in the name of God. That's a ranking, Dan said, and the ranking rests on a criterion Montaigne treats as universal: cruelty is measured by what you do to a living, feeling person, and by whether the act serves something beyond pure sadism. By that standard the cannibals come off better, not because the standard is "theirs," but because the Europeans fail even their own professed standard of humanity.

Priya came back: sure, he ranks them, but that doesn't make him a universalist. He's doing what a satirist does — borrowing the audience's own categories (piety, humanity, barbarism) and showing they generate an embarrassing verdict. But the deeper skepticism of the essay is that any such verdict is an artifact of a custom too. The fact that he picks "suffering inflicted on the living" as the measure is itself a choice, not a discovery. There are ways of ranking these acts where the Tupinambá come off much worse — terrorizing prisoners for months before killing them, treating the eating of a human body as fine in itself, and so on. Montaigne doesn't prove his criterion; he just switches which local lens he's looking through. And his lens, Priya said, is this idealized "nature" — he keeps calling the Tupinambá natural, unspoiled, close to the source — while describing a people with wars, rank, prophets, and elaborate ritual. That's not nature, it's a pastoral fantasy, which is just another custom wearing a different mask.

Dan answered that the living/dead distinction isn't a random local preference, and calling the Tupinambá "natural" is Montaigne's way of saying they haven't added a layer of cruelty that their own goals don't require — not that they have no culture. He pointed to the bit near the end where three Tupinambá visit France and are astounded that thousands of armed men obey a child king, and that the poor don't just kill the rich or burn their houses. The point of that anecdote, Dan said, is that the Tupinambá are judging the French by the very sort of rough natural criterion — why would the strong submit to a child, why would the starving tolerate the full? — that Montaigne endorses. So Montaigne is not just flipping local lenses; he's advancing a substantive standard, something like: force that isn't noble or self-imposed, and suffering that isn't necessary, are worse than acts that at least have valor and function inside a way of life.

Priya said that last bit gives the game away: "inside a way of life." If the valor and function of cannibalism can be certified only from inside the Tupinambá way of life, then the standard itself is internal to a way of life, and we're back to relativism with extra steps. Also, she added, Montaigne's skeptical opening — custom is the only criterion of truth and reason — applies to his own essay too. If he's right about that, then his appeal to "nature" is just another custom, and the whole comparative ranking is undercut by his own premise. If he's wrong about that, the relativist reading of the essay collapses for a different reason, but so does half the essay.

There was a bit more back and forth after that, mostly about whether Montaigne actually endorses the Tupinambá's judgments of France or is just using them to embarrass the French the way he used the French to lecture the Tupinambá, but I think I got the main thread.

I genuinely can't tell who got the better of it. I'm less interested in who wins than in which specific moves hold up.
