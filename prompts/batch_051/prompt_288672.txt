Consider the following argument, which aims to show that a person can acquire an empirically justified belief that their colour vision is reliable without checking it against any independent source.

(1) On many separated occasions, in apparently normal lighting, I look at an ordinary object, have a clear visual impression that it is red, and on that basis believe that it is red.
(2) In a large and varied sample of those occasions, the object is in fact red, just as I believed.
(3) So, in that sample, my colour vision produced true beliefs about the colours of the objects before me.
(4) From that record I may infer inductively that my colour vision is generally reliable, at least for judging such colours in such conditions.
(5) The only evidence for the claims collected in (2) came from my colour vision itself; I had no independent check on any object's colour.
(6) Therefore, I now possess an empirically justified belief that my colour vision is reliable, and it rests entirely on evidence produced by the very faculty whose reliability is at issue.

I want to know whether this reasoning succeeds. Treat the induction in (4) as a normally acceptable non-deductive inference, and concentrate on whether the argument is nevertheless invalid or otherwise epistemically defective, and on whether its conclusion can be justified given the circularity recorded in (5). If the argument fails, say which step is the first to fail and whether the failure is a false premise, an invalid or otherwise illegitimate inference, or a vicious epistemic circularity; if it succeeds, say why the circularity is not vicious. Please also state how confident you are in your assessment, single out the point you are least sure of, and write your answer in British English.
