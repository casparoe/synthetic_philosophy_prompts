I've been trying to sort out what I actually think about people who don't want to live longer, and I've hit a point where my stated view doesn't match my gut. I'm not a philosopher or an academic or anything, just a person who reads the news, so please answer in plain American English.

My stated view, the one I'd give at a party, is this: healthy years are good, and if someone can get more of them safely, that's a good thing. Dying isn't sacred. So wanting to live a lot longer is sane, and being against life extension is just fear, or sour grapes, or religion in disguise.

But then I actually line up specific people and my gut goes different ways.

1. My 82-year-old neighbor gets offered a new treatment that could plausibly give him another ten or fifteen healthy years, and he turns it down. He says he's had a good run and that at some point you're supposed to step aside. I don't think he's crazy or self-destructive. Honestly, I think he's kind of graceful, and I'd respect it completely.

2. A woman I know won't let her 12-year-old get a treatment that would add decades to the kid's life, because she says death is natural and we shouldn't fight it. I think thats not just wrong, it's close to evil. You don't get to make that call for your kid.

3. A friend in his forties refuses a safe anti-aging treatment because he says his life should be a complete thing, not something he drags out past the point where it makes sense. I think he's making a mistake. I'd argue with him hard, even though I wouldn't force him. His reason feels different from my neighbor's, but I can't put my finger on how.

4. Another friend wants to live to two hundred, and people call him selfish for hogging resources and not making room for younger people. I think those people are being jerks. Wanting to keep living isn't selfish in the way they mean; he's not stealing anything from them.

5. My town votes down funding for a cheap longevity clinic, and the main argument is that death gives life meaning and we shouldn't stretch life too far. I think that vote is outrageous. People who feel that way can die on time themselves, but they don't get to vote to make my life shorter.

6. Then I picture myself at 68, healthy, being handed a pill I'd have to take every morning to live to a hundred, and my first reaction is a wave of revulsion: it feels unnatural, like I'd be turning myself into a machine. That's the same reflex I hate in other people. I don't know whether to treat that feeling as information about what's actually good for me, or as a bug.

So the puzzle is this. My stated view says more healthy life is good, period, and opposition to it is just a bias. But cases 1 and 6 pull the other way, and case 3 makes me think there's a real difference between kinds of reasons for saying no that I can't quite state. Is there a principle that captures all six verdicts, or close? Test it against each case, tell me which verdicts I should drop if it can't get all of them, and say whether my revulsion in case 6 should count as a reason or just a feeling to overrule. Then apply the principle to a case I haven't listed: a healthy 55-year-old who refuses a one-time treatment that would add thirty good years because she's afraid she'd get bored. Does your principle say I should respect that refusal, and do I have to accept its answer?

Also, please write your answer in American English.
