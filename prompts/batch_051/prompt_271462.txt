I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I'm working on a piece about how much you should trust your own head when you get evidence that your head is currently off. The jargon is higher-order evidence: evidence about how reliable your first-order reasoning is. All the standard examples run one direction — someone is sleep-deprived, or distracted, or a peer says they misread the data, and the moral is dial down your confidence. I want the opposite case. Build me a scenario where an ordinary person gets genuine higher-order evidence that her reasoning about some question was compromised, and yet the rational response is to keep her first-order conclusion anyway — not out of stubborness, but because of how the case is set up. The usual line is that you shouldn't be epistemically akratic: you shouldn't think 'P, but my reasoning to P was lousy.' I want a case where the rational person ends up looking akratic in just that way. Constraints: realistic (a nurse reading a chart, an insurance adjuster, a mechanic — nothing exotic), one paragraph, and no cheating: don't make the first-order evidence so overwhelming that the higher-order evidence becomes irrelevant, and don't smuggle in new evidence after the doubt arrives that happens to cleanly settle things. After the scenario, walk me through it: state the exact first-order belief, the exact higher-order evidence, and why a conciliatory view about higher-order evidence (which says she should now lower her confidence) tells her to wobble, while the rational thing is actually not to wobble. Then say which feature of the case does the work, whether removing it collapses the case back into a standard 'you're tired, dial it down' situation, and how a conciliationist would try to stop your case from counting.
