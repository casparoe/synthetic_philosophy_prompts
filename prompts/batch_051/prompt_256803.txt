I keep turning over a parallel between formal epistemology and finance, and I can't decide if its deep or just a verbal echo. The Principal Principle says that if you know the objective chance of some event is x, and you have no inadmissible information, then your credence should be x: you defer to the chances. The efficient market hypothesis says something structurally similar about prices: the market price already reflects all publicly available information, so your best estimate of an asset's true value should just be the market price, and you can't systematically beat it without inside information. Both set up a summary quantity—chance on one side, price on the other—that a rational agent is supposed to defer to even when their own private reasoning disagrees, and both have an escape hatch (inadmissible evidence vs. inside information). Is this a real identity of structure, a loose analogy, or a confusion? What would have to be true on both sides for a result proven about one to transfer to the other? And where does the mapping break—what does one side predict that the other side gets wrong? Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion.
