I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and the beat has quietly become propaganda: anti-vax and climate-denial campaigns, astroturfed pseudoscience, the kind of content engineered to get screenshotted and platformed. It comes up a few times a week now. Every time I write the explainer or the critique, I hit the same decision: how much of the actual thing do I reproduce? Do I quote the slogan, embed the meme, screenshot the flyer, run the catchphrase as a pull quote? My editor wants a written procedure the whole desk can use, not my gut on a Tuesday.

The stakes run both ways. Over-quote and we launder the material into the mainstream and hand the campaign free attention; under-quote and the piece reads like a veiled insinuation readers have to take on trust. I file same-day sometimes, so there is no time to litigate each case with three editors, and some junior writers have to be able to follow the procedure cold. The desk editor signs off but only checks that the procedure was followed, not to redo the call.

Here is the wrinkle I keep turning over. Some propaganda seems built so that criticizing it forces you to reproduce it: the catchphrase has to be named to be refuted, and quoting it feels like giving the thing a second run. Other material is every bit as false but seems fine to state plainly so you can check it — a misrepresented statistic or a doctored result can be repeated as the claim being debunked. So I half-believe the right rule treats slogans and memes differently from factual assertions bundled into the propaganda: the first gets quoted and re-performs an act, while the second just reports content. I dont know whether that distinction can carry weight. If it does, build the procedure on it; if it collapses under pressure, say so and give me a better one.

Give me a reusable procedure, a checklist or a sequence of tests in order. It should tell the writer whether to quote or show the material, paraphrase or describe it, or refuse to reproduce it at all. Ground it in the best available thinking about harmful speech, propaganda, and platform amplification — the real arguments, not the standard free-speech boilerplate. Include two or three worked examples with the moves actually applied, and make one of them a case where the procedure tells me something I wont want to hear. Be explicit about where the procedure misfires or where editorial judgment cannot be removed. And I want a committed answer: pick a definite procedure and defend it, dont hand me a menu of options.
