I am not a trained philosopher — I work on database systems, and I pick most of this up from podcasts and from arguing with a friend who studied the subject years ago. Lately I have been stuck on a puzzle I first met in Plato's Meno: why is knowing something worth more than merely having a correct belief about it? The dialogue makes the puzzle sharp by imagining two people who can both lead you to a town you want to reach. One actually knows the way; the other only has a true belief about it, perhaps picked up secondhand. If both get you to the same place just as reliably, what does the knower have that the believer lacks? The traditional answer, as I understand it, is that a true belief is unmoored — it can slip away, be forgotten, or be swapped for a falsehood — whereas knowledge is secured by the person's own reasons, so it stays put. That stability is supposed to explain why we prize knowledge even when a lucky true belief would have produced the same action.

I have been wondering whether that answer still works now that so much of our believing is done outside our heads. I do not just mean that we can look things up. I mean that for a large share of the facts I rely on daily, what I have is best described as a standing capacity to produce the correct answer from a device within seconds, with the same answer available again tomorrow and to anyone who asks. If the chief defect of mere true belief was its instability, then a person with a phone appears to hold true beliefs that are just as firmly secured as much of the knowledge in my own memory — perhaps more firmly, since the device does not forget under stress or after a bad night. So my question is not about exam policy or whether we should let students use phones. It is about the argument itself. Does the old argument that knowledge is better than mere true belief because it is anchored by reasons survive this development? Which precise premise is now under pressure, what would the argument have to assume in order for its conclusion to still follow in this setting, and is there a repaired version that still explains why knowledge is worth more than a reliably retrievable true belief? If the argument is dead rather than repairable, I would like to hear that said plainly, with the reason why.
