I keep running into a feature of my own experience that I cannot name, and I would like both a name (if one exists) and, more importantly, a precise account of where the phenomenon begins and ends.

The clearest instance is ordinary vision. When I look at a blue cup, I can say a great deal about the cup — its color, its shape, its distance — but I cannot seem to describe the seeing of the cup. If I try to attend to the seeing rather than the cup, I find only the cup again. The seeing is not faint, the way a distant sound is faint; it is rather as if the experience were a window so clean that I see nothing but what lies beyond it. The same slide happens with other senses and even with thinking: the moment I try to catch the thinking as it happens, I find only the thing thought about.

Yet the invisibility is not constant, and this is what puzzles me most. A floater drifting across my visual field lets me briefly glimpse something like the medium of sight rather than what it shows. A lagging virtual-reality display can make the scene stop being a world and make the presentation itself show up. In a bad pain, it can be genuinely hard to say whether the pain is in my hand or is my hurting. So the machinery becomes visible mostly when it breaks.

I have also noticed a further move people make from observations like these: someone says that because they cannot find the experience when they look, there is no experience over and above the world; or that because the self is nowhere to be found in experience, the self is an illusion. I suspect that the first observation and the further move are connected, and I want to know how.

So my question is this. Is there an established concept for this character of experience? Do the breakdown cases — the floater, the lag, the pain — belong to the same phenomenon or to a neighbouring one? If the established terms cover only part of what I have described, what should the remainder be called? I am not after a bare label; I want the carving. I want to know how this differs from merely failing to attend, from the fact that experience is always of something, and from the kind of directness that realists claim for perception — and where exactly the boundary lies between an experience being this way and its becoming an object of attention in its own right.
