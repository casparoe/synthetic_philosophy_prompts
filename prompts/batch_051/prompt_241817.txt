Two people at work got into it the other day after Lila, the office manager, typed a shared-account password without looking, then realised she couldnt actually say the password out loud — she could only do it with her hands. It started as a joke and turned into a real argument. I was standing there with my coffee.

Marcus said that means she doesn't really know the password. If you can't tell someone the password, or even picture it, you don't know it; her fingers have it, like the keychain on her phone does. Knowledge has to be available to the person, and "the person" means the part of her that can say or at least think about it.

Dana said that's cheating, because it assumes knowledge has to be verbal from the start. She knows the password in the only way that matters for unlocking the account. Saying knowledge is sentences is like saying you don't know how to ride a bike because you can't write down how you balance.

Marcus said no — if you can only use it in the one memorised movement, it's not knowledge, it's a stored pattern. A thermostat doesn't know the room is cold.

Dana said a thermostat doesn't learn. Lila didn't have to relearn the password when she typed it on a phone with the same layout but a different angle; her hands just adjusted. That is not a recording. Her fingers have a feel for the whole keyboard, so it shows up to her hands as places-to-press rather than a list of coordinates.

Marcus said fine, it's flexible, but it's still habit, and habit is what the body does while the person is out of the loop. If you call that knowledge, you'd have to say your eye "knows" where a moving dot is when it tracks it, and that's obviously just a metaphor.

Dana said maybe it is not just a metaphor. The eye tracking the dot is closer to the typing than he thinks, and the real mistake is imagining that knowing has to happen somewhere behind the eyes, with the body as a glove the mind wears.

Marcus said then why does it feel different from actual knowledge? When Lila uses a fact she remembers she can stop and correct herself, but the body just does what it does and you're stuck praising it afterwards. Thats not thinking; thats being well-trained.

Dana said well-trained and thinking are not as far apart as that. Her hands make sense of the situation before she has words for it, and that is a kind of thought, just one that doesn't come in sentences.

They both asked me to settle it and I couldn't. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say who's right about what — including where either of them is right for the wrong reason, and what they're both missing? I don't just want a winner; I want to know which steps of the argument hold up and which don't.
