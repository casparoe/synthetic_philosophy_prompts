Ive been rereading Borges because time has gone weird for me lately; my dad died a few months ago and now whole days either collapse into one felt moment or stretch forever. So i picked up A New Refutation of Time. In the main argument Borges seems to take Berkeley's denial of matter and Hume's denial of the self and push them all the way to time: he says if the self is just a bundle of experiences, and two experiences with exactly the same content are indistinguishable, then repetition destroys succession, and time itself is a fallacy that arises only from memory and habit. But then in the final paragraph of the essay he steps back and says the refutation doesn't survive his own embodied life: the world is real, he is still Borges, and the passage of time he just denied is exactly what he feels when he closes the notebook. I keep going back and forth on whether that ending is an honest counterpoint, a quiet retraction, or just a literary shrug. Can the two parts be reconciled without making the denial merely rhetorical? If he really holds the idealist argument, what work is the ending doing? If the ending undercuts the argument, does Borges actually think time is real, or is the whole essay meant to perform the impossibility of escaping time? I want candidate readings and a verdict on which one is least special-pleading.
