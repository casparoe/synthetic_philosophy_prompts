I'm a retired physicist who's only recently started reading philosophy of physics, and I keep tripping over an asymmetry in how physicists talk. Collapse of the wavefunction gets treated as the sober, default story: the system's in a superposition, you measure, it lands in one definate outcome, no fuss. But say the wavefunction just keeps obeying the Schrödinger equation and the other branches stay real (Everett), and people roll there eyes and call it extravagant metaphysics. Yet as far as I can tell it's exactly backwards: collapse is the view that adds an extra, unexplained process on top of the linear dynamics, while Everett just declines to add it. So why is the view with the extra postulate treated as common sense and the leaner one as the wild sci-fi option? Is there a real justification for that asymmetry, or is it just habit and pedagogy — and how does it hold up against Bohm, which also drops collapse but doesn't draw the same eye-roll? Please mark what's actually settled here (the formalism, the measurement problem, the experimental results) versus what's genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't dress up one interpretation as the consensus.
