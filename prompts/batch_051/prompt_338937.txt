I'm in a Kant course and something's been nagging me since last week. We read this passage in the *Metaphysical Elements of Ethics* where Kant says:

"But if it were merely feeling that made it our duty to take this principle as a criterion, then this would not be dictated by reason, but only adopted instinctively and therefore blindly."

Then when we got to his comments about animals, he seems to argue that we have no direct duties to them, but that cruelty toward them is wrong because it dulls our capacity for sympathy, which is needed for duties to other people. I get the structure, but it feels weirdly instrumental: it makes the wrongness of kicking a dog about what it does to *me*, not about the dog. And my intuition that the dog matters directly is extremely firm. But the passage seems to make his view even less stable: if the animal duty is ultimately grounded in preserving a feeling, why doesn't his own warning about feeling-based duties rule it out? Maybe I'm overreading "feeling" there, and the sympathy point is just an empirical benefit rather than the actual moral principle. Present both sides at their strongest and tell me what should yield: my intuition, Kant's account, or my reading of the passage. If I've set this up as a false choice, say that plainly.
