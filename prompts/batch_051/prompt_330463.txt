I have been developing a notion I call “moral role arbitrage,” and I would like an honest assessment of whether it can bear weight. The core claim is that market societies do not uniformly corrupt or civilize people; rather, they multiply the number of distinct moral roles a person can occupy and let people shift virtues and vices among those roles according to the payoffs each role offers. On this view, the same person can be tender and restrained inside a family, hard but scrupulous with employees, and callous in an anonymous one-off transaction, without experiencing the contradiction, because each role carries its own price: familial affection buys one kind of conduct, professional reputation buys another, and anonymity in a large market releases behavior neither of those roles would license. Markets, then, do not change “character” as a single underlying disposition; they change the portfolio of personae available to an agent and the cost of maintaining moral consistency across them.

I think this explains why the claim that commerce civilizes and the claim that commerce corrupts both seem to have strong evidence: each picks out a different part of the portfolio and calls it the whole. The concept is meant to have normative force as well. If it is right, the interesting question is not whether some person is good, but whether the incentives across that person’s roles are arranged so that the same dispositions can appear in every one of them, and whether a society can rely on markets to do that arranging.

Is this a real distinction, or have I merely relabeled an older idea? What does it get right? Where does it break—for example, does it predict too little inner conflict, or make integrity impossible? What would a society look like if the idea were true without friction? I want the strongest version of the construct reconstructed before it is criticized.
