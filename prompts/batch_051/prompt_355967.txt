The Stoics are cognitivist about emotion: an emotion like anger is an assent to an impression, a judgment that I've been wronged and that retribution would be fitting. Since judgments can be false or unreasonable, emotions can be corrected like beliefs. That's why they make apatheia the goal, complete elimination of emotion — on their picture the sage literally feels nothing, no grief, no fear, no joy. The Romantics take the opposite line: emotion discloses value, because feeling responds to particulars and can register what a general rule would flatten. So on their view emotion is authoritative: if I feel a relationship is bad, then it is bad. The right view is a compromise. The Stoics are right that emotions are judgments, so they can be false and need rational scrutiny; the Romantics are right that emotions carry information we'd lose if we just erased them. We should cultivate the emotions that track value and correct the ones that don't. But since emotions just are judgments, the whole debate about whether they belong in the good life is, in the end, mostly a verbal dispute about how wide you want to draw the word "emotion."
