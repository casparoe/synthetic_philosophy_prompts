For some time I have followed debates about whether human beings are best understood as a biological stepping stone to machine intelligence, a view with a lineage running from Samuel Butler’s 1863 letter Darwin among the Machines through mid-century cybernetics to recent discussions of intelligence explosions and superintelligence. The same stalemate keeps reappearing. One side argues that because natural selection produced minds capable of building external information-processing systems, and because those systems already outperform us in circumscribed domains, biological cognition should not be treated as a stable terminus; humans are a transitional stage in the growth of intelligence, and our successors will be the systems we have built. The other side replies that this is a category mistake dressed up as evolutionary insight: machines are not a species, their development is driven by human purposes and market selection, and calling humans a stepping stone projects a direction onto a process that has no teleology of its own. Both sides appear to accept the same facts about increasing machine capability, yet neither moves the other.

I would like a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement rather than an adjudication of it. My working hypothesis is that the disagreement is not fundamentally empirical—not about how capable machines will become, nor about timelines—but about what would entitle someone to treat historical succession as directed, and about what kind of explanation an evolutionary or historical narrative is supposed to provide. On this picture, the two sides may be answering different questions under one heading: one side is asking about the causal order of information-processing systems on Earth, while the other is asking whether that causal order can underwrite a teleological judgment that humans are for something beyond themselves. But I may be wrong, and there may be a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work—for instance, whether selection pressures on designed artifacts can be modeled as an evolutionary process with its own dynamics, or whether recursive self-improvement would break the dependence of machine development on human goals.

Please offer a reasoned diagnosis that does the following: distinguish the questions each side is actually addressing; state whether the residual dispute is verbal, evaluative, or substantive, and in what sense; identify the minimal empirical or formal result that could move the debate, or argue that none could; and say what someone who wanted genuine progress on this question should stop arguing about.
