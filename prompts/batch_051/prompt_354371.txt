For several months I have been carrying around an argument that a colleague pressed upon me, and I cannot locate the fault in it. It is meant to show that the simulation argument, taken seriously, dampens fanaticism about the far future; but as stated it appears to prove far too much, and I should like a precise diagnosis.

(1) The simulation argument gives us a trilemma: at least one of the following is true—almost all civilisations at our stage go extinct before they acquire the capacity to run ancestor-simulations; almost all civilisations that acquire that capacity choose never to run them; or we are almost certainly living in a simulation.

(2) On the present evidence, we should regard the first two possibilities as improbable rather than merely uncertain. The proposed extinction filters and civilisation-wide taboos are ad hoc, whilst the computational capacity and the motives needed for large-scale ancestor-simulation look generic.

(3) We should therefore assign a substantial credence—say at least one third—to the proposition that we are simulated.

(4) If we are simulated, then every action we perform is realised within the simulation, and no such action can causally affect the base world or its far future.

(5) Far-future fanaticism holds that the overwhelming share of an action's expected value lies in its influence on the long-run future. But if we are simulated, the long-run future open to our causal influence is only the future internal to the simulation; the far future of the base world is causally closed to us.

(6) Therefore, to the extent that the simulation argument raises our credence in being simulated, it should sharply discount the expected value of actions justified by appeal to the far future—mitigating existential risk, building durable institutions, steering the long-term trajectory, and the like.

(7) Yet the same reasoning applies to every action. Conditional on our being simulated, keeping a promise, refraining from cruelty, and correcting an injustice are likewise events inside the simulation that make no difference to the base world's far future. If the consideration in (5) discounts far-future-directed action, it discounts these as well.

(8) That conclusion—that under a credible hypothesis none of our actions matters in the way ordinary moral concern presupposes—cannot be accepted. The argument was supposed to deflate one form of fanaticism, not morality itself.

(9) The argument must therefore go wrong somewhere; and if I want to retain the deflationary conclusion in (6) without accepting (8), I must defend a principled asymmetry between far-future-directed concern and ordinary moral concern.

Where does the argument go wrong? Identify the step or suppressed premise that should be rejected, explain why it looked harmless, and say what a defender of the deflationary conclusion would need to accept—or abandon—in order to repair the argument without licensing the nihilistic conclusion. I am confident about (4) and about the denial of (8); beyond that, I have no settled view.
