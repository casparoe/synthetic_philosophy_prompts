I'm in a reading group on metaphysical grounding — the relation of one fact's obtaining in virtue of other facts — and on fundamentality, understood as the question of whether chains of ground must bottom out. After a session on the possibility of infinite descent, one member sketched an argument that has been bothering me ever since. I've reconstructed it as a reductio, and every premise looked right as I wrote it out, but the conclusion feels absurdly strong.

(1) Suppose, for reductio, that nothing is fundamental: every fact is fully grounded in some further facts, and those in still further facts, and so on without end. (2) If things were that way, then there would be a fact to that effect — the fact that nothing is fundamental, equivalently that every fact is grounded. (3) By the supposition, that fact too must be grounded. (4) Its ground cannot be some particular fact further down, since no single grounded fact could make it the case that all facts are grounded; only the entire endless totality of grounded facts could do that. (5) But that totality contains, and in an obvious sense presupposes, the very fact at issue, since the fact that nothing is fundamental is a fact about that totality. (6) So the supposed ground is circular. Therefore the supposition is incoherent, and there must be at least one fundamental fact.

This seems to prove too much. The same pattern appears to rule out, by pure argument, the coherence of any endless chain described from above — an infinite sequence of causes with no first cause, say — and I did not think logic alone could settle those questions. If forced, I would rather keep the claim that every fact, including one about the whole, is apt for grounding than accept that my argument has proven fundamentality. But I cannot tell whether the problem lies in positing the fact in (2), in the totality move in (4), in the circularity charge in (5), or somewhere else entirely, and I want to know what each rejection would cost.

Please diagnose the argument as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. In doing so, separate what is settled among those who work on grounding from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as the consensus.
