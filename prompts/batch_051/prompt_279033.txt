I have spent most of my career pricing sports and political markets, so my working vocabulary for evaluating anyone who assigns numbers to uncertain outcomes is the bookmaker's: odds and implied probabilities, the overround, Dutch books and arbitrage, calibration versus resolution, and proper scoring rules such as the Brier and log scores. A philosopher I know keeps telling me that this is close to a live research program called accuracy-first epistemology, or epistemic utility theory, which holds that the fundamental epistemic norm governing degrees of belief is accuracy, and that substantive requirements on belief—starting with probabilism, the demand that credences obey the probability axioms—can be derived from assumptions about how accuracy is measured and how expected accuracy should be pursued. Every time I try to read the primary literature I lose the thread in phrases like dominance, strict propriety, expected epistemic value, and the immodesty of one's own credences.

Please translate the central claims and the main argumentative moves of accuracy-first epistemology into my vocabulary as systematically as you can: the scoring of a credence, the demand that belief states not be accuracy-dominated, the assumption that one evaluates expected accuracy by one's own lights, and the claim that accuracy is the sole fundamental epistemic good. Then give me the residue report. Which parts map cleanly onto pricing a market and scoring a forecaster, which parts have to be bent to fit, and which parts have no counterpart at all in a bookmaker's way of thinking? I particularly want to know whether the accuracy-based argument for probabilism is genuinely different from the Dutch book argument I already use, and whether my suspicion that the whole program is just applied proper scoring rules is correct or misses something that cannot be said in my terms. If something is left over, tell me whether that residue reflects a real insight my framework cannot express or a place where the philosophers' position is less precise than it looks.
