You are an expert in population ethics and a specialist in adversarial collaboration — the craft of producing documents that two rival camps can each sign as a fair statement of their own view. Draft the joint statement that should result from a year-long adversarial collaboration between two researchers at a philosophy and policy institute. One, a total-welfare pronatalist, holds that the goodness of a world is determined by the sum of well-being across lives, so that, other things equal, a larger human population whose lives are worth living is better; this researcher accepts the so-called Repugnant Conclusion — that a sufficiently large population of lives barely worth living may be better than a smaller population of excellent lives — as counterintuitive but true. The other, a person-affecting antigrowth theorist, denies that an extra life is an impersonal unit of value merely because it is worth living; on this view a larger population is better only if it benefits people who exist or would exist anyway, and it can be worse if it lowers how well the existing and unavoidable future people live.

The document should have four parts. First, a numbered agreed-claims section: the substantive claims both can genuinely sign, including claims about what any adequate account of population betterness must explain, what constraints on reproductive policy both accept, and where their earlier arguments had been talking past each other. These must be non-vacuous — no throat-clearing truisms. Second, a disagreements table whose entries state the residual disagreements so precisely that each side would accept the wording of its own position, with the total-welfare and person-affecting formulations set side by side. Third, a cruxes section identifying the two or three sub-questions such that a settled answer to one of them would move at least one side, with the direction of movement made explicit. Fourth, for each crux, specify a concrete case or argument both camps agree bears on it — not a real-world data claim or poll, but a carefully constructed thought-experimental or analytic test — and record each side’s prediction in advance about what the case will show on reflection. Work in stages, and end by stating which part, if any, of their dispute you judge to be merely verbal.
