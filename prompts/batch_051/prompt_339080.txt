I am a junior in high school, and for a philosophy club paper I have been asked to write about a proposal from AI-safety research. The proposal is this: when an AI system becomes too capable for a human to evaluate directly, we might train two such systems to argue opposite sides of any contested claim and have a human judge decide which side argued better. The motivation is that it is easier to judge a good argument than to produce one, so debate could let a human stay in control even when the machines know far more than she does. My paper asks what John Stuart Mill would have thought of this idea. Mill could not have anticipated modern AI, but in On Liberty he argues that truth is best established and kept alive through open disagreement, and that a belief which is never contested tends to lose its hold on people even if it is true. I want to know whether his reasons for valuing open discussion actually transfer to a case where the debaters are artificial, the judge is a single human facing systems far more capable than she is, and one side may be optimizing to persuade rather than to be accurate. Would Mill have endorsed debate as a way of supervising powerful AI systems, or would his own arguments predict specific ways it could fail? Please apply his actual claims about fallibility, truth, and liberty rather than just his general optimism about free speech, and say clearly where the analogy between a free society of human discussants and a supervised AI debate holds and where it breaks down.
