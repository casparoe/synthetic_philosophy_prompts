I sit on the grants committee of a family foundation whose charter says it exists for both wildlife conservation and the reduction of animal suffering. We're about to vote on a proposal to fund a wetland restoration that would turn a drained agricultural field into vernal pools and marsh. The staff report projects an enormous increase in native amphibians, small fish, and aquatic invertibrates, mostly of the small short-lived kind. The standard case for the grant is that this is about as close to an obviously good project as we see: more habitat, more populations, more native species. But one committee member, who has been reading about wild animal welfare, wants us to think about the lives those animals will actually lead. Many of the new individuals will be eaten as larvae, starve when the pools dry, or die of disease within days; in his view the project may be creating a net increase in suffering, and the foundation might help animals more by leaving the field drained. He also points out that if we count each individual equally, the tiny short-lived animals dominate the arithmetic, whereas if we discount them, the project looks much better—so the decision partly depends on a counting question we've never faced. Other members reply that this is speculation about the inner lives of frogs, and that conservation has always been about populations and species rather than individual welfare. I need to circulate a written opinion before our vote.

Please draft it as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. Make point 1 the concrete recommendation—fund, reject, or fund with conditions—and use the remaining points to give the reasoning, including the strongest consideration on the side you recomend against.
