I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am planning a feature on what the no-free-lunch theorems in machine learning do and do not show about the problem of induction. My readers are comfortable with overfitting and have some intuitive grasp of learning from data, but they could not state the theorems precisely, so the conceptual instrument I use has to carry all the technical content it needs on its surface. I want to map the landscape before I interview people, both so I can tell which camp each researcher falls into and so the piece can describe the real fault lines instead of rehashing the usual induction-is-impossible paragraph.

Please write a decision tree that takes a reader from the admission that the no-free-lunch theorem is true — loosely, that averaged uniformly over all possible target functions, no learning algorithm generalizes better than any other, so any apparent edge must come from assumptions that fail in some possible worlds — to a named position on induction. Build the tree from the questions and the full matrix of answers rather than from a list of labels. The load-bearing distinctions I want to see in it are: whether the uniform average over all possible functions is the right standard for judging an inductive rule; whether the actual world's regularities plausibly form a small and learnable subset; whether restricting the hypothesis class already begs the question; whether a rule with no universal guarantee can still be vindicated by its record in the actual world or in the set of worlds we could plausibly inhabit; and whether the theorem addresses ordinary induction at all rather than a deliberately hostile setup. The leaves should include at least a Humean skeptic, a pragmatic-vindication position of the sort associated with Reichenbach, a Bayesian or algorithmic-probability position that responds by committing to a non-uniform prior over target functions, a realism-about-a-compressible-world position, and a deflationary position that the theorem is irrelevant to ordinary induction because induction is not the attempt to learn arbitrary input-output mappings. Aim for no more than seven leaves and no more than six decision points. Where a combination of answers has no occupant, mark it and say in a sentence whether it is empty because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and flag any two leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate.

Please take the role of a philosopher who works on induction and is also fluent in learning theory. Think carefully: first brainstorm the candidate branch questions and the full matrix of answers they allow, then settle on the tree, and finally test it against each leaf to ensure it sorts people by what they actually maintain rather than by which label they prefer. Deliver the whole thing as continuous prose in the manner of a letter or an email, with no headings, no bullet points or lists, no bold or italics, and no tables; when the reader faces a choice, write the options out in ordinary sentences and then say where each choice leads.
