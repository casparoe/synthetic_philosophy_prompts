You are a professor of moral and political philosophy who has taught a course on the sociology of moral cultures. You are marking a final-year examination. Below are one question from the paper and one student's answer.

Give the answer a mark out of 25, and produce the point-by-point comments you would return to the student. Think carefully before assigning the mark: work through the answer systematically, beginning with what the student has understood and stated correctly, then considering each questionable or false claim and explaining precisely why it is wrong or unsupported, and only then settle on the mark. Where an argument is valid but incomplete, say so. Do not penalise the student for disagreeing with your own substantive moral views; penalise only descriptive inaccuracy, unsupported inference, or failure to address the question. The deliverable should be a point-by-point assessment rather than a general impression, and your full assessment should be written in British English (honour, defence, publicising, and so on).

Question (25 marks). Bradley Campbell and Jason Manning distinguish three moral cultures: honour, dignity, and victimhood. Explain the distinction, and critically assess the claim that dignity culture is morally superior to the other two. Your response should connect the assessment to a defensible criterion of moral evaluation.

Student's answer.

The distinction is best understood through the different ways in which the three cultures handle grievances, slights, and the maintenance of standing. In an honour culture, where public order is not reliably guaranteed by a strong state, a person whose reputation for toughness is challenged must respond, often with violence or the credible threat of it, because failure to retaliate invites further predation. In a dignity culture, by contrast, people are expected to ignore minor insults rather than retaliate: one's worth is not supposed to depend on other people's opinions, and serious wrongs are referred to formal institutions such as courts. A victimhood culture, which Campbell and Manning describe as having become prominent in some American university settings in recent decades, is highly sensitive to words and slights but responds neither by ignoring them nor by direct retaliation; instead, aggrieved parties publicise the offence and appeal to third parties, such as administrators or online audiences, for validation and intervention. All three cultures are therefore solutions to the same underlying problem of how a person can deter mistreatment and secure standing among others: honour relies on direct retaliation or the credible threat of it, dignity on law and outward self-command, and victimhood on public disclosure and appeals to third parties.

On the evaluative question, dignity culture is plainly superior. Since dignity culture suppresses violent retaliation, it directly reduces the amount of physical injury in society, and a change that reduces physical injury is precisely what moral improvement consists in. Furthermore, dignity culture is the only one of the three that treats each person's worth as intrinsic rather than as dependent on public standing, and this is why it is uniquely compatible with liberal democratic institutions. Victimhood culture, by contrast, rejects the ideal of equal dignity: by treating every unintended slight as an injury, and by ranking people according to how injured or vulnerable they are, it effectively reinstates the honour code's obsession with slight and standing. It therefore cannot be a genuine moral improvement over dignity culture.
