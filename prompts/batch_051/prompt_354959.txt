I have recently been appointed to a university committee charged with reviewing the names of several campus buildings, the most contentious of which honors a statesman whose legacy includes slave-owning and brutal colonial administration. The strongest defence of retaining the name, repeated in nearly every meeting, rests on an analogy. One member of the committee, a historian, argues that condemning such a man by today's moral standards is like condemning a nineteenth-century surgeon for operating with unwashed hands before germ theory was accepted: in each case the person failed to act on knowledge that was not available to him, and it would be unfair to treat that failure as a moral one. The analogy is being asked to do a great deal of work, not merely cautioning against crude denunciation but supporting the conclusion that the name should remain.

I would like you to audit this analogy. Begin by spelling out the correspondence explicitly: in the surgeon case, identify what plays the role of the wrong act, the standard by which it is judged, the agent's state of knowledge, the available alternatives, the harms, and the agent's relation to the people harmed; then map each of these onto the statesman's case. Then identify the disanalogies. Where does 'he could not have known better' genuinely hold in both cases, and where does it break down — for instance, where some of the man's own contemporaries did condemn slavery or colonial violence, where the harm was confrontable by the agent in a way microbes were not, or where the norm at issue was less a matter of undiscovered fact than of ordinary human recognition? Distinguish the disanalogies that are merely superficial from those that matter for the argument, and tell me which of the historian's conclusions — the refusal to condemn the man, the retention of the name, or both — survive once the real disanalogies are on the table. Finally, if this analogy misleads more than it illuminates, propose a comparison that better captures what is at stake in judging historical figures, and say what it would imply for my committee's decision.
