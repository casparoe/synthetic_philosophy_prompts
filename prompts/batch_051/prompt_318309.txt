I have a standing rule for forming judgments about whom to trust with consequential responsibilities in a small volunteer organization I help run. I treat a person's record on small, low-cost commitments—arriving when they said they would, replying within the window they set, completing the minor task they volunteered for—as a strong and often decisive signal about how they will behave when something large and irregular is at stake. I also discount evidence of reliability extracted under pressure or in emergencies, on the grounds that emergencies are rare, partly self-selected, and poor guides to ordinary dependability. I recognize that small commitments are cheap to keep, cheap to break, and cheap to monitor, and that chronic lateness or dropped minor tasks can reflect obstacles, competing obligations, or different standards rather than indifference. But I still act on the rule because, in my experience, it has worked. I want to know whether this practice is a defensible epistemic shortcut about human reliability, or a moralizing bias dressed up as prediction—punishing people for situational constraints and then laundering that judgment into a forecast of their character. When is the inference from small-promise keeping to high-stakes reliability likely to be sound, when does it break down, and is there a restatement of the policy that retains its predictive value without overreading punctuality? Please assess it as a policy appropriate to my circumstances rather than as a general theory of virtue.
