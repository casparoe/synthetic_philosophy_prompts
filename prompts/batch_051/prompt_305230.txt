I am a novelist. The center of the book I am drafting is a dispute inside a small, theologically conservative congregation over a member named Elena, who is undergoing chemotherapy and has lost all her hair. The congregation has asked her to lead a portion of morning prayer and to offer a short testimony she believes is prompted by the Spirit. She does so bareheaded, as she has done all her life, because there is now no hair to cover and because she regards a scarf as a way of concealing what God is doing in her body. A faction in the congregation objects from 1 Corinthians. The passage they press on her is this, in the New Revised Standard Version Updated Edition:

"Any man who prays or prophesies with something on his head shames his head, but any woman who prays or prophesies with her head unveiled shames her head—it is one and the same thing as having her head shaved. For if a woman will not veil herself, then she should cut off her hair, but if it is shameful for a woman to have her hair cut off or to be shaved, she should wear a veil."

The objectors argue that the veil is a commanded sign in the assembly, not a metaphor for hair, and that the passage's own reasoning treats a bare female head as the sign of shame; on their reading, Elena's hairlessness is exactly the state the command anticipates, so she should cover. Elena's defenders reply that the command aims at honoring God-given distinctions between the sexes, which Elena does, and that the fabric is a first-century instrument of that aim; once the hair itself is gone, the instrument has nothing to attach to and the principle remains satisfied. I do not want the scene to be a caricature of either side, so I need the passage read carefully rather than sloganistically.

What does the passage actually require of Elena? Work through the main approaches to interpreting a scriptural command: its plain wording, its apparent purpose, what its author would plausibly have said if asked about a worshipper made permanently hairless by medical treatment, and what a reasonable first-century reader would have taken it to mean. Then separate what is culturally local in the passage from what a transcultural principle would have to be for the command to carry beyond first-century Corinth. Where the readings diverge, tell me which divergence is doing the real work: the line between cultural form and transcultural principle, the line between hair and veil, or something else. I am less interested in a verdict than in understanding what the case exposes about the cultural/transcultural distinction itself when it is used on a text whose argument leans at once on a created order and on a local custom.
