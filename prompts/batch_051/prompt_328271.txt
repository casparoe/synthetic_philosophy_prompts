I'm running a research seminar on population ethics, and one of my students came to office hours with an argument he says shows that our ordinary moral thinking about population size is inconsistent. Ive been staring at it for days and I can't find the exit. He wrote it as follows:

(1) Most people think that whether a bigger family, city, or species is better depends on the quality of the lives in it, not just the number.

(2) So if adding a person lowers the average quality of life in a group, that addition is at least pro tanto bad — the group is no better off on the whole.

(3) But by the same reasoning, adding many people can be good even if each lowers the average a little, so long as the total well-being increases; a city of a million with slightly lower average happiness seems better than a village of fifty with slightly higher average happiness.

(4) Now apply this stepwise. Start with the village of fifty. Add one person whose life is worth living but slightly below the village average. By (3), that makes the city better. Repeat until you have a million people, each with a life barely worth living. By the same step, each addition made things better, so the million-person city with barely-worth-living lives is better than the original fifty-person village with very happy lives.

(5) Almost no one, including my student's gran, believes that about cities or families. So something has gone wrong.

(6) But the only way to stop the slide is to say that at some point the average matters in a way that blocks the stepwise improvement — yet if you say that, you're committed to the idea that a single very happy life can be better than a very large number of lives each just above the neutral point, which sounds equally mad.

(7) Since the same structure applies to a species, a city, or a family, it seems our ordinary moral thinking about population size has no consistent foundation at all.

He says this proves too much, because we do make family-planning and policy decisions about population without reaching for a knife between total and average theories. I want to know which premise or step to give up, and what it costs to give it up. I'd especially like to know where the equivocation or slide is, if there is one, and what the standard positions — total view, average view, person-affecting views, critical-level views — each have to pay to resolve it.
