I'm doing a book club on The Great Gatsby next month and I want to run the discussion through the philosophical problem of idolatry — not the religious sin, but the broader worry that there are wrong ways to value something even if the thing itself is genuinely valuable. The distinction I want the group to work with is roughly: loving Daisy, versus loving a picture of Daisy you've built up, versus loving the feeling of longing for her more than her actual self, versus making one finite person bear the weight of finally justifying your whole life. Read the novel through those as a lens. Is Gatsby's attachment best described by one of those, or a mix? Point me to the scenes that actually do the philosophical work — the green light, Nick's opening account of Gatsby's hope, the reunion, the shirts, the ending — and also to the places where the book resists a clean diagnosis, like whether it ends up treating Gatsby's illusion as the one genuine thing in a counterfeit world. I keep going back and forth on whether the novel can even tell the difference between idolatry and ordinary romantic idealization, given that we only ever get Nick's version. I don't want Gatsby turned into a cautionary tale; I want to know whether the book has a coherent account of what makes valuing go wrong, and whether that account is any good.
