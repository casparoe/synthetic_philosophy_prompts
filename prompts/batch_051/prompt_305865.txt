In Newcomb's problem, a transparent box always contains $1,000, while an opaque box contains $1,000,000 if a highly reliable predictor predicted that you would take only the opaque box and $0 if it predicted that you would take both boxes. The dominance argument for taking both boxes runs: the opaque box is either full or empty, and in either case you are $1,000 richer taking both boxes than taking only the opaque one. Now change one feature of the setup. The predictor's reliability is no longer a brute statistical fact: you are told, with full confidence, that it made its prediction by running a perfect simulation of you from your present state, confronting the simulation with the same choice, information, and incentives you now face, and then recording whatever act the simulation performs. Does the dominance argument survive this change as a reason to take both boxes? If it survives, state exactly what it still rules out and what it fails to touch; if it does not, identify the premise the simulation bears on and give the strongest repaired principle a one-boxer could defend. The question concerns the structure of the argument, not the engineering of the predictor.
