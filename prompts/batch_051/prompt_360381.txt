I want to know whether any general principle captures my verdicts about knowledge, and what it costs me if none does. I have a candidate in view — a tracking-style account of the sort associated with Nozick — but I also have a rival principle whose force I can't shake. I will state my verdicts first, then the candidate and the rival, and then ask you to do the work.

The verdicts:

1. At a zoo I see the black-and-white striped animal in the zebra enclosure and judge that it is a zebra. It is a zebra, and I know it is. Do I also know that it is not a mule painted to look like a zebra? I am not sure I do — the painted-mule possibility feels like something I would get right only by luck. Yet being a zebra entails not being a painted mule, and if I know the one, a familiar principle seems to force me to know the other.

2. On ordinary perceptual grounds I judge that I have hands, and I know that. But I do not think I know that I am not a brain in a vat being fed the experience of having hands, even though having hands entails not being a brain in a vat.

3. The night before a fair one-million-ticket lottery is drawn, a ticket holder says she will lose. She does lose. I do not think she knew she would lose, even though her belief was true and the odds made it overwhelmingly likely.

4. A man looks at a clock that, unbeknownst to him, stopped exactly twelve hours earlier. It reads 2:00, and it is 2:00, so he judges that it is 2:00. I do not think he knows it is 2:00, even though the clock happens to read correctly.

5. Driving through a county I am not told is full of fake barn facades, I point at the one structure that really is a barn and judge that it is a barn. It is a barn. I am not sure I know it is a barn, because I could so easily have been looking at a facade instead.

6. A grandmother sees her grandson across the room looking well and judges that he is well; he is well. But the family would have concealed any illness and told her he was well, so that if he had been unwell she would still have judged him well. I judge that she nonetheless knows he is well. This seems to break the simple tracking test unless the test is tied to her actual way of forming the belief — and I am not sure how to tie it.

The candidate: knowledge requires something like tracking — if the proposition had been false, I would not have believed it by the same method; and perhaps, conversely, if it had been true, I would have believed it. This handles the lottery, the stopped clock, and the barn facades, and it explains my hesitation in the first two cases. But it seems to say I know the zebra is a zebra while not knowing it is not a painted mule, and to say likewise about my hands and the brain-in-a-vat proposition — which means abandoning the rival principle.

The rival: knowledge is closed under known entailment. If I know that P and know that P entails Q, then I am in a position to know that Q. I find this nearly irresistible, but accepting it outright seems to force me either to give up my knowledge claim in the zebra and hands cases, or to claim knowledge of the entailed painted-mule and brain-in-a-vat denials, which I was sure I lacked.

Now the request. State the best principle — or a small set of candidate principles — that captures as many of these six verdicts as possible. For each candidate, say exactly how it treats the six cases, which verdicts it cannot accommodate, and which of my verdicts I must drop if I accept it. If the principle has several clauses, tell me which case forces each clause. Then apply the surviving candidate to further cases of your own choosing, including at least one in which closure and tracking come apart in a way these six do not already cover, and one in which the relativization to a method does real work. Finally, tell me whether there is a restricted closure principle that preserves the ordinary transmission of knowledge without forcing the skeptical conclusions, and what it would have to say about the painted-mule and brain-in-a-vat cases.
