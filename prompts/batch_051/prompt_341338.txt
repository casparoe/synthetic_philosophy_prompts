Here's a criterion list I've been taught for something being a genuine legal fiction:

1. The court treats a proposition as true for legal purposes.
2. The proposition is false, or at least wildly unlikely, in the actual case.
3. The falseness is not up for argument: parties can't bring evidence to rebut it.
4. The court adopts it to get around some practical or doctrinal difficulty, not because anyone actually believes it.

Now the borderline case: the fertile octogenarian doctrine in the rule against perpetuities. Under the old common law approach, when testing whether a future interest might vest too remotely, the law assumed that any living person, of any age or physical condition, could either bear or beget children. A woman of ninety, someone who had been surgically sterilised, a new-born baby, a man with no testes: all treated as potentially fertile. Evidence that a specific person could not have children was inadmissible. The point was to make the perpetuities calculation foolproof, because a what-if-they-have-a-child possibility could otherwise unsettle an estate plan years later.

Is that a legal fiction? Sure, it looks like one on criteria 1 and 2, but it's odd because it doesn't pretend something false about a particular person in a particular case; it adopts a blanket counterfactual about everyone. Also, the court isn't exactly pretending to believe the ninety-year-old is fertile — it's saying the law will not make that question open. Does the doctrine qualify, or is it better described as a rule of practical convenience, a definitional stipulation, or something else? And does it show that my criterion 3, irrebuttability, is doing too much or too little work?
