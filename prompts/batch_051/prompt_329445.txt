Retired physical chemist here — I spent most of my career teaching thermodynamics, and only recently started reading philosophy, mostly around time, convention, and how much of our framework is the world's contribution and how much is ours. Something I keep gnawing on: the argument over what the calendar's year zero should be (keep AD/BC, switch to CE/BCE, adopt the Holocene or Human Era calendar, add a year zero for easy arithmetic) looks to me exactly like an argument we used to have about temperature scales. I can't tell whether it's a real structural parallel or me just pattern-matching on the word "zero."

In the lab you learn there are two kinds of zero. There's the arbitrary kind: Celsius puts 0° where water freezes, Fahrenheit puts 0° near the freezing point of a brine mixture, and nothing about the world changes when you shift the zero — only the numerals get re-labeled. What's real is the interval between two temperatures; subtract, and the zero drops out. Then there's the privileged kind: 0 K is not a re-labeling. It's where the relevant quantity actually bottoms out, and you can't move it without breaking the physics. So "what should zero be?" is either a question of convenience or a question about where nature sets a floor.

Now calendars. If I want the number of years between Augustus and the Moon landing, the epoch cancels — it's an interval, so the Anno Domini era, the Byzantine era, astronomical year numbering, and the Holocene calendar all deliver the same answer. That makes a calendar epoch look like an arbitrary zero: a convention we need to turn events into numbers, with nothing factual at stake. Its a coordinate system for history, not a claim about where history bottoms out.

But the Holocene-calendar people seem to claim otherwise. They treat 10,000 BCE not as a relabeling but as a real break in the human record: roughly the start of the current geological epoch, and roughly when agriculture and settled life get going. That would make their zero Kelvin-like — a zero the world picks out, not one we paste on. Except the details make me suspicious. The current consensus for the geological start of the Holocene is 11,700 years before 2000 CE, i.e. 9701 BCE, which is about 300 years more recent than the round 10,000 BCE the calendar uses. And agriculture doesn't begin everywhere at once. So the "natural" zero is looking a lot like a round, fuzzy convention after all.

So here's the parallel: calendar epochs are to events as arbitrary zeros are to measurements. Both cancel out when you compute intervals, and both still manage to generate real arguments about which zero is "better." Is that deep or superficial? Three things I'm stuck on:

(i) Does the arbitrary-zero versus privileged-zero distinction even have a clean equivalent on the calendar side, or am I importing a physical idea into historical dating where it doesn't belong?

(ii) If the parallel is right, the debate over what year zero should be ought to split into a convenience question (Celsius vs Fahrenheit) and a nature question (finding a date-axis analogue of absolute zero). But actual epoch arguments are mostly about civic and symbolic meaning, which is neither. Does the analogy break exactly where the real argument lives?

(iii) The clean version of the analogy predicts that, since epochs cancel out of intervals, sensible people shouldn't loose sleep over them. That prediction looks false: people do lose sleep over BC/AD vs BCE/CE and over the Holocene calendar. Is the analogy wrong, or does the sleep just go to the labels rather than the intervals?
