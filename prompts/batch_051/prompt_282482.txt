I've been going back and forth with a friend about Bernard Williams's argument that an immortal human life would eventually become insupportably boring. The way I understand it, the argument isnt really about running out of things to watch: it's that a person is who they are partly in virtue of a finite set of "categorical" commitments—projects, attachments, desires that give them reasons to go on living at all—and an endless life would eventually use those up, so the person would face either dull repetition or a kind of self-alienating drift into being someone else. My friend says generative AI kills this worry. If a system can keep producing novel music, stories, images, games, and even novel social roles or virtual worlds, tailored to you more precisely than any human curator could, then the premise that experience must become repetitious just fails, and Williams's argument dies. I'm not convinced, but I cant articulate exactly why an infinite novelty engine isn't the refutation my friend thinks it is.

So here's what I want. Take Williams's argument and ask whether it survives the assumption that an immortal person has access to arbitrarily rich, endlessly personalized generative content. I don't want a yes or no about whether immortal life would be good. I want the diagnosis: which premise the development actually bears on, which premise it leaves alone, and whether the argument can be restated so it still does its work.

Please structure the response like this:

- "The argument, restated" (~150–250 words): lay out the argument in premise–conclusion form, and mark the premise you think the generative-content development is meant to undercut.
- "What the technology changes" (~150 words): say exactly which claim about experience the infinite-content scenario undermines, and which claims of Williams's it leaves untouched.
- "Diagnosis" (~300–400 words): does the argument survive? Does it need repair, or does the new development mainly show that the original was leaning on a cruder notion of boredom (repetition) than it actually needed (loss of categorical desire)? Keep the two notions separate.
- "The repaired argument, if any" (~100–150 words): put it in the strongest form that still works under the new assumption, or say clearly that no repair is possible and what should replace it.
- "Bottom line" (1–2 sentences).
