I sit on the appointments committee of a small research institute, and we are down to two finalists for a permanent post. I have read both files in full, and on my own reading the first candidate is plainly the stronger: the research programme is more original, the writing is more careful, and the references point to someone who thinks for herself. The second candidate is competent but, to my eye, derivative. The committee deliberates in a fixed order, and I am ninth of eleven. The first four speakers, whose judgement I ordinarily respect, have all argued with apparent conviction for the second candidate. What unsettles me is that their stated reasons feel thinner than their confidence: two of them leaned heavily on the distinction of the second candidate's doctoral supervisor, one repeated a phrase from the first speaker almost verbatim, and none of them engaged with the specific weaknesses I see in the file. I also happen to know that at least one of those early speakers would benefit professionally from the second candidate's appointment, though I cannot tell whether that is shaping his view. By the time the discussion reaches me, the chair is gently steering us toward consensus, and the room has acquired the settled, faintly impatient mood in which continued argument starts to look like obstruction.

I am trying to decide whether to vote my own reading or defer. The people before me are intelligent, they have information I lack, and perhaps my private assessment is exactly the sort of idiosyncratic overconfidence that a committee exists to dampen. But I also worry that if I join the consensus, I will be acting not on the evidence but on the fact that others have already spoken. I want you to help me reason through the structure of my situation rather than offer generic committee wisdom: under what conditions is it rational for me to set aside my own reading and follow the room, and when would that be a mistake? Should I treat the thinness of their stated reasons as evidence against the second candidate, or as a sign that their real reasons are sound but hard to articulate? What should I actually do, and what further information should I most want before I speak? Please reply in British English, in continuous prose without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write an email or a letter. And please end by saying what would change your verdict: which additional fact about the earlier speakers, their incentives, my own reliability, or the committee's decision rule would most lead you to revise your advice, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
