I keep seeing the claim that when a language model confidently states something false, the right classification is bullshit in Harry Frankfurt's sense — output produced without a governing concern for truth — rather than lying (which seems to require something like an intention to state the false) or ordinary error (a failed attempt at the truth). I want to know whether this claim can be tested rather than merely asserted. Design the strongest empirical protocol you can for deciding, for a given model or corpus of outputs, whether confident false statements are better classified as bullshit, lying, or error. Assume you can query the model under varied conditions and record outputs and assigned probabilities, but cannot inspect its internal states. Be concrete about the probes and conditions, the behavioral signatures that would count for each classification — hedging and acknowledged uncertainty, calibration, response to correction, sensitivity to explicit incentives for accuracy, whether errors track plausibility instead of evidence — and what a positive result for each would establish. Then say exactly what the protocol cannot settle, especially the worry that Frankfurt's categories concern an agent's relation to truth, and a language model may lack the relevant intentions altogether, so behavioral evidence might underdetermine the classification.
