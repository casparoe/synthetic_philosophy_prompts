I've been chewing on a problem about abandoned code and orphaned texts and I want someone to genuinely tear into an idea I've been calling a "keeper's easement", not just tell me it's interesting. The standard story treats an absent owner as all-or-nothing: either the work still belongs to the vanished creator or defunct company and any use is technically infringement until some absurdly long copyright term expires, or it's "abandonware" and everyone treats it as a free-for-all once the owner is unreachable. Both feel wrong. My proposal: if a identifiable community has kept a dead work alive for a substantial stretch — hosting it, patching it, keeping a text in print or answering questions about it — they should acquire a limited, use-specific right, but not title. They can continue doing exactly what they'd been doing (hosting the mirror, shipping bugfixes, distributing noncommercially to their actual users) even if the original rightsholder reappears and objects. But they can't expand into anything new, and the reappearing owner regains control of future direction, licensing, and commercial use. The keepers get an easement; the owner keeps the land.

The analogy I keep using is a footpath: someone owns the field, but if people have walked the same route for twenty years the owner usually can't just fence it off. The occupied ground here is a fork, a download page, a workflow. Example: a small scientific library from 2004 whose author vanished; a grad student maintains a mirror and patches it for fifteen years; the author's daughter surfaces and wants to sell a SaaS version. On my rule, she can't kill the mirror, but she can sell the SaaS.

I know pieces of this are hanging around in orphan-works policy, adverse possession, and copyleft. What I can't tell is whether they actually compose or I'm dragging in a real-estate idea that makes no sense for intangible goods. So: give me the three strongest objections to a keeper's easement, and say plainly which are fatal rather than just awkward. In particular — does treating "the community that kept it alive" as a single thing hide the fact that communities are loose, self-selected, and probably unaccountable to anyone? Does the whole idea just reward whoever was willing to infringe first and maintain the server? And is the owner's right to "future direction" even coherent given that keepers can usually fork and carry on — which of my two intuitions do I have to abandon to keep the other?
