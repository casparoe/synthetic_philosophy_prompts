A few of us in my economics department have been running an informal reading group on the foundations of game theory, and we've been stuck on the common prior assumption for two months. I don't think anyone has moved an inch. One camp — mostly the people who do mechanism design — treats the common prior assumption as basically a consistency condition. They lean on the agreement theorem: if two Bayesian agents share a prior and have common knowledge of each other's posterior beliefs, they cannot disagree. So on their view, if you want to explain persistent disagreement, you have to locate it either in differences in information or in a failure of common knowledge; letting two rational agents simply have different priors is, as they put it, close to licensing stubbornness. The other camp — mostly the more decision-theoretic people — says priors are just where you start, subjective degrees of belief, and the theorem is nearly vacuous; it only yields agreement because the setup already assumes it. They also keep pushing the point that the assumption is empirically slippery: mere disagreement never by itself forces you to reject a common prior, since you can always chalk it up to differences in information, so the common prior assumption doesn't really get falsified by observing disagreement.

I half suspect the two sides are using 'prior' in subtly different senses, or that this is really a fight about whether we're doing normative epistemology or positive economics, but I can't tell if that's right or just my exaustion talking. What kind of disagreement is this actually — a verbal one, a difference about what counts as a standard of rationality, a hidden formal question, or just a bedrock intuition about Bayesianism that neither side will argue for? And given your diagnosis, is there anything that would actually move it, or should we expect it to stay stuck?
