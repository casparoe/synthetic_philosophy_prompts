I have recently retired from a career in the natural sciences and have been using my time to read more philosophy, especially the epistemology of probability, coincidence, and anecdotal evidence. In an article written for a general scientific readership, I came across the following characterization of what the birthday paradox teaches us about coincidences:

"The birthday paradox shows that events that feel like million-to-one flukes are actually almost guaranteed to occur. With only 23 people there is already a better-than-even chance that two of them share a birthday; more generally, when a rare event has one chance in a million on any given occasion, but there are many millions of occasions on which it could occur, the chance that it occurs at least once becomes overwhelming. This is why stories of uncanny coincidence should not move us: each such story simply selects, after the fact, one of the many improbable-sounding events that chance was bound to produce somewhere or to someone eventually. The correct conclusion is that an anecdote of a striking coincidence can never be evidence for a hidden cause."

I can follow the birthday calculation itself, and the general warning that we need a baseline before being impressed by rare events seems to me sound. Yet the movement from the probability that *some* coincidence occurs, to the claim that *this particular* coincidence cannot be evidence, strikes me as too quick, and I would like to know whether that impression is right or whether I am merely defending my own fascination with such stories.

Could you audit this paragraph for me? Please work through it roughly sentence by sentence, distinguishing what is mathematically solid, what is a defensible simplification, and what is subtly wrong or overstated. In particular, I would like to understand what exactly the birthday paradox licenses us to conclude about striking coincidences; what conditions—such as independence, the way the class of possible coincidences is specified, and whether the event was specified in advance—a careful treatment would need to make explicit; and under what circumstances, if any, an anecdote of a coincidence can legitimately count as evidence for a non-chance explanation. I am less interested in a list of fallacies than in knowing which parts of the quoted paragraph I can keep and which I should revise before repeating it to others.
