I'm a philosophy professor, and in my graduate seminar on vagueness and social ontology last week a student stopped me mid-lecture and said "a language is a dialect with an army and navy" — he was objecting to the very question of when Latin becomes Italian. His point was that asking for the date, or even the century, misunderstands the phenomenon: there's no linguistic moment at which Latin turned into Italian, just an unbroken drift in the speech of ordinary people, and the only real break is political — at some point a Tuscan-based written standard got promoted as "Italian" and everything else got demoted to "dialect." I think there's something right in what he said, but I also think the slogan is doing a lot of hidden work, and different readings of it have very different amounts of support. Can you pull apart the precise claims that saying might be making — about mutual intelligibility, about the difference between a literary standard and naturally acquired speech, about the role of armies and navies (i.e. state power) in naming varieties, about whether the dialect/language distinction is ever a linguistic one, and about whether "Latin becomes Italian" is a fale question, a vague-boundary question, or a question about labels — and tell me for each reading how plausible it is and whether it actually licenses his conclusion? I want to hand the class a list of readings with verdicts on Tuesday.
