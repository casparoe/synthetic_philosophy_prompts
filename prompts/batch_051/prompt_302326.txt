I have been turning over an asymmetry in ordinary political judgment that I cannot justify, and I would like your help in deciding whether it is defensible. In most wealthy democracies, people treat it as a basic right that a citizen may move from a depressed region to a prosperous one for work and, after satisfying the usual residence conditions, draw the same benefits as everyone else; any proposal to restrict that movement on fiscal grounds would be condemned as creating second-class citizens. Yet many of the same people regard it as perfectly legitimate for a state to refuse a foreigner who is just as poor, just as employable, and just as able to integrate, permission to enter and do exactly the same thing, on the ground that a welfare state presupposes bounded solidarity and would unravel under free movement. What I do not see is why the relevant boundary should be national. Migration within a country also creates fiscal strain on receiving regions, and we do not give those regions the power to close their borders when that happens. Is there a principle that reproduces the exact asymmetry ordinary opinion defends, or is the asymmetry just an inconsistency we happen to live with? Please formulate the strongest candidate principle you can, test it against at least one nearby case that puts pressure on it, and say whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up. End with a final line in exactly this form: \answer{keep}, \answer{reverse}, or \answer{give up}.
