Take the 1991 Mount Pinatubo eruption and treat it as an unplanned, uncontrolled deployment of stratospheric aerosol injection, then analyze it through the lens of the three recurring debates in geoengineering: moral hazard, termination shock, and who decides. For anyone whose memory is fuzzy, the salient facts are these: Pinatubo injected something like 15–20 million tonnes of sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere, the sulfate aerosols it formed cooled the planet by roughly half a degree Celsius for a year or two, and the cooling snapped back when the aerosols washed out. Nobody planned it, nobody consented to it, and nobody could extend it or turn it off. I realize the eruption wasn't geoengineering; treat that as the point of the exercise and run the analogy hard.

Specifically: what would the moral-hazard argument predict that observers would take away from Pinatubo, and what would the termination-shock argument instead put at the center of the episode? Does the fact that the "deployment" had no decider make the who-decides objection more urgent, or does it show the objection is misframed when applied to a natural event? And then the two-way question: what does this case, read as a natural deployment, do to the theory — does it mostly confirm, mostly refute, or mostly complicate the standard moral-hazard and termination-shock worries about actual sulfate aerosol injection?
