I serve on the Municipal Code Revision Commission of a city that has finally begun clearing out its dead-letter ordinances, and one case has split us badly. A 1947 ordinance makes it a misdemeanor to offer fortune-telling, palm reading, phrenology, or similar services for a fee, yet the city attorney can find no record of a single citation issued under it in over sixty years. Meanwhile, our street fairs, seasonal markets, and two storefront psychics operate openly with ordinary business licenses, and no one in city government has objected in living memory. The immediate trigger is an application to open a permanent tarot parlor downtown. One bloc on the commission wants us to recommend that the council repeal the ordinance; another wants us to recommend that the city attorney begin enforcing it against both the new parlor and the existing shops; a third bloc wants to leave the ordinance on the books while continuing not to enforce it, treating it as a kind of formal statement of civic disapproval. I went into this believing that a law remains a law until it is repealed, and that non-enforcement is simply a discretionary choice that does not change the law's validity. Then I spent a long evening arguing with a friend who took the opposite view: she insisted that six decades of non-enforcement changes what the law is in practice, that suddenly enforcing it now would be a trap for people who reasonably built livelihoods on the city's own toleration, and that keeping an unenforced prohibition on the books corrodes respect for law more than honest repeal would. I need to bring the commission something more careful than either of us managed.

Draft the short written opinion I would submit to the commission. It should recommend one of the three options as its actionable bottom line, explain what desuetude is and whether a legal system committed to the rule of law should recognize something like it, and give the strongest argument for the option you ultimately reject along with an honest account of why it does not carry the day. Keep the reasoning at the level of legal philosophy and institutional practice rather than turning it into a survey of what particular jurisdictions have done or a treatise on First Amendment law; the other commissioners trust me to surface the governing principles and not just the case law.
