I am a PhD student in philosophy of mind, working at the interface with cognitive neuropsychiatry, and I am drafting a chapter on belief attribution. I keep returning to the debate about whether delusions are beliefs, but the literature often talks past itself because it rarely fixes a working account of belief before assessing particular cases. To avoid that, I have written down five criteria that I would like to test, and I have chosen a case that seems to sit precisely at the boundary.

Act as an expert philosopher of mind with a specialism in the philosophy of psychiatry and doxastic states. Think carefully before you settle on a verdict. Begin by briefly brainstorming the strongest considerations on each side, then work through the case criterion by criterion, and only then offer your overall judgement.

Here are my working criteria for S believes that P. I intend them as a cluster: no single criterion is necessary or sufficient on its own, but a substantial overlap across the five is required.

1. Sincere assertion: S is disposed to assert that P in appropriate contexts and to stand by the assertion without irony, play-acting, or merely parroting someone else.
2. Inferential integration: S is disposed to use P as a premise when reasoning about related matters and to accept at least the obvious consequences of P.
3. Action guidance: S is disposed to act in ways that would make sense if P were true whenever P is relevant to S's practical concerns.
4. Affective congruity: S's emotional and attentional responses fit P's truth, for example fear when P reports a credible danger or distress when P reports a loss.
5. Evidence sensitivity: S is responsive to counter-evidence about P, revising confidence, offering reasoned defence, or at least showing genuine discomfort when the grounds for P are challenged.

The case is R, a man in his sixties. R insists, apparently with full sincerity, that the woman with whom he has lived for forty years is not his wife but a nearly identical impostor. He defends this claim fluently in conversation and is not confused in other respects. Yet he continues to live in the same house as the woman, shares meals and finances with her, and has never contacted the police or searched for the woman he calls his real wife. His affect while discussing the supposed impostor is notably flat, without the alarm or grief one would expect. When challenged, he offers ad hoc explanations for how an impostor might know private family details, but he does not spontaneously draw further inferences from his claim, and he does not revise it in light of his friends' scepticism, which he acknowledges but dismisses as understandable.

I chose this case because it appears to split my criteria down the middle, and because it is exactly the kind of case the two-factor theory of delusions was framed to explain. On that theory, monothematic delusions like R's arise from two factors working together. The first is an anomalous experience: in Capgras-type cases, something like a striking loss of the felt sense of familiarity when seeing the known face, so that the woman no longer feels like his wife. This experience supplies the content that something is wrong with this woman. The second factor is a deficit in the system that evaluates candidate beliefs for plausibility and evidence, which would normally lead the person to reject an outlandish explanatory hypothesis. R accepts the hypothesis his mind generates but then quarantines it from full revision and from much of his practical reasoning. Under that description, the state looks partly belief-like and partly not.

The questions I would like you to answer are these. Does R's state satisfy my five criteria for the belief that the woman is an impostor? Go through the criteria one by one and say, for each, whether R clearly meets it, clearly fails it, or whether the criterion is too coarse to tell. Then give your all-things-considered verdict. Finally, tell me whether the case should make me revise any of my criteria, and if so how. In particular, is it acceptable for a theory of belief to say that R partially believes the content, or should an analysis of belief avoid verdicts of that kind?

Please also indicate how confident you are in your claims, and single out the single point on which you are least certain. Write in British English.
