ok so a friend who does evolutionary psychology keeps saying the mind is a swiss army knife and not a general purpose computer. his line is that cognition is a bundle of specialized gadgets — face recognition, cheater detection, naive physics, a language thing — each shaped for one job and pretty bad at the others, and he uses that to argue there's no real domain-general reasoning, just a thin wrapper over the toolbox. someone in my ai reading group has started importing the same move: if human cognition is a bag of special-purpose modules, then looking for one general learning algorithm is a mistake, and benchmarks that test "general" ability are testing a myth. i think the analogy is doing a lot of work in both places and i want to know how far it actually goes before i let it do either job.

so: give me the correspondence table. what's the blade, the scissors, the corcscrew, the handle, the little toothpick nobody uses, the fact that the manufacturer decides the toolset in advance. then push on where it breaks. a swiss army knife has a fixed set of tools chosen before you ever use it; the mind seems to build at least some abilities from experience and use. the tools in the knife don't share parts, but mental "tools" plausibly share memory, attention, an inference engine. and the knife's tools can be used one at a time, side by side, while cognitive tasks like planning a move mix physics, social prediction, timing, and spatial reasoning all at once — which is basically fodor's old worry that central cognition has to be domain-general and unencapsulated. which of these disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion that there's no domain-general reasoning, and which are just cosmetic? does the conclusion survive them, or is the knife doing the arguing? and if it's a bad fit, what's a better analogy — or is the whole tool metaphor part of the problem?
