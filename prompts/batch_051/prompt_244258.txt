I work on financial regulation, and I keep being puzzled by a practice whose philosophical commitments I have never seen anyone spell out. Consider quantitative easing as it has been conducted by major central banks in the years since the 2008 financial crisis. A central bank creates new reserve balances (liabilities of the central bank, held by commercial banks) and uses them to buy long-term government bonds, and sometimes other assets, from private holders. The private sector ends up holding a different financial claim rather than more real goods; nothing is produced or consumed in the transaction itself. Central bankers often insist that monetary policy cannot create real resources or real wealth. Yet the same institutions describe the operation as easing financial conditions, supporting asset prices, and boosting measured household wealth, and they defend it partly through the wealth effect: households that feel richer because their assets have appreciated will spend more.

Reconstruct the implicit theory of money, liquidity, and wealth that this practice presupposes. I want a careful reconstruction rather than a description of policy mechanics, so please address the following.

First, what must the practice presuppose about the nature of wealth for its practitioners to regard the same operation as not creating real wealth while also supporting wealth? Is that a coherent pair of commitments, or does the practice tacitly run two theories of wealth at once, one in which wealth is a claim on future real output and one in which wealth is whatever a marked-to-market balance sheet says it is?

Second, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Does it strictly presuppose that the liquidity and composition of private balance sheets matter for aggregate demand, or could several different transmission stories all license the same operations?

Third, which rival views of money, liquidity, and wealth does the practice quietly rule out, and which does it remain compatible with? Consider at least: a view on which liquidity is a property of an individual asset in a particular market rather than an aggregate condition that policy can create; a view on which the quantity of base money matters mainly through direct spending rather than through asset prices and portfolio effects; and a strict long-run monetary-neutrality view.

Fourth, where is the practice philosophically underdetermined? Identify the places where two different underlying theories of value or liquidity would license exactly the same balance-sheet operations, and say what turns on the choice between them.

Finally, tell me what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced. If a policymaker genuinely held that wealth consists only in claims on future real output, and that liquidity is not something a central bank can create but only something a market grants to an asset, how would they have to redescribe quantitative easing, and what would they be committed to changing about the policy itself?

Do not settle for the conclusion that the practice is merely about confidence or merely an accounting operation. The point is to extract the precise philosophical content about what money is, what liquidity is, and what wealth is that the practice needs in order to make sense, and to say where it is genuinely ambiguous.
