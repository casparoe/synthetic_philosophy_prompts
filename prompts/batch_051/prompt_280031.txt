On a rationalist forum I read regularly, someone recently ended a debate about whether writing systems shape cognition by declaring that Chinese characters are ideograms: they stand for concepts directly rather than for sounds, so Chinese writing is a fundamentally different kind of script from alphabetic writing, which merely encodes pronunciation. That move was doing real work in the argument, because it was then used to support the further claim that reading Chinese must take a less phonological route from print to meaning than reading English. I want to respond without talking past the person, but I can't pin down what exactly I disagree with, since "Chinese characters are ideograms" seems to bundle several claims together. Can you unpack the distinct precise things that slogan could be asserting — about what a script encodes (phonemes, syllables, morphemes, concepts), about the history of the characters, about how Chinese readers actually map signs to meanings, and about the contrast with alphabetic writing it presumes, including whether that contrast treats alphabets as faithfully phonemic — and say for each whether it is a platitude, a substantive truth, or false? Then identify which readings would actually support the conclusion that Chinese reading bypasses phonology, and which would be needed to support the stronger claim that Chinese characters encode semantics in a way English spelling does not. A menu of readings with verdicts would help more than a single interpretation, so I can see exactly where the slogan goes from oversimplified to false.
