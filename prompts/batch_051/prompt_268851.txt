I am the Secretary of the Society for the Study of Language, a learned society for linguists and philosophers of language. Our Code of Conduct, adopted in 1974, contains this clause, Article III, Section 2:

"Members shall not use, publish, or cause to be circulated any word or expression that holds any person or class of persons up to contempt or ridicule on account of race, color, religion, national origin, or sex."

A member, Dr. Ellis, has just released an open-source large language model trained on an uncurated corpus of billions of web pages. The training corpus contains racial and ethnic slurs; Dr. Ellis herself has never written or spoken them and says she would refuse to do so, but the model has learned to reproduce them. If asked, it will produce one as an example of a term that a content-moderation system would flag as abusive, and a determined user can prompt it to produce the same word as an insult. The release also includes a filter that labels these strings as hateful. Another member has filed a complaint, alleging that in releasing the model Dr. Ellis has "caused to be circulated" language that holds groups up to contempt or ridicule. Dr. Ellis replies that a model has no attitudes and so cannot hold anyone in contempt; that she circulated a research instrument, not a slur; and that a model unable to represent slurs could not be used to detect and study the very speech the clause was meant to suppress.

Please rule on the complaint. Set out what each of the following gives you: (i) the plain words of the clause; (ii) the purpose of the clause, as its 1974 drafters would have stated it; (iii) what the drafters would most plausibly have said if asked about a machine of this kind; and (iv) what a reasonable member today would take the clause to require. Then say which approach should control, and why. In ruling, address whether the use/mention distinction holds up when a system reproduces a slur with no speaker behind it, and whether Dr. Ellis's research purpose should matter to the verdict.
