I keep going back and forth on an essay idea about Keynes on probability and uncertainty, and I'd like comments on whether the reading of Keynes is fair and whether the argumemt itself is any good. Dont worry about bibliography or citations.

Working title: "Keynes's real break wasn't risk versus uncertainty."

The essay argues that the familiar Keynesian distinction between risk (probabilities you can quantify) and uncertainty (cases where you can't) is a later simplification and sort of misses his actual target. In the Treatise on Probability, Keynes treats probability as a logical relation between propositions: a rational degree of partial entailment, not a frequency and not a subjective degree of belief. His point there is that this relation is often non-numerical and even non-comparable. You can know that one hypothesis is more probable than another without being able to say by how much, and sometimes two probabilities can't be ranked at all. So expected-utility maximization, which needs exact cardinal probabilities, is rarely applicable even before we get to the radical uncertainty of the General Theory.

The General Theory adds a different claim: in many economic decisions the relevant probability relation is so weak that there is no scientific basis for any probability at all, and people fall back on convention, habit, and what he called animal spirits. The essay's thesis is that this isn't really a two-category ontology of risk and uncertainty. It's one continuous problem: most real inductive situations have too little structure for numerical probability, and the interesting question is what rational action can look like when probabilities are vague, non-comparable, or weakly grounded.

The second half would push on something. If probabilities can be non-comparable, what replaces the decision rule? Keynes doesn't give one, and maybe that's because his account is deliberately modest: he's describing what rational belief is under uncertainty, not handing out a mechanical procedure. I want to argue that this modesty is a feature, not a bug, but I worry that it makes the view less useful for anyone who actually has to act.

Comments I'd most like: (a) Is the reading of the Treatise as "probabilities are logical relations, often non-numerical and non-comparable" right, or am I overstating it? (b) Does the second half's argument about the missing decision rule hold up, or is there a natural Keynesian answer I'm ignoring? (c) Is the framing "one continuous problem rather than two categories" actually interesting, or is it just terminological?
