Me and my friend spent way too long tonight arguing about Nick Bostrom's singleton idea, and the whole thing kept coming back to this bit from "What Is a Singleton?":

"Nevertheless, all the possible singletons share one essential property: the ability to solve global coordination problems. Intelligent species that develop the capability to solve global coordination problems, such as those listed in the next section, may in the long run develop along very different trajectories than species that lack this capacity. This is what makes it useful to have a unifying concept for such a diverse set of structures."

He kept saying it's basically definitional—that a singleton just is the thing that can solve the big coordination failures, like arms races and space colonization races and so on, and that's why the concept earns its keep. I think that's definately off, or at least it does something sneaky with the word "solve." Please make the strongest case you can that Bostrom's "essential property" claim is false or misleading. Specifically: argue that a single decision-making agency at the highest level doesnt guarantee the ability to solve global coordination problems, and that the ability to solve them doesnt require a singleton either. Engage with the difference between imposing decisions and actually solving coordination problems, and with where the informational or computational bottleneck sits when one agency tries to "solve" coordination among a huge, dispersed population. Then say whether the friend's position, taken charitably, has any real content or whether it just collapses into "whatever works works."
