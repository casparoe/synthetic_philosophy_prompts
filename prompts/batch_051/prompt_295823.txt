In 2006 the German Federal Constitutional Court struck down a provision of the Aviation Security Act that authorized the military to shoot down a hijacked civilian aircraft if it was about to be used as a weapon against people on the ground. The court held that the state may not deliberately kill innocent passengers even to prevent a much larger number of deaths, because doing so would treat them as mere objects rather than as persons, and it grounded this prohibition in the guarantee of human dignity together with the right to life. Analyze this decision through the lens of the debate over whether human dignity is a foundational normative concept or an empty placeholder. On one side, the Kantian tradition treats dignity as the ground of rights and as the basis of the prohibition on using persons merely as means; on the other, deflationists such as Ruth Macklin argue that dignity does no independent work and that appeals to it can be replaced, without loss, by respect for autonomy or respect for persons. Give a disciplined mapping: what each side should say the court's reasoning really amounts to, which features of the decision a Kantian-dignity account explains and which it misdescribes, and whether a deflationary account can fully restate the court's reasoning without the concept of dignity. Press especially on whether the passengers' autonomy can bear the weight here, given that they are in a situation they cannot influence and their consent is unavailable. Conclude by saying what the case shows about the dignity debate: does dignity carry justificatory and explanatory weight in this decision, or is it a rhetorical overlay on a principle of respect for persons that can be stated without it?
