I recently agreed to facilitate a small adult-education discussion group at my church on how to read Scripture, and in the training materials the coordinator included a page summarizing the historical-critical method. The summary says, in effect, that the historical-critical method treats the Bible like any other ancient text and refuses to invoke divine inspiration as an explanatory factor; that it explains miracle stories as products of ordinary historical processes such as legend formation, redaction, and borrowing from neighboring cultures; that it reconstructs the sources, editorial layers, and community agendas behind the texts; and that, because it shows the Bible to be a patchwork of human interests and later revision, it makes the text impossible to receive as revelation in any robust sense. The coordinator plainly regards this as the settled, consensus view, and the materials frame the only live question as whether one accepts the method and gives up a high view of Scripture, or defends revelation by rejecting the method.

This strikes me as at least partly a caricature — I know enough to be aware that many devout scholars practice historical criticism without abandoning their faith — but I do not know the field well enough to say exactly where the account goes wrong or stretches the evidence. Could you go through the key claims in that summary and tell me which ones a careful practitioner of historical criticism would accept, which are overstated or imprecise, and which are quietly smuggling in a theological conclusion the method itself does not establish? In particular, I would like to know whether the final either/or — accept the method and abandon revelation, or keep revelation and reject the method — actually follows from anything accurate in the summary, or whether it forecloses a third position on how to read Scripture as both history and revelation.
