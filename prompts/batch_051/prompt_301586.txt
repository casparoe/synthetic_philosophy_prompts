I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I keep getting assigned pieces about whether some new technology is "political" — smart-city sensor networks, AI content-moderation systems, highway projects, that kind of thing. The newsroom argument is always the same: one editor says all technology is political because every design choice reflects somebody's interests; another says calling a technology political is activist hot air unless a human is discriminating on the spot. My assigning editor keeps reaching for technological determinism — the claim that a tool will inevitably reshape society in a certain direction — and I want a more precise alternative to print instead. A colleague pointed me to the old debate over whether artifacts have politics: the idea that some technical systems have politics built into their design or their normal operation, not just in how people later use them. I sort of remember a two-part distinction: (a) an artifact whose design settles a political question all by itself, like a highway overpass deliberately built too low for buses, so the design itself decides who gets to the beach; and (b) a technology whose routine operation requires or strongly pushes a society toward a particular kind of political organization, like a centralized energy system versus a distributed one. I'm not sure I have that line right, and I definately can't turn it into a reusable screen. This comes up every few weeks, and the stakes are real: overclaim and we look like we're editorializing; underclaim and we look oblivious.

Act as a philosopher of technology who also has a working journalist's sense of deadlines. I want a decision procedure I can run on any technology I'm assigned to cover, in less then an afternoon, using what a reporter can actually get — specs, plans, interviews with the designers, the history of the project, obvious physical or interface choices — to decide whether I'm justified in writing that the artifact itself has politics built in, and with how much confidence. The final procedure has to be usable by someone without the theory (me and my editors), so the key distinctions have to survive in plain language. Make it a checklist in order: the questions to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops and says don't make the stronger claim, and where it kicks the decision to an editor because the remaining question is a values call rather than a factual one. Mark the false-positive and false-negative traps explicitly: a technology that looks political but where the claim would be weak, and a technology that looks neutral but where the procedure should still catch something.

Before you write the final procedure, brainstorm the ways a framework like this could misfire in a real newsroom, and think carefully about where your own political sympathies might leak into the checklist. Then run the finished procedure on three cases: (1) a low-clearance overpass built on a road to a public beach; (2) a social media feed algorithm whose company-chosen objective function optimizes for engagement; (3) a city app for reporting potholes that requires a smartphone and a stable street address. Make sure at least one of the three comes out in a way I won't like — either the procedure says a technology I thought was neutral is political, or it forces me to drop a claim I wanted to print. End by saying what the checklist cannot catch, so I don't overuse it.
