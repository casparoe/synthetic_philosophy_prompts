I'm in an upper-division metaethics seminar, and after several weeks on evolutionary debunking arguments I've come to accept the core debunking claim. The position runs like this: evolutionary pressures have substantially shaped the content of human moral judgments, selecting for responses that served survival and reproduction in social environments, while those pressures were not selecting for accurate representation of any independent moral facts. If moral realism—especially the non-naturalist variety that posits stance-independent moral facts to which we somehow have reliable access—were true, it would be a striking coincidence if judgments shaped that way were by and large correct. So I regard our moral judgments as unreliable indicators of any such reality, and I'm inclined to draw a broadly constructivist lesson from this.

What I want is an honest map of what would count against this position, not a literature survey. Please respond with exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion. Rank them from a consideration that would be fatal to the debunking argument down to one that would merely be awkward. For each item, say which step of the argument it targets—the empirical premise about the extent of evolutionary influence on judgment contents, the claim that evolution did not track moral truth, the inference from non-tracking to unreliability, or the move to abandon realism—and say whether a sophisticated defender of the debunking view would concede that the consideration genuinely counts against the position or would dismiss it as already presupposing the realism in question. For my own part, I would find a concrete evolutionary mechanism by which selection on a general moral capacity could nevertheless press judgment contents toward accuracy more persuasive than an abstract safety-style reply, but rank by strength for the position generally rather than by what I happen to find intuitive.
