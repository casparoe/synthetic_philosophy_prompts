So here's the situation. Last year I got roped into editing our county historical society's quarterly. It's a volunteer operation, four issues a year, mostly local history pieces by amateur researchers and the occasional retired professor. After the spring issue we got two complaints. One letter called our article on the 1874 courthouse fire "not objective" because it kept guessing at motives. Another said a different piece was worse: it listed documents in order and pretended it had no point of view at all. The board has now decided I need too write up an actual procedure for deciding whether a submission is "objective enough" before we attach our name to it.

I've been reading around, and I understand this is an old fight in the philosophy of history. There's the Rankean idea that the historian should just show the past as it actually was, and there's the sceptical line you get from E.H. Carr and later from people like Hayden White: every historical account is selected, arranged into a story, and shaped by the historian's present concerns, so the real question isn't whether you have a point of view but whether you're honest and disciplined about it. I don't need another essay on that. I need it turned into something my volunteer editors can actually run, grounded in those arguments but with the theory kept out of the final checklist except where it earns its place.

Could you write me a step-by-step review procedure for a submitted article? The editors are smart but not trained in this, and they have about an hour and a half per piece. The procedure should be a list of questions in order, with what counts as passing or failing each step, where we stop and send the piece back with requested changes, and which steps are unavoidably judgment calls where a person has to decide. I especially want it to distinguish "objective" meaning accurate and fair from "objective" meaning a recording device, since I think those get conflated by everyone here, including me.

Then run the procedure on three submission types we've actually seen:

1. A piece on a 1920s mill strike that quotes the town records correctly but orders events so the strikers come out as the obvious aggressors, and never flags that choice.

2. A piece on an 1880s murder trial that reconstructs the accused's thoughts and dialogue from no sources at all, with a footnote calling it "imaginative reconstruction."

3. A dry chronicle of town council votes on road bonds, 1910–1930, which the author keeps telling us is the most objective thing we've ever been sent because there's no interpretation.

And do me a favor: tell me straight if the procedure kills article 3, because I'm pretty sure the board will hate that outcome. Finally, say plainly what the procedure cannot catch — the ways an article can still mislead even when it passes every step.
