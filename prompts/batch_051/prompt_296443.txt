I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm preparing a long piece on what the modern causal-reasoning toolbox—Judea Pearl's ladder of causation and the do-calculus in particular—can do retroactively for old scientific controversies. I want to use the mid-century smoking-and-lung-cancer dispute as the worked example. I know the standard story: the prospective cohort studies with their steep dose-response gradient, R. A. Fisher's objection that an unmeasured constitutional factor might make people both smoke and develop cancer, the practical impossibility of deciding the matter with a randomized trial, and the Surgeon General's 1964 judgment that the observational evidence added up to causation. What I want is a rigorous mapping onto Pearl's framework, in both directions.

First, classify the actual claims in the debate by rung of the ladder: which were associational, which were interventional, which were counterfactual, and where did the disagreement really sit? Second, reconstruct Fisher's objection as a formal confounding problem: specify a plausible structural causal model with smoking, an unmeasured genotype, and lung cancer, and explain precisely what the do-calculus would need—back-door adjustment, front-door considerations, or something else—to identify the effect of intervening on smoking from the data then available, and why it was not identifiable. Third, say what the much-cited criteria for causal judgment in epidemiology (strength, consistency, specificity, temporality, biological gradient, plausibility, coherence, experiment, analogy) amount to from the standpoint of the causal hierarchy: are they an informal identification strategy, a checklist of diagnostics for hidden confounding, or something the formalism cannot express? Then tell me where the framework breaks or falls silent in this case: the unmeasured factor that was never operationalized, the gap between a population-level average effect and the counterfactual for a particular smoker, the place of biological mechanism and plausibility, and the fact that the identifiable effect depends on the causal model one is willing to assert in the first place. Finally, argue in the other direction: does the smoking episode vindicate the causal hierarchy's way of disciplining causal talk, expose its assumptions, or both—and what should a working journalist say about the fact that one of the most consequential causal conclusions of the twentieth century rested not on a randomized experiment but on exactly the kind of structural assumptions Pearl's formalism renders explicit?
