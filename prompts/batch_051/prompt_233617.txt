I'm the journalist/lay member of our regional health system's scarce-resource steering commitee. We're rewriting the pandemic triage protocol and we're split three ways. One camp wants a scoring system that sends ventilators to whoever is expected to gain the most quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs) — which in practice favors younger patients and people without certain disabilities. A second camp wants to save the most lives, period, and not let any "quality of life" judgment into the allocation; they'd use a first-come-first-served queue among patients judged likely to survive ICU admission. A third camp says a public institution shouldn't be in the business of ranking the value of different patients' remaining years at all, and wants a lottery among those likely to benefit.

I cover science and ideas, mostly health economics, so my understanding is that QALYs were built for ex ante decisions about statistical lives, not for picking between two named patients in a hallway. But our protocol gets written ex ante and then applied to named patients later, which is exactly the trickiest spot. Draft the memo I can circulate this week. Recommend one approach (or a definite hybrid), with the real reasons, and treat the lottery camp as more than squeamishness — if they're wrong, explain where their argument about what a public institution may properly do actually fails.

Sections: "What we're deciding and when" (~100 words), "The case for the QALY/life-years score" (~250 words), "The case against ranking lives" (~300 words), "Recommendation" (~200 words).
