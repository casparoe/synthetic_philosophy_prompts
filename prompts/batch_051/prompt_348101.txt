I don't have any training in philosophy or mathematics beyond the usual, but I fell down a rabbit hole after learning that the natural numbers can be built up in set theory in different ways — in one construction 4 turns out to be the set {0,1,2,3}, so 2 is a member of 4, while in another construction each number is just the set containing only its predecessor, so 2 is not a member of 4 — and I came out the other side with a view I would like pressure-tested. The view is that a number has no hidden inner nature: it is just a position in a pattern, and everything about it is fixed by its relations to the other positions. All there is to being 2 is being the successor of 1, the predecessor of 3, the sum of 1 and 1, and so on; once all the relations of that kind are settled, there is nothing left over for 2 to be in itself. A consequence I accept is that the question of whether 2 is a member of 4 has no answer as a question about numbers themselves. If one encoding makes it true and another equally faithful encoding makes it false, I take that as evidence that membership is a feature of the encoding rather than of the number — rather like asking whether the chess queen is taller than the chess bishop: that is a fact about the physical pieces, not about the roles.

Given how obviously right this feels, I am suspicious of my own confidence, and I want a map of where the view is exposed. I am not looking for rival accounts or a survey; I want to know what would count against the position as I have described it. Which observations, arguments, or consequences should genuinely move me, and which would be decisive rather than merely awkward? For instance, I am unsettled by the thought of two positions that play exactly the same structural role within a single pattern — I think of i and -i in the complex numbers, which look interchangeable — and by the possibility that my view quietly strips such positions of their distinctness. I would also like to know whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability: if every proposed counterexample can be absorbed by saying that it is just a feature of the encoding rather than of the number, then I may have insulated the view from every possible objection, and I want an honest assessment of whether that is a defect for a view of this kind. Personally, I would be most persuaded by a case in which two equally faithful structures disagree about some question that I cannot convince myself is merely about the encoding.
