I’m a journalist covering science and ideas, and I’m working on a peice about mystics who claim the most important thing is ineffable. I keep getting stuck on whether “cannot be said” is supposed to mean “cannot be known” or just “cannot be put into ordinary propositional thought.” A source sent me this passage from The Cloud of Unknowing, an anonymous 14th-century English work, in Evelyn Underhill’s 1922 edition:

> For of all other creatures and their works, yea, and of the works of God’s self, may a man through grace have fullhead of knowing, and well he can think of them: but of God Himself can no man think. And therefore I would leave all that thing that I can think, and choose to my love that thing that I cannot think. For why; He may well be loved, but not thought. By love may He be gotten and holden; but by thought never. And therefore, although it be good sometime to think of the kindness and the worthiness of God in special, and although it be a light and a part of contemplation: nevertheless yet in this work it shall be cast down and covered with a cloud of forgetting. And thou shalt step above it stalwartly, but listily, with a devout and a pleasing stirring of love, and try for to pierce that darkness above thee. And smite upon that thick cloud of unknowing with a sharp dart of longing love; and go not thence for thing that befalleth.

I’m not after historical background; I want help seeing what philosophical move this writer is making. Help me work out whether he would say God can be known even though God can’t be thought, or whether “known” is the wrong word entirely; what the contrast between thought and love is doing; and what the “cloud of forgetting” is actually meant to cover. My editor wants it tight, so give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no intro or conclusion.
