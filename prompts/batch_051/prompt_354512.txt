Here's a rough set of criteria I use for when a chunk of the past deserves to be called a real historical period and not just a label we stick on afterwards. (1) Contemporaries had some sense they were in a distinct period -- not necessarily under the same name, but some shared self-conciousness. (2) The changes attributed to the period actually cluster: they move together in time, place and subject matter, rather than popping up all over the shop. (3) The boundaries can be drawn without absurd amounts of special pleading. Now test these on the 'Scientific Revolution', usually dated somewhere between about 1540 and 1700. Work through the three criteria one at a time and say whether the Scientific Revolution comes out real by my lights. In particular, is criterion (1) a problem -- surely nobody in 1640 went round calling themselves a participant in the 'Scientific Revolution'? If the case fails that criterion, does that show the criterion is too strict, or that the label still picks out something real retrospectively? To complicate things: the phrase itself only became standard well after the period ended, many of the natural philosophers involved thought they were recovering ancient learning rather than starting something new, and a fair amount of what counted as 'science' in 1600 still looked Aristotelian, alchemical or theological. But there is a real clustering of changes (heliocentrism, experimental anatomy, the new mechanics, mathematised physics). Tell me which criteria it passes, which it fails, whether that underdetermines the overall verdict, and whether my criteria need softening or a fourth condition. Keep it under 500 words.
