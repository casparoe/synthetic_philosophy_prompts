I want you to build me a thought experiment to a tight spec, about evidential cooperation in large worlds. I'll give you the setup so you don't need to know the literature.

So evidential decision theory (EDT) says: pick the act that would be the best news — maximize expected utility conditional on your act, not conditional on your causal influence. (That's why EDT one-boxes in Newcomb's problem.) Evidential cooperation in large worlds (ECL) pushes the idea further. Call a world "large" if it's big enough, or has enough independent agents, that basically any decision situation you're in is also being faced by lots of agents somewhere, with no causal contact with you. The ECL move is this: since your choice is evidence about what those similar agents choose, you should do whatever you'd most want to be true of the whole reference class, even when the act has zero causal effect on any of them. So in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma ECL can say cooperate, because conditional on you cooperating, the other guy and all the similar agents out there are more likely to cooperate, and you'd rather live in that world.

Now the spec. I want a unilateral case, not a prisoner's dilemma and not a Newcomb problem. The agent is deciding whether to do a small, costly, unobserved, unrewarded pro-social act — leaving a big tip at a restaraunt in a city they'll never revisit, say. Constraints: no perfect predictor, no clone or twin, no simulation, no exotic physics, and no hidden reputation or self-image effect. The correlation has to come from the structure of the large world itself, not from any stipulated predictor. The point of the exercise is to test whether ECL can generate a surprising "act" verdict in a completely mundane setting, or whether it collapses into a disguised Newcomb problem the moment the correlation is made explicit.

The verdicts to hit: causal decision theory says don't act; plain EDT with no large world (no similar agents anywhere) also says don't act; ECL says act.

Then run the checks. State the act, the options and payoffs, the prior about similar agents, and the reference class being used. Walk through the CDT, small-world EDT, and ECL calculations and show they come apart in the specified direction. Then identify exactly which structural feature flips the verdict, and remove just that feature — make the world provably small, a few thousand agents, and the agent knows it. Tell me whether the disagreement collapses and why. End with an honest paragraph on whether the case is an argument for ECL or just restates the view, and where a skeptic of ECL would attack it: the prior, the reference-class choice, or the inference from my act to others' acts.
