I’ve been down a rabbit hole lately on what people call repugnant markets—kidneys, price gouging, votes—and I keep getting stuck on one specific problem. The standard objection to vote selling, as I understand it, isn’t really about rich people buying elections or about sellers being coerced. It’s that putting a price on a vote corrupts what voting is supposed to mean. The vote is meant to be an inalienable expression of your standing as a citizen, and by treating it as a transferable asset you express the wrong kind of valuation, even if no coercion or inequality is involved. The act of selling itself, and the fact that it can be seen and known, is supposed to change the civic meaning of the institution.

Now suppose we make the whole thing invisible. Imagine a vote market where sellers and buyers interact only through a blind intermediary, payment is in an untraceable token, there is no bargaining or public record, and no one ever knows who sold or bought. There’s no moment of having sold your vote that others could witness; it just becomes a silent, anonymous drip of voting rights from people who care less to people who care more. Does the corruption objection survive this? I can see that worries about unequal influence and coercion might still be there, but the specifically anti-commodification argument seems to rely on the sale having social meaning—being seen, recognised, having a public shape. If the market is invisible and nobody experiences themselves as a seller in any public way, has the objection lost its object? Or does the corruption happen internally anyway, because the seller knows what they are doing, and anonymity just removes the shame that would otherwise restrain it? I’m interested in which premise of the argument is actually doing the work, and whether it can be repaired or whether it just turns into a different sort of objection.
