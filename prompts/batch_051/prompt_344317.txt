I'm preparing a short presentation for my school's philosophy club on self-deception, and I've gotten stuck on what looks like a basic inconsistency. If self-deception is genuinely a form of deception, then the person doing the deceiving has to know the unpleasant truth and deliberately hide it from the person being deceived. But when you deceive yourself, both roles are filled by the same person at the same time, which would seem to require simultaneously believing and not believing the same proposition. That leads me to think that self-deception, strictly speaking, cannot really occur, and that the term is just a loose label for ordinary wishful thinking, biased reasoning, or plain ignorance. But then I notice cases that do not fit the wishful-thinking story either: someone who persuades himself, against strong contrary evidence, that he is seriously ill, or that his friends secretly dislike him—beliefs that are painful rather than comforting. If self-deception is driven by wanting to feel better, these cases are hard to account for, and yet they still strike people as self-deception rather than mere error. So my question is this: granted that literal self-deception is impossible, what is the most reliable practical method for auditing my own beliefs and catching self-deception in progress, especially the painful kind, which cannot be unmasked just by asking what I want to be true? I am not looking for a taxonomy of theories; I want a concrete self-monitoring procedure, the way debiasing techniques are taught for confirmation bias.
