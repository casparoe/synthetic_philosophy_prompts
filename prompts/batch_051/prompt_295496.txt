I cover the science beat for a general-interest magazine, and lately a weird amount of my year has been about insects: mass-reared black soldier flies for animal feed, Wolbachia mosquito releases, RNAi sprays for crop pests, heat treatments for bedbugs in public housing, the usual roach-bait stuff. The questions from readers is always about whether the pest control is safe or effective, but the question that nags me is different: when is killing a very large number of insects a real ethical problem, and when is it basically a technical story I shouldn't frame as anything else? I keep seeing smart people argue past each other—one side treating insect suffering as a serious moral issue, the other saying the evidence for anything like felt experience is too weak to carry that weight—and I can't tell which parts of that disagreement are empirical, which are philosophical, and which are just a threshold chosen to deliver a conclusion.

What I'd like from you is not another essay but a decision procedure I can run in about 20 minutes before I frame a pest-control story: a sequence of questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to hand the decision to a person because the question is values rather then facts. It should be usable by someone with no formal ethics training and it should flag the steps that are really judgments dressed up as procedure. Then run it on three cases: a nursing home clearing a bedbug infestation with whole-room heat; a city releasing Wolbachia-infected mosquitoes to suppress dengue, where the mosquitoes aren't exactly killed one by one but do fail to reproduce; and a grain warehouse using pheromone disruption instead of pesticides for stored-product beetles, which doesn't poison the beetles but also does nothing about the ones already eating the grain. And please tell me where the procedure gives a result I probably won't like, because if it never does, it's probably not doing any work.
