In my corner of philosophy—metaphysics and the epistemology of modality—the debate over whether conceivability is a guide to possibility has gone stagnant in a way I find strange. The rationalist side claims that ideal positive conceivability tracks genuine metaphysical possibility: if a scenario can be positively conceived without contradiction by an ideally rational subject, that is evidence of possibility, perhaps even constitutive of it. The skeptical side replies that idealization is doing all the epistemic work, and that once you idealize sufficiently, the standard of rationality has already imported the modal facts it was supposed to track; real conceivers, they add, are never ideal. What puzzles me is that the two camps now grant the same familiar cases—philosophical zombies, water turning out to be XYZ, Hesperus distinct from Phosphorus—and simply read them differently: one side's evidence of robust modal knowledge is the other side's evidence of an over-strong standard. Nobody has moved in years. So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a verbal dispute about which epistemic practice gets to be called 'conceivability'? Is it a substantive dispute over whether there are strong necessities—necessities no amount of ideal a priori reflection could rule out—that could systematically mislead conceivability judgments? Or are the two sides answering different questions under a single heading: one about the cognitive capacities of actual reasoners, the other about the constitutive grounds of modal truth? My own hypothesis is that the debate is stalled because 'ideal conceivability' is being asked to serve two incompatible roles at once—a purified description of a human capacity and a constitutive criterion of possibility—and that separating these roles would dissolve much of the dispute. But I worry that diagnosis is too deflationary, since both sides take themselves to disagree about something real. Please diagnose the structure of the disagreement, engaging what each side actually says, and then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis—a formal result, an empirical finding, a distinction, or a reframing—or argue that no progress should be expected and why. Lead with your diagnosis in the first sentence, then give your reasoning without preamble and without restating the question.
