I am taking a course on Victorian literature and religion, and we have just finished reading Middlemarch alongside some of George Eliot’s early periodical essays. I am writing a final paper on the relation between Eliot’s critique of evangelicalism and her novelistic practice, and I want to work through one specific connection before I settle on an argument.

In her 1855 Westminster Review essay “Evangelical Teaching: Dr. Cumming,” George Eliot develops a charge that I can paraphrase in this way: the kind of preaching she examines invites believers to treat the firmness with which they hold certain doctrinal positions as the chief test of their religious seriousness, while treating the enlargement of ordinary human sympathy and the quality of their practical relations to other people as comparatively unimportant; the result, she suggests, is a piety that can coexist with narrowness of feeling and even with serious moral evasiveness. I want to test this charge against the novel.

Please read the Bulstrode narrative in Middlemarch through that claim from the essay. Does the novel bear out Eliot’s diagnosis of an evangelical cast of mind, or does it complicate it by separating Bulstrode’s particular bad faith from the content of his beliefs? Where does the essay illuminate what the novel is doing with Bulstrode’s benefactions, his sense of providential approval, and the sequence of choices that ends in Raffles’s death? And where does the novel resist being read as a straight application of the essay? I am especially interested in whether the narrator treats Bulstrode’s religious language as the cause of his moral failure or as the idiom through which a more ordinary self-deception expresses itself. A good answer will be faithful both to the essay’s argument and to the novel’s refusal to turn Bulstrode into a mere doctrinal case.
