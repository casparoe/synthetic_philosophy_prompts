I was talking to a friend who works at one of the frontier AI labs, and she described a proposal that's apparently circulating internally as a way to settle the standing fight between the slow-takeoff people and the fast-takeoff people—the ones who think capabilities will keep climbing on roughly smooth log-linear trends, and the ones who expect a self-reinforcing jump once models are doing a large chunk of the research themselves. The rough idea is a governor clause in the training policy. The lab would pre-register a broad internal eval suite. Whenever a newly trained model beats the previous best on that suite by more than 20% relative improvement, the lab must pause all training runs above a fixed compute threshold. A three person board—one alignment researcher, one capabilities researcher, one forecaster—can lift the pause by majority vote. But any board member can veto the lift by staking a year of their own salary on the claim that the next planned above-threshold run would produce another >20% jump. If the run eventually happens and the claim is right, the lab pays them triple their salary; if they're wrong, they forfeit the salary and lose their veto for two years. The rule doesn't say what happens if the lab just cancels the run or never schedules one.

I'm less interested in whether this is a good idea than in how it would actually behave. Walk me through the incentives it creates for the three board members, for the researchers who want to train bigger models, for lab leadership, and for rival labs watching from outside. Where does it get gamed first—sandbagging the eval, splitting one jump into several smaller runs, choosing the eval suite with an eye to triggering or avoiding the pause, stacking the board, or something else? Does it actually catch the thing it's aimed at, a genuinely discontinuous takeoff, or does it fail exactly there? And can any of the obvious failure modes be patched without turning the whole thing into a dead-letter policy, or do the patches just move the game somewhere else?
