I got into an argument with my sister about one of those mail-order DNA kits. She did it for the ancestry stuff, but the report also had a section about a gene variant that runs in our family and raises the chance of early-onset Alzheimer's, and she deleted the app without opening it. She says she'd actually pay extra to make the report not exist. I told her that cant be rational, and here's the argument I worked out. (1) If you're facing some decision and someone offers to tell you a free, purely informational fact before you choose, there's always a backup plan: learn the fact, then do exactly what you would have done anyway. (2) That ignore-the-info plan can't be worse than never learning the fact, because the action is the same, the world is the same, and the outcomes are the same — only your beliefs were briefly different. (3) But the plan where you learn the fact and then do whatever looks best given what you learned has to be at least as good as the ignore-the-info plan, since the ignore-the-info plan is just one of the things you could choose after learning, and you'd get to pick whatever scores highest. (4) So free information can only raise your expected value; it can never lower it. (5) So you should never pay to avoid a purely informational fact, and refusing free information is at most leaving an improvement on the table. By expected value I just mean the average result, weighting each possibility by how likely it is.

My sister's reply was that some knowledge changes what it's like to live — that you can't really just ignore a fact once you've seen it, because it sits in your head and recolors everything. I said that sounds like it changes her feelings, not the logic. But then I ask myself: I also wouldn't want to know the exact date of my own death, even if a genie offered it for free, and I might actually pay the genie to keep it to himself. So I'm stuck between thinking the proof is airtight and thinking my own preferences refute it.

Is the argument valid? If it is, why don't those cases refute it — do they break some hidden assumption, and which assumption exactly? If it isn't valid, tell me which numbered step fails and what the corrected version of that step would be.
