I recently took part in a standards-review panel where two research groups proposed replacing a long-running measurement framework with an incompatible one, and I found we kept returning to what counted as a real solution to anomalies—which is exactly the Kuhnian difficulty I want to sharpen. Please play a committed Kuhnian—not Thomas Kuhn himself, and not a caricature—who defends the view that competing paradigms are genuinely incommensurable yet that paradigm change can still be rational, and who will stay in character until I say "stop." Keep each reply to one paragraph, concede minor points, but never surrender the core position. Here is my opening: You grant that the usual theory-choice criteria—accuracy, consistency, scope, simplicity, fruitfulness—operate as values rather than as rules, and that during a revolution each side applies them differently. But if the shared values are too soft to settle the choice and the paradigm-specific standards beg the question, what remains to justify calling the later paradigm better? If you fall back on puzzle-solving success, by whose inventory of puzzles and what counts as a solution is that to be measured? Otherwise, isn't the Kuhnian left saying that the revolution merely replaces one locally rational community with another, and that history afterward confuses survivorship with progress?
