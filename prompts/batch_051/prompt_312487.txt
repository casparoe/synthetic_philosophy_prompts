In a workshop on the history of political rights, I presented a case that struck me as a clear instance of long-run moral backsliding: over roughly four decades, a society that had extended certain civic protections reversed them along several independent dimensions. When I asked whether a stretch of reversal like that should weaken our confidence that history tends, on the whole, toward justice, a senior scholar answered with the familiar slogan about the long arc of history bending toward justice, as though that settled the matter. I want to know exactly what work that slogan was doing. Lay out the distinct claims it could plausibly be advancing, from readings too vague to be tested, through readings that are substantive and defensible, to readings so strong they are not credible, and say for each whether it can absorb my forty-year reversal, whether it is compatible with an indefinite future of further reversal, and what kind of evidence would tell for or against it. I also want to know which of these readings the slogan is most charitably taken to assert, which readings would license treating my reversal as noise rather than counterevidence, and which are best understood as hope or commitment expressed in the grammar of a prediction.
