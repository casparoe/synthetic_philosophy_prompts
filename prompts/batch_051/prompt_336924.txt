Me and my friend got into a long argument last night about which computations make a system the kind of thing that can be wronged. His view was that what matters isnt raw behavioral sophistication or even self-awareness in the abstract, but what he called a world-to-self attribution loop. By that he meant: a system has to be able to take changes in its own internal state and attribute them to the right causes out in the world, then feed that attribution back into revising what it does next. So a rat flinches, registers that the shock came from the lever, and changes its behavior; a rock does nothing like this; and a language model, he thinks, is in a weird grey zone or outside the category entirely, because it models text, not itself. He claimed the presence of this loop is what makes a computation morally load-bearing. I pushed back that this is either unobservable or just behavior with extra steps, and we went in circles.

Here's what I want. Treat the empirical core of his claim as a hypothesis: that there is a real, detectable seperation between a system that runs a world-to-self attribution loop and a system that merely has the same information available but never uses it to revise itself, and that this difference tracks the moral boundary. Design the best test you can of that hypothesis, for at least one concrete target (a small robot, a simulated agent, or an animal). Tell me what result would count as evidence that the loop is doing real work, and what result would count as evidence that 'the loop' is just a fancy description we paste onto ordinary input-output mapping. Then be honest about what even a strongly positive result would not settle: does detecting the right computation actually license moral concern for the system, or does that last step stay philosophical no matter how good the test gets?
