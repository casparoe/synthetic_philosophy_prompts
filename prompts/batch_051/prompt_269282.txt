I have been writing up a philosophical profile in the tradition of Turing's remarks on learning machines, and before I circulate it I would like a consistency audit. The commitments I find myself making are these.

(1) The right route to machine intelligence is to build something like a child: a largely plastic learning system with the right initial architecture, which is then educated through interaction with an environment and a teacher, rather than to hand-code adult-level competencies.

(2) That education should not proceed mainly by the teacher inspecting or directly modifying the machine's internal states. It should run through a deliberately restricted interface — an unemotional channel of imperatives, rewards, punishments, and other training signals — so that the machine is forced to form its own concepts rather than having them inserted by the programmer.

(3) The old objection associated with Lady Lovelace, that a machine can only do what we already know how to order it to do, is best answered by giving up the demand for infallible predictability. A learning machine with a random element, whose subsequent history depends on what it happens to encounter, can do things its designer neither foresaw nor can fully explain. That is enough to dissolve the objection, even if the machine's behavior is in some broad sense determined.

(4) The question whether such a machine genuinely thinks should be settled behaviorally. If a machine could keep up its end of an imitation game flexibly and for a long time, that would be enough to establish, not merely suggest, that it thinks.

(5) Yet I also want to say that intelligence is not exhausted by current behavioral disposition: how the behavior was produced matters. A system that passed the same conversational test through brute storage and retrieval, with no learning history and no conceptual organization, would not be thinking in the sense I care about.

(6) I deny that randomness and disciplined education are in conflict. The random element supplies variation; the curriculum and the environment supply selection and correction. A learner can be probabilistic and disciplined at once, and disciplined education can remain creative.

Please audit this set. I want to know which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another, which pairs are only apparently in tension and dissolve under a reasonable clarification, and how the genuine tensions should be ranked by severity. For each genuine tension, say which commitment would most plausibly have to give and what the least costly revision would cost. Assess the commitments as stated, not as exegesis of Turing.
