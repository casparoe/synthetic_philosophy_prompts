Something keeps showing up in the way some religious people speak about their commitments, and I cannot tell whether it has been given a name or a systematic account.

Take two things the same person might say. In the first, they say they believe the bridge will hold, or that it will rain tomorrow, or that a certain battle happened in a certain year. Pressed, they produce evidence of the ordinary kind — load tests, weather records, documents — they distinguish degrees of confidence, and they revise when the evidence turns against them. In the second, they say they believe in a last judgment, or that a particular life is watched over and guided by God, or that certain terrible events happen because they are meant to. Pressed, they may cite a text or an experience, but they do not seem to treat it as evidence in the same way: counterevidence does not move them as a failed load test would, they may say the apparent counterevidence is itself part of the plan, and they report certainty rather than high probability. At the same time, this conviction organizes how they live — what they fear, how they treat other people, what they order their days around — in a way that a weather forecast never would.

For a long time I read this as a practical or emotional failing: the person has decided, for comfort, to treat a weakly supported claim as certain. But the more I look, the more it resembles a difference in kind rather than a difference in degree. The words believe, true, reason and doubt appear to be doing different work in the two cases, and evaluating the second by the standards of the first feels like marking a poem by the rules of a lab report. And yet the person insists that this is belief — not a metaphor, not a mood, not merely a moral attitude.

Is there an established position or concept that makes exactly this point: that religious belief is not a defective attempt at ordinary factual belief, but a different sort of commitment with its own logic, so that the usual demand for evidence is a category mistake? I want to know what the nearest positions are called and how they differ from one another — in particular, how this differs from the view that such claims are unverifiable and therefore empty, and from the view that they merely express an attitude or a wish. I also want to know how the position I am groping toward answers the obvious objection that if religious belief does not answer to evidence in the ordinary way, nothing distinguishes it from delusion, ideology or wishful thinking. A bare label will not settle it for me; what I want is the carving — which cases belong on the religious side, which on the ordinary-belief side, and where the boundary falls between believing religiously, trusting a person, and being loyal to a community, since those commitments also seem to resist piecemeal revision in the face of evidence.
