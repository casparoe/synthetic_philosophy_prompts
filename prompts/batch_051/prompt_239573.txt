After forty years in a lab I retired and joined a philosophy discussion group, and last month two of its members got into a genuine row about the Capgras delusion — the one where a patient insists a loved one has been replaced by a lookalike. I kept my mouth shut because I always say I'm just a chemist, but at the end they both looked at me and asked me to referee. I still can't. Here's roughly the exchange.

Elinor: "I don't see the mystery everyone else does. If a man looks at his wife and says, calmly and repeatedly, 'that woman is not my wife,' then that's what he believes, full stop. The two-factor account is actually on my side: one factor gives him a strange experience — he sees her face but doesn't get the usual rush of familiarity — and the second factor is a broken hypothesis-checker, so the impostor story gets through when it shouldn't. That's the machinery that produces an irrational belief. It's still a belief."

Davinder: "It produces the words, sure. But then he sits down and eats dinner with her, shares a bed, doesn't call the police, doesn't even ask where his real wife is. His behavior treats her as his wife all day long. If 'she is an impostor' functioned like a belief, you'd expect it to leak into some of that. I'd say the speech is more like a report of an experience he can't integrate — something is deeply wrong, the face doesn't feel like her, and 'impostor' is the only label that seems to fit."

Elinor: "That's too strong a test. I believe exercise would be good for me and I haven't been to the gym in a year. People hold beliefs that fail to guide action all the time — contradictory beliefs, half-spheres of belief, weakness of will. Delusion is just an extreme case of the same thing."

Davinder: "Maybe, but the Capgras claim isn't just failing to guide a bit of action. It is radically inert. The gap between the statement and the rest of the person's life is enormous, and the statement is practically loaded — if you really believed your wife had been replaced by a duplicate, a great many urgent actions would follow. The two-factor theory, by the way, explains this better if you take the 'belief' in its outcome to be something less than a full belief: the first factor gives a feeling of strangeness, and the second factor blocks the normal check that would say 'but that's absurd.' What results is a settled description the patient offers, not an action-guiding representation."

Elinor: "But the description is factive. The patient doesn't say 'I feel as though she's been replaced.' He denies she is the original. He insists on it, argues for it when challenged, and hangs onto it for years. Sincere factual assertion is our best criterion for belief. If you abandon that criterion, you owe me a better one — and 'action-guiding' won't work, because it demotes too many of the beliefs ordinary people obviously have."

Davinder: "I'll grant stickiness. But plausibility can override sincerity in special cases. Here the content is so preposterous and so tightly connected to urgent action that the total lack of action should make us reconsider. I don't think the patient believes nothing. I think we need a third kind of state — a sort of narrative the person lives inside, with belief-like speech on the surface, but not consumed by the rest of cognition the way beliefs are. The patient believes something like 'something about my wife has changed,' and the full impostor story is the pattern he imposes on that because it's the best story available. That's not nothing. It's just not a belief."

Then they both turned to me. I'd like to hear what someone who actually does this for a living makes of it: who is right about which specific point, and what are both of them missing? As a chemist I keep wanting a clean assay for 'belief,' but I suspect the concept jsut won't sit still.
