I've been going back and forth with a friend about the "embedded agency" stuff—the idea that an agent is actually part of the world it's modeling, not a neat Cartesian box that receives observations on one side and sends actions out the other. One of us think it's a genuine foundational problem: expected-utility theory quietly assumes a boundary between agent and environment, and that assumption breaks when the agent has to model a world that contains itself, when its actions can modify or delete the very computation doing the choosing, when its options are discovered rather than given, and when the observation it's making can depend on the fact that it's making it. So on this view we need new foundations, not patches. The other of us thinks the phenomenon is real but deflationary: those cases are all just bounded rationality. You can enlarge the model, treat your future self as another player, or approximate; the Cartesian picture is an idealization, and an idealization doesn't have to be literally true to be useful. So the real disagreement is whether embeddedness refutes the framework or just makes it expensive.

Could you write a short dialogue between a fictionalized version of us two, but make both sides as sharp as possible—no strawmen—and have each side concede a small point at least once without giving up the main one? Rather than have them survey every issue, I'd like them to actually engage on one point: start the dialogue with the problem of an agent trying to predict or model its own decision, where the standard formalism wants it to conditionalize on "I take this action" while its own reasoning is still in the middle of producing the action. Let each reply respond to the specific move the other just made, and try to get them, by the end, to articulate where the disagreement bottoms out—even if neither converts.
