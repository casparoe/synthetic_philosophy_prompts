Compare the institutional and geographic explanations of why some nations are rich, with this passage from the Introduction to Adam Smith's Wealth of Nations as your pivot: "But this proportion must in every nation be regulated by two different circumstances: first, by the skill, dexterity, and judgment with which its labour is generally applied; and, secondly, by the proportion between the number of those who are employed in useful labour, and that of those who are not so employed. Whatever be the soil, climate, or extent of territory of any particular nation, the abundance or scantiness of its annual supply must, in that particular situation, depend upon those two circumstances." Smith looks like he is treating soil, climate and territory as fixed background and saying the real difference between rich and poor nations is how labour is organised and how many people are doing useful work. That sounds like an early version of the institutional story, but I'm not sure the passage can carry that much. Is Smith actually making a causal claim about institutions in the modern sense, or is he just doing some national accounting while holding geography equal? And where do cultural explanations cut across the institutions-versus-geography contrast? I want a proper comparison of the two positions, and then a honest answer to whether the historical record can ever settle which of them is doing the causal work, given that history only runs once. Dont just list authors; work through the ideas.
