I'm a philosophy professor teaching a seminar on the philosophy of AI, and I'm re-reading Turing's 1950 paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence. I keep coming back to the ESP section, and I want to use it in class as a case study in how arguments age, but I'm not sure I have the through-line right.

As I read it, Turing's worry in that section is roughly this: the imitation game is supposed to force the interrogator to judge the machine and the hidden human only by the typed exchange. But if telepathy is a real thing, then the interrogator might be able to tell which contestant is the human through a channel the test isn't about — the interrogator just knows, or the human picks up on the interrogator's thoughts — and the machine loses the comparison unfairly. Turing seems to take the statistical evidence of his day seriously enough that he suggests a fix, something like putting the contestants in a room screened off from telepathy.

Here's where I get stuck. That whole worry seems to depend on the imitation game being the actual test, with a hidden human in the loop whose identity could leak. But the field has mostly abandoned the imitation game as its evaluation. Today's systems are scored on benchmarks, or users chat with them while knowing perfectly well they're a machine, or models are compared to each other with no hidden human anywhere. So my question: does Turing's ESP argument survive that shift, or is it dead because we designed out the hidden human whose mind could be read? Or does it reappear in another form — for example, is benchmark contamination or a model having memorized the test set the modern version of a channel the test didn't intend?
