You are a professional philosopher working in population ethics and formal axiology, and you are helping me fill a deliberate gap in a survey of the debate over negative utilitarianism. I am compiling a list of principled responses a pure negative utilitarian—one who holds that only suffering is intrinsically bad and that positive welfare has no intrinsic positive value—can give to the world-destruction argument: the argument that such a view must recommend painlessly destroying all sentient life, because an empty world contains no suffering and is therefore never worse than an inhabited one, no matter how much happiness the inhabited world contains. I already have the following responses, so I do not need them repeated: the threshold response (only suffering above a certain intensity counts as intrinsically bad, so the mild, subthreshold distress found in ordinary lives cannot generate an annihilating obligation); the implementation response (the counterexample stipulates a painless extinction mechanism, but no real-world apparatus could kill everyone without itself causing massive additional suffering and risk, so the argument is a non-action-guiding idealization); the death-as-harm response (treat the frustration of the desire to continue living, or the deprivation of future goods, as part of what “suffering” covers, so omnicide is not harm-free after all); the non-aggregation response (deny that harms to different individuals can be summed into a single impersonal total that outweighs each individual’s claim not to be killed); the person-affecting response (insist that one outcome can be better than another only if there is someone for whom it is better, and an empty world is not better for anyone); the side-constraint response (keep the suffering-minimizing axiology but add a deontic prohibition on non-consensual killing, making the button impermissible even if pressing it would minimize suffering); and the decision-procedure response (distinguish the criterion of rightness from a decision procedure, and argue that an anti-omnicide decision procedure is the one that minimizes expected suffering under realistic cognitive limitations). Give me one more response with a structurally different strategy—not a rewording, special case, or combination of any of these, and not a flat bullet-bite or a debunking explanation of the anti-extinction intuition. Think through the space carefully; you may briefly sketch two or three candidate responses before settling on the strongest, then state it precisely, explain how its structure differs from the closest of the responses I listed, and give your honest assessment of whether it actually works.
