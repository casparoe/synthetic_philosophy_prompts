Consider the relation we gesture at when we say that someone understands a proof. The phrase does more work than has verified the argument, but it is not obvious what that extra work consists in: we rely on it when we decide who passes, who gets hired, and what a colleague can safely build on. I would like you to articulate a working definition of the relation S understands proof P, and to test it against the following six cases, which are chosen to pull apart verified correctness, the ability to reconstruct the argument, and the ability to explain it.

(1) A student can reproduce a proof from a first-year topology course line for line and can confirm that every inference is valid, but when asked why the proof proceeds as it does, or what would go wrong if one hypothesis were weakened, she has nothing to say.

(2) A working mathematician sketches the correct overall strategy for a substantial existence proof and supplies the central lemma, but stalls at a final step that everyone in the field regards as a routine estimate; he says the remaining argument is bookkeeping and leaves it to a student.

(3) A proof assistant's kernel checks a fully formalized proof of a substantial theorem. The system certifies that every inference obeys the formal rules, but it has no summary of the argument, no representation of its main idea, and no capacity to answer counterfactual questions about it.

(4) A researcher can state the proof's main idea in two sentences, can explain why each hypothesis is needed by constructing counterexamples when any one is dropped, and can answer questions about nearby variants of the theorem, though she cannot write down the proof's technical details from memory.

(5) A student who read a proof a week earlier cannot begin it on a blank page. But after seeing the first two lines, he reconstructs the rest fluently and justifies every move. Asked whether he could have produced those two lines himself, he says he doubts it.

(6) A finite classification theorem is proved by a reduction that splits the problem into a huge finite number of cases, followed by an exhaustive computer search no single human has inspected. A mathematician can explain how the reduction works, why the exhaustiveness of the search would settle the theorem, and which parts of the argument rest on trusting the code, but she has not verified the cases herself.

My own preliminary verdicts are these: (1) and (3) are not understanding, (2) is; I am genuinely unsure about (4), (5), and (6). Propose your definition, run it through all six, and say where it forces you to revise or defend a surprising verdict. If you think understanding is degreed rather than all-or-nothing, explain how your definition orders the partial cases. Finally, identify the listed case about which you are least confident and say why.
