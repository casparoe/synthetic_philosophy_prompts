Exam question:

"In our unit on whether large language models understand language—the debate often framed around the charge that such models are stochastic parrots—we distinguished three candidate criteria for a system's understanding a given expression:

(A) Competence: the system reliably produces contextually appropriate linguistic behavior for the task at hand.
(B) Communicative grounding: the system's behavior is anchored in the world the expressions are about and issues from communicative intent.
(C) Functional tracking: the system's internal states preserve the distinctions that matter for the correct use and truth-conditional behavior of the expression.

We considered a thought experiment in which an octopus taps a transoceanic cable carrying messages between two island castaways. The octopus has never left the water and has no contact with the objects, animals, or weather the castaways discuss; it learns only the statistical regularities of the strings carried over the cable. It eventually produces replies so apt that the castaway still on the island cannot tell that she is no longer exchanging messages with her companion.

Answer both parts. (i) Which of (A)-(C) does this scenario put the most pressure on, and which does it leave least damaged? Explain. (ii) Present the strongest objection that could be made against the view you identified as least damaged."

Student's answer:

"The scenario's primary target is plainly the competence criterion, (A). By hypothesis the octopus meets (A): its replies track the conversation so well that the remaining castaway cannot detect the substitution. Yet we still would not say it understands the messages about coconuts and storms. So fluent, contextually apt performance is not sufficient for understanding. The candidate least damaged is (B), communicative grounding. The octopus has neither communicative intent nor contact with the world the signs are about, and a grounding theorist predicts exactly the intuition we have—that it does not understand. The scenario confirms that prediction, and because what the octopus is missing is precisely intent and grounding, the experiment provides direct positive support for (B), not merely a failure to refute it. Condition (C), by contrast, fails alongside (A). The octopus's states are, by the terms of the case, purely formal and statistical: they register co-occurrence patterns in the cable traffic. Such states cannot preserve the distinctions that matter for truth and correct use, so the functional-tracking criterion is unavailable to the octopus as well. The strongest remaining objection against the (B)-theorist is the vagueness of grounding: the octopus is perfectly well anchored in the cable signals and in its own sensory stream, so the proponent of (B) must say which anchorings count and why only those do. On balance, the octopus case does its main job: it severs fluent performance from understanding, and it shows that understanding is a matter of communicative intent plus a shared world."
