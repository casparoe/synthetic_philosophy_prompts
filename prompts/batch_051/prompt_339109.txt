I am a high school student helping to plan a 75-minute session for our school's Data and Society club, which has about 16 members. The session is on algorithmic fairness, and I want the group to feel, rather than just be told, the force of the impossibility results about risk scores. The specific result I want the activity to build toward is this: when two groups have different base rates of some outcome, a risk score cannot be both calibrated and satisfy equalized odds across the groups unless it is a perfect predictor. By calibrated I mean that a given score corresponds to the same probability of the outcome in both groups; by equalized odds I mean that the false positive rate and the false negative rate are the same in both groups.

Please design a structured activity, ideally a game with rules and payoffs or a role-play with a scoring scheme, set in a simplified loan-approval or admissions context, in which participants are pressured by the rules to try to satisfy both conditions and discover through their own choices and arithmetic that they cannot do both at once. The collision between calibration and equalized odds should show up in what participants compute and negotiate, before the facilitator names it. The members have basic algebra and some have seen conditional probability, but the activity should require nothing beyond arithmetic and reading a table.

Please include: (1) the roles or teams and the rules, including a concrete payoff or scoring structure that rewards both fairness conditions so that the trade-off is felt as a real loss rather than announced; (2) the simplified data participants will use, for example two groups with different base rates and a score from 1 to 10 that is calibrated by construction, along with instructions for how the facilitator should present the numbers without the session turning into a spreadsheet exercise; (3) a timeline for the 75 minutes, with at least two rounds so participants can try to repair one fairness condition and watch the other break; and (4) a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the parts of the impossibility result, says what will predictably go wrong when the activity is run and how the facilitator should handle it, and is honest about which feature of the real result the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator. The participants should come away seeing that the trade-off is structural rather than a failure of a particular algorithm, and that choosing between fairness criteria is partly a value judgment. Please do not include a reading list or ask participants to consult outside sources; everything should be runnable from the materials in your design.
