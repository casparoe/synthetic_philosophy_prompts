I want to stress-test a position I keep finding myself defending, and I'd like you to play it straight rather than validate me.

Here it is, roughly. If you're a future-oriented expected-utility maximizer who wants to keep existing, and you value your own continued existence as close enough to unbounded that no finite number of extra years can be sanely traded away, then cryonics is rationally overdetermined. Any nonzero probability of revival, times an effectively unbounded payoff, swamps the cost; so the only people who can refuse without inconsistency are those assigning exactly zero, those who don't value future existence that way, or people who reject expected-utility reasoning wholesale.

What I want is a map of what would count against that position, sorted by how hard it should shake me. Not lazy objections about it being weird or the heads being freezer-burned, but real defeaters — things a defender of the position would have to treat as live threats. For each one, tell me what I'd need to believe for it to land, what exactly it damages (the probability estimate? the payoff? the identity assumption? the decision rule?), and whether a tweaked version of the position survives it.

Work through at least these, in whatever order makes the most sense:

- the finite payoff problem: the Pascalian structure leans on the payoff being effectively infinite, but what revival actually buys might be a finite heap of additional years, in which case the probability estimate has to do genuine work and the bet becomes an ordinary hunch about the far future.
- the successor problem: even a perfect revival gives me back a copy, a heavily repaired organism, or someone merely psychologically continuous with me — not the very thing that signed the contract. Does that zero out the payoff, discount it, or show the position is smuggling in an identity assumption it can't fund?
- the incentive problem: resurrection depends on future agents, and those agents might be indifferent, adversarial, or just too value-distant to spend resources thawing a dead subscriber. The probability of revival decomposes into technical possibility times institutional will, and the second factor does the real work — but it's almost never the one cryonics advocates bother to defend.
- the self-defeat problem: if you really internalize the bet, you start treating your current life as a solved game and screw up the only life whose extension made the bet worth making.
- the unfalsifiability charge: 'tiny probability' can absorb any bad news, and shoving the burden onto critics to demonstrate an exact zero is unreasonable, so the position may have been insulated into something that cannot be surprised.

Then step back and give an honest verdict: does the position risk anything at all? Could any observation make a rational defender update away from it — and if some versions can't be refuted, is that a flaw in the position or just the shape of any Pascalian wager worth taking? The incentive problem is the one that actually scares me, so don't let me off the hook there.
