I'm a backend dev, not a philosopher, but I got pulled into a debate at work about free riding and I can't stop chewing on it. We have an internal engineering wiki that maybe 10% of the company actually contributes to. Everyone else just searches it, reads pages, and moves on. Some people say the lurkers are social parasites; others say that's unfair because a wiki with no readers wouldn't be worth maintaining, so reading is itself a contribution. Management is now floating a token economy to fix the imbalance. The proposal goes like this: everyone gets 100 tokens per quarter; viewing a unique wiki page costs 1 token per day; creating a page earns 10 tokens; editing a page earns 5 tokens; and tokens can be transferred between employees. At the end of the quarter, a negative balance means you lose edit access until you author a page, and a balance above 150 gets you a docs champion badge and a tiny bonus.

I can't decide if this solves the free-rider problem or just turns documentation into a badly designed video game. What would actually happen here? I'd like someone to work through the incentives, the ways people would game it, and the second-order effects. For example, I can already see a market forming where people sell tokens for lunch money, and I can see docs becoming more bloated because people write low-quality pages just to farm tokens. Is there a version of this that keeps the goal—more people contributing real knowledge—without wrecking the whole system? And more basically, does treating wiki access as a commodity imply that reading without writing was always a kind of theft, or is that just a category error?
