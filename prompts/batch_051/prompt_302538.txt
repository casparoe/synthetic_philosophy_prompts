For a while now I've been running my inner life on a policy, and I'm no longer sure whether it's a principle or a compulsion. I treat ongoing doubt as a moral duty, roughly the way I treat checking my blind spot before changing lanes: I'm not allowed to say "I believe X" unless I can produce, on the spot, the strongest case I know against X and explain why I don't find it decisive. If I can't do that, I demote the claim — out loud and to myself — to "I lean X" or "I'm trying it on for now." I apply this to moral and political views, not just factual ones, and I feel something close to guilt when I find a belief sitting in my head that I've never audited. A friend of mine says this is intellectual scrupulosity dressed up as virtue: it makes me easy to corner in an argument, because being permenantly uncertain looks fair-minded but is really a failure to commit, and the rule can't meet its own standard — do I have a strong case against my own demand for a strong case? I can see his point in one instance. A few months ago I realized I couldn't say "I believe my friend didn't lie to me," because I couldn't produce a decisive argument against the possibility that he had. I only had evidence, and evidence is always logically compatible with some worse story. That felt broken rather than rational.

So I'm asking: is my policy defensible, or is it a bias toward doubt wearing a duty costume? If there's a defensible version, what gets kept and what gets dropped — and should some kinds of beliefs, like trust in a friend, love, or the snap judgment that a situation is unsafe, be exempt? Or is inventing exemptions just a way to be sloppy exactly where it's most comfortable?
