A national online deliberative platform used by self-identified left and right political communities proposes the following rule. Any post arguing for a policy or candidate must include a section of 100–300 words headed: The strongest consideration on my own side against this is. In that section, the author must present an objection that a well-informed person who shares the author's left-right location and still supports the policy would take seriously. A rotating panel of readers from the same self-identified side rates each required section as internal dissent, laundered outgroup argument, performative doubt, or non-responsive. Posts rated non-responsive are hidden until revised. The platform's stated research aim is to test whether the left-right distinction has real internal structure or is mainly a coordination label: if left and right are held together by shared commitments, there should be a non-empty set of internal dissents for most concrete positions; if they are largely negative or coalitional, authors will be forced to borrow the other side's arguments and recast them as their own.

How would this rule actually behave over a year? What incentives does it create for coalitional discipline, logrolling of objections, status competition in authentic doubt, strategic use of the rating panel to suppress rivals, or the emergence of boutique factions? Where does it break down, and what do the failure modes reveal about what, if anything, holds left and right together? Could tweaks—such as rotating raters, anonymous posting, or allowing posts to argue that there is no coherent internal dissent—fix those failures without gutting the mechanism?
