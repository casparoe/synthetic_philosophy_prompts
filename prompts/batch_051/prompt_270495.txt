I work as a software engineer, and one of the first lessons of the job is that the person who wrote a change should not be the only person deciding whether it is correct; separating author from reviewer is good process, not an insult to the author. That background may be why a familiar feature of political argument has started to look like a double standard to me. Most people I know become genuinely angry about unelected judges overruling elected politicians — the United States Supreme Court invalidating a statute, for instance, or a British court issuing a declaration of incompatibility or quashing a minister's decision on human-rights grounds — yet they are perfectly relaxed about other unelected bodies doing what seems very similar. The Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee can raise interest rates, slow the economy, and put people out of work in order to meet an inflation target, thereby overriding the elected government's growth and borrowing plans, and this is widely accepted as a technical necessity. The Competition and Markets Authority can block a merger that a government minister publicly supported. Regulators issue binding codes and large fines that in practice determine how a statute applies, often settling value-laden questions about acceptable risk, privacy, or who should bear a cost.

I want to know whether anything actually justifies treating these cases differently. The strongest candidate distinction I can formulate is that courts decide matters of principle or rights, whereas the central bank and the regulators decide only technical means to ends the legislature has already set. But a central bank's decision has obvious moral and distributional stakes, and a privacy regulator deciding how much surveillance a platform may build is not applying a neutral algorithm. A second candidate is that courts can strike down primary legislation while agencies act only under delegated powers; but the delegated powers are often so broad that the real choice sits with the agency, and the legislature cannot realistically take it back. A third is that judges are generalists while regulators possess specialist expertise; that, however, would justify suspicion of generalists as such, not the specific democratic objection to courts.

Please give me a committed answer rather than a survey of the options. Supply the strongest principle you can find that would make judicial review uniquely problematic while leaving the central bank and the regulators acceptable, test that principle against the hardest nearby cases you can construct, and then say plainly whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up — and, if given up, in which direction.
