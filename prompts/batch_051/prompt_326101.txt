My epistemology reading group has spent the past month on the idea that no mental state is luminous, and how that interacts with the KK principle. For us non-specialists: the KK principle says that if you know p, you know that you know p (and on up the stack); luminosity is the thought that at least some states -- being in pain, being cold -- are self-intimating, so if you're in them you're automatically in a position to know you're in them. The anti-luminosity thought is that this stack never really closes: near the edges (mild cold, mild pain) there are cases where you're in the state but not in a position to know it, and even when you check, the checking seems to inherit the same problem rather than settle it. Our last meeting got into an argument about whether the whole thing is just a sorites with a margin-of-error principle wearing an epistemology costume.

Anyway, I'd like to close the semester with a poem: a Petrarchan sonnet (octave then sestet, volta at line nine). Have the octave voice the hopeful Cartesian picture -- knowing something brings its own certainty into view, the ladder of "I know that I know that I know" is secure and goes all the way up. Let the turn happen and have the sestet voice the collapse: the climb doesn't confirm the original state, it just adds another rung whose own status is now in question, and the more you try to check the less luminous the thing becomes. Keep "luminous" and "KK" out of the poem itself; the rhyme scheme can do some of the work, with the octave's rhymes closing neatly and the sestet refusing to resolve cleanly. A specialist hearing it should know exactly what argument this is, and a general reader should just find it a good poem about trying to know what you know.
