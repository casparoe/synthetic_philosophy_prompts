I've spent the last week getting quotes for health insurance on the individual market here in the US, and the pattern in the pricing is doing my head in. The application never asked about my dad's early heart attack or my own cholesterol, and the plan materials said preexisting conditions don't affect the premium. But ticking the box for tobacco use changed the price by a lot, and so did my age — which I obviously can't change. So there's no clean "they only price things you chose" story, even though a friend of mine says the system is basically luck egalitarian: protect people from bad brute luck, but let them bear the costs of their own risky choices (option luck, I think she called it). I want to know what the practice itself actually presupposes, as distinct from what people say it's for. Reconstruct the implicit theory of fairness, responsibility, and choice behind this pricing pattern as charitably as you can. Does the tobacco surcharge strictly presuppose that smoking is a chosen risk and smokers should bear the cost, or is it in princple just actuarial prediction with a political carve-out? Is the age rating a flat contradiction of the luck-egalitarian reading, or can a luck egalitarian accept age-priced premiums? Where does the practice underdetermine the philosophy — where could several different theories all license the same rules? And what would have to change under (a) a thoroughly actuarial view that used every predictor it could, and (b) a solidarity view that charged everyone the same regardless of age and health? I'm after the hidden theory and its stress points, not a health policy debate.
