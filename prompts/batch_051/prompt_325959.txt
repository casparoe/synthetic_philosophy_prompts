I'm giving a departmental seminar next month defending a 'bookkeeping' account of the units of selection debate: the question "what does natural selection act on?" is not best answered by picking genes, individuals, or groups as the real unit, but by showing why different explanatory projects legitimately track fitness at different levels. I want to open with this passage from Darwin's On the Origin of Species (1859), Chapter VII, on Instinct:

"It will be universally admitted that instincts are as important as corporeal structure for the welfare of each species, under its present conditions of life. Under changed conditions of life, it is at least possible that slight modifications of instinct might be profitable to a species; and if it can be shown that instincts do vary ever so little, then I can see no difficulty in natural selection preserving and continually accumulating variations of instinct to any extent that may be profitable. It is thus, as I believe, that all the most complex and wonderful instincts have originated."

My audience is a mixed biology department: two population geneticists who think the units-of-selection question was settled by the gene's-eye view, a behavioral ecologist who mostly defends multi-level selection, and a historian of biology who thinks everyone is misreading Darwin. Prepare me for the Q&A: the six or seven objections I'll actually get from this crowd, ordered by how damaging they'd be, phrased as they'd put them, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. In particular, I need to know whether using Darwin this way will get me accused of mere exegesis, and include the question I'm hoping nobody asks.
