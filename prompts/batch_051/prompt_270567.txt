Analyze the He Jiankui affair — the November 2018 announcement that a scientist at the Southern University of Science and Technology in Shenzhen had used CRISPR-Cas9 to disable the CCR5 gene in human embryos and that twin girls had been born as a result — as a case study in the unilateralist's curse. Take the following as common-ground facts rather than anything you need to establish: Jiankui's stated aim was to confer resistance to HIV, the father being HIV-positive; the work was presented at the Second International Summit on Human Genome Editing in Hong Kong and met with broad scientific condemnation; his claims of ethics approval were later contested; and a Chinese court eventually convicted him of illegal medical practice. I know the basic story; what I want is the analysis.

Begin with a disciplined mapping. Who are the relevant players in the sense the curse requires — not only Jiankui but collaborators, students, the university, funders, clinicians involved in the IVF, regulators, and science journalists who learned of the work before and after the births? For each, specify what unilateral action was actually open to them: proceeding with the edits, implanting, seeking genuine independent review, refusing to assist, reporting up the chain, going public before the births, blocking further pregnancies after the first. What did the situation look like from inside each player's position, and what would the curse predict — that someone with an idiosyncratically low threshold for action triggers an outcome the broader community would have rejected, or the reverse failure, in which many people who could each have stopped the work all defer because stopping it is nobody's individual job? Say what the theory's remedies, such as shifting the default, requiring a single explicit authorizer, or raising the threshold for irreversible interference, would have required here, and whether any of them actually existed.

Then turn to where the model breaks. The curse assumes roughly sincere actors whose independent estimates are noisy draws around a shared assessment of an initiative's value. What do you have to assume about Jiankui's motives, information, and independence from the rest of the scientific community for the curse to explain the case at all? Where does the episode outrun the theory — the germline-versus-somatic distinction, the existence of prior expert statements against heritable genome editing, the fact that he operated inside an institution rather than in a genuinely distributed group, the regulatory and reputational structure of Chinese science, and the distinctive wrong of imposing a heritable risk on future persons who could not consent? What does the case show about the theory: a confirmation, a refutation, or a case it was never built to handle? If a different model — a principal-agent problem, prestige competition in science, or moral hazard — better explains why the field's consensus failed to bind an actor, say so and defend the comparison.
