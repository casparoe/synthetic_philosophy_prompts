I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I'd like a precise diagnosis of an argument I keep seeing in discussions of AI development races. The argument applies rationalist explanations for interstate war to races between AI labs or states. It feels too neat, but I can't locate the mistake myself. Here is the argument as I understand it:

1. In Fearon's rationalist framework, war is puzzling because fighting is costly, so there should be some negotiated bargain that both sides prefer to war.
2. Fearon identifies three standard reasons such bargains fail: private information about capabilities or resolve with incentives to misrepresent, commitment problems, and indivisibility of the contested stakes.
3. An AI development race is also costly, and a coordinated slowdown with verification would leave the parties better off than an all-out race.
4. The same three mechanisms plausibly operate in AI races: labs have private information about their progress, they cannot credibly commit not to defect from a slowdown, and "first AGI" is often treated as an all-or-nothing prize.
5. Therefore, the rationalist framework explains why AI races occur, and the policy response should focus on transparency, credible commitment mechanisms, and making the prize divisible.

Is this argument valid? If not, exactly which step fails and why? In particular, I suspect step 4 may be doing something sneaky in moving from interstate war to AI races, but I can't say whether the problem is an equivocation, a false analogy, or a suppressed premise about what makes a rationalist bargain possible.
