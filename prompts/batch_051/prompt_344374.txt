I edit the science and ideas section of a monthly general-interest magazine, and a recurring editorial dispute has hardened into a standing question. Every month I receive pitches for features about what I have come to call self-justifying excellence: work pursued for its own sake, with no expected payoff for anyone beyond the person doing it. The cases that reached my desk last quarter are representative: a retired civil engineer who spent eight years hand-building an orrery accurate to a minute of arc; a short, elegant proof in a corner of combinatorics that even its author says has no foreseeable application; a new world record in a memory-sport event with only a few dozen serious competitors; and the youngest person in the country to have learned a notoriously difficult cycle of piano études. My culture editor argues that it is part of our remit to notice excellence even—perhaps especially—when nothing else hangs on it, because such work can change a reader's sense of what a human life can be. Our editor-in-chief simply wants a rule that survives the monthly pitch meeting.

Please write me a reusable editorial decision procedure for these pitches. It should be something an assigning editor without formal philosophy can run in about fifteen minutes. It should not reduce to "cover whatever is likely to benefit readers" (that begs the question against the culture editor) or to "cover whatever is impressive" (that makes us a hobbyist fanzine). Ground it in the best available thinking about the value of achievement and excellence—in particular, whether excellence is worth celebrating or promoting when it benefits no one in expectation, and how intrinsic value, if it counts, should weigh against scarce pages and readers' attention. The procedure should say which distinctions survive simplification, where it is likely to misfire, and which steps are irreducibly value-laden. Then run it on three of the cases above: the orrery, the proof, and the pianist. Include at least one case where the procedure returns a "no" to a piece I would personally love to run, and explain why. Lead with your bottom line, and keep the entire answer under 900 words.
