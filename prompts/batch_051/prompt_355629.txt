In a dispute over whether a town's official account of a labor clash from a century ago should be taught instead of the version preserved in local family memory, one person ended the discussion by saying, "History is written by the victors." The sentence was meant to do real work: it was supposed to show that the official account deserves less trust than the family memory. What does that sentence actually amount to? Lay out the distinct precise claims it could be making—about who gets to produce records, about which records survive, about how much official accounts should be corrected for the winners' bias, about whether any account can escape that bias, and about who gets to decide what counts as history. For each, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or a false one. Then say which of these readings, if any, would actually justify siding with the local memory against the official record.
