A conservation-policy working group I participate in has spent two years trying to reconcile two camps on deliberate genetic intervention in wild ecosystems, and the effort has repeatedly stalled. One side—call them the genetic-restorationists—defends the use of gene drives (for example, a heritable suppression drive aimed at invasive rodents on seabird islands) and is cautiously sympathetic to de-extinction of keystone species, treating both as continuations of ordinary ecological restoration. The other side—call them the precautionary stewards—accepts conventional restoration such as dam removal or native replanting but holds that self-propagating genetic interventions are different in kind: they can persist and spread beyond their release site, cannot be reliably recalled, and slide from repairing ecosystems into redesigning them. The two camps share more than their public arguments suggest: both want functioning, self-sustaining ecosystems, both take the welfare of native and invasive animals seriously, and neither treats nature as a mere instrument. Yet every attempt at a joint statement has collapsed along the same fault lines.

Draft the joint statement this group should have produced by carrying the dispute out as a genuine adversarial collaboration. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table.

In the agreed-claims section, include claims both camps can sign about the current state of the interventions and their uncertainty, about what actually distinguishes gene drives and de-extinction from conventional restoration, and about the structure of any precautionary obligation. In the disagreements table, give the precise residual disagreements, each worded so that the side attributed with a view would accept that wording. Then identify the cruxes: the sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one camp, rather than restatements of the overall dispute. For each crux, specify the concrete case, model, or class of evidence both sides would agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts.

To keep 'irreversibility' from doing all the work while meaning different things in different sentences, the collaboration should impose and use a three-way distinction: construct reversibility (whether the genetic modification itself can be halted, overwritten, or immunized against after release); ecological reversibility (whether the ecosystem would return to something like its prior state if the construct were later removed); and option reversibility (whether proceeding destroys information or future alternatives—for example, by eliminating the last population that a later reversal would need). The statement should make explicit where each camp stands on each of the three, and should not simply assume in advance which of them carries the moral weight.

The collaboration should also examine whether de-extinction and gene drives genuinely form a single category or have been fused by a slogan; if the two camps cannot treat them together, the statement should either split them or justify treating them jointly.

I want a committed draft, not a menu of options. Make the judgment calls: choose the wording you judge both sides could actually sign without becoming vacuous, decide which cruxes are real and which are proxy disputes, and if a residual disagreement turns out to be largely verbal or a difference about evidential standards, say so in a brief note after the table and build that diagnosis into how the disagreement is stated.
