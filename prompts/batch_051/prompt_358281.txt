I'm giving a paper next month to a research seminar run jointly by the philosophy department and the art conservation programme. The thesis I'm defending is that a perfect copy of a painting — same materials, same age, visually indistinguishable even under a microscope — has just as much aesthetic value as the original, and that the extra value we assign to originals is entirely a matter of social and historical facts like provenance, authenticity and market signalling, not anything in the work qua object of perception. I'm going for the strong version: if you and I can't tell the original from the perfect copy side by side, then any difference in our judgement of them can't be an aesthetic difference — it's a difference in what we believe about them, and beliefs about provenance aren't aesthetic. The room is mixed: three analytic aestheticians, two of whom work on forgery and think facts about authorship are themselves aesthetic facts; two conservators who've spent careers arguing that an object's history is in its very fabric, not just in its paperwork; and one economist working on cultural markets, who'll care mostly about information and signalling. I've already got the layperson's "the original is worth twenty million, mate" line covered — skip that unless this crowd would dress it up in something that actually bites. I want a Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections I'll actually get from these people, in roughly the order they'll come and in their vocabulary, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. Be honest about the weakest part too: tell me which objection, if pushed hard enough, would most change your mind about whether the strong version is defensible, and what specifically the room would have to get me to concede for me to drop the word entirely. At the very end, put a short label for the objection I should rehearse first in \answer{...}.
