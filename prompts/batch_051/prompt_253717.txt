ok so for my philosophy of language class (we're doing a unit on language change, with the Latin-to-Italian case as the running example) I have to write up where I stand before seminar, and I've ended up with a list of comittments that feels like it might not hang together. I genuinely can't tell where the problem is, so I'd like an audit.

Here's roughly where I am:

(1) A language is more like a historical individual — closer to a biological lineage or a species — than an abstract grammar. So being "the same language" is fundamentally about ancestry and descent, not about having the same rules.

(2) But my everyday criterion for whether two contemporary varieties are the same language is mutual intelligibility: if speakers understand each other without special training, same language, if not, different.

(3) I think Latin and Italian are not the same language, even though there was no moment when parents stopped speaking Latin and children started speaking Italian. The change was to gradual for a sharp boundary, but the endpoints are clearly different languages.

(4) I also think you can't have determinate facts about "same language" without a sharp boundary. Since every adjacent generation in the chain is mutually intelligible with the one before, and identity should be transitive, a sorites chain from Latin to Italian should make it indeterminate whether Latin and Italian are the same language — and I should probably just accept that vagueness.

(5) Yet I also want to insist that Italian descends from Latin and not from the lineage that became French. There's a determinate fact about the ancestry, even if the stages blur together.

(6) I think standard Italian is a weird case: it's basically a written standard built on medieval Florentine, and for a long time almost nobody in Italy actually spoke it natively. If a language is a historical lineage of native speakers, then standard Italian before, like, unification was more of a literary register than a real language.

(7) But I don't want to say that the language of that literary tradition wasn't a real language, or that the standard hasn't become a real spoken language by now.

So the ask: go through these and tell me which pairs are actually in tension and which only look like they are, rank the real tensions by severity, and for each say what would have to give and what the cheapest fix is. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the largest share of the mess. I'd rather not just have "these are all fine" or "these are all contradictory" — I want to know where the load-bearing stress is.
