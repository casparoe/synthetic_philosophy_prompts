I have tentatively landed on the following picture of the emotions, and I want you to stress-test whether its commitments fit together.

(1) Emotions are evaluative and object-directed: to fear is to experience a dog as dangerous; to resent is to experience someone as having slighted one.

(2) The evaluation is perception-like rather than belief-like. It presents its object a certain way without the person assenting to that presentation, which is why I can be afraid of flying while judging in full sincerity that flying is safe.

(3) The bodily disturbance is constitutive of the emotion, not a mere accompaniment. Subtract the racing heart, the knot in the stomach, and the urge to withdraw, and what remains is not fear as I understand it.

(4) Emotions carry their own standards of correctness. Fear is correct when its object really is dangerous; grief is correct only if what is grieved really is a loss. Saying an emotion is unfounded, apt, or excessive is not merely applying a convention.

(5) Yet I count some affective episodes as emotions even though they seem to have no object and no evaluative content at all: free-floating dread, anxious moods, objectless melancholy.

Audit this set. Which pairs are in genuine tension, and which apparent tensions dissolve under inspection? Rank the genuine tensions by severity and say, for each, what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would be. For each diagnosis, tell me how confident you are in it, and what consideration or distinction would most likely lead you to revise it.
