I teach a final-year PPE seminar on distributive justice, and next week we begin the Rawls–Harsanyi dispute. I want the discussion to be sorted by what decision rule each student actually uses under ignorance — not by which label or philosopher they think they endorse. Please write a sorting quiz with an explicit routing key that places respondents into a leaf position in the logical space of decision rules under complete ignorance (a known set of states and payoffs, but no probabilities for the states), and then maps each leaf onto the Rawls–Harsanyi debate where a mapping exists.

Construct the instrument from the questions outward, not from a list of names: first work out which diagnostic choices separate the positions, then locate the named positions in the resulting space. Each item should be a concrete forced choice between two or three acts in a small decision matrix, phrased so a student can answer without knowing which position the answer leads to. Use at least one matrix with non-monetary outcomes (crop yields, health, grades, housing), so respondents are not merely importing their attitude to cash risk. Include at least one item on which maximin and lexicographic maximin come apart, one on which maximin and minimax regret come apart, one on which the Hurwicz optimism–pessimism rule and Laplace's principle of insufficient reason come apart, and one that tests whether the respondent treats the absence of probabilities as genuine or quietly supplies subjective probabilities. The leaves should include at least: maximin, lexicographic maximin, minimax regret, Hurwicz, Laplace/insufficient reason, maximax, and a Bayesian expected-utility leaf. Note in the key where Rawls and Harsanyi sit: the instrument should not pretend Rawls's position is simply maximin applied everywhere; it should flag that Rawls restricts the rule to the choice of principles behind the veil of ignorance, and that a respondent who is maximin in ordinary decisions may still reject the difference principle, and vice versa. Likewise, Harsanyi's original-position argument is the special case of expected utility with equal probabilities over positions, not just any Bayesianism.

Your response should have these sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths:

1. "Instrument brief" (about 150 words): the target construct, the intended respondents, and the assumptions you are making about how complete ignorance differs from risk and from ambiguity.

2. "Diagnostic items" (about 500 words): five to seven forced-choice decision matrices with precise response options, and, where a follow-up reason is needed for routing, the exact follow-up question to ask.

3. "Routing logic" (about 400 words): the decision tree or flowchart as a series of branch nodes, each node a plain-language question or rule tied to specific items, leading to the leaves.

4. "Leaves and scoring key" (about 350 words): a one-sentence definition of each leaf, the answer pattern that routes to it, and the mapping — if any — to the Rawls–Harsanyi debate.

5. "Unoccupied and unstable cells" (about 250 words): combinations of answers the tree allows but that no named position occupies, with a diagnosis of whether each is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

6. "Inseparable pairs and caveats" (about 250 words): pairs of leaves the instrument cannot cleanly separate in practice, the boundary or inconsistent cases that reveal this, and what questions you would need to add to separate them if I insisted.
