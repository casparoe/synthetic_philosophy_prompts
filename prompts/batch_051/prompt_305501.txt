I've been reading about causal inference for a science project on how researchers distinguish correlation from causation, and I've become attached to a standard principle: when many causal structures fit the data, assume the true structure is the simplest one, and assume the data are faithful to it. Faithful, as I understand it, means that an observed independence reflects a real absence of causal connection rather than a fluke in which two or more causal pathways exactly cancel out. This principle is compelling because it is simple, uniform, and seems to be what makes causal learning from statistics manageable at all. Yet I keep returning to an example that shakes my confidence. Imagine a gene that affects height through two channels: it directly promotes bone growth, and it also suppresses a growth factor, and in some population the two effects precisely cancel, so the gene and height look statistically independent even though the gene is doing genuine causal work. The principle would conclude that the gene has no influence on height, and my intuition firmly resists: the method is not discovering the causal structure but assuming away a real kind of complexity. Should I trust the principle and dismiss my example as a freakish edge case, or does the example expose a real limitation in using the assumptions of faithfulness and minimality to track what actually causes what? Present the strongest version of each side and a negotiated verdict, explaining what would justify amending the principle, abandoning it, or reinterpreting it as a merely practical modeling convention.
