One of our chaplains sent this around for a philosophical once-over before the committee votes, and I want to think it through out loud. Their proposal: for one semester, all petitionary prayer in public worship must be retroactive. You may only ask God about events that are already finished but whose outcome you don't yet know — this morning's biopsy, last night's flight, the sealed verdict, the hours before a missing hiker was found. You may not pray about anything still to come: no exams tomorrow, no surgery not yet performed, no asking that a war be averted. Requests would be submitted on cards and screened by the chaplain before the service. The stated rationale is that God is omniscient and immutable, so a future-directed petition quietly implies that God could be informed or could alter a course of events; retroactive petition, they say, respects that the matter is settled in God's sight and cannot be changed, while keeping the first-person posture of asking about something hidden from us. They claim this is the only form of petition that takes divine atemporality seriously.

I'm meant to give a detailed account of how this would actually behave, not wether it's a good idea in the abstract. Treat it as a mechanism: what does it do to when and how people pray, to what gets requested, and to who leans in versus drops out? What incentives does it create for the laity, the clergy, and the committee itself? Where does it get gamed first — strategically not checking the news, praying about the past causes of future outcomes, exploiting the fact that every future event becomes past a moment later? What does it do to communal worship, and to the picture of God people absorb from it? Where is it most likely to break, and can any of that be fixed without simply abandoning the rule? Also be blunt about where your analysis is most likely to fail and what would change your mind about it.
