Was arguing with a friend about whether the risk of everyday driving is actually "worth it," and he kept bringing up micromorts — a one-in-a-million chance of death — and how people will panic about some pesticide scare that carries a one-micromort risk while not thinking twice about a few hundred miles in a car, which is roughly the same order of risk. That somehow turned into us messing with the trolley problem, and now I can't tell whether the change is superficial or whether it basically breaks the standard deontological analysis.

Standard version: runaway trolley heading for five people on the main track; you can throw a switch and divert it to a side track, where one person is on the track. The variant we got stuck on keeps the five on the main track but empties the side track. The side track instead runs along a crumbling embankment above a packed market. If you throw the switch, the vibration sends rocks into the crowd, and every one of the million people down there independently takes a one-micromort risk of being killed by debris — 1 in 1,000,000. Expected number of deaths is exactly one, same as the original switch, but ex ante there's no identifiable victim, and ex post it could be zero, one, two, or more. (Doing nothing still kills the five for sure.)

How does the standard deontological case against pulling change, exactly? Is there a morally relevant difference between imposing a near-certain death on one non-consenting person and imposing a 1 in 1,000,000 death risk on each of a million non-consenting people, when the expected body count is identical — and does that difference hold if the same expected death were instead spread over ten people at 10% risk each? What would a consequentialist say; should they care about anything besides the expectation, like variance or the possibility of multiple people getting unlucky? And does our comfort with driving-sized risks suggest we already accept the statistical-victim version while balking at the identified-victim version — and if so, is that principle defensible? Not after citations, just the analysis.
