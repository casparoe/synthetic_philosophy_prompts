I've been arguing with a friend about whether pity is a moral achievement or just a subtle kind of self-indulgence, and we kept landing in the Schopenhauer–Nietzsche fight. Schopenhauer makes something like pity the basis of all genuine moral motivation: in pity, another person's suffering is felt almost as if it were one's own, and that is what breaks through ordinary egoism. Nietzsche thinks this is backwards — pity is a pathology that creates a second sufferer and flatters the person who feels it instead of actually helping. I tried to turn the Nietzschean point into a cleaner argument than Nietzsche ever bothered to give, and now I can't tell whether its valid:

(1) If pity is the fundamental moral motive, then the only features of a situation I can be morally moved by are those that come through the felt character of my pity.
(2) What comes through the felt character of my pity is fixed by how vividly another person's pain strikes me: their proximity, their resemblance to me, whether their suffering has narrative salience, how tired I am.
(3) Those vividness factors are morally irrelevant — they don't track how badly off the person actually is, or what I owe them.
(4) So if pity is the fundamental moral motive, my moral responsiveness is hostage to factors that don't track what matters morally.
(5) A motive can be the foundation of morality only if it doesn't leave moral responsiveness hostage to morally irrelevant factors.
(6) Therefore pity cannot be the foundation of morality; at best it's a biased heuristic needing correction by a standard outside pity, which a pity-foundationalist can't supply without abandoning the view.

I sort of want the argument to be right. But I can't tell whether I've cheated somewhere. Walk me through it: is the argument valid as written? If not, which step or steps fail, and what would the corrected versions be? If it does go through, what exactly does it prove against Schopenhauer — and is there a version of the pity-foundation view that escapes it without changing the subject?
