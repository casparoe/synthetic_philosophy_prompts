I cover AI policy and the lab world for a science-and-ideas magazine, and next month I'm presenting a reported argument at an invitation-only workshop on strategic competition in emerging technologies. My thesis, condensed: the familiar "AI arms race" frame misdescribes the dynamics among the leading labs and between the United States and China, and Fearon's rationalist account of war as bargaining failure gives a better diagnosis of why acceleration continues even though most of the relevant actors say they would prefer a slower, safer trajectory. Specifically, I argue the binding failure is not private information — the broad outlines of capability and intent are fairly legible, and every side has incentives to exaggerate anyway — but a commitment problem: neither a lab nor a state can credibly promise that taking the lead won't look like full deployment and a hardened lead, and everyone knows it. There is also a winner-take-all flavor to whichever actor first sets global norms that makes the prize partly indivisible, though I think that is the softest of the three mechanisms. The policy upshot is that information-sharing and appeals to shared catastrophic risk will underdeliver unless paired with verification regimes and commitments that are genuinely costly to reverse.

The room will be roughly two dozen people, and it is not a philosophy seminar. Most are career civil servants from defense and foreign ministries who spent years writing the "strategic competition" language into official documents and think of rivalry between states as structural rather than chosen; a few are policy leads from major AI labs who negotiate compute and export rules and believe no individual firm can slow down without handing the frontier to a competitor or to a less scrupulous actor; and three are economists who model R&D races and will see my argument as a forced transplant from interstate war theory onto firm behavior, where the standard account is overinvestment driven by an appropriability problem rather than a bargaining failure. They are bright and adversarial, and they will know Fearon at least by reputation.

Mark which objections have real force and what I should concede up front rather than fight; identify the one reply that has to land if the thesis is going to survive the hour.
