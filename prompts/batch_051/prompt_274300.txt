I am a PhD student in formal epistemology, and I have spent the last few months trying to get clear on an asymmetry in the way we handle selection-based evidence. Consider a wholly ordinary inference first. A number is drawn at random from the integers 1 through N, with N unknown, and I am shown the number 7. If the only serious hypotheses are N = 10 and N = 10,000,000, then the observation I got is a million times more probable under the first hypothesis than under the second, and no one finds this troubling: seeing a small value is exactly what a small N predicts. Now take an indexical case with the same structure. I am told that my birth rank among all humans who will ever live is roughly 100 billion, and I compare two hypotheses about the eventual total: 200 billion or 20 trillion. If I regard myself as uniformly distributed among all the humans who will ever live, my observed rank is about a hundred times more probable on the first hypothesis than on the second. This is the skeleton of the doomsday argument, and many people who accept the urn inference without hesitation think something has gone wrong here. The standard complaints are that I was guaranteed to observe some rank or other, that someone exists at every rank, and that no actual sampling mechanism ever selected me. But the first complaint applies word for word to the urn: I was guaranteed to observe some number there as well, and the fact that I observed 7 was still informative.

Pushing in the other direction, consider the presumptuous philosopher. If I treat my existence as a random draw from all observers that a theory says will exist, then a cosmological theory that predicts a trillion observers is, before any physical evidence has been weighed, far more likely to account for my being here than a theory that predicts only a handful of observers; the number of observers alone does all the work. Almost no one—including people entirely comfortable with the urn—is willing to let that move settle a question about cosmology. We therefore seem to hold three verdicts: selection evidence is legitimate when the mechanism is a random number generator, dubious when the draw is my birth rank, and intolerable when the draw is my bare existence. I want to know whether there is a defensible principle that sorts these cases into exactly those verdicts. Is the asymmetry between ordinary sampling evidence and self-locating evidence justified, or is it an inconsistency we tolerate because the resulting conclusions are unwelcome? Give me the strongest non-ad hoc principle you can find, and tell me which of the three inferences—urn, birth-rank, bare-existence—it would keep, revise, or reject.
