I cover science and ideas for a living, and I keep tripping over an asymmetry I can't make go away. When an individual takes a 1-in-10,000 chance of dying for a big payout, we say its their choice and mostly respect it. When a city approves a policy with the same 1-in-10,000 chance of killing one random stranger in exchange for a large benefit spread across many people, we treat it as a different kind of decision entirely—controversial, political, in need of special justification. Harsanyi's aggregation theorem seems to collapse that distinction: if each person's preferences obey the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, and social preferences obey them too plus a Pareto condition, then social utility is just a weighted sum of individual utilities—the same expected-utility arithmetic, one level up. So is there a real difference between aggregating within one person and aggregating across persons, or are we just squeamish about doing the sums out loud? Give me the strongest principle that could justify treating the two cases differently, test it against a nearby case where it feels uncomfortable, and tell me honestly whether the asymmetry should stay or go.
