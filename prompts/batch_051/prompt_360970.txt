Last weekend a friend and I spent most of the evening arguing about whether my wife and I should continue the Father Christmas story for our seven-year-old daughter, and I would be grateful for a careful judgement on the disagreement rather than general reassurance about what parents ordinarily do.

The situation is this. Our daughter has been raised with Santa, the mince pie, the letter, the whole thing. A few weeks ago she looked up from her cereal and asked me, in a level tone, whether Father Christmas was real or whether it was Mummy and me all along. I asked her what she thought. She said she thought it was probably us, but that she wanted me to tell her the truth. I changed the subject, and since then she has continued to write the letter and leave out the food, though she has not asked again. She also has a four-year-old brother who is still wholly taken with the story.

My friend's position, as he developed it over the evening, was roughly this. Before a child asks the direct question, the story can be a kind of shared make-believe that both sides are free to enjoy. But once a child explicitly asks whether it is true, and in particular once she says she wants the truth, carrying on with the story stops being playful and becomes a lie aimed at someone who is testing whether her parents will tell her what they take the world to be like. On his view, the morally important line is the first direct question, not the first doubt; and to keep the story going past that line, as I have done, is to teach a child how to be managed rather than informed.

My own position, which he called self-serving, is that a seven-year-old's direct question is not always a request for information in the way he assumes. I think she often knows the answer already, and what she is really asking is whether we are still doing the game together, whether she is allowed to step into the pretence knowingly, the way one joins an agreed fiction, rather than whether she has been duped. If that is right, then answering 'of course he is real' is not a lie in the ordinary sense, because it is not functioning as a report about the world; it is functioning as a signal that the ritual is still open to her. He replied that this is exactly the sort of story liars tell about the difference between lying and keeping a game going, and that at seven a child is old enough for the two to be held apart.

So the disagreement has narrowed to a question I cannot settle. Is the practice still defensible now, after a direct request for the truth? And is the right line, as my friend insists, the first direct question, or does it matter what the child means by the question, or that telling her would end the story early for her younger brother? I would also like to know whether there is a way to preserve what is good about the ritual while being truthful with her, or whether my friend is right that the two aims simply come apart at this point.

I would like your verdict as an outsider, argued rather than asserted. Please say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail. Please write in continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, as you would in a letter, and in American English.
