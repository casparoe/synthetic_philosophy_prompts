I am taking a seminar in philosophy of physics, and we have been discussing gauge freedom and the question of how much of the formalism of a gauge theory should be taken to describe the world. I have been trying to make the deflationary answer precise, but I keep running into the Aharonov–Bohm effect. Here is the argument I have been toying with:

(1) In classical electromagnetism, a gauge transformation of the form A_μ → A_μ + ∂_μ λ leaves the electromagnetic field strength F_μν unchanged, and F_μν is standardly taken to be the complete physical content of the theory.

(2) If two mathematical states are related by a transformation that changes no physical fact, they represent the same physical possibility, and any structure that distinguishes them is surplus structure rather than part of the physical world.

(3) The vector potential A_μ distinguishes states that the gauge transformation maps to one another, while F_μν does not; therefore A_μ is surplus structure and is not physically real.

(4) But in the Aharonov–Bohm effect, the interference pattern of an electron passing around a solenoid depends on the loop integral of A_μ around the solenoid, even though the electron travels only through a region where F_μν = 0.

(5) If an observable interference pattern depends on a loop integral of A_μ in a region where F_μν is zero, then A_μ carries empirical information that goes beyond F_μν.

(6) Therefore A_μ is physically real after all, and the deflationary argument in steps (1) through (3) must fail at some point.

I suspect the conclusion is too quick, but I have not been able to pin down where the argument goes wrong. Is the argument through step (3) valid? Does step (5) follow from step (4), and does step (6) follow? Identify the exact step at which the argument fails, and state the strongest defensible version of that step. If the argument does not fail, explain why the Aharonov–Bohm effect does not force the conclusion.
