I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I just sat through a panel on whether death is bad for you that I'm trying to turn into a piece. The Epicureans kept running the symmetry line: the eternity before you existed doesn't bother you, so why should the eternity after? The deprivationists kept answering that the badness isn't in some felt state — when you're dead there's no you around to feel anything — it's in the good life you're robbed of. Both sides have been making essentially these moves since Lucretius, and nobody budged. What I want for the article isn't a verdict, it's a diagnosis of the impasse. Is this a verbal dispute about the word "harm," a substantive disagreement about whether a harm has to be experienced, two camps answering different questions (one about whether you should fear death, one about whether death is bad for the person who dies), or a clash of bedrock intuitions about time and value that no argument can reach? My hunch is there's something hidden about the metaphysics of the person in here — like whether you exist at the time of the deprivation — but I could be overcomplicating it. And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a sharper distinction, a thought experiment, a formal result — or is this one just doomed to run forever?
