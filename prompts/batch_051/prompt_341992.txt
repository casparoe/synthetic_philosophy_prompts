There's this argument I keep coming back to that fields only make real progress when they get fast, unambiguous feedback. Math: a proof is right or wrong and somebody eventually checks. Programming: your code runs or it doesn't, and the compiler tells you the second its wrong. Chess: you play a move and you win or lose. So those fields accumulate real knowledge, while fields where feedback is slow, vague, or mediated by reputation—philosophy, say, or macro forecasting—seem to circle the same debates for decades. I find that argument pretty persuasive. But then I think about what's happened in the last twenty years with cheap, legible feedback loops being installed everywhere: ML benchmark leaderboards, A/B tests in product design, automated graders, real-time metrics dashboards. And those settings seem to rediscover a failure mode over and over: people optimize the feedback itself. They overfit to the benchmark, hack the metric, chase the number that moves, and the field can rocket ahead on its yardstick while the underlying ability the yardstick was supposed to track stays flat. In some cases the yardstick wasn't even a good proxy to begin with, and fast feedback just locks the whole field onto the proxy. So my question is whether the original feedback-progress argument survives this. Does the argument predict that adding clear feedback to a field speeds up real progress, or only progress on whatever the feedback happens to measure? Is the metric-gaming just an edge case the argument can absorb, maybe by insisting the feedback has to be on the outcome you actually care about and not a proxy (and if so, how do you know when you have that?), or does it show the argument was under-specified enough to be almost empty? What does the repaired version of the argument look like, if there is one?
