Here's an argument a buddy sent me that seems to prove too much. Numbered version:

1. Self-deception is basically lying to yourself.
2. Lying requires the liar to believe not-p while intending the audience to believe p.
3. So self-deception about p requires you to believe not-p while getting yourself to believe p.
4. But that means your mind has to keep the not-p belief available for the deceiving part while hiding it from the deceived part. A single integrated agent with one self-model can't do that: the self-model would have to represent p and not-p about the same thing at the same time, which is incoherent.
5. So self-deception is impossible.

The problem: if this argument worked, it would also show confirmation bias is impossible. A person who is confirmation-biased about their favorite policy registers counterevidence at some level (otherwise it's just ignorance) while still ending up more convinced. That's the same double state, just without the word "lying." But confirmation bias is real, so something in the argument must not transfer. I'd rather keep confirmation bias and drop self-deception, but only if there's a non-ad-hoc way to do that.
