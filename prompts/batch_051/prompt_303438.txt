I'm a cognitive neuroscience postdoc whose lab works in the tradition of Bernard Baars's global workspace theory and Stanislas Dehaene's global neuronal workspace model. In our lab meetings, "conscious" basically means globally broadcast information that is available to executive functions, verbal report, planning, and memory; anything not in the workspace is unconscious, and the interesting scientific questions are about thresholds, ignition dynamics, and which representations win the competition for access. A colleague recently pressed me on whether this vocabulary can even state what Ned Block is worried about in his 1995 Behavioral and Brain Sciences article "On a Confusion about a Function of Consciousness." He pointed me to the following passage:

"Consciousness is a mongrel concept: there are a number of very different 'consciousnesses.' Phenomenal consciousness is experience; the phenomenally conscious aspect of a state is what it is like to be in that state. The mark of access-consciousness, by contrast, is availability for use in reasoning and rationally guiding speech and action."

Restate Block's distinction between phenomenal consciousness and access-consciousness, and his blindsight-based argument about the function of consciousness, in the vocabulary I actually use—global workspace, broadcasting, ignition, competition among specialized processors, availability to executive systems, reportability, and related notions—as faithfully as you can. Then give me the residue report: which of Block's claims translate cleanly into workspace-theoretic terms, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart at all in my vocabulary. In particular, I want to know whether the phenomenal/access distinction corresponds to any real architectural or functional difference within a global workspace framework, or whether the framework treats the distinction as a difference without a mechanistic consequence. Be honest about where the translation fails: is the residue a defect of my vocabulary, or a sign that Block's distinction is less clear than it looks from the outside?
