I am a retired natural scientist who has come to philosophy late, and the French Revolution has become my case study for understanding how historical explanations actually work. I keep meeting the same dictum, usually in the words "revolutions devour their children," and I recently heard it used to close an argument about whether the Terror was structurally built into the Revolution or was the contingent result of particular leaders and circumstances. The speaker treated it as though it settled the question; to me it settled nothing. I have also seen the phrase used to predict that any revolutionary coalition will eventually turn on its own leadership, and to imply that a revolution is self-undermining almost by definition.

I would like you to take the slogan seriously as language rather than leaving it as an inert metaphor. Lay out the distinct precise claims it could be expressing. For each reading, say whether it is a tautology or near-tautology, a substantive empirical hypothesis, an evaluative or moral judgment, or something else, and which of those readings are false or merely rhetorical. Then identify which readings would actually do the explanatory or predictive work the slogan is being used to do in the cases I described, and which only borrow an appearance of explanatory force from the metaphor of consumption. Finally, I would like to know whether the slogan, on its most defensible reading, commits one to any particular position in the debate between class-struggle, ideological, and contingency-based interpretations of the Terror, or whether it is compatible with all three and merely sounds as if it takes sides.
