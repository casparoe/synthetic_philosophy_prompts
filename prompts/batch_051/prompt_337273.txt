I'm in a collective action seminar and we keep using the standard four-way taxonomy for goods: a pure public good is non-excludable and non-rivalrous, a common-pool resource is rivalrous but hard to exclude, a club good is excludable but non-rivalrous up to congestion, and a private good is both. Fine. But my study group keeps getting stuck on a case that doesn't sit still. Suppose a city runs an open, ungated database of real-time air-quality readings produced by thousands of privately owned sensors mounted on people's homes. Anyone can query the data for free, so nobody is excluded from the readings. The catch is that the accuracy and spatial coverage of the whole dataset depends on enough owners keeping their sensors powered and calibrated, which costs them a little time and money. If people free-ride on everyone else's sensors, coverage gets patchy and everyone's readings get worse, so the value of the database is rivalrous in a weird way: one extra query doesn't degrade it, but one extra free-rider on maintenance does. Also, nothing technical stops the city from later charging for high-frequency API access, in which case the good would become excludable just by policy, even though its original design was open. So is this database a public good, a common-pool resource, a club good, or a case the taxonomy can't classify? Walk through the criteria honestly, say where it genuinely breaks the standard definitions, and then tell me whether the problem is the case or whether the taxonomy itself is confusing the infrastructure with the flow of benefits it produces.
