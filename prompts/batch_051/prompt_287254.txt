I run an advanced undergraduate seminar on probability and inference with fifteen students, and I have one eighty-minute session to spend on the question of whether a finite binary sequence can be random. I want a structured in-class game that makes the students feel why that question cannot be settled by inspecting the sequence itself, and that does this before I give any lecture: the rules, roles, and stakes should be arranged so that the students run into the facts on their own. In particular, I want them to collide with the fact that a conspicuously patterned string and a disorderly-looking string are exactly equally probable under a fair coin; that any short string they are shown could, for all the digits themselves say, be the early segment of some simple deterministic rule they simply have not guessed yet; and that the same finite string could be the output of a stochastic process in one run and a deterministic rule in the next, with the digits providing no way to tell. Design the game with concrete rules, a list of materials I can prepare, a payoff scheme that does not require real money, a timeline for the eighty minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments in the play onto the distinction between pseudorandomness and randomness, and onto the contrast among source-based, statistical, incompressibility-based, and betting-strategy-based accounts of randomness. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which feature of the idea the game cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself. Write the whole thing in continuous prose, as one would write an email or a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
