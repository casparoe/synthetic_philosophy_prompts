I serve on the collections governance board of a nonprofit digital archive. The board must issue a written decision about the Meridian Survivors' Forum collection, and I have volunteered to draft the decision memo. I am a senior software engineer by training, with no formal background in philosophy, so the memo should rest on the facts of the case and on plainly explained reasoning rather than on scholarly authority. Please do not rely on named theorists, citations, or outside reading.

The collection consists of roughly 84,000 posts made between 1998 and 2007 to a private, pseudonymous online forum used by former members of a closed religious sect. The forum functioned as a support network, and its posts include first-person accounts of forced labor, psychological abuse, sexual assault, and family separation, alongside ordinary material about rebuilding a life after leaving. The forum's organizers shut it down in 2007 and transferred a copy to a research foundation, whose digital collections later came to us. There was no terms-of-service provision at the time covering long-term archival use, and individual posters were never asked for consent to the transfer.

A group of former members has petitioned the board to delete the collection permanently. Their position is that the forum was a closed, therapeutic space; that posters reasonably expected their words to remain within the community; that many posts are detailed enough to identify people to employers, neighbors, or family; and that the archive has already led journalists to contact former members. They argue that preserving the collection keeps people tethered to the worst period of their lives, and that the opportunity to forget is a condition of dignity and recovery.

Another group, including other former members, two historians, and a human-rights organization that documents institutional abuse, asks the board to retain the collection as a read-only, searchable record. Their position is that these posts are among the few contemporaneous documents of how the sect operated; that the sect now denies the abuses and runs a well-funded effort to control the historical record; and that deleting the collection would make the archive complicit in that erasure. They propose instead to exclude the posts from public web search, require applications for access, log all use, and prohibit bulk download, while keeping the full collection intact.

Two further facts bear on the decision. First, a third-party web-crawling project already holds an incomplete mirror of the forum, including some highly identifying posts, and has refused past requests to take material down. Second, the donor agreement by which the collection reached us calls for long-term preservation and permits access restrictions for privacy reasons, but does not permit destruction except for a cause recorded at the time of deposit, which this petition would not satisfy. Our counsel advises that deletion would carry modest legal risk, while retention with access restrictions is the most defensible legal posture.

The disagreement has exposed a deeper issue the board cannot avoid, and I would like the memo to work through it directly: whether there can be an obligation to remember wrongdoing, and whether forgetting is sometimes the more defensible moral response. I have not read the academic literature on either side, and the memo should not lean on it.

The board must choose among three options: (1) delete the collection and all backups, and request that the mirror project and search engines remove it; (2) retain the collection under the present access conditions; or (3) retain it under a restricted-access model, with a 25-year access embargo on the most identifying posts measured from the date of the decision, an application-and-review procedure for the rest, and a mechanism for individual posters to request redaction. Please draft the decision memo recommending one option, with reasons the losing parties could actually answer, and with an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side.
