For our doctoral seminar in social choice theory, write a decision tree that takes a participant from the starting commitment "I accept the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem, and I am not willing to settle for dictatorship" to a named position on how collective choice should proceed. Build the tree out of the logical space of escape routes rather than a list of names. The six branch questions should correspond to: (i) whether the rule must be fully strategy-proof or may tolerate some profitable misreporting; (ii) whether it must accept every logically possible preference profile or may restrict the domain, for instance to single-peaked rankings; (iii) whether it must select one definite alternative or may randomize over alternatives; (iv) whether it may use only ordinal rankings or may also use cardinal intensities, possibly with transfers; (v) whether it must be onto, so that each alternative could in principle win under some profile; and (vi) whether it must handle three or more alternatives or may be limited to two. Phrase every node as a concrete case or trade-off that a participant can answer without already knowing which named position it leads to, and place the strategy-proofness question first, since abandoning it changes the force of all the later questions.

The leaves must include the median-voter rule under a single-peaked domain; a strategy-proof cardinal mechanism with transfers in the Vickrey–Clarke–Groves family; random dictatorship, where one voter is selected at random and their top choice is implemented; accepting manipulable rules such as the Borda count or instant-runoff voting; majority rule over exactly two alternatives; an imposed or range-restricted rule that excludes some alternatives from ever being chosen; and dictatorship itself as the limiting leaf for a participant who declines every escape route. Make the tree display where the theorem does its work: the cell that keeps full strategy-proofness, ordinal information, deterministic outcomes, universal domain, onto-ness, and three or more alternatives is occupied only by dictatorial rules, and once non-dictatorship is demanded as well that cell is empty. Mark at least two further cells of the logical space that no established position occupies—for example, a rule that surrenders both strategy-proofness and universal domain—and briefly diagnose whether each is empty because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely underexplored. Flag any pair of leaves that the question set cannot actually separate, and say why. Aim for roughly six questions and seven leaves.
