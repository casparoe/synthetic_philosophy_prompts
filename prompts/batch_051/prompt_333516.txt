For the past two years my partner and I have been deciding whether to have a child, which has made the ethics of bringing a person into existence feel immediate rather than academic. The idea I keep returning to is the procreation asymmetry: roughly, that a possible child's prospective pain counts as a reason not to bring her into existence, while her prospective pleasure does not count as a parallel reason in favor, because a child who is never born is not deprived of any missed pleasure, whereas a child who is born genuinely bears whatever pain she undergoes. I am not asking you to adjudicate whether that asymmetry is correct. I want to work through its consequences in a world where the relevant facts are knowable in advance.

Imagine a society otherwise like a contemporary affluent democracy. In this society a cheap, safe, and universally available technology has existed for one full generation. Before conceiving, prospective parents can obtain a well-calibrated estimate of the hedonic profile of the child they would have: total lifetime pain, total lifetime pleasure, their distribution across the life course, and explicit confidence intervals. The estimate is not biographical; it says nothing about specific events, relationships, or achievements, only about experienced well-being and ill-being. No one is required to consult it, but doing so is ordinary, and refusing to look is widely regarded as a significant choice.

Predict, with reasoning, how this society looks fifty years later. I want a visible chain of mechanisms rather than a narrative. Among the questions I hope you will address: Does the procreation asymmetry become behaviorally dominant, so that creating a forecast net-negative life is treated as a serious wrong while declining to create a forecast net-positive life remains largely free of censure? Who still chooses to have children, and how might parental expectations and anxieties shift once a child's hedonic trajectory is sketched in advance? What secondary pressures would arise around screening, stigma, insurance, or regulation? For each prediction, indicate whether it is robust or merely plausible, and specify what would have to be true about human risk attitudes, moral psychology, or institutional design for it to fail.
