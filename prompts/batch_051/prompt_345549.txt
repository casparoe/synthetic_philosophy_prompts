In Leviathan, chapter VI, Hobbes offers an account of laughter as the expression of what he calls sudden glory: a pleasure that arises when we compare ourselves favourably with others, or with our own former state. A colleague has pressed the following argument against treating that account as a literal causal thesis about all laughter, and I cannot find the weak joint.

(1) If Hobbes is right, every episode of laughter is caused by felt superiority—sudden glory in some comparison in our favour.  
(2) So when someone laughs at a clever pun, a fine incongruity, or an elegant absurdity, they are not really responding to the wordplay or incongruity as such; they are covertly enjoying their own comparative advantage.  
(3) But many such cases manifestly lack any target treated as inferior: we laugh at tickling, at nervous relief, and at benign violations in which no one is demeaned.  
(4) A sophisticated Hobbesian can redescribe each case: the person who gets the pun feels superior to those who miss it; nervous laughter marks superiority over a danger now past; even the tickled person is superior to their own body’s helplessness.  
(5) This flexibility proves too much. If every imaginable case can be redescribed as superiority, the theory is compatible with everything and excludes nothing, which means it cannot be a genuine explanation of what distinguishes the funny from the unfunny.  
(6) Therefore the literal superiority theory must be rejected.

I am inclined to keep premise (1) and the conclusion rather than accept the methodological demand in (5), but I want to know what either choice costs for a viable theory of humour. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
