I am a retired organic chemist, so my philosophical reading habits are still those of someone trained to look for the actual mechanism rather than the school label. Recently I have been working through F. H. Bradley's *Appearance and Reality*, and I keep returning to this passage from Chapter III, “Relation and Quality”:

“Relation presupposes quality, and quality relation. Each can be something neither together with, nor apart from, the other; and the vicious circle in which they turn is not the truth about reality.”

I take it that the famous regress follows from the first sentence: if a relation is something distinct from its terms, then we appear to need a further relation to attach it to each of them, and that further relation in turn needs its own attachments, and so on. What I want is to make sure I have not missed a possible stopping point.

So far I have collected the following families of response:

(1) Frege’s unsaturatedness move: the relation is not an object-like entity but a concept with a gap; it is completed by its arguments, so no second relation is needed to bind it to them.

(2) Russell’s 1903 move in *The Principles of Mathematics*: relations are themselves terms, and their relating is primitive; the regress arises only if one treats the fact that R relates a and b as yet another term that must stand in a further relation to them.

(3) Armstrong’s states-of-affairs move: a state of affairs such as a’s being R to b is the fundamental unit, and its unity is not itself a further constituent; there is no tie conceived as a relation among the constituents.

(4) Bradley’s own conclusion: the regress is real but cannot be solved within relational thought; the relational form is appearance, and the true unity is non-relational.

(5) The eliminativist nominalist move: external relations are not real entities at all, so the regress is dissolved rather than stopped.

I want one more that is genuinely different from these five, not a restatement or a hybrid of them. In particular, since (2) and (5) already seem to exhaust the obvious options—either the relation’s relating is primitive, or the relation is abandoned—I am especially interested in any response that keeps relations is real and does not simply take their relating as primitive in Russell’s way. Is there such a response? If there is, tell me how it differs specifically from (2) and (5). If there is not, tell me honestly whether that means a relational realist is forced back to Russell’s 1903 position.
