I am a novelist several drafts into a courtroom novel, and the pivotal trial scene turns on what the standard of proof beyond a reasonable doubt actually requires. I need from you a working definition of that standard—not the familiar jury-instruction language about firm conviction or moral certainty, but a criterion precise enough to sort the cases below into proven and not proven. In each case, treat the question as whether the stated proposition is proven beyond a reasonable doubt.

Please do three things. First, state your definition. Second, apply it to every case in the order given and say explicitly what verdict it yields; if the definition misclassifies one of the cases I have marked as clear, revise the definition rather than relabeling the case, unless you intend to defend a revision of my classification on the merits. Third, tell me which of your final verdicts you regard as least secure, and which case or pair of cases most exposes a weakness in your definition. I am not looking for citations or a survey of the literature; I am looking for an analysis I could put in the mouth of a thoughtful judge. Do not merely say that a reasonable doubt is one grounded in the evidence; a juror can always ground a doubt in the base rate, so the definition must do more than that.

Here are the cases.

1. The Corroborated Confession. The accused confesses and supplies several non-public details of the crime, including the location of a weapon that investigators had not yet found and that he could not plausibly have guessed. The recovered weapon matches the victim's injuries. I take the proposition that he committed the offence to be a clear case of proof.

2. The Familiar Assailant. The victim identifies a neighbor she has known for twenty years as her attacker. The neighbor's fingerprints are found on the window used to enter, and the victim's blood is found on a jacket in his apartment. I take the proposition that he attacked her to be a clear case of proof.

3. The Gatecrasher. A thousand spectators attend an event, but only 499 of them purchased admission. The defendant is shown to have been one of the thousand, and there is no other evidence bearing on whether he paid. It is more likely than not that he gatecrashed, but I take it to be clear that the proposition that he gatecrashed is not proven beyond a reasonable doubt.

4. The Matching Description. The only evidence is that the defendant matches a witness's description of a man in his thirties, about six feet tall, in a red jacket, seen near the scene. The description fits thousands of people in the city. I take the proposition that he was the man seen to be a clear case of non-proof.

5. The Blue Bus Base Rate. A pedestrian is struck by a bus that flees. It is established that eighty per cent of the buses on that street at that hour are operated by the Blue Bus Company, and no witness can identify the company. The question is whether the proposition that the offending bus belonged to the Blue Bus Company is proven beyond a reasonable doubt. My inclination is that it is not, but I cannot articulate why the eighty per cent figure fails to do the work it would do elsewhere.

6. The Two Mediocre Eyewitnesses. Two independent witnesses, each with a demonstrated accuracy rate of about seventy per cent under these viewing conditions, both identify the defendant as the person fleeing the scene. Each identification alone would be weak. On the simplest independence model, the chance that both are in error is just under one in ten, and together they intuitively seem to constitute proof. I lean toward saying this is proven, but I need the definition to explain any difference from Case 5, where the probability looks at least as strong.

7. The Cold-Hit DNA Match. A single-source DNA profile is recovered from the scene, with a random-match probability of one in ten million. The defendant was not a suspect; he was identified only because his profile sat in a database of five million profiles searched against the scene sample, and there is no other evidence tying him to the crime. I am unsure whether the proposition that he was the source is proven beyond a reasonable doubt.

8. The Interlocking Circumstantial Web. The defendant was near the scene that evening, lied about his whereabouts, had recently quarreled with the victim, owned an unusual tool that could have produced the wound pattern, and a fibre on the victim's sleeve is of a type common in the defendant's workplace. None of these facts is sufficient alone; each is weak and partly statistical. Together they form a coherent narrative, though no alternative narrative is positively supported—only logical possibilities remain. I am unsure whether this is proof.

9. The Streetwise Background Rate. The facts are those of Case 5, except that the eighty per cent figure is never introduced in evidence. Instead, the jurors already know from living on the street that four of every five buses on that route are Blue Company buses, and the witness can say only that a bus was involved. If Case 5 is not proven, must this case also be not proven, or does the provenance of the base rate change the epistemology? I am unsure, and I want the definition to handle the point without making the verdict depend merely on whether the same fact was formally admitted into evidence.
