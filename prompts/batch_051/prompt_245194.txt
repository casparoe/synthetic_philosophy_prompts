I work in causal inference and data science, and I am comfortable with the standard machinery: potential outcomes, directed acyclic graphs, the do-operator, identifiability conditions, treatment effects, mediators, confounders, and counterfactual contrasts. A historian friend keeps telling me that the debate over the Great Divergence — roughly, why sustained modern economic growth emerged in northwestern Europe, and Britain in particular, rather than in the Yangzi Delta or elsewhere in China — is really about the same things I study, only under different names. The explanatory literature seems to sort into families: institutionalist accounts (secure property rights, parliamentary constraints on the executive, relatively independent courts), geographic and resource accounts (coal deposits, Atlantic trade, New World land, disease environments), cultural accounts (norms around work, thrift, literacy, or scientific curiosity), and demographic or Malthusian accounts (fertility restraint, labor scarcity, the relative price of labor versus capital). There is also the prior question, associated with the Needham question and with Kenneth Pomeranz's work, of whether "why Europe and not China?" is even well posed, given that the Yangzi Delta and England may have been broadly comparable in commercial development and living standards until surprisingly late.

I would like you to do two things. First, restate the main families of explanation for the Great Divergence as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary of causal inference: treatments, potential outcomes, confounders, mediators, interactions, counterfactual contrasts, and identification strategies. Second, and more important, give me a residue report: which claims translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which have no expression at all in that vocabulary. In particular, I want your view on whether the question "why Britain and not China?" corresponds to a well-defined causal contrast — for example, an intervention on some institutional or geographic variable with China as the counterfactual — or whether the question itself dissolves under the translation. Be honest about whether the residue reflects a limitation of the causal-inference vocabulary, a sign that some historical claims are less precise than they look, or both. Please write in American English.
