I write about science and ideas for a living, but for my sins I also sit on the board of our county historical society: the director asked me to stay on after I did a long feature about the city finally taking down its Confederate monument, and I said yes without thinking it through. We vote Thursday, and the chair wants a decision memo from me. The curator has proposed pulling the old decommissioned memorial panels out of storage — the bronze ones from the old courthouse lawn — and installing them in a permanent exhibit tentatively titled The Lost Cause: How a Region Wrote Its Own History. Her line is that right now the only people keeping the panels' story alive are cranks on the internet, and a museum is exactly where you show a myth as myth: object up front, consensus history on the walls around it. Her camp keeps saying this is explaining, not honoring. Half the board agrees. The other half thinks that's a distinction without a difference when the object was cast to honor: a Confederate memorial in a museum is still a Confederate memorial, visitors will pose with it and leave flowers, and no amount of wall text will cancel the act of honor the thing itself performs.

The chair has asked me to recommend approve, reject, or approve with conditions, with an actual bottom line and a date attached, and with the losing side's best argument handled head on rather than straw-manned. I suspect the real crux isn't these particular panels but whether a public institution can ever display an identity-founding national myth without endorsing it — and whether an explanatory frame changes the speech act or just launders it. Write the memo I should circulate. If you pick conditions, make them concrete enough that the curator could either meet them or refuse them by, say, the end of the year.
