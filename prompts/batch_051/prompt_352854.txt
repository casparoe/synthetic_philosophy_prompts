Here is a reconstructed argument that Boethius's eternity doctrine does not solve the problem of divine foreknowledge but only relocates it. I use "S has a choice about X" to mean: it is within S's power to make X not obtain.

(1) Let A be some ordinary act that a human S performs at a future time t. An essentially omniscient, infallible God knows, from eternity, the true proposition that S does A at t.

(2) God's knowing that S does A at t is fixed relative to S: S has no more choice about whether God has this knowledge than S has about what has already happened. If it is objected that God's knowledge is eternal rather than in the past, that only tightens the point, since an immutable eternal state is no less beyond S's control than a past event is.

(3) Necessarily, if God knows that S does A at t, then S does A at t.

(4) No-choice transfers across entailment: if S has no choice about a state of affairs, and that state of affairs entails another, then S has no choice about the entailed one.

(5) Therefore S has no choice about doing A at t.

(6) An act is free only if the agent has a choice about whether she performs it.

(7) Therefore S does not do A freely.

The Boethian move, as I understand it, is to grant that the connection between God's seeing and the thing seen is necessary, but to call it only the necessity of the inference and not the necessity of the thing inferred; S's act itself remains contingent. But that seems to answer a different argument. Steps (2) through (5) never claim that S's act is logically necessary; they claim only that S has no choice about it, which is what matters for freedom. So the eternity doctrine appears to leave (5) standing, and fatalism seems to return without any actual foreknowledge, purely from God's eternal infallible cognition of the act.

Is the argument valid? If not, say exactly which step fails and under which reading of "no choice" it fails. If it is valid, say which premise a Boethian should reject, what reading of the distinction between the necessity of the inference and the necessity of the thing inferred would block the argument, and what that reading commits him to. Whichever way you rule, say which consideration would most likely make you change your verdict, and where your own reasoning about this is most likely to fail.
