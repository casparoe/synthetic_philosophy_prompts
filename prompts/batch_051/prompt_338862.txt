I'm a site-reliability engineer, and I do my clearest thinking in the vocabulary of distributed systems: nodes, partitions, quorums, leader election, fencing, consistency models, and the operational failure modes of split-brain states. A friend in cognitive science has been trying to explain the philosophical literature on split-brain patients to me — people whose corpus callosum has been surgically divided as a treatment for intractable epilepsy. In the classic experiments, a stimulus is presented so that only one hemisphere receives it, and the hemisphere that can speak reports seeing something different from what the other hemisphere indicates by guiding the hand, or reports seeing nothing while the hand points correctly. She tells me the main philosophical positions are that the surgery results in two distinct centers of consciousness; that there remains exactly one subject of experience whose stream of consciousness is now disunified or degraded; and that there is no fully determinate fact about how many subjects exist after the surgery. The disagreement appears to turn on notions like a subject of experience, phenomenal unity, and what it is like to be such a patient.

I would like you to restate this debate in the vocabulary I actually use, as faithfully as you can, and then give me the residue report. What in the philosophers' positions maps cleanly onto nodes, partitions, shared state, consistency, replication, and conflict resolution? What maps only with distortion, and what has no counterpart at all? The obvious trouble is that with only two hemispheres there is no majority quorum to arbitrate between them, so I keep reaching for this way of putting it: an intact mind is a healthy cluster maintaining a single consistent state across components, while a split-brain patient is a two-node cluster running through a permanent network partition, each node executing its own consistent view of the world until some later reconciliation or mixed output. Is that translation illuminating or misleading? Does my vocabulary have a way to express the claim that there is still one subject despite the partition, or does it inevitably force a two-node reading? And is the absence of anything like a quorum a feature of the clinical situation that philosophers are tracking when they call the patient disunified, or a sign that unity in their sense was never a consistency property to begin with? I'm less interested in being told which position is correct than in learning what the translation carries, what it distorts, and what it leaves behind.
