I'm a novelist working on a historical novel set in the Warring States, and there's a set piece I keep failing to get right. Two advisers at a local court—one a convinced Mohist, one a Confucian ritualist—have been arguing for years, and the lord finally loses patience and orders them to produce a joint memorial before he'll fund either of their proposals: a list of what they actually agree on, where exactly they seperate, and what would settle the rest. I want to write the text they produce as an actual document in the novel, so it has to read like something both could genuinely sign, with neither side turned into a straw man.

The Mohist keeps insisting that partiality is the root of the whole mess: everyone cares for his own family and his own state and not for other people's, and that's why you get robbery, feuds, and wars, so the fix is to care for other people's parents and children and states the way you care for your own. The Confucian keeps answering that care has to start somewhere, and it starts with your own father and mother; graded love isn't a compromise, it's the root the whole thing grows out of, and an impartiality that makes your own father count for no more than a stranger cuts that root. The Mohist also has a jab he likes: if you won't look after a stranger's aged father, what right do you have to expect that stranger to look after yours? And the Confucian has his reply ready: nobody gets to the wider world by pretending every old man is his own father; you get there by enlarging a circle that starts at home. Then the Mohist usually grumbles that he isn't asking for equal warmth, he's asking for equal benefit—that other people's parents get fed and clothed, that the sick get treated, that the abandoned dead get buried—and the Confucian grumbles back that this just proves the Mohist has turned care into a ledger.

Write the joint memorial they'd produce. Format it as a numbered list of agreed claims, each phrased so neither of them would flinch, then a table of the residual disagreements, with each side's own position phrased in wording that side would accept. Then identify the cruxes: the sub-questions such that if one of them went the other way, at least one side would have to move. For each crux, give the concrete case or piece of experience both would agree bears on it, and write down in advance what each side predicts about it. Be specific to how Mohists and Confucians actually argued where you can, but don't pad it with citations; I need the ideas, not footnotes. And if you think some part of their fight is verbal—say, one of them means a felt attitude and the other means a pattern of action—say which part and why.

At the end, be self-critical: tell me what would change your mind about where you've drawn the line between what they can and can't jointly sign—which further argument or bit of the actual texts, if I put it in front of you, would make you redraw something—and where your reconstruction is shakiest.
