I'm a software engineer who builds data models and backend services for a legal-operations platform; I have no formal philosophy background, and everything I know about this problem has come from trying to encode legal rules without breaking them. Our system converts contracts, court rules, and compliance obligations into structured objects — parties, obligations, statuses, deadlines, events — that drive customer workflows. The rules we encode are full of statements that one thing is "deemed" or "treated as" another thing that it plainly is not: a corporation "acts" and "knows" as a party, a notice is "deemed received" three days after mailing, a buyer is "deemed to have accepted" goods they never inspected, and the reasonable person keeps appearing in standards even though no such person exists.

Our team calls these legal fictions, and we currently model them inconsistently. Sometimes a fiction becomes a first-class entity in the schema (the corporation as a subtype of party); sometimes it becomes a boolean or an enum field; sometimes it exists only in a code comment and is enforced nowhere. These choices compound. An entity accumulates attributes, hooks into integrations, and shows up in reporting; a field changes the meaning of a query; a comment enforces nothing. When we get it wrong, we rework the schema or, worse, ship automated logic whose behavior contradicts the legal effect we were supposed to implement.

I want a reusable procedure my team can run to decide how to model any legal fiction we encounter, grounded in whatever the philosophy of law actually knows about what legal fictions are and how they function — but translated into an operational checklist, not an essay. I need to know which distinctions from that literature must survive in the checklist and which can safely be dropped for implementation purposes.

Constraints and setting: the engineering team has no legal training. Product counsel reviews specifications for compliance, but her time is scarce, so the procedure should escalate only the genuinely hard cases. We hit a construct of this kind every couple of weeks; each one gets roughly thirty minutes of first-pass modeling before design review, and the result feeds schemas that customers depend on.

Please write the procedure in words (I can turn it into a flowchart or spec template myself). For each step, give me the question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, and whether the step yields a definite modeling choice, stops us from taking some plausible approach, or hands the decision to counsel. Mark the points where judgment cannot be eliminated. Then run the procedure on three cases we actually face: the reasonable person inside a negligence standard we encode as a scoring rule; the corporation as a contracting party in a master services agreement; and a clause reading "the buyer is deemed to have inspected the goods on delivery." Make at least one of these be a case where the procedure tells us to do something we would rather not do — for instance, where our instinct to reduce everything to a flag, or to reuse the existing party model, turns out to be the wrong move — and say plainly why. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: the kinds of fictions or modeling choices where no checklist removes the need for legal judgment, or for a substantive view about what law is.
