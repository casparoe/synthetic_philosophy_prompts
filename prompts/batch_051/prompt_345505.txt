I recently had a long argument with a friend who holds that excellence is valuable only as an instrument. On her view, a mathematician's proof matters only insofar as other mathematicians read it, a mountaineer's climb only insofar as it is recorded or inspires someone, and a musician's mastery only insofar as an audience hears it. I found myself resisting her conclusion without being able to say what I actually believe. I wrote out my case judgments afterward, and I want to know whether one principle lies behind them.

Here are my verdicts.

A mathematician spends a decade proving a difficult conjecture; on the day she finishes, her only copy is destroyed and no one else has seen the result. I still think the decade was worth something, and not merely because she enjoyed it.

A mountaineer makes a first ascent of a dangerous unclimbed face alone, leaves no record, and tells no one. I think the accomplishment is a real good. But when I compare that climb to an otherwise identical climb that is photographed and admired, I have an unshakeable feeling that the photographed one is worth more. That pair bothers me.

A violinist becomes a world-class player through years of exacting practice but never performs for anyone, and the skill dies with her. I think her life contained something of value, though I waver about whether it was as valuable as it would have been had she performed.

A novice who has never played golf takes a blind swing and scores a hole-in-one. I do not regard this as an achievement; it is a lucky event, even though a hole-in-one is normally an excellent outcome.

A child prodigy performs at a world-class level effortlessly, without struggle or honing. I regard the performance as excellent, but I hesitate to credit it as an achievement in the same sense as the mathematician's proof or the mountaineer's climb.

A forger becomes virtuosically good at producing undetectable imitations, and a con artist develops an extraordinarily fine sense of how to manipulate people. There is something impressive in the skill itself, but I am not willing to say the world is better because these people are excellent at what they do.

A plagiarist publishes a novel of genuine literary merit, copied from a destroyed manuscript no one has ever seen. The book moves many readers. I do not credit the plagiarist with an achievement, even though the product is excellent and real people benefit from it.

Someone becomes the world's greatest at stacking small pebbles into tall towers, a feat of tremendous difficulty and control. I cannot say this is worthless—mastery is being exercised—but I also cannot believe it is as valuable as the mathematician's proof. The object of the mastery seems to matter.

The last surviving person on Earth continues composing music in an abandoned house, knowing no one will ever hear it. I am inclined to say it remains good that she does this, but I cannot say why.

The nearest I can come to a principle is this: an achievement is valuable insofar as it is a difficult, successful exercise of a worthwhile skill, and its value as an achievement does not wait on any audience. Yet that principle is embarrassed by the effortless prodigy, the forger, and the pebble-stacker. Is there a repaired version of this principle, or a different one, that captures these judgments—or nearly all of them? If a clean principle forces me to revise some verdicts, which should I give up, and why? What would the principle say about a case I have not listed: a mediocre but devoted amateur cellist whose performance moves one other person? And where does the value of achievement ultimately reside—in the excellent activity, in the overcoming of difficulty, in the product, in the benefit to others, or in some combination?
