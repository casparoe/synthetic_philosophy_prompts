I am preparing an upper-level undergraduate lecture on the end of the Western Roman Empire, and I want to give students a defensible allocation of explanatory responsibility across causes rather than a single-villain story. Here are nine candidate explanations. I would like you to sort them into a four-tier ranking — first-order causes, important contributing causes, minor contributing causes, and largely mistaken or negligible — according to how much explanatory weight each deserves in a serious account of the Western Empire's dissolution. Begin by stating in one sentence what criterion of explanatory weight you are using (for example, necessity, sufficiency, magnitude of effect, or how well the explanation accounts for the timing). Then place each item in a tier and give a one-sentence justification for its placement. After the ranking, take the item you are least confident about, and say what single piece of evidence or argument would most justify moving it up one tier and what would most justify moving it down one tier. End by addressing item (i): is the transformation-rather-than-fall thesis best treated as one causal explanation ranked alongside the others, or as a challenge to the framing of the question itself? Defend that answer in two or three sentences.

The candidate explanations:

(a) External military pressure: warfare on the Rhine–Danube frontier, the arrival of the Huns, and the consequent migrations of Gothic, Vandal, and other groups into imperial territory.

(b) Civil war and usurpation: recurring internal wars over the imperial office, with rival armies stripping frontier defenses and extracting concessions that weakened central authority.

(c) Fiscal and administrative overstretch: a shrinking tax base, debasement of the coinage, the cost of the army and bureaucracy, and an administrative apparatus that could no longer pay for its own defense.

(d) Moral and civic decline: Gibbon's thesis that luxury, Christianity, and loss of civic virtue sapped the willingness of Romans to defend their state.

(e) Contingent decisions: particular choices by fifth-century emperors, generals, and barbarian leaders that might plausibly have gone otherwise and, had they done so, would have produced a substantially different outcome.

(f) Climate and disease: Late Antique climate variability and epidemic mortality that reduced agricultural output and population, undercutting recruitment and tax revenue.

(g) Barbarian settlement as accommodation: the settling of armed barbarian groups as federates on Roman territory, which gradually transferred land, tax revenue, and coercive force out of imperial hands.

(h) Lead poisoning: chronic lead exposure through water pipes, cooking vessels, and sweetened wine, impairing the health and possibly the fertility of the elite.

(i) Transformation rather than fall: there was no single collapse; the fifth century is better understood as a renegotiation of Roman rule in which provincial elites and barbarian rulers preserved much of Rome's institutional inheritance under new management.
