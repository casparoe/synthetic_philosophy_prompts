I can't stop thinking about the line between strict liability and negligence, and I've half-convinced myself there isn't one — or not the one people usually mean. Here's the argument I keep going in circles on.

(1) A rule punishes without fault exactly when it can convict someone who did everything it was fair to ask of them.
(2) An objective negligence standard can convict someone who did everything they personally could have done: it asks what a reasonable person would have done, and some defendants can't meet that benchmark — bad training, panic, slow reactions, whatever — through no choice of their own.
(3) So negligence already convicts people for failures that were unavoidable for them; the fault it finds is an objective shortfall, not something they chose.
(4) Strict liability does the same move, except the objective standard is set at a level nobody can count on meeting: if the prohibited harm comes about, you've fallen short, regardless of what you did to stop it.
(5) So the difference between them is only where the unavoidable-failure line gets drawn — a difference in demandingness, not a difference between fault-based and no-fault liability.
(6) So the usual objection that strict liability is uniquely wrong because it dispenses with mens rea and punishes the faultless rests on a line that can't be drawn in principle.

Is this valid? If not, which step fails, and is there a patched version of that step that saves the argument or kills it? I keep suspecting step (3) just sneaks in a redifinition of fault, but I can't make it precise.
