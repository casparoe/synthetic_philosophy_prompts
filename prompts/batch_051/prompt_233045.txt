In a discussion I keep returning to, someone dismissed the thought that an indefinitely extended human life could remain desirable by saying, "Familiarity breeds contempt." His argument, as best I can reconstruct it, ran as follows: a finite mind can realize only finitely many distinct kinds of experience; a sufficiently long life would eventually pass through all of them; every later experience would be a matter of familiarity; and since familiarity breeds contempt, the rest of that life would be lived in boredom or loathing. Therefore, an indefinitely long life would not be worth living. I have the sense that the single proverb is being asked to carry several different claims at once, and I want them separated before I decide how much of the argument survives.

Please take the sentence "Familiarity breeds contempt" and lay out the distinct precise claims it could be expressing. In particular, disambiguate the following: (1) what "contempt" designates — active dislike, boredom, hedonic flattening, loss of felt meaningfulness, or something else; (2) what kind of repetition is at issue — repetition of the very same token occurrence, repetition of a type across varying contexts, or general habituation to a domain; (3) the force of the claim — a rough and merely statistical generalization about human beings, a robust contingent law of human psychology, or a necessary truth about any finite representational system; (4) whether the proverb is purely descriptive or covertly evaluative, that is, whether it also asserts that whatever has become familiar is thereby less worth having; and (5) the connection to finitude — under what interpretation of "finite mind" would a finite mind exhaust the space of available experience-types, and is that the interpretation under which the proverb, whichever of the above claims it is being used to express, would be true. For each sharpened reading you identify, say whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false. Then say which reading a person who uses the proverb to argue that a very long life must turn into contempt for everything is most plausibly relying on, whether ordinary speakers usually mean that reading when they utter the sentence, whether that reading actually supports the conclusion, and what the strongest defensible reading supports instead.
