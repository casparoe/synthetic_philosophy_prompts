I've been volunteering at a wildlife rescue for a few months now (mostly orphaned songbirds, the occasional rabbit), and the work has left me with a theory I want someone to stress-test honestly, not politely. I've been calling it the legibility gap. The idea is that there are two kinds of wild-animal suffering. The first is calamity-suffering: one animal in visible distress, presented as an event — a hawk tearing into a fledgling, a fox with mange, a deer with a shattered leg. It has a victim and a scene, and it tugs at you. The second I call census-suffering: suffering that only shows up in population arithmetic. A frog lays thousands of eggs in a drying pond and nearly all die as tadpoles; a cottontail produces litter after litter and most are eaten within weeks; parasites and winter kill move through a population as percentages. There's no scene to point at, because the relevant moment is smeared across thousands of anonymous lives at once. Here's the claim: our moral responses track the first kind almost exclusively and systematically underweight the second. The gazelle being chased on a nature documentary is one animal in one chase, and it feels more urgent than several thousand tadpoles suffocating in a shrinking puddle, even if the tadpoles add up to vastly more suffering on any additive count.

I dont think this is simply the identifiable-victim effect or scope insensitivity, because the specific point is that most wild-animal suffering is built into life-history strategies (r-selection, roughly), so it can never become legible at all; our sense that nature is basically balanced may be an artifact of this blindness, not the other way around. I want a candid assessment: is the distinction doing genuine moral work, or is it an epistemic glitch dressed up as a principle? Does this already exist under another name? And where does the theory break — for instance, does it lean to hard on the word "suffering" for animals like tadpoles or baby mice, whose nervous systems might not support the amount I'm attributing to them?
