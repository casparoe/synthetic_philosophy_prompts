Here is a pattern I keep noticing in discussions of biblical inerrancy, and I would like it reconstructed philosophically rather than defended or attacked.

The practice is this. Faced with two biblical passages that apparently conflict, a common inerrantist move is to hold that both accounts are true because the events were more numerous than either account records. The death of Judas in Matthew and in Acts is reconciled by saying that Judas hanged himself and that the body afterwards fell and split open; the differing census figures in Samuel and Chronicles are reconciled by positing more than one enumeration or different counting criteria; the differing accounts of the empty tomb are reconciled by positing several visits by several groups of women; the cleansing of the temple, placed earlier in John and later in the other Gospels, is reconciled by positing two cleansings. In each case the harmonisation adds an extra event, person, or statement for which neither text supplies evidence and which a plain reading of either passage alone would not suggest. The aim seems to be to keep every sentence true, whatever the cost in added hypotheses.

Yet the same interpreters, when they read Livy, Josephus, the Qumran documents, or any other ancient text, treat a discrepancy between two accounts as evidence that at least one is wrong, that the text has been edited, or that the sources conflict. They would not rescue a non-biblical text by positing unrecorded extra events.

What philosophical commitments does this practice presuppose? I want to know what view of textual meaning, truth, or epistemic responsibility is doing the work; whether the practice actually requires the doctrine of inerrancy or is compatible with several rival theories; whether its two halves presuppose inconsistent epistemologies; whether the harmonising method has any principled stopping rule or burden of proof; and what would have to change in the practice if its presupposition were replaced by a straightforwardly evidentialist or probabilistic treatment of ancient sources. Please distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and mark the places where it is genuinely underdetermined.

Start with a one-sentence statement of the core commitment you think the practice rests on. Keep the entire response under 900 words.
