In decision theory, the ergodicity critique charges that expected-wealth maximisation conflates two different averages. The ensemble average is computed across many parallel copies of a gamble; the time average is computed along the single path that one gambler actually lives through. These averages can come apart sharply in a repeated multiplicative gamble. In the standard illustration, a coin is tossed repeatedly and wealth is multiplied by 1.5 on heads and 0.6 on tails. The expected multiplier per toss is 1.05, so expected wealth grows without bound; but the per-toss time-average multiplier is the geometric mean of 1.5 and 0.6, namely the square root of 0.9, or roughly 0.949, so the wealth of any single gambler who follows this policy tends to zero with probability one. The conclusion drawn is that choices under uncertainty should be evaluated by time-average growth rates rather than by ensemble expectations.

Now suppose the gamble is genuinely one-shot: there is exactly one toss, no repetition, no relevantly similar future gambles, and no opportunity to diversify across independent copies — for instance, a single decision whether to exchange a guaranteed sum for an asset whose value will be multiplied by 1.5 or 0.6 depending on the toss. Does the ergodicity critique survive this change of assumption? Identify the premise that the one-shot setting puts under pressure, and determine which description is correct: the critique collapses, it survives unchanged, or it survives only in a repaired form — perhaps as a claim about policies rather than individual acts, or about the shape of the utility function rather than about which average to compute.
