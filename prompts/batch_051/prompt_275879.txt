Let's say Adam Smith came back today and learned about the endowment effect—people demand way more to sell a mug they were just given than they'd pay to buy it, losses sting more than equivalent gains feel good, all that. In The Theory of Moral Sentiments he already seems to think something like the pain of losing is sharper than the pleasure of gaining. Would he have treated loss aversion as a cognitive bug to correct, or as a legitimate feature of how humans rightly value what they have? I don't want his exact wording; I want you to reconstruct how he'd line up with the modern debate over whether the endowment effect is bias or authentic preference. Give me a real Smithian position, not a caricature. Say something that could actually make a modern behavioral economist uncomfortable.
