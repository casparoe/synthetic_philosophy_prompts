so i just got back from an hour-long argument with a friend about whether tourists are on the hook for local conventions, and somewhere in the middle she kept reaching for the same image: a social convention is like the house rule of a board game you've sat down to join. if you're playing spades at someone's table and their house rule is you can't lead spades until they're broken, you follow it even if you think it's a stupid rule, because that's what it is to play this game at this table. you don't get to say "at my table we play it differently," and you don't get a vote. so a visitor who tips 18 percent at an american diner, or queues properly in london, or doesn't jaywalk in berlin is just playing by the local house rules, and refusing is a kind of bad sportsmanship at best. i pushed back that games are joined voluntarily in a way a city isn't, that house rules are usually stated up front and can be refused before you sit down, and that some conventions (elders speak first, women serve the guests) aren't harmless table rules at all. we went in circles.

can you audit that analogy properly for me? lay out the correspondence table — the local custom, the visitor, the "table," the other players, the house rule, the moment of sitting down, the penalty for breaking a rule — and then show where the mapping does real work and where it quietly imports things conventions don't have, like explicit consent to the ruleset or a clean way to leave the game. which disanalogies actually matter for her conclusion, and which are just superficial? and after all that, what's left of the idea that visitors must follow local ones? it feels like the board-game analogy is carrying the whole moral load, but i cant tell whether my objections are real or i'm just being contrarian. if there's a better analogy out there i'd want to hear that too.
