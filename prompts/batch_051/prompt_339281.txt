I'm putting together a small magazine of short pieces by experimental philosophers for a semi-popular audience, and I'd like you to write one contribution: a deadpan corporate memorandum of about 700 words, in the voice of a fictional chief risk officer at a mid-sized pharmaceutical company, addressed to senior managers. The situation is that the company's most profitable drug has been shown to cause a rare but serious side effect, and leadership is worried about how regulators, doctors, and juries will describe what the company did. The memo's authors have read just enough experimental philosophy to know the Knobe effect — the finding that a foreseen harmful side effect of a decision is robustly judged intentional, while an equally foreseen beneficial side effect is robustly judged not intentional — and they intend to shape the company's public and internal language around that asymmetry.

The piece should be a satire of how organizations launder moral judgment through neutral-sounding psychological language, so it must never break character, never explain the joke, and never mention the Knobe effect within the memo itself. The internal logic has to be consistent: these are intelligent, self-interested managers, not cartoons, and the tactics they propose should be recognizably close to real corporate risk communication pushed one step further. A specialist reader should recognize that the author understands the asymmetry and its philosophical stakes — above all, that attributions of intentional action appear to track moral evaluation rather than purely factual foresight, and that a company exploiting this is engaged in manipulation rather than mere disclosure — but those points should come through only in the deadpan proposals, template language, and rationales of the memo.
