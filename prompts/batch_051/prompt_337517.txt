I keep noticing a move in political argument that I don't have a word for, and I don't want to settle for the broad label populism. The move concerns the phrase "the will of the people" and its relatives — "the people have spoken," "the country has decided," "the real people," "the silent majority." A speaker invokes the people as a single subject whose decision settles a question, and the authority of the claim seems to depend on that unity. But when evidence arrives that the actual population is divided or opposed — a close vote, later polls, protests, an adverse court ruling — the speaker does not withdraw the claim. Instead, the referent of "the people" shifts.

Let me give two or three instances of the kind of thing I mean. First: a referendum passes 52–48, and the winning side announces that the people have decided, then proceeds as if the result were a near-unanimous national verdict. Six months later, when polls run against the measure, the same side says the poll respondents aren't the real people, or that the real people don't answer pollsters. Second: a candidate who loses the popular vote but takes office claims to represent the real people, or a silent majority that isn't captured by the official count, and cites the size of a rally as better evidence of the popular will than the election returns. Third, a slightly different case: after losing a vote, the loser says the people were misled or intimidated, so the true popular will is what they would have chosen had they understood the facts.

What strikes me is that the phrase keeps its force while its content changes. It starts as a claim about an actual distribution of votes, then becomes a claim about an underlying or authentic public that no particular measurement could refute, then perhaps a claim about a future or properly informed public, and sometimes just a claim about the speaker's own supporters. This may not be ordinary equivocation, because the speaker is not confused between two senses; the ambiguity is doing the work. But it also doesn't seem quite like moving the goalposts, because what shifts is not the standard of success but the subject whose will is being cited.

Is there an established name for this specific maneuver? I am not asking for a bare label. I want the carving: what exactly the maneuver is, how it differs from the nearest concepts — equivocation, motte-and-bailey, synecdoche, the reification of a collective, the legal fiction of a popular sovereign, and populism as a broader family — and which of those, if any, fits it best. If it fits none exactly, I would like you to propose a name, define it precisely, and mark its boundary cases. In particular: how should one distinguish this maneuver from legitimate but loose uses of "the people" in democratic politics — say, a candidate who says the people have spoken after a genuine landslide, or a constitutional court that speaks for the people as an ongoing political community rather than for any present majority? Where exactly does the illegitimate shifting begin, and can it be recognized before the speaker has to explain away an inconvenient result?
