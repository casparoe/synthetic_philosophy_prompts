Working title: The Units of Selection Debate Is Largely a Dispute About Which Question Gets the Name

When scientists disagree about the unit of selection, they appear to be disagreeing about a single factual question: does natural selection act on genes, on organisms, or on groups? My suspicion is that at least three different questions are packed into that phrase, and that much of the conflict arises because different traditions are answering different questions under one heading.

The first is the replicator question: what is copied across generations with enough fidelity for cumulative selection to build on it? Here the gene, understood in the gene's-eye tradition as any hereditary element that is copied with high fidelity and whose differential copying can be accumulated over many generations, has the strongest claim. Whole organisms are not copied across generations; groups are copied with even less fidelity.

The second is the interactor question: what entity presents an integrated phenotype to the environment, so that its success or failure determines the fate of the replicators inside or below it? For many traits, such as camouflage, foraging efficiency, or thermoregulation, the organism is plainly the interactor. For other traits, such as the shared anti-predator behaviour of a colony or the density-dependent virulence of a parasite population within a single host, a group is at least a plausible interactor. In this sense, the term unit picks out something ecological and causal, not something copied.

The third is the benefit question: at what level does a trait look like an adaptation, or for whose good does it exist? A trait can be individually costly but group-advantageous, or group-advantageous at one scale and gene-advantageous at another; which description is apt depends on the covariance between trait and fitness at each level. To say that a trait is group-beneficial is therefore a claim about fitness covariance, not yet a claim about mechanism.

Once these questions are separated, a good deal of the familiar shouting match dissolves. A gene-centred view is first and foremost a thesis about the first question; many multilevel-selectionists are making claims about the second and third. The rival camps are often filling in different entries of the same table rather than contradicting one another. The Price equation and contextual analysis already presuppose this: group selection is detected when there is a between-group component of the covariance between trait and fitness that remains after within-group effects are taken into account. That is a statistical claim, and it is compatible with the fact that all the mechanical pushing and shoving happens at the level of individual survival and reproduction.

But I do not think the entire debate is verbal. The substantive residue is whether group-level properties can ever be causes, or whether they are merely redescriptions of lower-level events. A critic may say that because every birth and every death is an individual event, the group level cannot be genuinely causal. I would reply that this confuses causation with mechanism. Temperature and humidity cause condensation even though molecules do all the colliding; the macro-level variables are genuine difference-makers because interventions on them, holding lower-level composition fixed, are systematically associated with different outcomes. Population structure, including migration rates, group size, and the rules of group formation, often passes exactly this interventionist test: change the structure while holding the organisms' individual properties fixed, and the population's trajectory changes. So group-level structure can be a cause even though the mechanism runs through individuals.

My conclusion, then, is that the debate should be reframed rather than won. The productive empirical question is not which unit is the unit, but how much of the variance in fitness in a particular population is explained at which level, and whether the between-group component is large enough to shape the trait of interest. On that question, the two traditions may share more ground than their rhetoric suggests.
