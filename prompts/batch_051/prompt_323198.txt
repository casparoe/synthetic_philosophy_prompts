I am pulled in two directions by the modal ontological argument in the style of Gödel and Plantinga, and I want a negotiated verdict rather than a ruling on soundness. On the theory's side: define a maximally great being as one that, if it exists in any possible world, exists in every possible world. The argument asks only that I grant the possibility premise — that some possible world contains such a being — and S5 does the rest: all worlds are mutually accessible, so the being exists here as well. The derivation is valid, the logic is one I already accept, and a possibility claim is normally the weakest claim one can make. On the intuition's side: once the definition is unpacked, the possibility premise is not weak at all. To say that a world contains a being that exists in every world already asserts that the being exists in every world, including this one; the premise is the conclusion with a diamond in front of it. And the bare absence of a visible contradiction in a concept does not look like evidence capable of settling what actually exists. I want to know what would have to be true for the intuition to be a debunkable confusion about modal operators rather than a real constraint on a priori proof; what would have to be true for the argument to be amended rather than abandoned, whether by weakening S5, by finding independent grounds for the possibility premise, or by downgrading the conclusion to a conditional about the concept; and whether there is a reading on which the proof is correct but does not settle actual existence. I am not asking which side wins; I am asking what it would cost to make either side win, and how to tell in a principled way.
