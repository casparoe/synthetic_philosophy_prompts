I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar called Minds, Machines, and Patterns (sixteen students, one 80-minute meeting). We have just read Dennett's account of the physical, design, and intentional stances and his claim that some patterns are real. My students can recite the taxonomy in their sleep, but they do not feel the force of the deeper view: that taking the intentional stance toward something is a practical wager rather than a discovery about what is inside it; that the same system can support different stances, but at different costs; and that a pattern earns its reality by keeping its predictive bets paid.

Here is a mechanic I would like you to develop into a runnable 60-minute game with a 20-minute debrief. Divide the class into three departments — Physical, Design, and Intentional — each with a budget of pattern credits. In each round the departments are shown the recent input–output history of a black-box target (a thermostat with hysteresis, a chess engine mid-game, a vending machine with one broken button, a border collie, six simple trading agents, an autocorrect system, a friend replying to texts). Each department writes a predictive rule in a constrained vocabulary: Physical may use only measurements and laws; Design only functions and failures; Intentional only beliefs, desires, and assumptions of rationality. Predictions earn points for accuracy, but rules are priced by the symbol, so a compact rule that occasionally errs can beat an exact but bloated one. Halfway through, allow departments to switch stances at a steep cost, so switching itself becomes a strategic resource. After each round run a pattern audit: the winning rule is projected next to the history it compresses, and the class sees which regularities paid. End with a run in which the targets are, in succession, a person, a thermostat, and a thermostat running a mildly deceptive schedule, so the class must confront that the intentional stance can win on all three and that winning is the only vindication the stance gets.

Please turn this into a complete session: exact rules, a no-money payoff scheme, the list of targets with their input–output cards, a minute-by-minute timeline, a debrief that maps what happened onto the three stances and onto the idea that real patterns are the compressed regularities that survive being staked on, a list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each, and a frank note on which part of Dennett's view the game cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in discussion.
