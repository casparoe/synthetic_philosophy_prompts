I'm on my university's graduate standards committee, and we've been going in circles on a case that's small in its pieces and big in what people think it means. It concerns a third-year PhD student in our department -- call her M. -- who is, on paper, a very strong researcher: a first-authored paper at a top venue, two more under review, and her supervisor is emphatic that the quality of the research is not in question. The file, instead, is a collection of small things that have piled up over about eighteen months. She arrives 15 or 20 minutes late to the weekly lab meeting most weeks, often still eating breakfast through the first agenda item. She missed two scheduled one-on-ones with her supervisor without emailing beforehand. On three occasions she promised a master's student or a more junior PhD student that she would read a draft and return comments by a specific date; each time the comments came a week or more late, once only after a reminder, and without an apology. And for the department open day she signed up as the designated safety officer for one lab tour slot and then simply didn't turn up, later saying she had overslept.

The committee is split down the middle. Her supervisor, who is a co-author, says these are time-management slip-ups, not integrity problems, and that a formal sanction would be disproportionite for someone who has never missed an external deadline and never misrepresented anything. Another member keeps arguing that a person who quietly breaks small promises to people with less power than her is exactly the person you cannot trust with a shared codebase, a co-authored paper, or a junior student, and that treating the pattern as mere disorganization sends the wrong message about what we think reliability is. We have three options on the table -- a formal written warning that stays in her file until graduation, a lighter mentoring-plus-probation arrangement, or no formal action -- and I have to circulate a draft decision memo before Thursday's meeting. Draft the memo: it needs a concrete bottom line, reasons the committee can actually adopt, and a fair treatment of the strongest consideration on the side we end up not taking, put in terms that side could recognize as its own. The student also wrote in her response that she distinguishes 'hard' external deadlines from the 'soft' expectations of lab life and considers only the former to be promises; I want the memo to actually say something about that distinction rather than wave it away.
