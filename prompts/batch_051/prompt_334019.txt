Consider a familiar argumentative move in the debate about higher-order evidence. A reasoner forms a first-order judgment — for instance, that a certain job candidate is the strongest, or that a certain medical diagnosis is correct — on the basis of what feels like careful deliberation. They then receive higher-order evidence: they are informed that they have been given a mild reasoning-disrupting drug, that they are severely sleep-deprived, or that reasoners with their cognitive profile are known to misjudge cases of exactly this kind. Many philosophers think the reasoner ought to lower their confidence in the first-order judgment; otherwise they are epistemically akratic, believing something while also believing that their evidence does not support it, or that the belief-forming process behind it is unreliable. One standard way of pressing this conclusion is an analogy with instruments. If you learned that the thermometer, fuel gauge, or diagnostic test you were about to trust was miscalibrated or otherwise unreliable, it would be irrational to keep trusting its reading. The relation between your higher-order evidence and your first-order judgment, the argument suggests, is relevantly like that. Refusing to revise, then, is supposed to be like staring at a gauge you know to be broken and still believing what it says.

Audit this analogy as thoroughly as you can. Give an explicit mapping between the source (the unreliable instrument and its user) and the target (the reasoner, their first-order judgment, and their higher-order evidence), and mark where the mapping breaks. Distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the anti-akrasia conclusion from the ones that are merely superficial. In particular, examine what the analogy quietly imports — a user who is distinct from the instrument, an independent calibration check, a reading that is separate from reasoning — that may be absent in the epistemic case. Then say whether the conclusion the analogy is meant to support (that the epistemically akratic agent, or at least this one, is irrational) survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and under what conditions it would fail. Finally, either offer what you take to be a better analogy or explain why the instrument analogy, suitably qualified, is still the best one available.
