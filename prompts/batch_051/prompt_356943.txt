You are a professional political philosopher who works on public reason and the role of religious argument in democratic politics. Take this question seriously, reason carefully, and before giving your settled view, brainstorm the distinct readings the quoted clause can bear and say where they pull apart.

I am a retired research chemist who now sits on the council of a town of about forty thousand people. Since retiring I have been working my way through political philosophy, mostly the debate over public reason, and I recently realized that a rule our council inherited is a live instance of that debate.

The town's standing orders contain a clause adopted in 1998, after an acrimonious fight about a religious display on public land, when the council wanted to keep sectarian preaching out of its debates. The clause reads:

"14(c). No member shall ground a motion or amendment in a religious belief. Where the consideration supporting a motion or amendment is religious in character, the member moving or seconding it shall state, or on request provide, a secular reason sufficient for the motion or amendment."

Last month two cases arose that the 1998 council plainly did not anticipate.

In the first, I moved to defer a capital program and rested the motion on an argument from a thoroughly secular but technically demanding version of impartial consequentialism. The argument turned on claims about obligations to possible future people across astronomical timescales. Nothing about it is religious in the ordinary sense, but I doubt that more than one or two of my colleagues could say what would count as a reason for or against the central claim.

In the second, a colleague opposed a flood-control scheme by saying that we are stewards of God's creation, and added that no sound secular reason captures why the scheme is wrong. It is generally agreed that she could give a standard environmental-harm justification that most members would accept as reasonable, but she refuses to, because in her view that justification understates the matter.

The chair has asked whether either of us is in breach of clause 14(c). I care less about the immediate procedural outcome than about what the clause is doing. Please work through, in turn: (i) the plain textual meaning of the clause; (ii) the purpose the 1998 drafters had; (iii) what those drafters would have said had these two cases been put to them; and (iv) what a reasonable councillor reading the clause today would take it to require. Then say which approach should govern here and why. Throughout, address whether the clause tracks a distinction between public and non-public reasons or only one between religious and non-religious reasons, and what follows when those two distinctions come apart in the ways these cases illustrate.
