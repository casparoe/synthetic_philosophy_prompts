My friend runs a seminar on obedience and authority, and she has a developed view about when deferring to a superior is a genuine virtue rather than a failure of nerve. She rejects the excuse that a subordinate was merely following orders, but she also thinks some organizations err in treating every subordinate with a second thought as though they were insubordinate. I have her verdicts on six earlier cases:

- A trainee air traffic controller follows a supervisor's instruction to clear an aircraft onto a taxiway he wrongly thinks is occupied; the supervisor can see a ground-radar return the trainee cannot, and the taxiway is in fact clear. Her verdict: the deference was a virtue.
- A junior engineer signs off on a lead engineer's design change without fully understanding the thermal analysis, trusting the lead's specialized expertise; the change is later confirmed as sound. Her verdict: the deference was a virtue.
- A hospital orderly follows a nurse's instruction to move a patient using a lifting technique the orderly is untrained to assess; the nurse is following a physiotherapist's plan. Her verdict: the deference was a virtue.
- A data scientist notices that a senior colleague's analysis double-counts some cases, objects once, then drops the objection when the colleague instructs her to use the result as is; the published figure is wrong. Her verdict: the deference was not a virtue.
- In a Milgram-style study, a participant keeps increasing the shocks after the learner has gone silent, because the experimenter insists the experiment must continue. Her verdict: the deference was not a virtue.
- A soldier follows an order to withdraw to a fallback line although he believes the forward position is still defensible; the officer who issued the order can see the full battle picture on the command net. Her verdict: the deference was a virtue.

The new case: a nurse with twelve years of experience defers to a newly qualified physician who orders a patient's discharge based on the chart; the nurse has just observed the patient's breathing to be slightly more labored than the chart indicates and thinks it may be early fluid overload, but it is not an emergency, and she says nothing and prepares the discharge. Was the nurse's deference a virtue?

I want a prediction, but more than the bare prediction I want the rule. Please answer in exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion:

1. Your predicted verdict for the new case.
2. The closest precedent among the six earlier cases, and why it is the closest.
3. One candidate rule that fits all six earlier verdicts, stated precisely, and what it predicts for the new case.
4. A second, distinct candidate rule that also fits all six, and what it predicts for the new case.
5. Your best guess as to which rule she is actually running, with the reason, and the further test case you would want to ask her about to settle it.
