I've been going back and forth with a friend about whether Darwin's account of the moral sense in The Descent of Man pushes more toward moral realism or toward the idea that morality is just a useful set of instincts, and it struck me that he never actually got to face the later arguments that use his own material against him. On the moral side, he has almost all the ingredients of modern evolutionary debunking: he explains how sympathy and conscience could establish themselves through natural selection, he floats the hive-bee counterfactual where creatures raised differently would find completely different acts morally sacred, and he even flirts with an early group-selectionist thought that tribes with more sympathetic members would outcompete less cohesive ones. On religion, he's similarly unfinished: late in life he describes himself as having to remain agnostic about ultimate origins, but he never saw the later idea that religious belief is itself a byproduct of mental machinery evolved for other purposes (detecting agents and intentions out in the world), or the further claim that this should lower our confidence in theism instead of just explaining it.

So what I want is for you to predict, not just what Darwin did say, but what he would have said if someone had handed him these debunking arguments. Based on what he actually wrote (above all the Descent of Man chapters where he works out the moral sense, but also the autobiographical remarks on his own agnosticism), give me exactly five predictions, numbered 1 through 5. Make three of them about his likely position on the evolutionary debunking of morality—would he treat it as undermining the authority of conscience, as vindicating it, or something subtler?—and make the other two about his likely position on the debunking of theistic belief. Each item should be one or two sentences, the list should have no introduction or conclusion, and in each item tell me whether you think the texts point fairly clearly that way or whether your really speculating.
