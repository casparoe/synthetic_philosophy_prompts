My partner and I have just been through an adoption assessment, and I would value a principled verdict on the panel's reasoning. We were not rejected because of anything involving abuse, neglect, poverty, or lack of affection. The panel's central objection was to our plan to raise the child entirely within our small, devout religious community. The child would be educated within the community, would have little unstructured contact with outsiders, and would be expected, though never forced, to take on an adult role within the community. The social worker argued that children should reach adulthood with as many meaningful options still available to them as possible, and that our plan would close off too many of those options before the child could decide for herself.

My partner and I are confused rather than bitter. On the one hand, this criterion sounds right; on the other, every upbringing closes off options, since nobody can give a child all possible futures, and every family shapes a child's habits, beliefs, and tastes long before she can consent. So we cannot tell whether the panel was applying a genuine moral principle or merely dressing up a prejudice against insular communities. Was the panel's criterion defensible? Did we wrong the child by proposing this upbringing, given that she would very likely have been happy and secure within the community? And is it legitimate for the state to screen prospective parents in this way at all, rather than intervening only after a child has actually been harmed?

Please answer in British English.
