I've been trying to explain to a friend why Spinoza's necessitarianism isn't just fatalism with nicer vocabulary, and why the payoff at the end of the Ethics is specifically an intellectual love of God (amor Dei intellectualis) rather than gratitude, comfort, or resignation. I want a poem that does that explanatory work for me.

Write a strict villanelle in the voice of someone who has come to accept Spinoza's claim that everything follows from God or Nature with the same necessity that the three angles of a triangle equal two right angles. The two refrains should carry the two halves of the doctrine: one refrain as the iron claim, that nothing could ever have been otherwise; the other as the surprising payoff, that understanding precisely this necessity is the mind's greatest joy, the intellectual love of God. The form needs to enact the idea, not just describe it: the refrains should return with the same inevitability with which Spinoza thinks every apparent alternative collapses back into the one actual world, and the final couplet, where both refrains meet, should make a reader feel why Spinoza treats the hardest doctrine and the highest blessedness as the same thought seen from two sides. Keep it from turning into a theistic consolation poem — the God in question is Nature, and the love is a cognitive joy in seeing how the whole hangs together, not a warm person who loves you back. Dont let the voice slip into free will by the back door either.

After the poem, add a short note of four or five sentences saying which interpretive choices you made and which standard readings of Spinoza you relied on, and briefly defend the claim that the intellectual love of God is compatible with there being no free will in the ordinary sense. I want you to commit to a definite reading rather than hedging between several.
