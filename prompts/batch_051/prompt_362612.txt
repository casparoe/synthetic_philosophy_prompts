My university's academic integrity code has a line that reads: 'A student found to have submitted work that is not substantially their own shall receive a grade of zero in the course and be suspended for one term.' It was written back when everyone knew what that meant: copying essays off the internet, buying a paper, pasting paragraphs from a book. A first-year at my college just got caught doing something weirder. He paid a friend, a third-year in a different program, to do all the reading for his final seminar paper and to talk him through the argument in detail over a couple of weeks. Then he wrote every sentence himself. The words are his writing, but the ideas, the structure, the actual engagement with the sources are mostly the friend's. The university is treating it as a violation and applying the zero-and-suspension.

I'm trying to get clear on two things, and I'd rather have the hard analysis than the 'is this technically allowed' answer.

First, does the quoted clause even reach this case, on its own words? I can see three readings tugging in diffrent directions: the literal sense of the words, the antifraud purpose, and what the people who drafted it would have said if you'd asked them at the time. Give me each one and say which you think is the best reconstruction of what the rule actually prohibits.

Second, set aside whether it's a violation and suppose it is one. Evaluate the sanction — a fixed zero grade and a one-term suspension — through retributivism and through consequentialism about punishment. What would each theory need to know about the case that I haven't told you, and which details would matter most to which? And does the fact that the penalty is a mandatory flat sanction, not something a panel can adjust, change how each theory ought to view it? I have a suspicion it cuts harder against one of them than the other, but I want you to show me whether that's right.
