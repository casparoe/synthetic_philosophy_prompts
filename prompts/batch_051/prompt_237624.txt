Take the trolley problem but replace the side-track death with an unconsented risk, and then tell me how the standard analyses adjust.

Set it up like this: five people are tied to the main track and will certainly die if you do nothing. If you pull the lever, the five are safe no matter what. Pulling sends the trolley onto a secondary track that forks: a mechanism with a known probability p routes it onto branch A, where one stranger is tied and would be killed, or onto branch B, an empty siding where nobody is hurt. The stranger never agreed to face this risk.

Work through p = 0.01, 0.5, and 0.99. For each, what does the intended-versus-foreseen distinction (and the doing/allowing distinction, where relevant) actually say about pulling? Is there a threshold p* at which the deontological verdict flips from permissible to impermissible, and what sets it: the size of the risk itself, the fact that it is imposed without consent, the number of lives saved, or something else? Where does that threshold sit relative to the act utilitarian's expected-value calculation, and does the disagreement between the two views grow or shrink as p changes? Then add one twist: the person on branch A has told you in advance that he refuses to be put at any risk. Does that move the threshold, and through what principle?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating the question. American English.
