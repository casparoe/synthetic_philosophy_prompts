I've been trying out a distinction I call "fiduciary partiality" versus "sentimental partiality." Fiduciary partiality is when you're sort of authorized to favor someone: a parent advocating for their own kid's school placement, a public defender keeping quiet about a client's guilt, a union rep defending a member who genuinely screwed up. The partiality isn't a feeling leaking into your judgment; it's a role you've taken on, with limits and expectations attached to it. Sentimental partiality is the raw stuff underneath: you just feel warmer toward your kid, your country, your in-group, and you let that tilt what you would otherwise do impartially, without any role or authorization behind it. My claim is that almost all defensible loyalty is fiduciary, and when people defend group loyalty in politics or daily life they're either (a) actually gesturing at a fiduciary role they haven't articulated, or (b) just dressing up favoritism. Sentimental partiality, as such, is never a virtue; it's a bias that happens to be prosocial in small doses.

I want an honest assessment, not vindication. Is the distinction doing real work or is it basically the old role-obligations/agent-relative-reasons contrast with new labels? Where does it break? The case that genuinely worries me is the parent who saves their child from a burning building before two strangers: that feels fully defensible even if the parent doesn't take themselves to have any role, authorization, or "office," just love. If you think my view is wrong or only half right, I also want to know what consideration would change your mind in the other direction—whichever side you land on—and roughly where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
