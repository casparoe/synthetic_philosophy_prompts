This is the position I'm defending in a workshop paper next month, and below is a precise description of the audience I'll defend it to. Please turn this into a Q&A prep sheet rather than a summary of my talk.

The thesis is that path dependence is real, but the strong "lock-in" version has been systematically oversold. I distinguish three claims that popular and scholarly discussions run together. (1) Rerun sensitivity: if history were rerun with small early differences, the eventual outcome would be different. (2) Persistence: once an early choice is made, increasing returns, learning effects, and coordination externalities make it self-reinforcing and costly to reverse. (3) Lock-in: the outcome that persists is inferior in a welfare-relevant sense, and a feasible superior alternative was available at the moment of choice — so the bad outcome is genuinely an accident rather than merely one of several acceptable endpoints. My claim is that the standard cases (QWERTY, VHS versus Betamax, railway gauge) support (1) and (2) far better than they support (3). The alleged inferiority usually ignores transition costs, judges the choice ex post rather than ex ante, or mistakes "different" for "worse." I also propose a diagnostic for genuine lock-in: a persuasive case must identify a feasible alternative trajectory that would have been superior even after counting transition costs, and must show that the selection mechanism was informationally arbitrary at the moment of choice.

The audience is a workshop of economic historians, most of them trained in cliometrics and comfortable with explicit counterfactuals, plus two or three historical-institutionalist political scientists who think the economists in the room underestimate increasing returns, positive feedback, and endogenous change in preferences and institutions. No one present is a philosopher; a purely conceptual distinction will be treated as idle unless it changes research design or historiography.

Please give me the objections in roughly the order I'll actually receive them, phrased the way this audience would phrase them. For each, give a short reply, an indication of what I should concede versus what I should hold, and a note on where the reply is weakest. Order them by how likely and how damaging they are. End with the question I'm hoping nobody asks, and explain why it is dangerous.
