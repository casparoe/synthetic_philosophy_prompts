I've been reading Locke's *Essay Concerning Human Understanding*, Book II, Chapter XXVII, and this bit stopped me: “From whence it follows, that one thing cannot have two beginnings of existence, nor two things one beginning ; it being impossible for two things of the same kind to be or exist in the same instant, in the very same place ; or one and the same thing in different places.” Then he says living creatures' identity doesn't depend on the same particles, but on something else. I'm a retired neurobiologist, not a philosopher, so I'll just report my gut verdicts on some cases and ask what principle, if any, captures them.

1. Replace one neuron at a time with a synthetic copy over the course of a year, without loss of consciousness: still me.
2. Scan-and-delete uploading: the scan destroys the original brain while building an emulation that remembers being me. I say the original person dies and a new person appears, even though the new one feels continuous.
3. Scan-without-delete: original walks away, copy lives in the machine. Two people, obviously. If the original then dies, I dont say he survives in the copy.
4. Same as 1, but halfway through the replacement we stop for a year, then finish. Still me, though I'm less certain.
5. A patient whose corpus callosum is split, where each hemisphere seems to run its own awareness. I honestly dont know what to say if one hemisphere is gradually replaced and the other is destroyed.

Is there a single principle that gets all of these, or close? And what does it say about the case I keep going back and forth on: a person whose brain is replaced molecule-by-molecule with inorganic parts during a fully awake, conversation-filled afternoon? Also, tell me which of my verdicts, if any, you think has to go, and what specific argument or case would change your mind about it.
