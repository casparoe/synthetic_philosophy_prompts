I cover science and ideas for a regional paper, and after last month's multi-county continuity-of-government drill I fell down a rabbit hole reading the actual succession language in our county charter. Section 11(c) says: "If the mayor and all four commissioners are killed, missing, or otherwise unable to exercise the powers of their office, the county administrator shall exercise all powers of the mayor until a special election is held." The drill quietly assumed the administrator would be kept at an undisclosed backup site whenever all five elected officials were in one room — a designated survivor. So picture the real thing: an attack during the annual address kills the mayor and the entire commission. Six hours later the administrator, who has never won an election in her life, emerges from the bunker and, citing 11(c), starts signing budgets and issuing emergency orders.

Here's what I can't shake. The people who drafted that clause were probably imagining a plane crash or a gas explosion, not a plan that deliberately holds one unelected person back so that someone inherits the county. So my question is what the clause actually require or permits in that case read each way — plain words, the purpose of continuity, what the drafters would have said if asked about designated survivors, and what a reasonable county resident would think they were owed. And then the harder question: does the fact that her survival was engineered by the very people who died change the ethics of her governing, or is the honest move to act as a caretaker whose only job is to call the special election? I don't want a tour of the interpretive options — tell me what the clause requires and what she should do, and commit to it.
