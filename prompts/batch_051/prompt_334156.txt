Last weekend I was hiking with two friends, Lena and Mark, and at some point Lena started telling us that her five-year-old had asked her whether boy ducks lay eggs too. She'd said no, but then realized the nature book she'd been reading him literally says "ducks lay eggs," so now the kid was confused about which one to believe. Mark, who writes database code for a living, said the book was just wrong, strictly speaking, and that's where it started. It sounds like a ridiculous thing to argue about for an hour, but it got weirdly deep, and I've never been able to settle it. Here's the back-and-forth as best I remember it:

1. Mark said "ducks lay eggs" is a claim about all ducks. Drakes are ducks. Drakes don't lay eggs. So the sentence is false. If they'd wanted accuracy they'd have written "female ducks lay eggs." It's not a crime, but it is false.
2. Lena said that can't be right, because by that standard half of ordinary talk is false — "birds fly," "dogs bark," "trees have leaves" — and nobody thinks those are false. The book isn't lying to children; it's saying what ducks are like.
3. Mark said "birds fly" is also false if you're being strict, because of ostriches. She's just listing other loose sentences. Useful, or how we talk, isn't the same as true. People said "the sun rises" for centuries and meant something true by it, but the sentence still isn't literally true.
4. Lena said the sun sentence is about astronomy, whereas this is about how language actually works. "Ducks lay eggs" isn't meant as a claim about each duck one by one, any more than "the room was full of people talking" means literally everyone was talking. It's a claim about the kind.
5. Mark said "about the kind" needs explaining. Only the females lay eggs, and they do it mainly in spring, so at any given time most ducks aren't laying eggs. So "typical" can't mean "most members." If it just means "important or expected," then the sentence is true whenever someone feels it's important — that's not truth, that's just vibes.
6. Lena said it's not vibes; reproduction is part of what ducks are, the way having eight legs is part of what spiders are even though some spiders lose legs. A dog that can't bark is still a dog, but "dogs bark" is true because barking is the kind of thing dogs do as a kind. Headcounts aren't the point.
7. Mark said a dog that can't bark is a broken or unusual dog, but a male duck isn't broken, he's a perfectly normal duck. And look at "mosquitoes carry malaria": only a few species, only the females, and most mosquitoes never carry it. If that sentence is "true," then a true sentence can still send every mosquito in the yard to its death out of panic. So maybe usefulness is doing all the work in her examples, not truth.
8. Lena said the malaria sentence can be misleading in a way that matters, but that doesn't make it false — it makes it true but dangerous. And if Mark is right, then "ducks lay eggs" is false, so what's a children's book supposed to print that sounds like English — "some female ducks of breeding age lay eggs under certain conditions"? He isn't defending accuracy, he's defending a rule for scoring sentences that would make ordinary speech unusable.
9. Mark said maybe ordinary speech just is loose, and the kid's confusion is the price of that looseness. The five-year-old heard "ducks lay eggs" and asked whether the green-headed ones do too — which proves the sentence communicated exactly the false universal. So either the sentence is false, or it's true but systematically communicates a falsehood; either way the book caused the problem.

They both turned to me to referee, and I genuinely couldn't. They're not mad about it, but it's become a running joke that they need a judge. Can you adjudicate this move by move — who's right about which specific point, where each of them overreaches, and what, if anything, are they both missing?
