I am trying to work out what I actually believe about unrealistic assumptions in scientific models, and I would like you to help me extract the principle behind my gut verdicts. Here are the cases as I currently judge them.

In mechanics, treating a pendulum bob as a point mass on a massless string bothers me not at all, even though I know both assumptions are false. The ideal gas law treats molecules as zero-volume particles with no intermolecular forces, and I am fine with that too, at least in the low-pressure regimes where it is used. In population genetics, the Hardy–Weinberg model assumes an infinitely large, randomly mating population, and I do not just tolerate this; I think the false assumption is doing useful work as a null baseline.

I am also comfortable with the classic story of an expert billiard player modeled as if solving the equations of motion for each shot. The assumption about what is going on inside the player is plainly false, but I find it acceptable as a way of predicting shot outcomes. Yet I am not comfortable with a macroeconomic model built around a single infinitely lived representative household whose expectations are in equilibrium with the model itself, even when it fits some aggregate time series, and especially when it is used to evaluate counterfactual policy changes. I notice that I accept one as-if optimization story and reject another, though they look structurally similar, and I cannot currently articulate what the difference is.

A model of voter turnout that assumes voters are purely self-interested expected-utility maximizers, and therefore predicts that almost no one should vote, strikes me as actively misleading rather than merely simplified. By contrast, a climate model that represents sub-grid cloud effects through a tunable parameter rather than explicit microphysics leaves me uneasy, but less uneasy than the representative-agent macroeconomic model; I want to say it is fine as long as the parameter is stable across regimes, but I am not sure that is the real principle.

I also have an undecided case: a pedestrian-crowd model that treats people as self-propelled particles responding only to local density and forces, with no model of individual psychology. I genuinely do not know whether I should rank it with the ideal gas or with the voter turnout case.

I would like you to state the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that best captures these verdicts and explains what distinguishes the cases I accept from the cases I reject. Please state it precisely, say which clause is motivated by which case, and give your recommended verdict on the pedestrian-crowd model. Be explicit about which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and whether the failure lies with the principle or with the verdict. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which argument, counterexample, or consideration would lead you to substantially revise the principle, and where in your reasoning it is most likely to break.
