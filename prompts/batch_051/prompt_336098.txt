My friend and I spent a long car ride arguing about whether one person's choices make any difference to factory farming, and I keep coming back to an inconsistency in his position that neither of us could close. He says going vegetarian is symbolically nice but causally inert: the supermarket orders roughly the same number of chickens whether or not you buy one, so the same animals get raised and killed either way. Then later the same day he was adamant that if a firing squad has six shooters and only five bullets actually hit, all six are equally guilty of murder — not five murderers plus one attempted murderer — and he insisted this was the right moral verdict, not just a quirk of the law. I pushed him to name the principle that seperates the sixth shooter from the weekly meat-buyer, and he couldn't. My candidate principles were the size of your expected contribution, whether you're part of a coordinated plan or institution, whether someone else would step in if you stepped out, and whether your act changes anything in a statistically detectable way. Each one warps in some nearby case: a voter in an election decided by five votes, a small shareholder in a company that dumps waste in a river, a soldier in an unjust war who mostly does paperwork, the one person in a thousand whose shouting tips a frightened crowd into a stampede. I want the strongest defense there is of treating the sixth shooter as a full murderer while letting the weekly meat-buyer off the hook — or, if no defense survives those cases, I want you to say plainly which of the two judgments we should give up. Don't list possible principles and leave me to choose; commit to the one you'd defend and show me how it handles the case that seems to break it.
