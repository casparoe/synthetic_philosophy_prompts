Please explain a conceptual difficulty inside lexical-threshold negative utilitarianism. The view holds that suffering has absolute priority over happiness: no amount of happiness can compensate for a sufficiently bad instance of suffering, just as no number of second-priority goods can compensate for the loss of a first-priority good. It further holds that this priority is triggered only once suffering passes some threshold. The difficulty I want to understand is one of commensurability arising in three places at once. First, there is the question of what the threshold applies to: a single moment of agony at a given intensity, the lifetime aggregate of one person's suffering, or the summed suffering across many lives? Second, once threshold-crossing suffering is in view, how does the view compare two states of the world that both contain such suffering but in different amounts — one person enduring it briefly at extreme intensity versus many people enduring it moderately for a long time? Third, if sub-threshold happiness is genuinely incommensurable with threshold-crossing suffering, what does the threshold correspond to on the scale of pain, and can it be a sharp point rather than a vague range without secretly introducing a cardinal unit of comparison between pleasure and pain? Please explain in your own words why these three questions interact, and why a defender of the view cannot answer them one at a time.
