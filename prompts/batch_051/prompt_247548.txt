I've been trying to square something. My neighborhood set up a small local scrip to keep spending inside the community, and someone justified it by handing me Berkeley's *The Querist*—the part where he more or less asks whether pieces of paper or counters, stamped by public authority, couldn't do everything gold does, since what makes gold money is just that people will take it and pass it on. I kept nodding along, but then I realized I can't tell weather Berkeley is making an empirical prediction (if the state issues tokens, they will circulate) or just recommending a definition of money (money is whatever a community accepts in payment). That bugs me.

I want a concrete way to test the conceptual claim underneath: that the value of money is not in the metal but in the collective habit or credit or state backing. Like, what observation or experiment would distinguish Berkeley's view from the view that precious metal has some non-conventional monetary role? Design the best test you can, say which result would favor which view, and then be explicit about what even a positive result would fail to settle—like whether money should be defined functionally, or whether people's mental states about the token matter. I'd rather have a test that's only partly adequate than a bunch of a priori intuitions.
