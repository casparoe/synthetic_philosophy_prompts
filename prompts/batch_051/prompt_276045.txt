I have been teaching an introductory course in formal logic, and I have found myself puzzled by a small but far-reaching feature of how deductive inference is presented. In the natural-deduction system used in our textbook, and in the proof assistants used to formalise mathematics, modus ponens is introduced not as an axiom or theorem but as a rule of inference: from the formulae φ and φ → ψ one may infer ψ, with the premises written above a horizontal line and the conclusion below. The rule is not itself a formula of the object language, it is assigned no truth value, and it never appears among the numbered assumptions. Yet it seems one could always write down the corresponding conditional — if φ and φ → ψ, then ψ — and add it as a further premise. That is exactly what the Tortoise does in Lewis Carroll's dialogue 'What the Tortoise Said to Achilles':

"You should call it D, not Z," said Achilles. "It comes next to the other three. If you accept A and B and C, you must accept Z."
"And why must I?"
"Because it follows logically from them. If A and B and C are true, Z must be true. You don't dispute that, I imagine?"
"If A and B and C are true, Z must be true," the Tortoise thoughtfully repeated. "That's another Hypothetical, isn't it? And, if I failed to see its truth, I might accept A and B and C, and still not accept Z, mightn't I?"
"You might," the candid hero admitted; "though such obtuseness would certainly be phenomenal. Still, the event is possible. So I must ask you to grant one more Hypothetical."
"Very good. I'm quite willing to grant it, as soon as you've written it down. We will call it
(D) If A and B and C are true, then Z must be true.
Have you entered that in your note-book?"

The textbook treatment looks like a deliberate refusal of the Tortoise's move: rather than allow the principle of inference to become yet another assertion that must itself be applied, one classifies it as a rule, a different kind of thing altogether. I would like to know what philosophical commitments that refusal presupposes. Does the practice strictly presuppose that rules of inference belong to a different logical category from propositions — that they are permissions or acts rather than truths — or does it merely presuppose a practical need to stop somewhere? Does it commit the textbook to the view that the justification of deduction comes to an end in our practice of following rules rather than in any further truth, or perhaps to the view that the meaning of the conditional is constituted by the rule for using it? How much of this is underdetermined, in the sense that several rival theories could have produced this same design for quite different reasons? And if someone took the Tortoise's demand at face value and required every inference principle to be written down as an explicit premise, what would the resulting system look like, and what would that reveal about whether the rule/premise distinction is a genuine insight or merely a device for halting the regress?
