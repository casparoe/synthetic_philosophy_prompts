Just got back from a long, circular argument with a friend about whether privacy can survive everyone posting their whole life. His line was: you put it out there voluntarily, you can't complain when someone uses it. I kept saying the point of privacy was never only about secrecy, though I'll admit I lost on points. So I dug up the thing that more or less started modern privacy law in the US — Samuel Warren and Louis Brandeis, "The Right to Privacy," Harvard Law Review, 1890 — and they write:

"The intensity and complexity of life, attendant upon advancing civilization, have rendered necessary some retreat from the world, and man, under the refining influence of culture, has become more sensitive to publicity, so that solitude and privacy have become more essential to the individual; but modern enterprise and invention have, through invasions upon his privacy, subjected him to mental pain and distress, far greater than could be inflicted by mere bodily injury."

Here's the case I actually want to run through that passage. A person has never said a word publicly about his health. An acquaintance scrapes years of his perfectly ordinary public posts — timestamps, geotags, what he ate, favorite walks, a few weeks of silence — and a model correctly infers that he's in treatment for a serious condition, then tells mutual friends. No hacking, no hidden data; every input was something the person posted willingly. Does the passage, read as a reason for why privacy matters, cover that? Or does its logic only protect what someone has actually kept private, so this falls outside it? Walk me through what the words alone give, what the purpose of the passage gives, and what Warren and Brandeis would probably have said if you'd stopped them on the street and asked. And if those readings come apart, which should win here and why?
