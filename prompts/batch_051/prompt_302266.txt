Okay so I just spent two hours arguing with my friend about whether forgiveness can ever be genuinely obligatory, and we stumbled onto an asymmetry in our own judgments that neither of us could explain. Start with a concrete wrong: a friend reads your journal and tells other people what he found. Before the friend does anything about it, the same people who say forgiveness can't be demanded also say that forgiving him anyway would be generous, even saintly, but obviously not required — you get to stay angry, and nobody can tell you to let it go, because you're the one who was wronged. Now change exactly one thing. The friend comes to you, confesses fully, takes responsibility, shows real remorse, and tries to make amends. Suddenly those same people start treating you as the problem if you refuse: you're bitter, you're nursing a grudge, he did everything right and you still can't move on. My friend's whole line was that an apology changes the relationship, but he couldn't explain why that change should land as a new demand on the person who was already harmed. The wrong itself hasn't changed; the only difference is what the wrongdoer did afterward, and somehow that's supposed to create a duty in the victim — a duty the victim never agreed to and didnt choose. So here's my question. Is there one defensible principle that yields both verdicts: forgiveness before repentance is supererogatory, but forgiveness after a full and sincere apology is at least pro tanto required? Or are we just granting repentance a kind of magic power to place obligations on the person it already harmed? If you defend the asymmetry, defend it against the case where the apology is sincere but strategically timed to make the victim look unreasonable, and against the case where someone confesses to you for a wrong they did to someone else and expects your forgiveness to count. Give me the strongest principled account you can, and an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept or given up.
