I am a novelist rather than a philosopher, and I want to understand a problem I encounter constantly in my work: why human beings demand to be recognized, and why the recognition they get so often fails to satisfy them. I would like to test an old philosophical argument against a form of recognition that did not exist when it was written.

In The Phenomenology of Mind, J. B. Baillie's translation of Hegel's Phenomenology of Spirit, G. W. F. Hegel writes:

"This pure conception of recognition, of duplication of self-consciousness within its unity, we must now consider in the way its process appears for self-consciousness. It will, in the first place, present the aspect of the disparity of the two, or the break-up of the middle term into the extremes, which, qua extremes, are opposed to one another, and of which one is merely recognized, while the other only recognizes."

I take the passage to be doing two things. It states what mutual recognition would be: each self-consciousness sees itself duplicated in another that it also recognizes. And it warns that the first actual shape this process takes is unequal: one party is recognized but does not recognize, while the other only recognizes. The master–slave section that follows treats that unequal shape as unstable and ultimately unsatisfying for the master, because his recognition comes from someone he has reduced to an instrument.

Here is the development I want the argument tested against. Consider an author—or more generally any person—who acquires a large audience through a platform that records attention. Readers return to the work, respond to it, are moved by it, and the author receives this recognition through numbers, comments, and reputation. The author does not recognize each member of the audience as a determinate other; the audience is, for the author, a public, a following, a market, or a count. Nothing was staked on either side, and there is no reciprocity between two exposed individuals. Yet the experience can feel, to the author, like being seen.

Does Hegel's argument in this passage survive that development? I would like you to say whether the unequal structure he describes is the same structure the platform produces, or whether something essential has changed. In particular, tell me which premise of the argument is under pressure—the premise that recognition must be mutual, the premise that it must take place between two self-consciousnesses who expose themselves to one another, or the premise that one-sided recognition collapses for the one who receives it—and whether a repaired version of Hegel's argument can still say something true, even if weaker, about why algorithmic or mass recognition may turn out unstable, hollow, or exhausting. I am less interested in a policy verdict about platforms than in learning what remains of the argument.
