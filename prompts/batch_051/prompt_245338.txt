I read a short online explainer about the problem of dirty hands in politics, and I want to know how much of it to trust before I repeat any of it. The article reconstructs the position roughly as follows. Michael Walzer argues that a good politician sometimes has to do things that are morally wrong — ordering the torture of a suspect, authorizing a bombing, lying to the public — because the consequences of every alternative would be worse. Since the choice is justified by those consequences, "dirty hands" is really just Walzer's label for what utilitarians have always meant by a necessary evil. On this reading, the politician who authorizes the torture is morally in the same position as a surgeon who amputates a leg: she rightly regrets the damage, but her hands are no more stained than the surgeon's, because she did exactly what she ought to have done. The author adds that, for Walzer, the guilt the politician feels afterward is what settles the matter: the feeling itself pays off the moral debt and restores her standing, so once she has felt bad enough there is nothing left to answer for. The article closes by saying that the problem of dirty hands therefore reduces to a problem about how to calculate consequences under uncertainty, and that philosophers who treat it as a distinct kind of moral phenomenon are adding emotional drama rather than content. I found the surgeon comparison persuasive, but the claim that guilt functions as a kind of payment struck me as possibly too neat. Is this a fair summary of the view Walzer actually defends, and of what later defenders of the distinctiveness of dirty hands claim? Go through the summary point by point and tell me what a defender would accept, what they would say is subtly wrong, and whether the closing deflationary conclusion survives once the errors are corrected.
