I sit on a state commission that approves or rejects history textbooks. Our recent record: we approved a U.S. history textbook that argued the American Revolution was substantially a slaveholders' response to abolitionist pressure and organized the era around primary sources from enslaved people; we rejected the rival U.S. textbook that cast the founding as a struggle for liberty and treated slavery as a contradiction the nation gradually overcame, with a majority saying the rival let the nation off too easily; we approved a jointly produced Japanese-Korean modern-history textbook that identified the Imperial Japanese military's system of sexual slavery plainly, treated Nanjing as a massacre, and relied on military and court archives; and we rejected a Japanese publisher's textbook that mentioned Nanjing but said the death toll was disputed and otherwise framed the Pacific War as Japan's defensive response to Western encirclement.

A South Korean publisher has submitted a modern Korean history textbook. It describes Japanese colonial rule in more explicit, better-documented detail than anything we have approved; but it tells a uniformly heroic story of the Korean resistance, never mentions colonial-era collaboration or the violence of postwar authoritarian rule, and returns constantly to the theme of the Korean people's unbroken virtue. I'm trying to figure out how the commission should vote if it wants to be consistent with its own precedents. Which of our earlier decisions is the closest analog? If our record doesn't determine the answer, what principle would an approval commit us to, and what principle would a rejection commit us to?
