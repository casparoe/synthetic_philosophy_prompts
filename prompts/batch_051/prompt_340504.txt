I keep getting tangled up in something about the law and motivation, and I'd like help reconstructing what view of moral worth it actually rests on. In most US jurisdictions, a person who gives emergency first aid at a car crash and accidently breaks the victim's ribs while pulling her out is shielded from being sued under a so-called Good Samaritan statute. The legal standard looks at whether the rescuer acted reasonably and in good faith, and it does not ask whether she helped from genuine compassion, because a crowd was filming and she didn't want to look heartless, or because she wanted to feel like a hero afterwards. In that corner of the law, the worth of the act seems to lie entirely in what was done, not in why.

But the same system treats an otherwise identical punch as a much more serious crime if it was thrown because of the victim's race than if it was thrown in a squabble over a parking space; when the defendant singles the victim out on racial grounds, motive changes the charge and the sentence. So motives are treated as irrelevant to how the law evaluates a rescuer, and as central to how it evaluates an assailant. What theory of when a person's reasons or motives matter to the moral worth of their act is presupposed by this pattern, if any single theory is? Reconstruct the implicit view or views, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and say what would have to change if the legal system tried to be consistent on this. Is it actually inconsistent, or are they just borrowing different theories for different jobs without noticing?
