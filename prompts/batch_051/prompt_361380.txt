I have a pet idea I keep calling "smooth breakdown" and I want it honestly stress-tested.

The standard Heidegger story is that equipment is mostly ready-to-hand: the hammer disappears into the hammering, and only when it fails does it become unready-to-hand — conspicuous, obtrusive, obstinate. I think that story misses a big category. A phone's autocorrect quietly changes a word you got right into a different word; a maps app reroutes you onto a worse road without announcing it; a recommendation feed slowly narrows until it's feeding you the same three things. Nothing ever feels broken. The tool goes on being transparent and seamless while objectively doing the wrong thing.

So I want to say there's a distinct mode, smooth breakdown: equipment that is unready-to-hand in the sense that it isn't doing it's job, and ready-to-hand in the sense that it's still experienced as working. And I want to make the stronger claim that smooth breakdown is now the dominant failure mode, because software is designed exactly to keep failures invisible.

The obvious comeback is that I'm mixing levels — readiness-to-hand is about how things show up, not whether they do their job, so "broken but experienced as working" isn't a new mode of equipment, just a fact about my ignorance. But that's exactly what I'm suspicious of: if the rupture is what forces the tool to become visible, and the rupture never shows up, then a whole lot of our background practices can quietly go wrong and never get the revealing moment Heidegger thought breakdown provided.

So my question: am I adding something real, or just relabeling unnoticed malfunction (or reinventing something already worked out under a name I don't know)? What does the distinction get right, and where does it snap? Be candid — if it's the kind of idea that dies under pressure, say so. Also tell me how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you're least sure of. Keep it under 400 words, and use American English.
