I'm working through the Searle–Dennett debate and keep getting stuck on how Dennett's two-bitser argument transfers to current systems. As I understand it, Dennett's reply to the Chinese Room is that the room's apparent understanding is just derived intentionality: the system borrows its aboutness from the programmers and users outside it, and that borrowed intentionality is enough for the room, considered as a whole, to understand Chinese. Searle's mistake, on this reading, is to demand that the man inside have original intentionality when no one should expect that.

But the setup Dennett was responding to had a clear chain of borrowing: programmers who already understood the language wrote a rulebook, and the room mechanically followed it. A modern large language model is different in a way that seems to scramble that picture. There is no hand-coded rulebook; the system is trained by gradient descent on a corpus of human text, and whatever competence it has emerges from the statistical structure of that corpus. The human intentionality the system allegedly borrows is dispersed across billions of authors, none of whom wrote anything with this system in mind, and the training process itself is not a set of semantic rules but an optimization over predictive success. Does Dennett's two-bitser argument survive this change, or does it depend on a now-obsolete assumption about how AI systems get their competence? If it survives, which premise carries it? If it needs repair, what weaker version remains, and what exactly does it no longer entitle us to say?
