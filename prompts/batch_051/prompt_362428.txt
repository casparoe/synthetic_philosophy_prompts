I've been going in circles on the bootstrapping problem lately — the worry that you can't use a source to verify its own reliability, because the whole track record gets delivered by the very thing your trying to test. Like, I can't confirm my color vision just by looking at a bunch of objects, calling them red or green, and then noting I was right most of the time, because the confirmation step itself uses my color vision. The obvious fix is to demand an independent check. But I've realized I have no working account of what "independent" means here; my intuitions seem to track something about not sharing the same error source, but then the cases below break that.

So here's the task. Give me a working definition of an independent check (or independent evidence for a source's reliability, whichever label you prefer), then apply it to each of the following and say whether that check is independent. Flag any place where the definition gives a verdict you want to resist, and either revise the definition or defend the verdict.

Cases:
1. Checking a gas gauge by reading the same gauge again ten seconds later.
2. Checking the gauge by installing a second gauge of the same make and model, wired to the same sending unit in the tank.
3. Checking the gauge by comparing it to a different kind of fuel-level sensor — say an ultrasonic one clipped to the top of the tank.
4. Checking the gauge by filling the tank from empty, driving until the trip odometer plus known mpg says you've burned about a quarter tank, and seeing if the needle sits near three-quarters.
5. Over a year, noticing that whenever the gauge reads empty the engine sputters and dies within a couple of miles, and whenever you fill up the gauge reads full; counting that accumulated correlation as a check of the gauge.
6. Testing your color vision by taking the standard Ishihara test (the colored-dot numbers at the optometrist).
7. Testing your color vision by having a friend with normal color vision name the colors of a set of paint chips, and comparing their labels to how the chips look to you.
8. Testing your memory by trying to recall as many past cases as you can where your memory failed you.
9. Testing your memory by checking a few weeks of recollections against photos, receipts, and saved emails.
10. Testing a news site by seeing whether another site owned by the same company and running the same wire story says the same thing.

My gut says 1, 2, 8, and 10 are clearly not independent, 3, 4, and 9 clearly are, and 5, 6, and 7 are the ones I can't settle. But I want the definition to do the work: run it through all ten and tell me where it forces a reclassification, or where it goes silent.
