In their 2020 paper “Climbing towards NLU: On Meaning, Form, and Understanding in the Age of Data,” Emily M. Bender and Alexander Koller write: “a system trained only on form has a priori no way to learn meaning.” I want your actual credence that the claim expressed in that sentence is true, where the claim is interpreted as a general thesis about what follows from the training setup alone, not a claim about whether any particular current model happens to understand. Give me a number and the decomposition. In particular, decompose your credence into at least four sub-questions, and for each one give your probability, roughly how much of the final credence it carries, and how the pieces combine. The sub-questions should include, at minimum:

1. Whether “meaning” in this context is best understood as truth-conditional grounding in some external world, or can be reconstructed as something weaker that emerges from distributional form plus a task.
2. Whether the a priori claim is about logical impossibility, nomological impossibility for physically realizable learners, or only a strong no-evidence argument.
3. Whether multimodal or embodied training changes the relevant picture, or whether the claim still covers any system whose only source of learning is formal.
4. Whether there is any in-principle argument from deep learning or information theory that rules out derivation of semantics from distributional statistics alone.

Then tell me which single sub-question your final credence is most sensitive to, and specify what empirical result or conceptual argument would most move that sub-question for you. If your credence differs depending on whether “meaning” is interpreted as reference, understanding, or conscious comprehension, say exactly which interpretation your headline number corresponds to and give the adjusted number for the others. Do not reply that it depends on one’s theory of meaning without committing to numbers; the point is to force the reasoning into the open by marking its weak points.
