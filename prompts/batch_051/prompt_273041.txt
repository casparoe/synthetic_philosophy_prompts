I work as a software engineer and have no formal background in philosophy or law, but I spend much of my time reasoning about probabilities, base rates, and thresholds. When a dashboard shows that 90 percent of this hour's errors share one signature, I am comfortable treating that signature as the cause for the purpose of reverting the change, even though I cannot point to any single failed request and trace it to that signature. I noticed recently that legal fact-finding seems to refuse the same move in structurally similar cases, and I cannot tell whether that refusal is principled.

Consider a pedestrian injured by a bus that flees. The only buses on that street are operated by the Blue Bus Company and the Red Bus Company, and during the hour of the collision 80 percent of the buses on the street are Blue Bus. The probability that the offending bus was Blue Bus is therefore 0.8, above the civil standard of more likely than not; yet most people, and the courts as the standard discussions present them, regard the bare statistic as insufficient to find Blue Bus liable. Now hold the probability fixed while changing the type of evidence: an eyewitness testifies that the bus was blue, and the witness is known to identify bus colors correctly under these conditions 80 percent of the time, with nothing else known. Here a finding of liability seems unremarkable, although the probability appears to be the same. A sharper version of the puzzle: 1,000 people attend a show and 501 entered without paying, but no one can tell which; for any randomly selected attendee, the probability of being a gatecrasher is 0.501, above the civil standard, yet almost no one would hold that attendee liable on the statistic alone. In criminal cases the refusal is even stronger: naked statistical evidence is thought unable, by itself, to support proof beyond a reasonable doubt.

The asymmetry I want explained or dissolved is this: aggregate evidence about a class is treated as inadequate to fix responsibility for a particular event, even when it assigns a probability that would be sufficient if it came from fallible individualized evidence such as eyewitness testimony. Is there a real property that separates the two kinds of evidence? If you defend the asymmetry, give me the strongest principle that draws the line - a principle that covers the blue bus and the gatecrasher and also survives a case where the statistical evidence is nearly conclusive, say 0.99, and is recorded by a reliable instrument at the very time and place of the event, so the defendant's identity is the only unknown. If you conclude the asymmetry is not justified, say which side should move: should civil law admit naked statistics, should criminal law, and what would become of proof beyond a reasonable doubt?
