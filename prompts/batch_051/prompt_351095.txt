I'm a faculty member teaching a graduate seminar in analytic epistemology. Next week's session is a ninety-minute introduction to the Nyāya account of the pramāṇas — the classical Indian thesis that there are four irreducible means of knowledge: perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna), comparison (upamāna), and verbal testimony (śabda). The students are analytically trained and have read the post-Gettier literature, but none of them has touched Indian philosophy. Don't explain the view to me; plan how I should teach it. The session has to make the fourfold distinction feel principled rather than like a list. Give me the dependency structure: the question students need to feel before the pramāṇas count as an answer, what has to come first — I suspect it is the idea of a pramāṇa as an instrument of a veridical cognition rather than a justified-true-belief in miniature, but tell me if I'm wrong — and what must be in place before each of the remaining three is introduced. Tell me whether one carrying example can do real work across all four, or whether the classical stock examples (smoke on the hill for inference, the forester's description of a gavaya for comparison) are the better choice, and specify what the carrying example must have and lack. Then map the misconceptions I should expect: I'm guessing the students will collapse testimony into inference, treat comparison as mere analogical reasoning or concept-learning, and assume perception is foundational with the others parasitic. Tell me in what order these will surface and exactly where in the sequence to head each one off. Say what I should deliberately leave out at this level and announce as left out. End with one question I can put to the room that would show they have understood why Nyāya keeps four categories rather than two, not just that they can gloss the Sanskrit terms. Finally, tell me how confident you are in this plan and single out the point you are least sure of.
