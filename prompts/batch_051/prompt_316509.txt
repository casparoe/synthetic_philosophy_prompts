Ive spent the last couple years lurking in the open-weight versus closed-lab arguments on LessWrong and the ML Discords around it, and I've started to think the interesting object isn't the AI risk dispute itself but the fact that the argument is frozen solid. The open-release side and the closed side mostly grant the same intermediate facts: once weights are out, fine-tuning can strip most safety training; open models lag the frontier by a year or two and the gap keeps shrinking; labs can't reliably prevent leaks or espionage; and there's no natural experiment for what a fully open ecosystem does once seriously dangerous capabilities are on the table. Yet every thread ends the same way, with the open side saying concentrated power in a handful of companies is the actual catastrophe and the closed side saying you cant un-ring a bell. Years of new evals, leaks, and policy fights later, nobody has moved.

My working diagnosis, and I genuinely am unsure of it, is that what we're looking at is a disagreement about base rates for event classes we've basically never observed, plus a disagreement about who bears the burden of proof under that kind of uncertainty, plus a deeper disagreement about whether centralization and oligopoly should be priced into the same expected-value calculation as their own unrecoverable failure mode. On that reading, the two sides are selecting reference classes on purpose: the open side reaches for Linux, the web, open science; the closed side reaches for nuclear weapons, gain-of-function research, synthetic biology. If that's right, then the two stances — release until shown dangerous versus restrict until shown safe — aren't conclusions at all, just priors, and no benchmark or misuse case study will ever move them.

But I could be wrong. What I actually want is a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, not a verdict on open weights. Is it a verbal dispute riding on the ambiguity of the word open — inspectable, fine-tunable, redistributable? Is there a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work that everyone is talking around? Is it basically the unilateralist's curse in disguise, where the relevant decision theory changes because lots of independent actors can each unilaterally release? Or is it a bedrock difference about centralization that will never be evidence-responsive? And under whatever diagnosis you give, what would actually count as progress: a conceptual distinction, a feasible experiment, a mechanism, an institutional design, or the honest answer that the question as posed is ill-posed and we should swap it for a narrower one?
