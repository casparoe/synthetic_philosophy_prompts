I cover AI and economics for a general-audience magazine, and lately I've been reading Keynes's 1921 book A Treatise on Probability. He makes a distinction there that won't leave me alone. For Keynes, a probability is the degree of rational belief a proposition deserves on the evidence you have. But he says that isn't the only thing worth measuring: there's also what he calls the weight of an argument — roughly, how much relevant evidence you have and how complete it is. His point is that a conclusion can be highly probable relative to a tiny, shoddy body of evidence. You can assign 0.9 confidence on the strength of one half-remembered anecdote, or 0.9 on the strength of a huge controlled study, and those are wildly different situations even though the number is the same.

This maps almost too neatly onto a distinction ML people draw between a model's output confidence and its epistemic uncertainty, or between in-distribution and out-of-distribution prediction. A classifier can put .97 on 'dog' for an image that looks nothing like anything in it's training set. Both Keynes and the ML folks seem to be saying that how strongly a claim should be believed and how much backing stands behind that belief are two separate things, and running them together is a mistake.

I can't tell whether this is a genuine identity of structure or just two fields independently noticing something superficially similar. If the map is real, what's the precise ML analogue of Keynes's claim that new evidence can leave the probability unchanged while raising the weight? And where does the analogy break? I half expect the answer to be that a Bayesian would collapse both into a single posterior distribution over hypotheses, so the distinction is only psychological — but then I don't know why epistemic uncertainty in ML seems so hard to compute and so practically useful. What am I missing?
