I've got a set of gut reactions about how much we owe people in our attitudes, and I want someone to pressure-test them against a principle. A nurse takes perfect care of an awful patient and then, in the break room, calls him an asshole to a colleague: I don't think she wronged the patient. A boss pays his staff well and never does anything untoward, but privately thinks of them as losers who stalled out: this strikes me as a real wrong, even though nothing ever shows. A friend helps me move all day and never lets on, but privately thinks my career choices are pathetic: I can't say he wronged me, yet it bugs me that he thinks it. A son visits his mother every Sunday but spends the visit sighing and checking his watch: what he's doing is wrong, even though the visits happen. A colleague congratulates me on a promotion, but later I hear she'd told others I didn't deserve it and resented me: I think she wronged me. And a doctor fixes an unconscious patient's injury with total competence and zero malice but also zero regard for him as a person—just a body to fix: there's no bad action to point to, but I want to say something is absent. Is there a principle that gets all of these, or close to it? Which of my verdicts would it force me to drop, and what does it say about the case I keep going back and forth on: someone who performs warmth perfectly toward a person she privately despises because she thinks acting respectful is all that's owed—does she owe the actual attitude, and if not, what is it we're complaining about when the faking feels wrong to the rest of us?
