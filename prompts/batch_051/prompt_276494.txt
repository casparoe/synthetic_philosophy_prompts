I'm a journalist who covers the ideas beat, and a recent interview left me with an argument I can't referee on my own. I had a philosopher and a psychologist on the show to talk about whether respect is just a matter of how we treat people or whether what we privately think of them matters too. After we stopped recording they kept going about a manager I'd described, and they asked me to settle it and I couldn't.

The case: a manager has an impeccable record on paper. He calls on junior staff in meetings, credits their contributions, mentors women on his team, never interrupts, and his promotion recommendations show no statistical bias. Then a leaked email chain reveals that privately he regards several of his female reports as "lightweights" who will "cap out" because they lack a certain killer instinct. The leak humiliates the women, but his visible conduct doesn't change one bit.

The psychologist said the manager did nothing wrong before the leak: duties govern actions, attitudes become morally relevant only when they surface in action, and the leak was the first action that created a problem. The philosopher said that gets it backwards: regarding someone as an equal is something you can owe them, and the private regard itself is the wrong, whether or not it ever goes public.

Then they went back and forth:

(1) Psychologist: You can't be morally required to feel respect. Feelings aren't under direct voluntary control, and ought implies can. So any duty has to be about conduct, not private evaluations.

(2) Philosopher: Recognition respect isn't a feeling. It's a standing way of regarding someone—a stance about their equal claim on you—and that's under our control in the relevant sense. We redirect attention, question snap judgments, change habits. We hold people responsible for being fair-minded, not just for acting fair.

(3) Psychologist: Even granting that, a stance with no behavioral effects is outside morality. If it never leaks, no one can tell and no one is harmed. You're just policing minds.

(4) Philosopher: It isn't inert. Evaluations like "lightweight, will cap out" shape micro-decisions—who gets the stretch assignment, how much slack you cut someone—even when the manager thinks they don't. And wrongs don't require harms anyone notices. If a friend had treated you well but privately thought you were a buffoon, you'd say you were wronged, not just upset.

(5) Psychologist: That friend case does different work. The employees weren't wronged before the leak; the leak was the public act that exposed and humiliated them, and exposure is the wrong-making thing. Before that, a private thought about someone is no more a wrong to them than a private fantasy.

(6) Philosopher: But the leak doesn't change how he regards them—it reveals it. If the revealed state is the problem, it was there all along. Imagine the women never see the email but a coworker does: the coworker now knows the manager holds them as lesser, and still everyone treats them the same. Is nothing wrong there? I'd say the relation itself is defective, and the defect is his regard, not the disclosure.

(7) Psychologist: Morality coordinates conduct. A "defective relation" that changes no conduct does no coordinating. If we make unexpressed attitudes wrong, we get an unverifiable duty, and people get blamed for what they were discovered to have thought, not for what they did.

(8) Philosopher: Unverifiable isn't the same as not wrong. We know attitudes from slips, leaks, and confession, and imperfect evidence is true of every mental state in the law. Also, your view turns respect into theater: perfectly performed conduct with an inner script that can say anything. Can you really treat someone fully as an equal while privately judging them a lightweight?

(9) Psychologist: You keep smuggling "fully" into the requirement. I deny that private regard is part of it. The manager does everything respect demands—credits, mentors, corrects his numbers. His inner script is it's own business. Show me the decision he'd make differently, and I'll agree there's a problem.

(10) Philosopher: The decision he'd make differently is the judgment in the email: that these women will cap out because they lack the killer instinct. That is a decision he has already made about their prospects and limits. It just doesn't show up in the personnel file—until it does.

So: can you go through that exchange step by step and say who is right about which specific point, where each of them overreaches, and what both of them are missing? Then say whether the manager owed those women anything in his private regard, or whether all the obligations arrived with the leak. Finally, give an overall verdict: is either of them more right, or do there arguments just leave the underlying disagreement unsettled?
