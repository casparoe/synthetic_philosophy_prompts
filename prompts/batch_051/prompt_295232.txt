I cover science and ideas, and I am working on a long-form piece about a style of management that has spread through research, medicine, schools, and technology: the attempt to govern by numbers. Funders rank scientists partly by citation counts. Hospitals link pay to patient satisfaction scores. School systems evaluate teachers by gains on standardized tests. AI developers train systems to maximize a scalar reward meant to stand in for a more elusive objective. In each case, the institution picks an indicator that seems correlated with the real thing—scientific quality, patient welfare, learning, carrying out the intended task—then makes that indicator a target and attaches stakes to it. The failure pattern is well known enough to have a name, Goodhart's law: once an indicator becomes a target, the pressure to optimize the indicator itself weakens its correlation with the goal it was chosen to track. Institutions then condemn the resulting conduct—salami-sliced papers, teaching to the test, patients nudged into reporting satisfaction, reward hacking in AI—while continuing to lean on the very indicators whose distortion they deplore.

For my piece I want the philosophy underneath the practice, not another policy lament. Reconstruct what this practice of metric-based governance presupposes about the nature of the objectives themselves and about measurement. What would have to be true of a good such as scientific quality or patient welfare for a numerical proxy to bear the weight these institutions place on it? Which rival views of value does the practice quietly rule out—for instance, the idea that important goods are plural, or incommensurable, or discoverable only through trained judgment? Is the practice internally coherent? It seems to treat the indicator as a mere proxy at the design stage but as constitutive of the goal at the reward stage, and then to be surprised when optimizing the proxy severs it from the goal—which is close to predicting surprise at the very law the system is built on. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined. Close by telling me what would have to change in how these institutions evaluate quality if the presupposed picture were replaced by a pluralist or judgment-centered one.
