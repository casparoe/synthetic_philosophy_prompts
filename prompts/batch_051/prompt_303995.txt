Analyse the exposure of the Tuskegee syphilis study as a case study in the epistemology of conspiracy theories. From 1932 until 1972 the United States Public Health Service monitored several hundred Black men in and around Tuskegee, Alabama, most of whom had syphilis; the men were not told their diagnosis and were denied penicillin after it became the standard cure, and the study ended only after a whistleblower and an Associated Press report made it public. Take the two poles of the debate to be particularism, which holds that each conspiracy theory must be assessed on its own evidence and that official accounts enjoy no standing epistemic advantage, and generalism, which holds that conspiracy theories are suspect as a class and that accepting one therefore requires evidence of an exceptional kind. Discuss what each position would have said a reasonable person hearing early rumours about the study should have believed before the 1972 exposé, and what each would have implied about the evidential situation of the reporters and the whistleblower. Explain which features of the episode each view accounts for and which it misdescribes, and assess whether the verified existence of this conspiracy is best treated as a counterexample to generalism, as a case generalism can accommodate, or as an exceptional case that carries little weight for the assessment of conspiracy theories generally. Address in particular the complication that the conspirator was itself an official government institution, so generalism's usual preference for official accounts comes under unusual pressure.
