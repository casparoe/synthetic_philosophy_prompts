Metaethics was my first love, but I still cant get a clean account of why the internal vs. external reasons debate won't die. Internalists (the Williams-influenced ones) say you only have a reason to do something if, starting from your actual motives and deliberating correctly, you'd end up moved to do it. Externalists say there are reasons that don't connect to anything you currently care about—moral reasons being the obvious sticking point. Both sides agree on the standard cases and neither is making an obviously empirical claim about psychology, yet nobody moves. My current hypothesis: the whole fight is over how much the phrase 'deliberating correctly' is allowed to assume. Too thin, and the thesis is trivial (you have reasons to do what you already want); too thick with normative constraints (no factual mistakes, no bias, etc.), and external reasons get smuggled back in, so the debate just relocates rather than settles. So I can't tell if this is (a) a verbal dispute between two senses of 'has a reason'—can-be-moved versus would-be-wrong-to-ignore—(b) a hidden empirical bet about whether recognizing a consideration can itself move someone, or (c) a bedrock clash about whether normativity has to answer to the agent's own cares. I want a diagnosis of the disagreement, not a survey, and a straight answer about whether anything—a distinction, a formal model, a psychology result, a change of question—would count as actual progress, or whether we should stop expecting it to settle.
