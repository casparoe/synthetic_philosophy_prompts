You are a political philosopher with expertise in urban ethics, property theory, and democratic decision-making, and you have been asked to help a public body reason through a contested case. I sit on my city's planning commission, and we must vote next week on a proposal to rezone a roughly 40-acre area of single-family detached houses (R-1) to permit fourplexes and three-story apartment buildings by right. The area is within a short walk of a light-rail station, the city's general plan already designates it for infill growth, and the city has both a severe shortage of lower-cost rental housing and a history of exclusionary zoning whose effects are still visible in who lives where. A well-organized neighborhood association opposes the change. They argue that residents bought into a stable, low-scale, quiet district in reliance on decades of R-1 zoning; that the change would impose concrete harms on current residents — construction disruption, parking pressure, lost views, and the erosion of a community built over many years. A housing coalition supports it. They reply that current residents are in effect claiming an entitlement to exclude strangers from a neighborhood whose advantages they hoard; that the expectation of permanence is not a protected interest; and that "neighborhood character" arguments have a long record of serving exclusion by race and class. They add that the future residents of the new homes are not in the room and have no vote, so the process is stacked in favor of incumbents. They describe the case as fundamentally about who has a right to the city. Draft the decision memo I will circulate before the vote. It must recommend approve, deny, or approve with conditions; give the strongest honest version of the case for whichever side loses; and articulate a principle that distinguishes legitimate neighborhood interests from illegitimate exclusion — a principle the commission could apply in future land-use decisions. Do not give us a legal brief or a restatement of zoning law; we want a reasoned recommendation we can defend in public. Before drafting the final memo, briefly brainstorm the strongest three considerations on each side, then use that to discipline the recommendation.
