Daniel Dennett has argued, in his late writing on AI, that systems engineered to pass as human in ordinary interaction should be understood as counterfeit people rather than as mere tools, bots, or instances of ordinary fraud. The argument does most of its work through an analogy with counterfeit money. On Dennett's telling, a counterfeit bill is not harmful just because it cheats the person who ends up holding it; it is harmful because every note that passes undetected reduces confidence in the currency as a whole, so that even dealings involving entirely genuine notes become more costly and uncertain. He claims that counterfeit people impose the same kind of harm on the currency of social trust: each system that successfully impersonates a person gives everyone a reason to discount the presumption that an accountable human being stands behind a voice, a face, a testimonial, or a plea, and this degrades the background of accountability and good faith on which genuine human interaction depends. His target is specifically impersonating systems, not automation that presents itself as such. The conclusion he wants to draw is that producing counterfeit people ought to be treated as the sort of grave offense that counterfeiting currency is, rather than as an ordinary matter of consumer protection or fraud law.

I want this analogy audited rather than accepted as an intuitive frame. First, give the most charitable correspondence table you can: what plays the role of the genuine currency; of an individual counterfeit note; of the counterfeiter; of the merchant or customer left holding a bad note; of the central bank or monetary authority; of anti-counterfeiting measures; and of the debasement or loss of confidence that gives the analogy its normative force. Second, identify where the mapping breaks. Distinguish disanalogies that are incidental from those that are load-bearing, and for each load-bearing break, specify the feature of money or currency that the analogy imports into the case of human interaction without argument, and how the argument would need to be revised if that feature is absent. Third, deliver a verdict: does the conclusion that counterfeit people are more like counterfeit currency than like ordinary fraud survive the audit intact, survive only in a narrowed form, or collapse once the disanalogies are made explicit? If some other analogy would carry the underlying concern more honestly, supply it and show what policy conclusion it actually licenses.
