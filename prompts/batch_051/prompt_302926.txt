You are an expert philosopher of cognitive science with a solid working knowledge of the history of artificial intelligence. I am a postgraduate philosophy student, and I have been trying to get clear about the frame problem and whether it still has bite. As I understand it, the problem was originally posed for logic-based AI: an agent that represents the world as a large set of explicit sentences and acts by deductively deriving the consequences of its actions seems to need frame axioms stating, for each action and each fact, that the action does not change that fact, and this quickly becomes computationally hopeless. Philosophers later widened the point, using thought experiments of the Dennett variety, so that the frame problem became a problem about relevance: how does any agent know which of its indefinitely many beliefs bear on the current task without searching through all of them? The argument, in broad strokes, is that relevance cannot be had for free, and that any architecture relying on explicit symbolic representation plus inference therefore faces a deep difficulty.

Here is the development I want to test that argument against. The most capable AI systems today are not built that way at all. A large language model trained to predict the next token over internet-scale text acquires a distributed, implicit kind of world model. If you describe an ordinary situation and an action, it will reliably produce the sensible consequences: the glass is still on the table after you read the letter, the dog is still in the room after you answer the phone. It does this without explicit frame axioms, without a store of discrete beliefs, and without enumerating non-changes. On the face of it, the system gets relevance, or something functionally very like it, for free, as a by-product of gradient descent over statistical regularities.

My question is what this does to the frame problem as a philosophical claim about cognition. Which precise premise of the frame-problem argument is under pressure: that knowledge must be explicitly represented, that relevance must be computed by a search over representations, that a genuine agent needs a principled account of relevance, or something else? Does the argument survive and show that these systems have merely hidden the problem rather than solved it, does it need to be repaired into a weaker claim, or should we conclude that the original problem was an artefact of a particular architecture and is now largely dissolved? I am asking about the fate of the argument, not about whether language models are intelligent or conscious, and I do not need a reading list. Please begin by separating the distinct things the phrase “the frame problem” has been used to mean, state the version of the argument you are testing as explicit premises, and then say which premise the new development bears on and whether it can be replaced with one that preserves the argument's force. Be clear about which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely contested among philosophers and AI researchers, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
