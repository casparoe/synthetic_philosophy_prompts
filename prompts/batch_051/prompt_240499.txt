I am writing a worksheet for a philosophy seminar on dispositions, and I want an exercise in which the distinction between finks and masks does real work rather than remaining a piece of terminology. Let me set out the machinery the exercise should use. The simple conditional analysis of dispositions says that an object is disposed to give manifestation M in response to stimulus S just in case, were it to undergo S, it would give M. A fink defeats this analysis: the occurrence of S would itself destroy the causal basis of the disposition before M could occur, so the counterfactual is false even though the object plainly has the disposition. A mask defeats it differently: S occurs and the causal basis survives intact, but some additional factor stops that basis from producing M, so the counterfactual is again false even though the disposition is present. A common repair is the basis-retention analysis: the object has the disposition just in case it possesses a causal basis B such that, were it to undergo S while retaining B, S and B would jointly cause M. This repair is standardly said to solve the fink problem while leaving the mask problem in place, but I would like my students to see why in a concrete case.

My request is a matched pair of thought experiments built around one ordinary household appliance. In both cases a single component of the appliance should have one and the same disposition, the same stimulus should occur, and the manifestation should fail to occur in both. In the first case the failure should be a fink: the occurrence of the stimulus removes the causal basis of the disposition quickly enough to prevent the manifestation. In the second case the failure should be a mask: the causal basis remains fully in place when the stimulus occurs, but a further factor blocks its causal contribution. The two cases should be as physically close as possible, diverging only where the fink/mask distinction demands it, and they should involve no magic, no futuristic technology, and no contrived guardian mechanism; unremarkable safety or insulating features are acceptable. The pair should be designed so that the simple conditional analysis says the component lacks the disposition in both cases, whereas the basis-retention analysis says it has the disposition in the fink case but still says it lacks it in the mask case. Do not simply stipulate those verdicts; show, for each case, how the two analyses deliver them.

Please give your answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) the two scenarios; (2) what the simple conditional analysis says about each and why; (3) what the basis-retention analysis says about each and why; (4) the single feature of the pair that does the work, and what would happen were that feature removed or the two cases' causal order reversed; (5) how a defender of the simple conditional analysis would best attack the pair, and what that attack still fails to explain.
