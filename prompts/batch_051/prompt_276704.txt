I have been using a term in my own work that I want assessed properly, not politely. I call it explanatory laundering. Start from the familiar idea of computational irreducibility: there are systems — a one-line cellular automaton whose trajectory looks random is the canonical case — for which no shortcut to a later state exists, and the quickest route to the answer is simply to run the system. I take a genuine explanation to earn its keep by compressing: it lets an ideal reasoner reach some aspects of the outcome without replaying the whole history. If that is right, then computational irreducibility is also a limit on explanation: for such systems the only faithful account is a rerun, and a rerun is not yet an explanation. My further claim is that in practice we refuse to accept that limit. We take the rerun and dress it in causal narrative until it yields the subjective feeling of understanding, without yielding any of the inferential savings that made it an explanation. That dressing-up is explanatory laundering. Witnessing a cellular automaton, someone says: "The pattern spreads this way because this cell switched on early and sent a cascade down the line." The sentence is true, but it is the trace read aloud; it gives you nothing you could not have had by watching the run. I suspect much of what passes for explanation in complex systems, macroeconomics, and the social sciences is of this kind — plausible post hoc narration that could not have predicted an untested case and whose apparent insight is an artifact of hindsight. I have settled on two marks of laundered explanation. First, an ideal reasoner cannot use it to predict an untested instance any sooner than by brute simulation; it adds no predictive power beyond what the observed history already supplied. Second, it nevertheless produces the conviction of insight, which is precisely how it survives and spreads. Please reconstruct the proposal in its strongest form and tell me whether it is a real distinction or only a pejorative redescription. Does it already exist under another name? Is compression the right standard for explanation, or does that standard beg the question against my targets? Where does the concept break — I can already see that an account might be illuminating without predicting, by unifying phenomena or picking out real causal joints, and I may be overreaching when I treat every post hoc narrative as laundering. Test the idea against those worries, and give me at least one clear case of laundering on my marks and one case that superficially looks like it but should not count, explaining what the difference is.
