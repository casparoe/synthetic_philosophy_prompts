Since I retired from the lab bench I've been reading my way through decision theory, and last week two people in my reading group had a proper row about money pumps. I kept notes afterwards because I honestly couldnt work out who was right.

Marian set things off by saying that if someone prefers A to B, B to C and C to A, that's straightforwardly irrational. Give her A and she'll pay a penny to trade it for C, because she prefers C to A; then, holding C, she'll pay a penny to get B because she prefers B to C; then she'll pay a penny to get back to A because she prefers A to B. She ends up where she started, three pence down. The mere possibility of such a scheme, Marian said, shows the preferences themselves are broken. Roger said that was too quick: a canny agent sees the cycle coming and simply refuses the final trade, so no money is lost, and you've only shown that a myopic fool gets pumped. Marian came back that refusing the final trade is itself acting against the agent's own current judgement — if she really prefers A to B, turning down a B-to-A swap is rejecting an improvement, so either she keeps the preferences and gets pumped or she acts against them. Roger then said she can refuse the whole scheme at the outset: her pairwise preferences don't commit her to every multi-step sequence someone can construct out of them, and choosing at the level of plans rather than isolated trades is perfectly rational. Marian replied that the bookie needn't ever announce a scheme; he can offer the trades one at a time, and at each step the answer to "is this swap better by your lights?" is yes, so the plan-level refusal just amounts to ignoring an improvement later for no reason except that earlier improvements happened already. Roger answered that the pump only shows greedy, context-free pairwise choice can be exploited, not that the underlying pairwise judgements are irrational; a reflective agent holding those same judgements simply declines to be a sequence-myope. Marian finished by saying that in that case she no longer knows what it means to say the agent still prefers A to B: if a preference can be suspended whenever it would complete a bad cycle, "prefers" has quietly turned into "would sometimes choose," and if preference just is choice then her actual choices would be intransitive and she's back in the bookie's net.

Please referee the whole exchange properly: go through it move by move, give a rough verdict on who has the better of each step, say what each side is systematically missing, and then give your overall judgement about who is more right and what stays unresolved.
