Legal fictions are often characterised by the following features: (1) the law asserts or presupposes a proposition of fact; (2) the proposition is false, or at least is not established by evidence and is not meant to be; (3) this is done knowingly by a court or legislature, so that the assertion cannot be rebutted; and (4) the assertion is conclusive only for a defined purpose, with legal consequences following as if it were true. Now consider a hypothetical statute providing that, while a vehicle is in automated mode, the automated driving system is deemed to be the driver of the vehicle for the purposes of road-traffic offences. The system is not a person and cannot form an intention in the ordinary sense; yet the statute does not pretend that any human was driving, and it exists to assign responsibility for a new technology rather than to avoid an inquiry that could in principle be made. Is the statute a legal fiction in the sense of (1)-(4), or is it a substantive rule cast in the language of fiction? Work through the features, say where they underdetermine the verdict, and indicate whether the example suggests that the criteria themselves need revising.
