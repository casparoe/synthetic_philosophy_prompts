I've agreed to lead a two-hour discussion for a reading group of thoughtful non-specialists — mostly lawyers, engineers, and one retired psychiatrist — on what is usually called Wittgenstein's private language argument. None of them will have read the source text; they will have only a short primer I wrote myself, which states in neutral terms the central idea that a private language would be one whose words refer to the speaker's own immediate sensations and whose meanings could in principle be grasped only by that speaker. I don't want to hand down the conclusion as a doctrine; I want the group to feel the force of the problem and arrive at the argument's shape themselves.

Plan the session's explanatory arc for me, rather than giving me the explanation itself. Which ideas have to be in place before the question of a private language even has a definite sense, and in what order, with what depending on what? What single carrying example should I develop across the whole session — a diarist recording a recurring sensation, a person raised alone naming their pains, a community that keeps its definitions secret, or something else — and what features must that example have, and lack, so that it doesn't beg the question against either side? What misconceptions should I expect from this exact audience — for instance, that the argument is the sociological claim that meaning requires a community's actual agreement, that Wittgenstein denies sensations are real, that the argument depends on verificationism, or that an in-principle-unshareable code is a counterexample — and at which point in the sequence should each be headed off? Tell me what I should openly decline to settle in two hours, and give me one question for the final ten minutes that would show whether they have grasped the structure of the argument rather than merely learned the slogan that a private language is impossible.
