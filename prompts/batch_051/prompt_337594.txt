I've been reading the repugnant-markets debate for years, mostly through market-design and rationalist-adjacent forums rather than academic philosophy, so I may be compressing distinctions I shouldn't. The anti-commodification argument as I understand it — the corruption objection associated with Sandel and the broader commodification critique associated with Radin — runs roughly as follows. Some goods are partly constituted by the norms governing how they may be exchanged. Putting a price on a kidney, even if it increased supply and saved lives, would express a wrong relation to the body, would replace the norm of donation with a norm of sale, and would thereby corrupt something valuable in a way that no amount of post-transplant efficiency gain can register. The argument presumes, fairly explicitly, that the corrupting mechanism runs through price and payment; an unpaid organ economy is supposed to escape the objection.

What keeps nagging me is that the unpaid organ economy no longer consists only of individuals donating to loved ones. Kidney exchange has become a large centralized clearinghouse: an algorithm matches donors and recipients across national pools, and the allocation problem doesn't let the algorithm remain neutral about value. It has to trade blood-type compatibility off against sensitization level, expected graft survival, recipient age, waiting time, travel logistics, and cold ischemia time. Those weights are shadow prices, even though no buyer pays them and no donor receives them: the algorithm declares how many expected graft-years are worth accepting in exchange for a longer transport time or a harder-to-match recipient. A kidney is being treated as a fungible, commensurated unit inside an optimization objective, just not one denominated in cash.

My question is whether the corruption/commodification argument survives this change. Does it still condemn organ purchase but remain silent about algorithmic commensuration, because whatever harms commodification it requires an actual offer of money or an actual choice to sell? Or does the argument now generalize and cut against the clearinghouse itself, on the grounds that the algorithm performs exactly the commensuration the objection targets, only invisibly and without anyone being paid? Or does the whole line of argument simply become inapplicable once no one receives compensation? I'm asking what happens to the philosophical argument, not whether kidney exchange is good policy. One further request: wherever the literature is divided, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present a contested reading of the argument as if it were the consensus.
