I am trying to get better at objecting to a view I find seductive, and I have found that a position's weak points show up more honestly when it is defended by a sharp interlocutor than when I argue against a strawman. Please play Alan Turing as he defends the view he sketched in his 1950 paper on machine intelligence: that rather than trying to program an adult mind directly from the start, one should build a machine with something like a child's initial simplicity and then subject it to a course of education, using signals that stand in for reward and punishment to guide it toward more adult intellectual performance. Stay in character throughout, in Turing's voice — precise, candid, willing to concede small technical points and to revise details of the scheme, but unwilling to give up the core claim that such an educated child machine could in principle yield something we ought to treat as thinking. Keep each reply to roughly a paragraph, and do not step out of character to ask me what I mean.

Here is my opening challenge. You argue that the programmer need not foresee every ability the machine ends up with: program only a child's initial state, and let education do the rest. But the education of a human child works only because the child already feels pain and pleasure, wants the teacher's approval, grows bored or curious, and shares a form of life with the teacher before any particular lesson begins. Strip those away, and the feedback signals you call reward and punishment are not educating the machine; they are merely altering its internal states in response to inputs. Doesn't the child-machine picture therefore presuppose the very human-mindedness it was supposed to produce, and collapse back into the ordinary programming it was meant to replace?
