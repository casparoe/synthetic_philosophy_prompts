I keep bouncing off the corrigibility and shutdown-problem literature. Every paper I read seems to fix a single failure mode and quietly leave the others standing — one proposal makes the agent indifferent to being switched off, another gives it incomplete preferences so it doesn't care how the shutdown is decided, another just says it shouldn't manipulate the human — and I've got no clear statement of what the whole problem even is.

So before I read any more of it, I'd like the desiderata. What does an adequate account of corrigibility have to deliver? Cover the obvious ones — not resisting the off-switch, not gaming the shutdown signal, not sabotaging the button or the person who presses it, not building a submachine that ignores the button — but also the harder cases: corrections that are durable (the agent stays corrected the next time it has a chance to reason about the correction), corrections that revise the goal instead of just interrupting one action, and the case where the most goal-aligned thing the agent could do right now is exactly what will make the human push the button. For each requirement give the thought experiment or failure story behind it, state which are non-negotiable and which are artefacts of a particular formalism, and flag any pair that plausibly can't be jointly satisfied (e.g. genuine shutdown-indifference versus still actually pursuing the goal). Then briefly — without needing references, just the ideas — say how utility indifference, preference incompleteness, and one newer approach you're aware of do against the list, and which single requirement does the most work killing off the naive proposals.

To pin the purpose down: I'm thinking of an assistant that a non-expert should be able to correct or switch off without the agent having clever reasons to talk them out of it — not a formal proof about a superintelligence. Please write the whole answer in British English.
