You are an expert research ethicist specializing in the ethics of randomized controlled trials and in clinical equipoise. I chair an ethics triage subcommittee at a mid-sized hospital system. My two staff members have clinical research backgrounds but no formal training in philosophy or bioethics, and we have about forty minutes per protocol to decide whether a proposed randomized trial raises an ethics problem serious enough to require full-board attention, revision, or rejection. I want you to write us a reusable decision procedure — a checklist in words — grounded in the best current thinking about clinical equipoise, which we can actually run.

The procedure should consist of questions asked in order, with, for each question, a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it must hand the decision to a human because the issue is one of values or judgment rather than fact. It must be usable by people who do not know the theory, and it should not require naming philosophers or schools. But do not water the theory down to something like 'is the trial balanced?'. The substantive distinctions must survive the translation. In particular, the procedure must handle at least these hard distinctions:

- the difference between an individual clinician's own uncertainty about which arm is best and genuine uncertainty or disagreement within the relevant community of competent experts, including what to do when individual investigators are genuinely uncertain but the expert community is not, or vice versa;
- when a placebo arm is permissible even though an effective treatment exists, and when it is not — including trials that use an add-on design, an active control, or an early-escape or rescue rule;
- non-inferiority trials, where the question is whether a new treatment is not unacceptably worse than the standard one within a chosen margin, and how the size and justification of that margin bears on whether community equipoise really obtains;
- response-adaptive designs, in which the randomization ratio shifts toward whichever arm is performing better as data accumulate, and whether a design that is already shifting allocation has thereby undermined the community equipoise that was supposed to justify randomizing people at all.

Then run the finished procedure on the following three protocols:

1. A placebo-controlled trial of a new antidepressant for moderate major depression. Two SSRIs are approved first-line treatments, but many patients discontinue them because of sexual side effects. The protocol includes a six-week placebo run-in, offers no rescue medication, and has an eight-week primary endpoint.

2. A response-adaptive trial comparing two surgical techniques for severe secondary peritonitis. The adaptive rule can move the allocation ratio to 90:10 on the basis of interim survival data, and the only stopping rule is for overwhelming efficacy.

3. A non-inferiority trial of a much cheaper generic oral anticoagulant against a branded one for stroke prevention in atrial fibrillation. The chosen non-inferiority margin allows an absolute increase in stroke risk of 1.5 percentage points, and the investigators justify the margin mainly by savings to the health system's drug budget.

For each case, show where the procedure yields a clear answer and where it hands off to judgment. At least one of the worked applications must produce an answer I will not like — the procedure must be willing to tell my committee to block or substantially revise a design we would otherwise be tempted to approve.

Finally, state plainly what the procedure cannot do: which decisions are unavoidably judgment calls, which positions the procedure cannot cleanly separate, and where a conscientious clinician's individual refusal to enroll or to treat should take over even if the committee approves.

Before you write the procedure itself, brainstorm briefly: list the candidate tests you considered, which distinction each tracks, and why you kept or rejected each. Then present the procedure, then the three worked applications. Do not include citations, footnotes, or a bibliography; the reasoning must stand on its own.
