I have a standing policy for moral questions I cannot settle by principle or by asking the people affected. I try to take up the standpoint of an impartial spectator: someone well informed about the circumstances and with no stake in the outcome, whose emotional responses—unease, resentment, approval—I would normally take seriously. I deliberately avoid asking what an omniscient or ideal observer would think; I ask what a real, decent, disinterested person in my own social setting would feel, and I try to reach that verdict by sympathetically entering into the feelings of everyone involved rather than by applying abstract rules from above. When the imagined verdict goes against my inclination, I treat that as a reason to refrain, even if I cannot yet articulate the reason. I lean on this most when the stakes are modest and I suspect I am rationalising. Is this a defensible way of forming moral beliefs, or am I laundering my own biases and my social circle's conventions through an invented judge? Under what conditions would such a jury-of-one spectator actually track the judgment I would reach if I were not self-interested, and when should I distrust it?
