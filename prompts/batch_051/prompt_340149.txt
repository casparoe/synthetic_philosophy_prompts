A friend and I recently spent an evening arguing about whether science can be rational during a scientific revolution, and his whole position rested on one analogy: a scientific revolution is a political revolution under another name. On his version, the reigning paradigm is not falsified point by point; it is overthrown, and only after the overthrow do the new winners determine what counted as evidence, anomaly, or a decisive test. From this he concluded that Popper's account, on which revolution is simply ordinary conjecture and falsification made more visible, cannot be right, and that Kuhn's paradigm change just is regime change carried out by scientific means. I want the analogy audited rather than merely restated. Lay out the mapping as a correspondence table: what plays the role of the old regime, the legitimacy crisis, the revolutionaries, the seizure of power, and the history written by the victors. Then identify where the comparison strains or breaks, and which features of the political source it quietly imports that scientific practice may lack, or the reverse. Finally, give a verdict on the argument that prompted all this: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the anti-Popper conclusion survive, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? If it fails in that use, say what the analogy still captures about the rationality of scientific revolutions.
