I write novels, and I've come round to thinking epiphenomenalism is probably true, which is awkward because my whole craft is built on denying it. The physical world is causally closed: every physical event has sufficient physical causes, and adding a mental shove from consciousness looks like double-counting, or worse, like a violation of the conservation of energy. So felt experience ends up as the steam whistle on a locomotive — produced by the engine, doing no work of its own. But my firm intuition, the one I rely on to get to the end of a chapter, is that the felt quality of being a person is what drives a person: a character smells wet earth, something shifts in her, and then she burns the letter. In my own case, it feels as if the ache of a feeling is what makes me reach for the right sentance, not just the neural traffic beneath it. And here is the thing I can't get past. If feelings have no causal powers, then my writing the words 'I have feelings' cannot be because I have them — the belief that I am conscious is not caused by my being conscious. I know the standard reply, that my belief is caused by the brain state that accompanies the feeling and can still reliably track it, so the belief isn't a coincidence. But then I don't see what the feeling itself is doing for my judgment — the justification seems to run entirely through the brain state, and I'm stuck again. It seems to me epiphenomenalism can't have it both ways: either the feeling matters to why I believe I'm conscious, which sounds like it's doing causal work, or it doesn't, and my belief about my inner life is about something that plays no role in my having it. I don't know which side should yield. Is the intuition just a folk story to be debunked, or is this self-undermining worry strong enough to force a retreat from epiphenomenalism, or is there a reading where conscious life stays causally inert and yet my judgments about it are still trustworthy? Walk me through what each option costs and what would justify it.
