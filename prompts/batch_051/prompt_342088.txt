My colleague has been ruling on a stack of succession hypotheticals for an article she is writing about the ethics of designated survivors, and I am trying to predict how she will rule next. The setting is always a constitutional democracy with a written line of succession, and the catastrophe is always the same: the president, the vice president, and nearly the entire cabinet are killed in an attack.

Her rulings so far:

1. The officially designated survivor — the cabinet minister deliberately kept away from the event, who becomes the highest-ranking survivor in the written line of succession — takes office the same evening, is recognized that week by the surviving high court, and governs under the standing emergency provisions until elections are held on the constitutional timetable. Verdict: legitimate.

2. The speaker of the assembly, fourth in the line of succession, who was abroad during the attack and had never been sworn in, returns and takes the oath before a municipal judge, restores order, and holds elections on schedule; the surviving upper house later approves her interim acts. Verdict: legitimate.

3. A junior minister with no place in the line of succession and no applicable fallback clause is by chance the most senior executive officer left alive. She keeps the police and civil service running, announces a concrete timetable for new elections, and is widely obeyed; the surviving provincial governors issue a joint statement recognizing her interim government retroactively. Verdict: legitimate.

4. A general outside the chain of command takes control, suspends the constitution, preserves civil order for two years, and only stages elections under foreign pressure. Verdict: not legitimate.

5. The ceremonial head of state, who under the constitution has no governing powers, announces that she is assuming emergency leadership. No surviving constitutional body endorses her, her decrees are ignored outside the capital, and local councils keep order on their own. Verdict: not legitimate.

6. A designated survivor who is the highest surviving name in the line of succession cancels the constitutionally required elections, saying stability must come first, governs by decree for three years, and remains popular; no surviving national body ever meets to ratify or reject her acts. Verdict: not legitimate.

Next week's case is this. A deputy minister who is, under the constitution, automatically acting president once every name above her is unavailable — though she was abroad and never sworn in — returns, takes the oath before a municipal judge, takes charge, runs the government effectively, and schedules elections for nine months out. The only surviving constitutional body, a rump senate, votes to withhold recognition of her authority, citing the form of her oath and her failure to consult it before issuing emergency decrees. The public and the civil service nonetheless comply with her orders and generally describe her as the legitimate leader.

My question is what she will say about the new case and why, and in particular which principle is really sorting her six rulings. Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Across those five items: identify the principle she seems to be applying and the other principles that fit her six rulings just as well; say what each candidate principle predicts about the new case; give your best prediction with the closest earlier case as analog; and specify a further case that would most cleanly separate the principle that is actually doing the work.
