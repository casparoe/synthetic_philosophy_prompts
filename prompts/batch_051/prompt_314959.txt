You are an expert in Kantian ethics and early modern moral philosophy. I am a novelist drafting a short philosophical essay, and I would like your substantive comments on its line of argument before I write the full piece. The working title is Telling the Truth by Indirection: Why Kant’s Murderer Case Is Misanalyzed.

My central claim is that Kant’s essay On a Supposed Right to Lie from Benevolent Motives is usually framed as a contest between truthfulness and harm prevention, but that framing overlooks a distinction I care about as a novelist: the difference between asserting a falsehood and allowing another person to draw a false conclusion from true statements. I suspect Kant’s duty of truthfulness is best reconstructed not as a duty of propositional accuracy but as a duty not to exempt oneself from the public use of one’s communicative capacities. That, I think, is why Kant denies that the murderer at the door has a right to a lie rather than a right to silence, evasion, or reserved speech.

My notes argue three things. First, the murderer case is under-described, since a novelist can construct the same fatal outcome through nothing but true sentences arranged to mislead. Second, if Kantian truthfulness includes all foreseeable implications of one’s speech, it collapses into an impossible communicative liability; but if it excludes implications, its rigorism is much more limited than usually supposed. Third, Kant’s argument that a lie makes the speaker responsible for unforeseen consequences actually cuts more sharply against withheld warnings than against false statements.

Before you evaluate, please take time to reconstruct the Kantian argument as you understand it. Then give me comments on the following: whether the three-layer distinction between assertion, implication, and reserve is already present in Kant; whether my second claim unfairly narrows Kant’s notion of a statement; and whether this idea is worth developing for philosophers or remains too parochial to fiction. Be direct about where the essay would need more exegesis.
