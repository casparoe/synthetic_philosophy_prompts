During a reading group on classical East Asian philosophy we spent a session on the sixteenth-century Korean debate over the Four Beginnings and the Seven Emotions. The Four Beginnings — commiseration, shame and dislike, deference, and the sense of right and wrong — are treated by Mencius as unlearned and morally good, evidence that human nature is good. The Seven Emotions — joy, anger, sorrow, fear, love, hatred, and desire — are the ordinary passions, which can plainly go either way. The puzzle driving the debate is why, if both kinds of feeling arise from one and the same human nature, the Four never seem to go wrong while the Seven often do. Yi Hwang (Toegye) proposed a division: the Four are issued by principle (li) with material force (qi) following; the Seven are issued by qi with principle riding on it. A classmate defending Toegye kept glossing that last phrase through a horse-and-rider picture: qi is the horse, supplying motion, vitality, and inclination; li is the rider, supplying direction, order, and authority. On this picture, the rider is not the horse's own power, so when the rider is in charge we get the pure moral sprouts, and when the horse has its own impetus we get the unruly passions. Yi I (Yulgok) answered that li is not the sort of thing that can ride, steer, or issue anything: only qi acts, and li is just the pattern according to which it acts.

I would like you to audit this analogy as an argumentative metaphor. Set out the correspondence table — the horse, the rider, forward motion, steering, temperament, being thrown, and any further correspondences the picture suggests — and then mark where the mapping breaks. Keep the failures that matter for Toegye's conclusion, that the Four and the Seven have two different sources with one of them putting principle in charge, separate from the failures that are only cosmetic. What does the rider image quietly import from our experience of two separate things, one of which controls the other, that li and qi do not actually have? And once the disanalogies are on the table, does Toegye's two-source conclusion survive, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? If some other analogy would do less damage — a vessel and its shape, a wave and the water, a program and its execution — say which and why.
