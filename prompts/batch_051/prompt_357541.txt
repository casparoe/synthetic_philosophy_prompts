I'm a PhD student in political philosophy working on the ethics of voting, and before I read yet another paper that argues from a single case—the third-party voter in a safe district, the person who stays home knowing their candidate will lose, the reluctant lesser-of-two-evils voter—I want the full specification those arguments are supposed to answer to. Write, as precisely as you can, the desiderata that any adequate theory of the ethics of voting must meet: the cases it has to classify plausibly, the distinctions it cannot collapse, and the structural facts about elections and individual agency it cannot ignore. For each requirement, give the consideration or case that motivates it, and mark which requirements are non-negotiable constraints on adequacy and which are parochial commitments of particular camps (for example, an insistence that a vote be sincere, or that only causal consequences matter). Feel free to fix the purpose of the theory, since adequacy is relative to what a theory is for—but say what you are fixing it for.

Some ground I want covered, though do not let this foreclose the list: the fact that a single vote almost never decides a large election, alongside the fact that some elections really are decided by hundreds of votes, and what these two facts together demand of any defense of a duty to vote; the difference between whether one should vote at all and how one should vote given that one votes; the difference between causing an outcome, being complicit in it, and expressing support for it; the distinction between an individual duty falling on each eligible voter severally and a collective duty falling on the electorate, and whether a theory may slide between the two; the secret ballot and whether one owes others a justification for how one votes; and whether the theory must be sensitive to institutional features—plurality rule, compulsory voting, safe seats, referendums—or can be stated in abstraction from them. I would also like you to say whether any two desiderata on your list are jointly unsatisfiable or in deep tension. My suspicion is that a robust duty to vote and a hard-headed acknowledgement of individual causal inefficacy will be difficult to secure together without shifting ground, and that expressive and harm-minimizing demands pull apart precisely in the lesser-evil cases where the decision matters most—but I want to know if that is right, and which tensions are genuine rather than merely apparent.

Finally, briefly score the main families of view—instrumental or expected-utility, expressive, civic-republican or duty-based, virtue-based, and complicity-based—against the list, but keep that section short; a table is fine. The requirements are the deliverable, and the scoring is only there to show the list doing work. Keep the response under 1,200 words.
