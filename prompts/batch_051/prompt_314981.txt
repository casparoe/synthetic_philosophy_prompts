I have recently joined the working group charged with writing the decision principles for a care-coordination assistant deployed by a regional health authority. The system allocates scarce home-visit slots, decides whom to remind about preventive screenings, and, in a few defined emergencies, may alert clinicians to a risk a patient poses to themselves or others. The group is divided between what the engineers call the utilitarian faction and the deontological faction, and I have been asked to bring the disagreement to a point. I do not find my own responses in either camp.

My provisional rule has been this: the system should honor the commitments it has made to users, should never use a person merely as a means, and, where those constraints do not settle the question, should choose the action with the greatest expected benefit. My verdicts on the following cases keep pulling away from that rule.

1. The last home-visit slot of the week. Patient A is an 83-year-old recovering from a hip replacement and would regain modest mobility from the visit; Patient B is 40 and would avoid an otherwise likely hospital readmission. I think the system should give the slot to B, and I would support removing the clinician's discretion so that sympathy for A cannot override the allocation. This feels like straightforward benefit-maximizing.

2. The intake form tells users that what they share is used only to plan their care and will not be shared outside the care team. The system then records a patient saying that they have stockpiled medication and plan to end their life next Tuesday. I think the system should break the confidentiality commitment and alert the care team, despite the promise made at intake.

3. The system could substantially increase uptake of a diabetes-prevention program by telling prediabetic users that nearly everyone who completes the program avoids diabetes. The true effect is a modest reduction in progression. I think the system must not say this, even though the misleading message would produce better health outcomes than the accurate one.

4. During a severe medication shortage, the system must decide whether the remaining doses go to one high-risk patient who is likely to die without them or to five patients at moderate risk of serious complications. I think this decision should be made by a rule set in advance, with no on-the-spot weighing of the identified life against the five. I also do not think the rule is required to save the five; I would accept a rule that gives the last doses to the one high-risk patient if that is the rule the health authority adopted openly.

5. The system can tailor reminders to a user's reading level, and this improves engagement. It can also tailor them to the same user's grief, having learned that she responds best to messages that mention her late husband, and this improves engagement further. I think the first tailoring is fine and the second is not, though both exploit features of the user to produce better outcomes.

6. A user has opted out of having her records used for any purpose beyond her own direct care. The system notices that she qualifies for a subsidy she would clearly want and cannot find. I think the system should respect the opt-out and not notify her, even though the benefit to her would be large and she would almost certainly consent if asked.

7. In an influenza surge, the system implements a triage protocol that deprioritizes older patients for intensive care in favor of younger patients with longer expected life. I accept the protocol if it was written in advance and made public. I would not accept the identical outcomes if a clinician or the system produced them by an all-things-considered judgment about the particular patients in front of it.

I cannot articulate what separates case 1 from case 3 or case 5. In all three, the system acts to produce a larger expected benefit; yet I regard the first as clearly right and the third and fifth as clearly wrong. Allocation versus manipulation is the nearest I can get, but that distinction seems merely to label my reaction.

Is there a single principle — or a small, well-ordered set of principles — that captures these verdicts? If not, which verdicts should I drop, and what is the best case for dropping each? Please state the resulting principle precisely, identify the case or cases that motivate each clause, tell me what it implies about two cases I have not listed — (a) a risk-score calibration that knowingly disadvantages a small group of high-need patients to help a much larger group, and (b) a system that gives false reassurance to keep a patient calm before a procedure with a small risk — and say which of my stated verdicts it cannot accommodate. Finally, tell me whether the resulting architecture should be described as utilitarian, deontological, or something else, and where that label misleads.
