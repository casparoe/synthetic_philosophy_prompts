Design a fifty-minute classroom game for a seminar of about twenty students on AI safety or the philosophy of mind. The game should make participants feel, before the concept is named, the underdetermination of inferring an optimizer's objective and its search space from observed behavior alone.

Set it up as follows. A player or small team acts as a hidden optimizer: they possess a secret scoring rule over a finite set of available options and a menu of options that changes from round to round, representing the search space. Their only job is to pick, each round, the option available to them that maximizes their hidden score. The rest of the class acts as interpreters, seeing only the choices and, in some rounds, the menu of options that was available, and must announce what they think is being optimized and over what space.

Build in at least two designed reveals. First, after the class confidently announces a unique inferred objective, reveal that a different objective paired with a different search space produced exactly the same sequence of choices, so that their inference was underdetermined by behavior even though it looked decisive. Second, reveal that a choice everyone read as a preference was actually a constraint: the optimizer wanted something else entirely but could not reach it, which the available menu and not the scoring rule explained. Include an incentive scheme that keeps the hidden optimizer faithful without real money, and keep all materials to paper, index cards, markers, and tokens. End with a debrief that maps concrete moments in the game onto the formal distinctions between objective and search space, revealed preference, reward underdetermination — many scoring rules producing the same observed choices — and satisficing versus maximizing. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time the game is run and how to handle it, and be explicit about which features of the real problem the activity cannot reproduce — high-dimensional or continuous behavior, stochastic policies, optimizers that modify their own search space — and must therefore be supplied by the facilitator.
