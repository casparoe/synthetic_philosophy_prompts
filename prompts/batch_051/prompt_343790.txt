I've been chewing on this argument about grammar and it's starting to bother me, because I can't find the flaw but the conclusion seems wrong. Here it is as best I can state it.

(1) A grammatical rule like "past tense = add -ed" is basically a pattern covering the regular verbs, with the exceptions (sing/sang, go/went) listed off to the side.

(2) Which words count as regular and which as exceptions is a matter of history, not of anything in the language right now. Most verbs got funneled into the -ed pattern over centuries; go/went survived because its common enough that children memorize it before they can "fix" it (suppletion, I think its called, where one word's forms come from different roots).

(3) So the line between a rule and an exception is just frequency plus history, a sliding scale, not a real split. Same with sound change: a pronunciation change that sweeps through most words but misses a few still gets called regular, and the misses get filed as exceptions.

(4) If that's right, a grammar has no rules at all, just a mental list of forms ranked by how likely they are: walk-walked high, sing-sang lower, go-went special but still on the list.

(5) But that can't be right. A kid who says "goed" is making a mistake, not just using a lower-rank form, and if I invent a verb, say "to zorp", I know right away its past tense is "zorped", before I've heard it or looked at the list.

(6) So there has to be a real difference between a rule and an exception, and one of the earlier steps is wrong.

Where does this go off the rails? If I have to pick a claim to hang onto, I'd keep that a new verb gets a past tense by a rule, not by a vote, but I want to know what that costs. Answer as exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no introduction or conclusion: which step is weak, why it looked fine, and what each way out costs.
