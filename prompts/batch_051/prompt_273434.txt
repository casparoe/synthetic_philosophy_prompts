I have spent the past year defending a version of the higher-order theory of consciousness: the view that a mental state is phenomenally conscious only if it is the object of a suitable higher-order representation, a state that represents that the subject is in that first-order state. My committee is split. The sympathetic members value the way the view explains why conscious states are precisely the ones we can report, reflect on, and use in deliberation; the skeptical members favor some version of a first-order theory on which consciousness is not a matter of being represented by another state but of the intrinsic profile of the first-order state itself, such as its strength, its recurrence, its integration, or its availability to executive systems. Their standing challenge is that the higher-order theory looks unfalsifiable: the only evidence we ever accept that a subject is conscious of a stimulus is a report, a confidence judgment, a visibility rating, or a wager, and all of these already presuppose the higher-order access whose absence is supposed to explain unconscious perception. On their telling, the experimental game is rigged from the start.

The claim I want to put to the test is the higher-order necessity claim: phenomenal consciousness of a first-order state requires a higher-order representation of that state, and first-order processing on its own is never sufficient. Please design the best empirical strategy you can for separating this claim from the strongest first-order alternative, not a strawman, and be explicit about the logic of the crucial comparison: what exactly is manipulated or measured, in what task, in what population, and which pattern of results would favor the higher-order claim rather than the first-order alternative, and which would do the reverse. If the design implicitly tests one variant of the necessity claim rather than another — for example, that the higher-order state must actually occur, as opposed to the first-order state merely being available to higher-order systems — say which variant is being tested and whether the two can be pulled apart at all.

Then give me the sober debrief: what a clean positive result would establish, what a clean negative result would establish, and what both would leave entirely open. In particular, confront the skeptics' worry directly. Is it merely a measurement limitation that every behavioral indicator of consciousness seems to load the dice in favor of higher-order access, or does that fact reveal that the necessity claim is not a purely empirical hypothesis at all, so that some residue of the dispute must always be settled by conceptual argument rather than experiment?
