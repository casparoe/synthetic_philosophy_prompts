I teach a seminar on machine ethics, and an engineer in the room keeps making a deflationary argument I don't have a good reply to. She says the difference between an AI that "reasons as a deontologist" and one that "reasons as a utilitarian" is just the shape of the loss function: a deontological AI is a utility maximizer with very steep but finite penalties attached to certain acts, like lying or deliberately killing an innocent. So the talk about side constraints and absolute prohibitions is, on her view, either confused or window dressing. I suspect this is too quick, but I'm also not sure the distinction is the sort of thing that could ever show up in behavior rather than in a design doc.

Suppose we have a language-model agent trained to answer moral dilemmas, and the company says it "reasons as a deontologist" because it refuses to lie and refuses to kill one to save five. Design the best test you can of the underlying claim: that the system is actually applying something like a side constraint, which vetoes certain acts regardless of expected consequences, instead of maximizing expected utility with a steep but finite penalty. Be concrete: what cases would you probe, what dose-response or robustness checks would you run, and which outcomes would count as evidence for a genuine constraint versus a steep trade-off. Then say what a positive result would and would not settle. What I most want to know is whether these two hypotheses could be behaviorally equivalent in principle—and if they could be, does that mean the engineer is right that the dispute is empty, or is she just changing the subject?
