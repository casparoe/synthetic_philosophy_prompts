I cover science and ideas, and I keep coming back to this sentence from Robin Hanson's 1998 essay "The Great Filter – Are We Almost Past It?": "The Great Silence must force us to revise a standard view in one or more area of biology, astronomy, physics, or the social sciences." Its a deadpan line, but I think there's a poem hiding in it. Write a sonnet that makes the reader feel the force of that sentence. The octave should build the confident standard story: chemistry to replicators to cells to sex to multicellularity to tool-using minds, each step looking like it more or less had to happen. The volta should be the dead sky arriving: the speaker realizes one of those steps — or one of the quiet assumptions borrowed from physics or social science — has to be wrong, but not which one. The closing couplet should say the filter's cruel inversion outright: the easier the past, the bleaker the future. Keep the reasoning precise enough that someone who knows the Great Filter literature recognizes it, not just the vibe.
