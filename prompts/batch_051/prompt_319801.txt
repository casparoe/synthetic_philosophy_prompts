For a piece I am working on, I have been reading Locke's account of Molyneux's problem and thinking about the way it has resurfaced in an unexpected place. In Book II, Chapter IX, Section 8 of An Essay Concerning Human Understanding (second edition, 1694), John Locke quotes William Molyneux's problem as follows:

"Suppose a Man born blind, and now adult, and taught by his touch to distinguish between a Cube, and a Sphere of the same metal, and nighly of the same bigness, so as to tell, when he felt one and t’other; which is the Cube, which the Sphere. Suppose then the Cube and Sphere placed on a Table, and the Blind Man to be made to see. Quaere, Whether by his sight, before he touch’d them, he could now distinguish, and tell, which is the Globe, which the Cube."

I cover ideas for a general audience, and I keep noticing what seems to me like a structural parallel between this thought experiment and a live question in multimodal machine learning. In the modern case, researchers often ask whether a model that has been trained only on language about shapes can, when connected to a vision module without joint training, classify a cube and a sphere by sight. Both cases ask whether a concept acquired through one informational channel is available to a newly connected channel, or whether cross-modal use requires paired experience. Molyneux's subject has touch and is given sight; the language model has text and is given vision. Both sides involve a representation that must be redeployed in a different format.

I cannot decide whether this is a deep isomorphism or a loose analogy. Please give me a committed answer: either defend the parallel as structurally deep or argue that it breaks, and in doing so do more than list similarities. State the mapping explicitly—what on the machine-learning side corresponds to the blind man, the tactile concept, the cube, the sphere, the table, and the first visual encounter—then say what would have to be true of both sides for a result to transfer. Name at least one thing the parallel predicts that you think is actually false.
