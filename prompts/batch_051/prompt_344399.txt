I'm a science journalist on the ideas desk of a weekly magazine. I cover cognitive science, neuroscience, and the occasional robotics paper, and I make a call like this every few days: should I describe a lab's claim with words like "representation," "internal model," "computing," or "belief," or with words like "coupled," "dynamical," "skillful," or "history-dependent"? Some researchers are very deliberate about this, some just reach for the fanciest term, and my editors just want a headline. I honestly can't always tell whether the distinction is a big deal or a stylistic tic. The background debate, as I understand it, is roughly representationalism versus enactivism and embodied cognition: does the mind store and manipulate stand-ins for the world, or is it better seen as an ongoing, history-laden coupling with the world?

What I want is a reusable decision procedure — a checklist, or a flowhart in words — that I can run in under an hour before filing, and that my editor can apply when they sign off. The procedure should tell me, for a specific claim in a study, whether it's genuinely representationalist, how much that should matter to how I describe the work, and when a paper can be described either way with no observable difference (in which case I'll just pick the plainer language). It should be usable by a reporter with no philosophy-of-mind background, grounded in what's actually known about when a state counts as a representation, and it should flag the steps where I'm really making a judgment call rather than applying a rule.

Then apply the procedure to three cases. (a) A robotics paper says a visually guided robot "maintains an internal model of the room" and "updates its beliefs" as it moves; the methods show it's a deep network tuned end-to-end, with no explicit map layer and no belief variable. (b) An embodied-cognition group studying cricket escape says the cricket "does not represent the predator's location; it maintains a posture-dependent sensorimotor relation and the escape emerges from the coupling," but their data are just as well predicted by a model that stores a vector pointing toward the predator. (c) A neuroscience paper says a population of cells "represents place" because its firing correlates with the animal's location, but there's no manipulation showing the rest of the brain uses that activity as a stand-in for location. For each case, what should I say in the piece, and why? Include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer I won't like, and say plainly where the procedure can't settle things.
