I got dragged into a fight in my town over whether we can cap short-term rentals in residential zones, and one guy at the council meeting kept saying housing policy is "too big" for a town and that the state or the feds ought to be doing it. I ended up reading Alexander Hamilton just so I'd have something better than "towns know their own streets," and in Federalist No. 17 he says:

"The administration of private justice between the citizens of the same State, the supervision of agriculture and of other concerns of a similar nature, all those things, in short, which are proper to be provided for by local legislation, can never be desirable cares of a general jurisdiction."

That was basically my instinct until I started testing it against my own gut on a pile of actual cases, and now I can't state what principle I actually hold. I'd have said I'm a subsidiarity person before I wrote these out, but right now it just looks like "whatever I happen to like." Could you tell me what, if anything, unifies my verdicts, which of them has to go, and what the resulting principle says about a case I'm still flipping on? I want each clause of the principle tied to one of the numbered cases so I can see what's doing the work.

Here are my gut calls, with "fine" meaning legitimately decided at that level:

1. My town capping short-term rentals in residential zones — fine, that should be our call.
2. California setting tailpipe standards stricter than the federal EPA floor — fine; states should be able to go further than Washington.
3. Texas setting tailpipe standards looser than the federal floor — not fine.
4. A German Land refusing to take its share of refugees under the federal distribution formula — not fine. A Land volunteering to take extra — fine.
5. A Swiss canton setting the language of instruction in its schools — fine.
6. A Swiss commune setting its own local tax multiplier — fine.
7. The EU standardizing phone charging ports — fine; that's what a single market is for.
8. Congress using highway money to push states into a 21 drinking age — fine, though it feels a bit like blackmail.
9. A city setting a minimum wage above its state's — fine.
10. A state preempting sanctuary cities, i.e., forbidding cities from limiting how much local police cooperate with ICE — not fine; a city's own police priorities should be its business.

The pair that bothers me is 2 and 3 against 10. With emissions I'm fine with a federal floor that California can go above but Texas can't go under. But when the state imposes its own enforcement "floor" on cities about immigration, I call it overreach. The obvious difference is that air pollution physically crosses borders and immigration enforcement is local police work—but immigration is federal too, so isn't there a spillover there as well? Maybe my sanctuary verdict is the one that has to give. Or maybe the principle is something like: higher levels get floors, not ceilings—which makes me right on 2 and 3 but maybe wrong on 10. I keep tying myself in knots.

So: is there one principle that gets all or nearly all of these? If you have to sacrifice a verdict or two, which should they be? And apply the principle to the one I keep going back and forth on: should my state be allowed to set a uniform school-closure threshold for the next bad flu season (close schools once county hospitalizations hit some number), or does that have to stay district-by-district?
