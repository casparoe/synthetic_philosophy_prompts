When I sat in on a colleague's philosophy of mind seminar, she kept returning to a fourfold classification that she credited to Daniel Dennett: Darwinian creatures, whose behavioral possibilities are fixed by natural selection across generations; Skinnerian creatures, which revise their behavior through reinforcement within a single lifetime; Popperian creatures, which can let candidate actions die in an internal model rather than in the world; and Gregorian creatures, which think by means of language and other transmitted mind-tools. I have been keeping a list of her rulings, because I suspect she is applying a stricter and more interesting principle than the four labels alone suggest.

Her assignments so far are these. A bacterium is Darwinian and nothing more. A garden snail and a honeybee are each Darwinian and Skinnerian, but she denied that either was Popperian, even though she granted that a bee colony solves problems no individual bee could solve; her comment was that solving a problem in the world is not the same as modeling a solution before acting. A rat, a border collie, and a New Caledonian crow are each Darwinian, Skinnerian, and Popperian, but not Gregorian. A chimpanzee was, in her accounting, a near miss for Gregorian, but still not there. Only a human adult has received all four labels. She declined to classify the thermostat in the seminar room at all, and when a student asked whether the rungs are cumulative or whether a creature simply belongs to its highest rung, she said only that the four labels pick out different kinds of cognitive work, not boxes in a filing cabinet.

Next week she has promised to rule on an octopus, a two-month-old human infant, and a large language model, and I want to predict her verdicts before we walk in. What rule or rules would make her earlier assignments cohere, what does each candidate rule say about the three new cases, which rule would you bet she is running, and what further case would do the most to separate the remaining candidates? I am less interested in the verdicts themselves than in the principle behind them.
