I'm a retired chemist and I've recently been reading Schopenhauer's The World as Will and Idea, mostly the Haldane and Kemp translation. I'm aware I might be overcomplicating this, but I want a sharp distinction between two things he runs together near the end of Book Four: the temporary silencing of the will in aesthetic contemplation, and what he calls the denial of the will to life. My problem is that I can't settle a borderline case.

Here are the criteria I've extracted from that part of the book (I'm paraphrasing from memory, so if I've got the emphasis wrong, tell me): (i) the will is quieted by knowledge of the nature of suffering, not by mere exhaustion or dulling; (ii) it is the will itself that is turned and abolisht, not just one motive being swapped for another; (iii) the state should show up as a settled resignation—poverty, chastity, fasting, acceptance of whatever happens—rather than a passing aesthetic mood; (iv) suicide doesn't count, becuase according to Schopenhauer it is a violent affirmation of the will against particular circumstances.

The case: a monastic meditator with no philosophical training has, after decades of attention practice, stopped caring about food, sex, reputation, comfort, or the future. He seems genuinely equanimous, not depressed or suppressed. But he arrived there by following the breath and repeatedly letting go of preferences, not by grasping that the will is the thing-in-itself or that suffering is built into willing. If you asked him why he doesn't mind cold or hunger, he would say something about impermanence and non-attachment, not about the will denying itself.

Does this qualify as denial of the will in Schopenhauer's sense? My criteria say no, because condition (i) fails. But that feels too intellectualist, and maybe it reveals that my criterion (i) is really about my own prejudice for insight over technique. Which of the criteria would you revise, and is there a reading of Schopenhauer where this meditator counts as a genuine case of denial?
