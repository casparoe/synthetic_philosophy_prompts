I teach an upper-division philosophy of perception seminar, and for the unit on cognitive penetration I want a diagnostic instrument that sorts students by what they say about cases rather than by which label they find most flattering. By the end of the unit they can all define modularism, attention-mediated penetration, direct penetration, and the deflationary view that most alleged cases are really effects on judgment, memory, or response bias rather than on perception itself; what I cannot tell is which position each student actually occupies.

Please write a ten-question sorting quiz titled Which View of Cognitive Penetration Do You Actually Hold? Each item should present a concrete vignette — a hiker judging a hill's slant while wearing a heavy backpack; the Müller-Lyer arrows still looking unequal when the viewer knows the two lines match; a subject briefly primed with a banana outline before viewing an ambiguous figure; a snake-fearful observer and a non-fearful observer deciding whether a partly shadowed garden hose is a snake; a radiologist whose diagnostic expectation changes whether a faint shadow looks like a nodule or a smudge; a batter on a hot streak reporting that the ball looks larger. The vignettes should be described neutrally, as one condition versus another, without committing to any empirical finding. Each set of answer options should commit the respondent on the distinctions that actually separate the positions: whether the effect is in visual experience itself or only in the subsequent report or judgment; whether the effect persists despite explicit knowledge; whether the effect can be fully explained by differences in attention, fixation, stimulus selection, or memory; and whether explaining the effect requires a direct causal route from belief, desire, or emotion to the construction of the experience. No item should transparently ask which theory the respondent accepts.

Then provide a scoring key that maps patterns of answers onto the named positions: strict impenetrability or modularism, indirect or attention-mediated penetration, direct penetration, and the deflationary view. Build the key from the logical space of answers rather than from the labels: note any answer-combinations that correspond to no defended position, and say briefly whether each is unoccupied because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely under-explored. Also flag any pair of positions the quiz cannot cleanly separate, and explain where a better test would have to do more work. Assume the students know the standard debate but no specialist psychology, and define technical terms inline when a vignette requires them.
