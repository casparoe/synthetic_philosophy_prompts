Analyze the trial of the major German war criminals at Nuremberg in 1945 and 1946 as a case study in the fairness of binding people to rules they could not have known. The tribunal tried individuals for crimes against peace, chiefly planning or waging aggressive war, and crimes against humanity, chiefly systematic atrocities against civilian populations; these categories had largely not been promulgated before the war as criminal prohibitions carrying individual responsibility. The defendants' objection was not simply that they personally had been unaware of the rules, but that no rules of this kind existed for them to know. Bring to bear the principle of fair notice, understood as the claim that it is unjust to punish someone for violating a standard whose content and binding force they could not reasonably have known in advance. Work through what that principle would have required the tribunal to do, which features of the episode the principle captures well and which it misses or misdescribes, and what the episode in turn shows about the principle. In particular, consider whether the trial is best read as a justified exception to the principle, as evidence that some standards are knowable through ordinary moral reflection even without prior enactment, or as a sign that fair notice is only one value among others rather than an absolute condition of legitimate punishment. Be disciplined about the mapping: identify who the relevant agent is, say what it would have meant for a defendant to know the rule in this setting, keep separate the ignorance of an existing rule and the absence of a rule to know, specify the options the tribunal actually had, and mark where the principle itself is silent instead of forcing it to speak. Write your answer in continuous prose, as in a letter or essay, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
