I've been trying to make a clean philosophical case against strict liability in the criminal law, and my argument keeps feeling both airtight and somehow too fast. Strict-liability offenses are those in which a conviction does not require proof of the defendant's mental state with respect to at least one element of the offense — for instance, a sales clerk can be convicted for selling alcohol to a minor even if she reasonably checked an ID that turned out to be a good forgery, and in many jurisdictions a reasonable mistake about a sexual partner's age is no defense. It seems wrong to say such a person is being punished for her fault; but I also want to respect the fact that the clerk or the older partner very often was careless or reckless about something, even if not about the precise fact the statute singles out. So I tried to state the argument in a way that captures exactly what objection remains.

(1) Punishment, unlike a tax or a quarantine, is not just an imposition; it expresses a public judgment that the person being punished was at fault.
(2) A public judgment that someone was at fault is justified only if that person really was at fault.
(3) To be at fault, in the sense relevant to criminal punishment, is to have acted with a culpable mental state — intent, knowledge, or at least conscious disregard of a risk — with respect to the wrong-making features of one's conduct.
(4) Strict-liability offenses are defined so that no such mental state needs to be proven with respect to at least one element of the offense; a conviction can be entered even where the incriminating fact was unknowable and the defendant's mistake was entirely reasonable.
(5) Therefore strict-liability offenses authorize the state to issue a public judgment of fault without the factual predicate that would justify such a judgment.
(6) Therefore strict liability in the criminal law is unjustified wherever it does away with mens rea.

When I look over the steps, (3) bothers me most: I suspect the word "fault" may be doing two different jobs, one legal and one moral, and the argument may be trading on the slide between them. But my attempts to fix (3) either make the argument invalid or just reassert the conclusion in other words. Where exactly does the argument go wrong, if it does? If a step fails, say which one and how it should be amended; if no step fails, say so and explain why the conclusion follows despite seeming too strong. Please answer in American English.
