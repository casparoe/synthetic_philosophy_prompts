I've been looking into a commercial service that builds interactive simulations of dead people from their texts, emails, and voice recordings, and I want to understand what philosophical picture its operating rules presuppose. The service is unnamed, so I will describe the relevant policies directly. A living person can voluntarily sign up and contribute material; while alive they may review, revise, or delete everything they have supplied. If someone dies without having opted in, the company will not build a simulation for surviving relatives, no matter how much they request one. If the person did opt in, the simulation is trained only on communications from the final eighteen months of their life. It speaks in the first person, but when asked directly it is required to say that it is a simulation. It is instructed never to express opinions about events after the person's death, and if it does, the model is rolled back to its original state. The deceased's pre-mortem wishes bind the family in one direction: a surviving spouse cannot commission a simulation that the person had refused while alive, and if the deceased set an expiration date for the simulation, the company honors that date even over the family's objection. Yet the binding does not reach the other direction: once the simulation exists, the spouse, parent, or adult child can have it deleted at any time and for any reason. The company's public materials are emphatic that the simulation is not the person, not conscious, and not a bearer of interests; they describe it as a keepsake that belongs to the bereaved.

I cannot tell what theory of persons and death makes this combination coherent. Sometimes the dead overrule the living, as with the opt-out and the expiration date. Sometimes the living overrule the dead, as with deleting an opted-in simulation. And the company simultaneously says the simulation is not the person while treating the deceased's refusal as if it would wrong the deceased to build one against their wishes. What view of personal identity, posthumous interests, or the moral status of the dead does this practice presuppose? Which parts of it are strictly presupposed, and which are merely compatible with several different views? Is the asymmetry between the dead person's control over creation and the family's control over deletion defensible under a single theory, and if so, which one? Finally, select two rival theories — one on which the dead have no interests and the simulation is merely a representation, and one on which the simulation is itself a bearer of interests — and say exactly which of the company's policies would have to change under each.
