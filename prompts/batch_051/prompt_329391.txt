so I'm writing a piece on aesthetic testimony and I've wound up with a set of commitments I can't tell whether they cohere. Roughly:

(1) I'm a pessimist about aesthetic testimony: you can't get a justified belief that a film is beautiful, or come to know it, just by taking a well-calibrated critic's word for it.
(2) In ordinary empirical matters I'm a reliabilist about testimony — a reliable informant's word that the bridge is out is as good as seeing it for yourself.
(3) I also think critics can know that a film is beautiful, because there taste reliably tracks the features that make it beautiful.
(4) I explain the difference in (1) by saying aesthetic appreciation is a kind of know-how aimed at the particular work, and know-how doesn't travel through sentences; you have to build it yourself.
(5) I think it's fine to believe my friend when they tell me a comedy special is funny — that gives me a justified belief that it's funny. I'm just not sure funniness counts as an aesthetic property.
(6) Still, a good critic's verdict is the best evidence I have before seeing a film about whether I'll find it beautiful.
(7) And I'm attracted to response-dependence: for a work to be beautiful just is for suitably placed critics to respond to it with a certain kind of pleasure.

I'm not sure where the pressure points are. Walk me through which pairs actually conflict and which only look that way, order the real conflicts by severity, and say for each what would have to give and what the cheapest fix is.
