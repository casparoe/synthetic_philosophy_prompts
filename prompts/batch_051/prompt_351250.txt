Question:

Deontologists sometimes claim that a type of action can be wrong even when performing that very type of action is the only way to prevent a greater number of performances of that same type of action by others. Explain why this structure has seemed paradoxical to critics, and evaluate the response that the paradox disappears once deontological reasons are understood as agent-relative. In your answer, distinguish a constraint from a goal, and say what, if anything, remains problematic for the deontologist.

Student answer:

"The paradox of deontology is that a deontological constraint forbids, for example, killing one innocent person even when that killing is the only way to stop five other people from being killed. Nozick captures this by saying that moral constraints are side constraints on action rather than goals to be promoted. A constraint does not identify a bad state of affairs that we should minimize, because treating violations as a bad to be minimized would license violating the constraint whenever that would reduce violations of it. The agent-relative reply says that a deontologist's reason not to kill is indexed to her own agency. It is a reason for her not to kill, not a reason to minimize killings wherever they occur. So the paradox is only an artifact of assuming agent-neutral reasons. Once we give up that assumption, the constraint is perfectly coherent: I may not kill one to prevent five killings, and the five killings are attributable to the people who perform them, not to me. The agent-relative structure also explains the doing/allowing distinction and why I may not harvest one healthy person's organs to save five patients. The only residual worry is that agent-relative reasons can seem mysterious, but Kantian and contractualist accounts of duties to others provide adequate grounds for them."
