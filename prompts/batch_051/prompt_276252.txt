In a seminar on strategic reasoning, we played the dollar auction, and a post-game argument has been running ever since. The rules: an auctioneer sells a one-dollar bill to the highest bidder, who pays their final bid and takes the dollar, while the second-highest bidder also pays their final bid and receives nothing. With ten-cent minimum raises, two of my classmates ran the price well past a dollar.

Callum, one of the bidders, dismissed the escalation as textbook sunk-cost reasoning. "Once I've bid a dollar seventy," he said, "that money is gone whatever happens next. The only real question is whether the next increment plus the risk of losing is worth the dollar I might win — and it isn't." On his view, continuing past the prize is irrational, and the auction is a purified version of the escalation of commitment people show in failing projects. Priya, the other bidder, disagreed. "You're describing the wrong dilemma," she said. "If I'm the second-highest bidder at a dollar seventy and you're at a dollar eighty, dropping out loses me a dollar seventy for certain; raising to a dollar ninety either loses me ninety cents if you then stop, or puts me back in the same dilemma at a higher figure if you raise again. That's forward-looking expected-loss reasoning, not throwing good money after bad." Callum replied that this is exactly the trap: the same comparison is available at every stage, so if it vindicates each individual raise, it also produces the jointly disastrous outcome. A rational person, he insisted, would commit in advance to a hard stopping rule rather than let the auction's arithmetic push each bid upward. Priya answered that a stopping rule may be prudent self-management, but it does not settle what it is rational to do once you are already at the node; in a one-shot auction, only the expected value of the options available now matters, and an earlier plan has no independent normative force. She added that real-world escalation of commitment is usually powered by reputational and group pressures that were absent in the game, so the auction shows that escalation itself is not automatically a bias.

They asked me to referee, and I couldn't. Please adjudicate point by point: who is right about which specific claims, whether anything in their shared framing is itself mistaken, and who is more right overall.
