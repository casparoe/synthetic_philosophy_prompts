I'm a philosophy PhD student working on value theory and population ethics, and I also sit as the graduate student member on my university's IACUC — the committee that has to sign off on any research using animals. We meet once a month and review maybe thirty protocols, each of which has a pain category box checked and a two-sentence justification for why the expected benefit is worth the harm. In practice the committee argues from gut feeling about whether a study is "worth it," and the result depends a lot on who shows up that month and what species is involved.

I've been trying to think through what negative utilitarianism, and specifically a lexical-threshold version of it, would look like as an actual working procedure. The idea as I understand it: below some intensity of suffering, pain and benefits can be traded off on a single scale; above that threshold the suffering is lexically prior, so no amount of expected benefit counts — not a Nature paper, not a therapy, nothing. There's also the further question of whether harms to an animal and benefits to humans (or future patients) are commensurable at all, or whether we're fooling ourselves by putting "a rat's hour of fear" and "a hundred citations" on the same spreadsheet.

Could you write me a reusable decision procedure the committee could actually run to decide whether a protocol should be approved? It should be a list of questions in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, where it hands the decision back to a person because its a value judgment rather than a fact, and where it permits an explicit trade-off. The people using it are mostly biologists and vets, not philosophers, and it has to work in about ten minutes per protocol. Then run the procedure on three protocols: (a) tail-vein injections in mice for a modest but real study of a new antibiotic; (b) a cancer study where mice are allowed to grow tumors until they're in visible, unrelieved pain, for a genuinely high-impact result; and (c) a study causing moderate but transient pain to dogs in order to test a treatment for chronic pain in dogs. I'd like at least one case where the procedure says no to something the committee would probably have approved, plus an honest statement of which steps the procedure can't decide for us.
