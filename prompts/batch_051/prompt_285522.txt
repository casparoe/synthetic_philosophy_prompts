I've been going down a causality rabbit hole at work, because we're trying to answer questions like what would have happened to retention if we'd changed the onboarding flow, instead of just saying users who did X churned less. A colleague pointed me to Pearl's causal hierarchy, and as I understand it the argument is: there are three rungs — association, intervention, counterfactual — and each step up requires stronger assumptions. Observational data alone can't even get you to the intervention rung; the do-calculus is the machinery for moving between associations and interventions once you've already committed to a causal graph; and for full counterfactuals you need the structural equations themselves, not just the graph. So the claim seems to be that something outside the data — a structural causal model — has to supply the causal assumptions, otherwise you're stuck on the bottom rung.

But then I started thinking about LLMs. No one hands an LLM a structural causal model for the domain you're asking about, and during training it wasn't estimating do-probabilities or being given a graph for, I don't know, churn or supply chains. Yet it'll happily answer intervention and counterfactual questions and often sound like it knows what would of happened. So now I'm unsure whether Pearl's argument survives that development, whether it needs a repair, or whether the model just isn't doing the thing Pearl said required an SCM. Which premise is actually under pressure here? And is there a weaker version of the claim that still holds — and if so, what exactly does it say and what does it give up? I care more about the fate of the argument than about whether LLMs are good at causal reasoning.
