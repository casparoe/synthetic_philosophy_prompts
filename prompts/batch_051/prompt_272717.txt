I've been chewing on something from a course on international criminal law and I'd like your take. In ordinary domestic systems we treat ex post facto criminal law as about the clearest violation of the rule of law there is: a person is supposed to be on notice of what's prohibited before they act, and a legislature that tried to ban something retroactively would get struck down immediately. Yet most of us, including plenty of people who would never countenance retroactive punishment at home, think the Nuremberg and Tokyo tribunals after the Second World War were broadly just or at least defensible. But look at what they actually did: they convicted people of crimes against peace and crimes against humanity, categories that were either nonexistent or nowhere near settled law when the acts were committed, and the defendants were in the dock because their side lost. So the very principle we treat as nonnegotiable in our own courts gets suspended exactly when the accused are most hated and the evidence against them is at its strongest. Tokyo is if anything less comfortable, since there wasn't a single atrocity on the scale of the Holocaust to do the moral work, and the whole affair looked to a lot of contempories like the winners punishing the losers.

Is there something that justifies this asymmetry, or is it just victors' justice with better tailoring? If you defend it, defend it against this nearby case: after a civil war or revolution, the winning side enacts a statute criminalizing acts by the losing side that were legal when done — starting the war, ordering reprisals, running detention camps under then-existing laws — and then tries the losers' leaders under it. If the rule of law principle matters there, why didn't it matter at Nuremberg? And if it doesn't matter there, what is left of the principle except that it applies to the defendants we feel bad for? I'm genuinely unsure where you could draw the line that isn't just: the worse the defendant, the less the rules apply.
