I am a student in a seminar on formal epistemology and epistemic game theory, and we have spent the last several weeks on the common prior assumption. Roughly, the assumption says that any difference between two agents' probability assignments should ultimately be traceable to a difference in the information available to them, rather than to a brute difference in the priors they start from; formally, it amounts to the idea that each agent's posterior is obtained by conditioning a single shared prior on that agent's private information.

The seminar has divided into two camps that keep talking past one another. The first defends the common prior assumption as a substantive constraint of rationality. This camp leans on Aumann's agreement theorem: if two agents share a prior and their posterior probabilities for some event become common knowledge, then those posterior probabilities must coincide. For this camp, a disagreement that survives full disclosure of beliefs is a sign that at least one side has reasoned from an arbitrary starting point, and abandoning the common prior would make persistent disagreement too easy to rationalize. The second camp treats priors as subjective degrees of belief. On their view, Bayesian rationality requires probabilistic coherence and updating on evidence, but it does not single out one admissible prior; the common prior is at best a modeling convention, Aumann's theorem is a conditional whose antecedent need not hold, and no non-question-begging argument can get from rationality alone to an identical prior.

I have been asked to draft the document these two camps would produce if they agreed to an adversarial collaboration, and I would like you to write it. Please include: (1) a section of substantive claims both sides can sign, covering what the formalism requires, what the theorem establishes, and what it does not; (2) the residual disagreements, stated exactly and in wording each side would accept as its own; (3) the cruxes, the sub-questions on which the dispute actually turns; and (4) for each crux, a concrete case or argument that both sides agree bears on it, together with each side's prediction recorded in advance. I would also like you to flag any part of the dispute that turns out to be merely verbal. My own suspicion is that one crux will concern whether a non-arbitrary shared prior can be specified without sneaking in contentious assumptions, and another will concern whether the common prior assumption has the kind of empirical content its defenders claim — but I am happy to be corrected if the resulting statement exposes different cruxes.
