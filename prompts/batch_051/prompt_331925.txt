I keep going back and forth on what feels like a basic question: does printing money create anything real? The theory I was taught says no. Wealth is goods and services and productive capacity; money is just the chalkboard we keep score on. Printing more units of score can't make more actual stuff — at best it redistributes, at worst it debases the units and you get inflation, and if it worked, macro policy would be a free lunch. I find this view compelling because it's so tidy, and it seems to explain Weimar, Zimbabwe, and why central banks can't just buy everyone a house.

But I can't shake the intuition that its false in exactly the cases that matter. In 2008 and 2020, when financial plumbing locked up, central banks created enormous amounts of money and it looked like it saved real things — firms kept workers on, homes weren't fire-sold, output didn't collapse. If creating claims lets idle factories reopen and unemployed people work, hasn't the world ended up with more stuff than would otherwise exist? Liquidity feels like a real good when markets freeze, not just a relabeling.

I don't want a flat 'it depends.' I want to know which side gives way. Is my intuition a scope mistake, taking what's true for one agent with a busted balance sheet and smuggling it up to the whole economy? Or is the money-is-a-veil idea a long-run simplification that fails in exactly the short-run world where printing does create real output? Or is there a reading where both are true, and if so, which distinction does the work — flow vs stock, inside vs outside money, levels vs growth? Walk me through what each concession costs.
