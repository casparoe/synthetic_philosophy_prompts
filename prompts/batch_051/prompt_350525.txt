I cover science and ideas for a living, which means I keep having to write about citizens' assemblies, sortition (picking people by lottery), term limits and the case against career politicians without ever having sorted out what I actually think. So I wrote down my gut calls on a bunch of concrete arrangements, and now I want to know whether there's a principle behind them or whether I'm just a mess.

Here they are, roughly as they came to me:

- A criminal jury picked by lot from the electoral roll: fine. Actually I'd be more uneasy if juries were elected.
- A citizens' assembly picked by lot that advises the council on climate policy: fine, no problem.
- A citizens' assembly picked by lot that can pass the local budget outright: I hesitate. I think I'd want an elected body to sign off on it.
- A national parliament chosen entirely by lot: no. I cant get there. It feels like nobody is answerable for anything.
- A president limited to two terms: fine, maybe even good.
- A law capping MPs at four terms: I'd probably vote against it, but I'd also feel a little relief when a long-serving incumbent finally goes.
- A recall election that lets voters sack a mayor mid-term: good, more accountability, though I worry about rich donors abusing it.
- An independent commission appointing the committee that sets interest rates, insulated from voters: fine. I don't want voters deciding interest rates.
- A hereditary monarch with only ceremonial powers: fine, wierd but harmless. Give that monarch a real veto and I'd say not fine.
- A trade union general secretary re-elected unopposed for 25 years: something feels off, but if the members keep voting for them I can't say they're wrong.
- A rule barring anyone over 75 from standing for parliament: wrong, ageist. But I do worry about cognitive decline.
- A city that picks its budget committee by lot but every decision has to be ratified in a referendum: I actually like this a lot. Maybe that's the sweet spot.

The best I can do by way of a principle is something like: elections are for choosing people to be answerable, lotteries are for getting a representative sample to deliberate, and appointment is for expertise. But that doesn't explain why I like recall elections, or why term limits feel different for presidents and MPs, or why a lottery is fine for juries but not for a parliament.

So: is there a principle, or a small set of them, that gets all or nearly all of these? State it precisely, run it through my cases, tell me which of my gut calls it forces me to drop, and then apply it to the one I keep going back and forth on: should a citizens' assembly be allowed to draft a constitutional amendment that goes straight to a referendum, bypassing the elected legislature?

And could you write the answer in British English? House style.
