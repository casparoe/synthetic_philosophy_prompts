I cover the science of consciousness, and I have been trying to settle a question about whether a classic argument in the field has been quietly overtaken by a change in how the field collects its evidence.

The classic argument concerns the colour phi phenomenon: a red spot is flashed, and then, after a short interval, a green spot is flashed nearby. Observers report seeing a single spot move across the gap and change colour midway, as if the green of the second flash had been available before that flash could have been processed. Two rival stories suggest themselves. In the Orwellian story, the subject first has a conscious experience of the red spot alone, and the arrival of the green spot then causes the record of what was experienced to be revised after the fact; memory is rewritten. In the Stalinesque story, consciousness is held up until all the relevant information has arrived, and the subject is then presented with a single, already edited episode; there never was a conscious red-only state to revise. The standard philosophical argument, made most forcefully by Daniel Dennett, is that this contrast cannot be settled by any possible evidence, because both stories are built to produce exactly the same retrospective report. Any finding that seems to favour one can be absorbed by the other; the appearance of a substantive dispute, on that view, is an artefact of assuming that consciousness happens at some single, privileged time and place in the brain.

Now the development. Over the past couple of decades, consciousness researchers have increasingly turned to no-report paradigms: they attempt to determine whether and when a stimulus was seen without asking subjects to say anything after the fact, using behavioural proxies such as eye movements or pupil changes, or neural decoding, to track the unfolding representation of a stimulus on a single trial. The explicit motive is that spoken report mixes perception with memory, decision, and motor output, and may hand the brain exactly the latitude that the Orwellian story exploits. These methods appear to offer what the older debate was said to lack: a real-time, trial-by-trial trace of the representational trajectory, independent of the subject's later account of it.

I am writing a piece on this, and I want a sharp judgement rather than a survey. Does the Dennett argument survive the shift to no-report evidence, or does it need repair, or is it dead? Which premise exactly is under pressure? Would a repaired version resemble the original claim, or would it be something weaker, such as the claim that no-report measures can track subpersonal drafts but cannot by themselves tell us which draft was conscious? I would like the analysis to say whether the Orwellian and Stalinesque stories become empirically separable once report is no longer the only witness, or whether the philosophical difficulty simply moves from the report to the interpretation of the no-report signal.
