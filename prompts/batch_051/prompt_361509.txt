I am a novelist, and I have been drafting a novel set in two communities whose moral frameworks are almost perfectly opposed. The writing has made a relativist account of morality more attractive to me than it ever was in the abstract: I now find myself thinking that moral judgements are correct or incorrect only relative to a framework of practices, attachments, and concerns, and that there is no neutral, framework-transcendent standpoint from which one framework can be judged rationally superior to another. The view earns its keep for me in three ways. It explains why my characters, each reasoning scrupulously from inside their own world, reach opposite verdicts about a single act without either of them committing a simple factual error. It explains how intelligent, sincere, fully informed people can disagree intractably about matters such as honour, chastity, and the claims of kin. And it is metaphysically economical: it does not posit a realm of moral facts for judgements to answer to, only patterns of living and the standards internal to them.

Yet I also have an intuition I cannot shake, and the book will not let me set it aside. In the central scene, a character arranges the marriage of a girl of twelve to an older man, in settlement of a debt, over the girl's explicit and articulate protests. I find that I cannot accept that this is wrong only relative to a framework. I cannot make myself believe that a way of life in which this is permissible is merely different rather than worse. The difficulty is not confined to my opinions; it has invaded the prose. When I try to write the scene in a flat, ethnographic register, the draft condemns the act anyway. The narrator lingers on the girl's fear rather than on the family's reasoning; the verbs carry an evaluative weight I did not deliberately give them; the comic irony I grant to other characters is withheld from the man who arranges the match. I set out to write with relativist discipline, and the narration keeps taking sides.

The obvious reply is that I am mistaking my own enculturation for moral insight, and that I have engineered the scene to make condemnation feel inescapable by giving the girl an interior life and the man none. I can see that. But I am not satisfied by it, because the intuition survives my attempts to neutralise it, and because the prose's resistance feels less like a habit and more like a constraint: the novel, as a form, seems to demand a verdict that the theory says is unavailable.

I am not asking to be told which side wins. I want to know what would have to be true for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what would have to be true for the theory to be the thing that goes, and whether there is a reading on which both survive — for example, a way of saying that narrative voice may condemn from within one framework, in full knowledge that it is doing so, without claiming a neutral standpoint above all frameworks.

Please structure the reply in five titled sections, in this order:

1. "The case for relativism" (about 250 words): give the strongest version of the view and state what the world would have to be like for it to be correct.
2. "The case for the intuition" (about 250 words): give the strongest version of the conviction that this act is wrong full stop, including what the prose's resistance might be tracking.
3. "Where the tension really lies" (about 150 words): locate the exact point of conflict rather than the broad clash between theory and sentiment.
4. "Three ways out" (about 300 words): explain what would justify debunking the intuition, what would justify amending or abandoning the theory, and what a successful compatibilist reading would need to establish.
5. "My verdict" (about 200 words): state which move I should favour and on what grounds.
