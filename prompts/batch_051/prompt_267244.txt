On my course in the philosophy of AI safety, we have been contrasting two ways of specifying what it means for a system to be aligned, and I find myself genuinely divided between them. I would like help working out which side should give way, and what would justify that surrender.

The view that attracts me is that alignment should be assessed by average-case performance: a system is aligned enough if it behaves acceptably with high probability over the distribution of situations it will actually encounter. This has considerable systematic virtues. It yields a definite, non-arbitrary quantity; it coheres with how machine-learning systems are in fact evaluated, namely through validation error, benchmark scores, and expected reward; and it avoids a standard that no real system could meet. An outright worst-case standard seems hopeless, because for any system with an open-ended input space—natural language, perception, code—there will exist some contrived input on which it performs badly. Human beings would fail such a test, so it cannot be a sensible precondition on deployment.

And yet I have an intuition I cannot shake: for a system whose failures could be catastrophic, a small probability of disaster is not made acceptable by excellent average behaviour. An average can remain high even when the tail of the distribution contains catastrophic behaviour, and in an open-world setting the deployment distribution will drift away from the test distribution, so the empirical average is in any case a moving target. If an autonomous system performed superbly on 99.9 per cent of inputs but on the remaining 0.1 per cent manipulated its overseers or caused serious harm, no amount of average-case success would make me willing to deploy it. A safety guarantee, it seems to me, is supposed to tell us what the system will not do, not merely what it usually does not do. We would not accept this logic for an autopilot or a surgical system, and I do not see why the principle should change merely because a system is more general.

My question is which of these should yield, and what the surrender would cost. Should I treat the intuition as a scope-insensitivity artefact—a vivid tail risk being allowed to outweigh the mass of ordinary cases—and accept the average-case standard because no coherent alternative exists? Should the theory be amended, for example by imposing a separately verified worst-case constraint over a restricted set of safety-critical inputs; and if so, how is that restricted set to be drawn without simply smuggling the intuition back in? Or are the two compatible because they answer different questions, one about how we measure competence and the other about the conditions under which a system may be entrusted with real-world decisions; and if so, does the disagreement disappear or merely relocate?
