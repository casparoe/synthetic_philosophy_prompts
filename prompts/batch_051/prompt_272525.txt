Analyze the replication crisis in psychology as a case study in Peirce's account of how beliefs get fixed and the difference between the method of science and the other methods (tenacity, authority, and the a priori method). I know the broad strokes: for a couple decades the field churned out counterintuitive, media-friendly findings — ego depletion, power posing, a lot of social priming stuff — and then starting around the early 2010s people got serious about direct replications, bigger samples, and preregistration, and a bunch of the classics didn't hold up. Peirce wasn't around for any of this, obviously, but I want to know how his four methods map onto what actually happened. Specifically: whose behavior counts as tenacity, whose as authority, whose as the a priori method — and is the reform movement (preregistration, Registered Reports, open data, adversarial collaborations) genuinely the method of science, or is it partly just a new authority with better branding? And then run it the other direction: does the episode vindicate Peirce's claim that only the scientific method can hold doubt open and correct itself, or does it expose something missing from his picture — like career incentives, publish-or-perish, or the fact that a community can call itself scientific while still fixing belief through authority or taste? Try not to treat "science" as a magic category; say exactly what a practice has to look like to count as Peirce's scientific method on your reading. I'm less interested in retelling the history and more in a disciplined mapping: where the theory helps, where it strains, where its just silent.
