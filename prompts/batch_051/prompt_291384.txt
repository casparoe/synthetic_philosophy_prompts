Here's a asymmetry I keep noticing in how people talk about constitutional power. When a governor acts outside the letter of the law in a sudden emergency — pulling down a house to stop a fire, freezing a bank during a run — everyone nod and says that's what prerogative is for. But when a constitution simply runs out (a head of state dies and the rule of succession is ambiguous, or an election produces no winner), someone who steps in 'just until order returns' and starts doing roughly the same kind of thing gets called a usurper, even when the result is as obviously good. Both are acting without a determinate legal warrant; both appeal to public necessity and to the mismatch between what the law provides and what the situation requires. I want to know whether the two cases can actually be told apart, or whether the only real difference is that the first person already holds an office whose authority is recognized and the second doesn't — which might just push the question back a step.

Locke gives the standard defense of the first sort of case, in the Second Treatise of Government, Chapter XIV ('Of Prerogative'): 'for since many accidents may happen, wherein a strict and rigid observation of the laws may do harm; (as not to pull down an innocent man's house to stop the fire, when the next to it is burning) and a man may come sometimes within the reach of the law, which makes no distinction of persons, by an action that may deserve reward and pardon; 'tis fit the ruler should have a power, in many cases, to mitigate the severity of the law, and pardon some offenders: for the end of government being the preservation of all, as much as may be, even the guilty are to be spared, where it can prove no prejudice to the innocent.'

Take that passage as the test. Does it, on its own terms, cover the person who fills an interregnum? If it does, the asymmetry looks unjustified; if it doesn't, I want to know exactly which part of Locke's reasoning excludes them — and whether that part is doing any real work or just assuming the thing to be shown.
