I've been mulling over whether moral agency can come apart from the ability to act, and I keep going in circles. Suppose we built a system that was excellent at moral judgment—it could examine a situation and tell you what ought to be done, with plausible reasons—but it had no way to intervene in the world, or everything it said was routinely overridden by a human operator. Would it be a moral agent? My first instinct is no, but I can't say exactly what it's missing, and maybe it's better described as a partial or derivative kind of agent.

So I want a working definition of "moral agent"—not a survey of the literature, just your best reconstruction of the concept—that I can test against the following cases. It should count a typical human adult as a moral agent, and count a rock, a thermostat, and a pocket calculator as not. It should give a definite answer either way for each of these: a corporation; a chess engine that selects moves but has no moral concepts; a self-driving car that makes morally loaded traffic decisions without grasping why they matter; an AI that forms careful moral judgments but cannot act on them at all; a person with locked-in syndrome who can still communicate judgments through an eye-tracking device; and a dog that can be trained not to bite but presumably doesn't deliberate about right and wrong. Then tell me where the definition strains or gives suprising verdicts, and whether you'd revise the definition or defend the verdict. I'm especially curious whether you end up requiring both judgment and the capacity to act, and whether "capacity to act" should include communicating or only doing something beyond speech.
