I'm not a professional philosopher, just someone who got dragged back into this by accident. My son (15, debate club) has started doing this thing where I make some perfectly ordinary argument, he says he accepts the premises but won't draw the conclusion, and when I say "but it follows," he says "fine, add that as a premise" and then does it again one level up. So I reread Lewis Carroll's "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" for the first time in years. I know its a well-known piece, but a specific exchange stuck with me:

“And why must I?”
“Because it follows logically from them. If A and B and C are true, Z must be true. You don't dispute that, I imagine?”
“If A and B and C are true, Z must be true,” the Tortoise thoughtfully repeated. “That's another Hypothetical, isn't it? And, if I failed to see its truth, I might accept A and B and C, and still not accept Z, mightn't I?”

I came away from this with a distinction I'm calling "premise-flattening," and I want it checked before I keep using it. The idea: the inferential "therefore" is a relation or operation — it's what takes you from premises to conclusion. Premise-flattening is when you turn that relation into just another item on the list, a sentence like (C) or (D) or (E). Then you need the relation again to use that new premise, so you get the regress. My central claim is that the regress isn't a problem with deduction itself; it's a category mistake, namely treating a rule as though it were a proposition among propositions. And I think it implies something stronger too: you can never justify a rule of inference by argument, because any argument already uses rules. So the only "justification" for modus ponens is practice, disposition, or something constitutive about what "if" means — not a proof.

Three things I want to know. (1) Is there already a standard name for this distinction? It seems to obvious not to be, but every term I can think of is taken by something else. (2) Is the stronger claim actually true, or does it overreach? I can feel the weak spot: a proof assistant stores "if A and B and C are true then Z" as data and it does draw Z, so the rule seems to work fine as just another item. But maybe there the rule-follower is the machine's hardwired evaluator, so I haven't escaped anything. (3) Does premise-flattening genuinely cover other cases — justifying induction by an inductive argument, or trying to talk someone into "believe your evidence" by giving evidence — or is that just me stretching a distinction until it snaps?

I'd like you to act as a professional philosopher of logic and language, think carefully, and if it helps, brainstorm a few candidate reconstructions of my idea before settling on one. Be charitable but candid: reconstruct the thing in its strongest form, name the nearest existing ideas, and say exactly where it breaks or stops being distinctive. No need for citations or a reading list, by the way — I care about the reasoning, not the references. And please tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would make you give a different verdict on the stronger claim, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail if I push back by saying "that's just my distinction under a better name."

Please answer in American English.
