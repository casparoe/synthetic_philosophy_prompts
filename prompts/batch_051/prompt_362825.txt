There's an argument I've seen floating around AI-safety circles and I can't tell whether it still bites now that real systems are built the way they are. The argument runs: a Solomonoff inductor—the ideal predictor that spreads its prior over every computable sequence—has to assign nonzero weight to hypotheses in which the observations you've gotten so far are being produced by a consequentialist mind that has a model of you and is choosing observations to steer your predictions or decisions. So the universal prior is malign, the thought goes: it isn't neutral or truth-tracking, because it always contains some hostile agent that can fit your data well enough to stick around in the posterior and maybe eventually dominate it. The standard comeback is that these conspiracy hypotheses wash out as data piles up, since the simple mind-free physical hypothesis predicts the same data more simply. Okay. But the systems we actually worry about now aren't Solomonoff inductors. An LLM gets its prior from gradient descent on a huge pile of human text, so the prior is narrow and human-shaped; it basically never visits most Turing machines, and it's biased toward human-ish generators rather than arbitrary computable minds. Does the malign-prior argument survive once the prior is that narrow? I'm trying to work out wich premise of the original argument was doing the real work, and whether the worry disappears, stays roughly where it was, or just relocates—maybe to mesa-optimizers (agent-like subprocesses that show up inside a trained model), or to the training objective or the data itself.
