In a passage from Émilie du Châtelet's *Discours sur le bonheur*, she argues that the love of study is the passion most necessary for happiness because it is the only strong passion whose success does not depend on other people: the lover of study can always take up a problem, while the lover or the ambitious person has placed happiness in someone else's hands. I have been reading this as a claim about how human beings actually work, not just as advice, and I want to know whether it can be tested. Design the strongest empirical test you can of the hypothesis that activities whose rewards depend mainly on oneself produce more stable, self-renewing happiness than activities whose rewards depend on other people's cooperation, using the passage's contrast between study, love, and ambition as your guide. Specify what population you would study, what you would measure, what time scale you would use, and what results would count for or against du Châtelet's position. Then explain what even a positive result would fail to settle, such as whether study causes happiness or whether the absence of dependence is doing more work than the activity itself, and whether her word necessary is testable at all.
