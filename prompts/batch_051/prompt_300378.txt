I moved into a new flat in March and inherited a glass dining table from the previous tenant. After a friend of a friend's kid tripped into a coffee table and needed stiches, I got a safety film fitted to both sides of ours. It's become a whole debate between my brother Sam and my flatmate Ed, and I made it worse last weekend by asking whether I should still put the table down as fragile on the insurance.

Sam said obviously yes, it's glass. Ed said don't be daft, there's a film on it, we whacked a chair into it earlier and nothing happened. They went back and forth like this:

Sam: The pane under the film is still fragile. The film just stops that fragility from showing. If someone dropped a heavy cast iron pan on it the glass would crack underneath even if the film held the shards together.

Ed: Then that's not the table being fragile, is it? It's the table being protected. You don't call a phone fragile once the screen protector's on.

Sam: You do, you just say it's protected. The glass still has the disposition to break. The film is a mask — an outside thing that blocks the effect without removing the tendency. A vase wrapped in bubble wrap is still a fragile vase.

Ed: Bubble wrap comes off in a second and gets thrown away. This film is bonded on and meant to stay. It's part of the table now, so the object we're talking about isn't a bare pane, it's a laminate, and a laminate isn't fragile.

Sam: You've quietly swapped which object you're describing. The pane is still in there unchanged, and it's what would crack if the film got cut. The film's tensile strength is doing the work, not the glass's. That's exactly what a mask is.

Ed: So by your logic, if I bolted a steel frame under the whole thing, the glass would still be fragile. But at some point the assembled thing just isn't fragile, and that's the sensible claim about the table sitting in front of us.

Sam: The assembly might be safe, but the glass is fragile. 'Fragile' doesn't mean 'will break no matter what.' There are finkish cases too. Imagine a glass with a curse on it so it turns to plastic a split second before anything hits it. You'd still say it's fragile now, even though 'if struck it breaks' comes out false, because something keeps switching it. So the simple conditional is the wrong test.

Ed: A curse is not your situation. My point is that this film is a permanent part of the thing. When you talk about the table, you talk about the whole table. If the whole thing doesn't crack under ordinary knocks, it's not fragile, whatever you want to say about one layer inside it.

Sam: So if I glue steel plates around an egg, the egg stops being fragile?

Ed: In the everyday sense that matters, yes — the object in front of you isn't fragile. The egg inside would be, if you peeled it, but that's not the thing we're handling.

Sam: So fragility is just a label for the packaging, not a property of the material. Then two identical panes could be fragile and not fragile depending on where someone draws the wrapping. That seems daft.

Ed: Only because you keep sliding between 'the pane' and 'the whole table.' Words track which thing we're talking about. The insurer wants to know about the table.

Sam: The insurer wants to know whether it's likely to break in normal life, fine. But the original question was whether the table is fragile, and you can only answer by changing which object you mean.

They looked at me and I honestly couldn't call it. Please lay out who is right about which specific point, what if anything they're both missing, and who is more right overall. Please write your answer in British English.
