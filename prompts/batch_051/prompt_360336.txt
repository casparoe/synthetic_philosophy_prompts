Most people who would refuse to torture a captive even in the textbook ticking-bomb case are far less troubled by an airstrike that foreseeably kills a dozen noncombatants. Both involve the state's knowingly imposing severe suffering or death on non-consenting people in order to prevent a worse outcome. Is there a defensible principle that keeps these judgments apart, or is our special revulsion at torture an outlier we should either generalize or surrender? If you defend the asymmetry, test your defense against this variation: the captive is tortured to learn the bomb's location precisely so that a planned airstrike, and the civilian deaths it would foreseeably cause, can be called off. If you reject the asymmetry, say which of the two judgments you would move, and why.
