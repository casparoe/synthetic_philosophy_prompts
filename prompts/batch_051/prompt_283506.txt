I've got a habit with contrastive questions in history, the "why X here and not Y there" kind. Like why the industrial revolution started in Britain and not China, or why modern science took off in Europe and not in the Islamic world instead. My policy is to mostly refuse to engage with them as real questions. My line is: you can't rerun history with China in Britain's situation, so any answer is just a narrative the historian chose. The question doesnt pick out a cause, it picks out people who already have a pet explanation and then fit the facts around it. A friend of mine who's doing a history degree keeps pushing back on this. She says the point isn't to rerun history, it's that a contrast case is the only way to check whether your explanation of Britain actually isolates a cause instead of just redescribing what happend. She says answering "why not China" is a test of the explanation you'd give for Britain, not a demand for a counterfactual simulation. Last week we argued about the Needham question and I held the line, but she got under my skin a bit. Is my policy actually defensible, or am I just using "the question is ill-posed" as a shield so I don't have to compare cases? Is there a version of a "why here and not there" question that is well posed, and what would make it different from the ones I keep dismissing?
