I work with nineteenth-century parish records. My rule is that when a group—household servants, unmarried mothers—appears only in records of trouble (arrests, poor relief, church discipline), I treat the surrounding silence as positive evidence about the institution that kept the records: it saw these people chiefly as problems, and that way of seeing is part of what the archive preserves. A colleague says a gap is just a gap and that I am inferring record-keepers' attitudes from missing data, which no archive can license. I reply that the pattern of when a group does and does not appear is itself a record, not an absence. When is my rule defensible, and when should silence be read as mere missing data rather than as testimony about power? Please answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
