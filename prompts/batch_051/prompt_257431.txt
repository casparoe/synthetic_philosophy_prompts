I work at a polling firm, and I am the person who has to defend the generational banners on our cross-tabs. Every cycle we write that Boomers think one thing and Millennials think another, and every cycle I have the same argument with a colleague about whether these labels pick out real groups or are just a periodization we impose and then mistake for reality. I am now convinced the ordinary word "generation" does at least two jobs: it can mean a demographic birth-cohort fact, and it can mean a claim about shared historical formation. What I want is a single working definition of a generation as a real social or historical kind — not a natural kind, and not a mere marketing category — that I can apply the same way each time. I also want the definition to keep three things apart that our reporting constantly bundles together: how old someone is now, the time at which we measure them, and the year they were born.

Here are the cases the definition needs to handle. (1) The U.S. Baby Boom cohort, births roughly 1946–1964; this one should clearly count. (2) The cohort that came of age during the Great Depression and the Second World War; this seems like a generation of a different sort, defined by a shared historical ordeal rather than a demographic bulge, and it should also count. (3) The set of all people born in years divisible by seven; this should clearly not count. (4) Two people born in December 1964 and January 1965, separated by the standard Boomer/Gen X line; I want the definition to say what, if anything, changed for them during those few weeks. (5) The "Xennial" or "Oregon Trail" micro-cohort, roughly 1977–1983, which feels like a real cusp but whose existence suggests the main labels were drawn too cleanly. (6) The "1.5 generation," children who immigrated at school age and grew up across two countries; migration researchers call it a generation, but it is not a birth cohort. (7) "Gen Z" applied globally to births roughly 1997–2012, as if a fifteen-year-old in Nairobi and a fifteen-year-old in Des Moines form one kind despite sharing little of their formative environment. (8) "Gen Alpha," already applied to children born after roughly 2012, before anyone can know what their shared formative period will be. I have settled views only about the first three; the remaining five are the ones I need the definition to genuinely decide, rather than merely re-label.

Please do not turn your answer into a literature review; I want the distinction worked out from the inside, so give no citations. Propose the definition, run it through all eight cases, and be explicit about where the definition surprises you or gives a verdict you suspect I will resist. At the end, tell me how confident you are in the proposal overall and identify the single point you are least sure of.
