I keep coming back to this for my philosophy of technology seminar, and I want the room to work through the implicit theory rather than relitigate the statistics. A bunch of U.S. jurisdictions use pretrial risk-assessment tools (COMPAS being the famous one) to help decide who gets detained before trial. The vendors defend these tools by calibration: at any given risk score, Black and white defendants are rearrested at about the same rate. ProPublica and other critics point instead at the unequal false-positive rates: Black defendants who are never rearrested are more likely to be scored high-risk than white defendants who are never rearrested. Here's the kicker for a philosopher: when the two groups have different base rates, a score generally can't satisfy calibration and also give both groups equal false-positive and false-negative rates, short of perfect prediction (or trivial cases) — so the tool's defenders and its critics are each picking a different horn of an impossibility result. My question is: what theory of fairness does this practice presuppose? Does it strictly commit to the calibration view, or is the practice compatible with rival accounts — equal error rates, equal treatment, something else? Where is it underdetermined, are its parts consistent with eachother, and what concretely would have to change in how these tools are built and used if we swapped in an equal-error-rates view instead?
