Okay so this is a bit personal but I think it has real philosophy under it. My aunt has been in a property dispute for a few months: she spent about twelve years using and tending a corner of a vacant lot next to her house—fencing it in, clearing out the brush, planting vegetables, fixing the drainage, even paying the property taxes when nobody else did—and now the person who actually holds the title showed up and wants to sell. Her lawyer says she might get ownership through adverse possession, which as I understand it says that if you possess land openly, continuously, exclusively, and without the owner's permission for long enough, the original owner's claim just expires and you get title. So the law seems to be saying that sitting on a piece of paper while doing nothing with land loses to actually using and improving it, and that a stranger's labor can generate ownership. I cant stop turning over what picture of property that presupposes. Is it basically Lockean—you mix your labor with a thing and it becomes yours—or is it actually in tension with the Lockean proviso, the idea that you can only appropriate if there's still enough left over of similar quality for other people, since here she's pulling a specific parcel out from under an existing owner? Does the practice presuppose that ownership is grounded in use and improvement rather than title and consent, or could several different theories equally justify it, including a boring one about simply needing a cutoff so old claims can't hang around forever? And is it consistent with how the same legal system treats other property—an untouched bank account doesn't get handed to whoever starts spending from it, as far as I know. What I'm hoping for is this: reconstruct the implicit theory of property the practice is committed to, distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what it's merely compatible with, and say what would have to change if you replaced that underlying picture with the view that ownership only comes from consent and formal transfer.
