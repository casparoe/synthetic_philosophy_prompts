I keep coming across a pattern in debates over modularity of mind that I do not have a stable term for. A researcher proposes that some cognitive capacity—syntax, theory of mind, face recognition, cheater detection—is modular in Fodor’s sense, initially appealing to several signature properties: domain specificity, informational encapsulation, fast and mandatory operation, fixed neural realization, and characteristic breakdown patterns. When experimental evidence challenges one of those properties, the typical move is not to retract the modularity claim but to weaken the criterion at issue: the system is still called modular even though encapsulation is now said to be only partial, or it may be central rather than peripheral, or it need not be innate. Each weakening is individually justified, but across a body of work the result is that module is asked to cover systems sharing only a family resemblance. I know this is related to conceptual change and to the problem of auxiliary hypotheses, but I want to know whether this exact pattern—successive, individually reasonable weakening of a theoretical concept under empirical pressure—has a name in philosophy of cognitive science, and if not, how it should be named and distinguished from ordinary theoretical refinement.
