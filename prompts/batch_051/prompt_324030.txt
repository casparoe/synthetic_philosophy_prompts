I'm a PhD student, and next month I'm giving a paper at a mixed philosophy/theology colloquium defending a broadly Ayerian line on life after death. The thesis: "could consciousness survive the death of the body?" only has content as long as we're covertly picturing a working brain. Once you strip away the neural machinery, words like "experience," "memory," and "identity" loose their grip, so the survival hypothesis isn't quite false—it's a picture we mistake for a factual question. I also lean on Ayer's own near-death episode as a case study: after he choked and his heart stopped for a few minutes, he had a strange experience, wrote it up afterward, and came away unpersuaded that he'd bumped into an afterlife. I argue that's exactly what a materialist should expect, and that the NDE literature is better read as evidence about how a dying brain generates vivid states than as evidence about anything after.

The audience is the problem. Most of them think verificationism died in the 60s and isn't worth reviving. One philosopher of mind works on near-death experiences and keeps saying the flat-EEG veridical-perception reports are not nothing. There's a New Testament scholar who will be friendly but thinks I've defined the resurrection debate out of existence. And an analytic philosopher who is going to hit me with the self-refutation of the verification principle inside five minutes.

Give me a Q&A prep sheet: the objections in roughly the order they'll actually come, with which ones have real force, what I should concede up front vs. hold, a short reply for each that fits a two-minute answer, and a note on where each reply is weakest—plus the question I'm hoping nobody asks. If the framing is confused—say Ayer's actual view is messier than my thesis, or I'm leaning on a verificationism nobody really held—say so plainly rather than politely writing the sheet. I'd rather get the correction than a confident prep sheet for a talk that collapses.
