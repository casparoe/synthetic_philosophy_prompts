I'm a PhD student in political theory, and I keep coming back to the old doux commerce line — the idea that ran through Montesquieu and Smith and a bunch of other eighteenth-century people that trade doesn't just make us richer, it civilizes us: the merchant who wants repeat customers has to be honest, punctual, reliable. Hirschman's typology sets this against the self-destruction thesis, where markets gradually eat away the very moral dispositions they need in order to function. I'd like a proper reading of Glengarry Glen Ross through this debate (the film; I've never actually seen the play staged). The salesmen in that office are drowning in exactly the kind of competition that's supposed to civilize them, and instead they lie to clients, steal the leads, bully a weak-willed buyer into a deal he can't afford, and turn on one another. So is the film a flat rebuttal of the doux commerce thesis? Or is it doing something narrower — showing that a rigged sales contest with sadistic stakes isn't a real market, or that the office is a market with exactly the civilizing parts stripped out? Where does the film leave room for the idea that these men knew they could be decent and chose otherwise, or for the idea that the offstage bosses and the closing-obsessed sales culture do the corrupting rather than the exchange itself? I'm not after plot summary, I've seen it a dozen times, I want the philosophical reading: which scenes would a doux-commerce defender have to explain away, and is there any scene that quietly vindicates them? And if you think the film resists the typology entirely, say what it adds that the Hirschman-style debate leaves out — but also: does the film have a position on whether the market corrupts character, or merely reveales the character that was already there?
