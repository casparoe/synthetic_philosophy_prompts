I'm in an upper-level epistemology of markets course, and last night two of my housemates got into an argument that I ended up having to referee, and I honestly couldn't. We've been doing no-trade theorems in class, so of course I brought them up, but neither of them is in the course and they pushed back in ways I didn't know how to answer. Could you adjudicate the whole thing, move by move? The question was whether you should ever take the other side of a purely speculative trade, like a straight bet on a company's earnings when neither side is hedging anything.

Rachel started with the no-trade point. She said: if two people are rational, share a prior, and are trading only on different opinions, then the fact that the other person wants to trade is itself evidence that you're missing something. So after you update on their willingness, the trade can't look good to both sides. Somebody is the sucker.

Tom said this only works if you know the other person is rational and shares your prior. In a real trade I don't know that. The other persons willingness to trade is noisy evidence about what they know, and updating on noisy evidence doesn't automatically push my valuation below the price. So the theorem can't tell me what to do with an actual counterparty.

Rachel said that's a fair caveat but it dodges the interesting part. The theorem is explicitly about ideal agents with common priors and common knowledge of rationality. What it shows is that under those assumptions, a pure disagreement can't turn into a mutually acceptable trade. So if ordinary people do trade on disagreement, something must be failing: someone's irrational, or they have different priors, or there's some other motive.

Tom came back and said that makes the theorem close to empty as a guide to real markets. People have all sorts of non-informational motives, like liquidity, rebalancing, hedging, or just needing cash, and most of the time you don't know whether the person on the other side is trading on information or on one of those. So the leap from "no trade among ideal agents" to "be suspicious of actual offers" is a heuristic, not a theorem.

Rachel replied that fine, but its a good heuristic. If I think someone is more rational and better informed than I am, I should treat their willingness to take the other side as strong evidence against my view. That doesn't mean never trade; it means update harder on the counterparty's eagerness before deciding I'm the smart one in the room.
