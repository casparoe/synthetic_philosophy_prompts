One thing I have been trying to think through, in preparing a unit on theories of meaning, is what philosophical commitments are built into a practice that is now standard in natural language processing and in the tools my students increasingly rely on. Here is the practice. Words and larger expressions are represented as vectors in a high-dimensional space, and the meaning-relation between two expressions is read off the resulting geometry: two expressions are treated as meaning-similar in proportion to how close their vectors are, standardly measured by the cosine of the angle between them. The vectors are learned from distributional evidence — roughly, expressions that occur in similar linguistic environments are assigned nearby vectors. The geometry also supports arithmetic: subtracting the vector for man from the vector for king and adding the vector for woman lands one near the vector for queen, and this is treated as capturing an analogical relation. The same kind of geometric comparison is used for paraphrase detection and for retrieving relevant passages.

I would like an analysis of what this practice presupposes, if anything, about the nature of meaning. In particular: does it strictly presuppose a use-theory or distributional theory, on which a word's meaning is constituted — and not merely indicated — by its patterns of co-occurrence? Does it presuppose that word meaning is context-invariant; and if so, is the turn to contextualized embeddings, which assign a word different vectors in different sentential contexts, a way of keeping the underlying geometry while abandoning only the invariance, or a deeper break with the picture? Does the success of vector arithmetic presuppose that meanings have a stable componential or algebraic structure, and how does that sit with holism or with the context-sensitivity of ordinary language? The distance measure is symmetric, so it models semantic similarity as symmetric and graded: does that quietly rule out asymmetric meaning-relations such as entailment, dependence, or aboutness, or can those be recovered within the geometry? Please reconstruct the implicit theory of meaning as precisely as you can, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, flag any place where different parts of the practice presuppose inconsistent things, and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — that is, which rival theories of meaning, such as a Fregean theory of sense, an externalist semantics tied to reference, or a conceptual-role semantics, could adopt the same computational practice for different reasons. Finally, say what in the practice would have to change, and what would be lost, if we concluded that meaning is constitutively tied to the extralinguistic world or too context-sensitive to be captured by a single static geometry.
