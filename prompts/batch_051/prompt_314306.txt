You are a political theorist working on the relationship between language, technology, and public discourse. Think carefully, and begin by reconstructing Orwell's argument in your own words, separating it into distinct premises, before you assess whether it survives.

In his 1946 essay Politics and the English Language, Orwell argued that political language tends to decay into prefabricated phrases, worn-out figures of speech, jargon, and euphemism, and that this is not merely an aesthetic failure. The decay matters because language and thought form a loop: evasive political motives produce vague, inflated prose, and vague, inflated prose then makes accurate thought harder, so that indefensible policies can be advanced without anyone being forced to face what is actually being said. Orwell also drew a practical conclusion: individual verbal discipline, with writers choosing concrete words and refusing any phrase they do not fully understand or mean, could at least slow the corruption and make political dishonesty harder to sustain.

Now suppose this argument meets a development Orwell could not have anticipated. Large language models are now used to draft a great deal of institutional and political prose, including press releases, policy documents, public statements, and responses to controversy. These systems are trained to produce highly probable, fluent, conventional continuations and are routinely steered toward hedged, anodyne, audience-tested phrasing; the writer's task shifts from choosing words in the grip of a thought to prompting, selecting, and lightly editing machine output. At the same time, the same systems can be prompted to do the reverse, translating bureaucratic prose into plain, concrete language on demand. The disease Orwell described is now automatable, and so is the cure he recommended.

The question is not about policy but about what happens to Orwell's argument. Does it survive this development, does it need repair, or does it become inapplicable? Identify the premise the development actually bears on: the source of the bad language, the direction of causation between language and thought, or the viability of the individual remedy of verbal discipline. Then state what a repaired version of the argument would claim, and whether it is weaker or stronger than the original. Before you commit, work through at least two conflicting lines of thought, and be explicit about what the repaired argument can no longer say.
