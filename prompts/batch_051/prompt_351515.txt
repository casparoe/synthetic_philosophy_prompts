Substance rather than line edits.

Working title: "The internet meme is a bad mascot for cultural evolution."

The notes so far:

- When Dawkins floated the meme idea some decades back, the bet was that culture could be Darwinian too: little units of culture vary, get copied, and get selected.
- The internet then handed us a very literal version of that: a meme as a captioned image or short clip that spreads by being copied almost exactly.
- I want to argue the irony is that internet memes are maybe the one cultural object that really does replicate with high fidelity, precisely because the artifact is digital and copying is nearly free. And that's what makes them a terrible prototype for culture in general.
- Most culture is not copied the way a jpeg is copied. A joke gets retold differently, a recipe gets tweaked, a song gets misremembered and re-performed. The stable thing across retellings is something more like a pattern, a rule, or a norm than a bitstring.
- So a general theory of culture built on the internet-meme model is generalizing from the one weird case where copying is nearly perfect.
- A better picture might be the cultural-attractor idea associated with Dan Sperber: transmission tends to converge on stable forms because of our perceptual and cognitive biases, not because copies survive the way genes survive. The property that matters might be reconstructability — how likely someone is to infer roughly the same thing from any of its variants — rather than fecundity or fidelity.
- If that's right, Darwinian vocabulary (selection, fitness, lineage) is doing less explanatory work in culture than people often want it to, and the interesting question is where it does and doesn't apply.

Before I pitch, I'd like comments on three things: (a) Is the high-fidelity vs. reconstructive distinction too neat? Internet memes get remixed and templated constantly, so maybe the copying isn't as exact as I'm making out. (b) Does "cultural attractors" do any real work, or is it just a fancier way of saying "people find it catchy"? (c) Is this actually interesting to a general science audience, or is it inside baseball? And where does the strongest objection land?
