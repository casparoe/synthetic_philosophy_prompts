Hey, I'm a software engineer with basically no formal philosophy background, so dont expect precise terminology. I maintain a small forecasting model at work. It takes six inputs and I have a couple hundred rows of real data, so estimating how the inputs depend on each other is off the table. I default to treating them as independent. But this nags at me: independence is not the absence of information about a relationship; it is a very specific joint claim, P(A,B) = P(A)P(B), and there are infinitely many joint distributions consistent with the one-variable rates I can actually estimate. So why is independence the honest default instead of just another arbitrary choice?

I keep hearing five different moves for justifying it, and I want help sorting out which ones actually carry epistemic weight. Here is my attempt to state them:

1. Max entropy: if all you know are the marginal rates, the maximum-entropy joint distribution is the product of the marginals. Independence is what you get when you refuse to invent correlation you have not earned. Any particular dependence would need a story about sign and size.

2. Symmetry: if the input labels carry no information, the only defensible joint distribution should be symmetric under relabeling. But symmetric and independent are not the same thing. A joint where all six inputs always move together is perfectly symmetric but about as dependent as it gets, so this move seems to need an extra step I can't reconstruct.

3. Causal modularity: the inputs are close to independent because of how the system is built. Different teams, different data sources, few shared code paths, few shared failure modes. That is a causal claim about the world, not something the probability axioms hand you for free.

4. Adversarial betting: if you pick any particular non-independent default, you are asserting a direction and a magnitude for a correlation before you have evidence about it, and someone who knows the real dependence can exploit that. Independence at least refuses to choose a side. I have heard this described as a minimax-style argument, though I could not defend that claim myself.

5. Regularization: independence is just a model-shrinking trick. It stops the model from doing something silly with 200 rows, and you compensate with wider error bars. It is a prior in the engineering sense, like L2 regularization, not a claim about reality.

Rank these five from strongest to weakest as epistemic justifications, meaning by how much they give me a reason to believe the independence assumption, not just make my model behave well. If two of them look like the same move to you, you can tie them, but still give each a sentence.

Please organize your answer in exactly these sections, in this order:

1. Ranking. Strongest to weakest, one sentence per item (roughly 10-20 words each) saying why it lands where it does.

2. The cutoff. About 150 words. Which of the five you would count as genuine epistemic justifications, which are just pragmatic hacks, and where the line sits.

3. The hard case. About 100 words. Take the top-ranked item, give the strongest objection to it, and say how much that objection should bother me as an engineer with 200 rows of data.
