I have been thinking about a service that would let people keep talking, for a limited time, with a simulation of someone they have lost. The design I keep coming back to is this. A person must opt in while alive and allow their texts, emails, voice notes, and videos to be collected. After their death, the service builds a conversational simulation from that archive, available only to up to five people the deceased named in advance. The simulation never learns from the conversations: its range of possible responses is fixed when it is created. It must always speak as the deceased, in the first person, and it may never admit that it is a program; but when it is asked about anything that happened after the death, or about anything the archive does not record, it must answer only with some version of "I don't know." Each mourner's access lasts two years, and each mourner may transfer the remainder of their access exactly once, to one person of their choice. At the end of the two years, the simulation plays a final message that the deceased recorded while alive, and then the simulation and the archive are deleted. The estate pays a one-time fee; the company may not sell the data, show advertising, or allow anyone who was not named to query the simulation.

I would like you to think through how this arrangement would actually behave in practice, rather than whether it is a good idea in principle. Walk through who would sign up for it, how its existence would change what people write and record while alive, how mourners would use it and where they would push against its limits, where the company's incentives would drift, and which of the resulting failure modes could be fixed without abandoning the central mechanism.
