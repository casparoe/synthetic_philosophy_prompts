I'm a high school student, and I've been having a recurring argument with a friend about whether the amount of suffering in the world is evidence against God's existence. In those arguments I rely on a personal rule for deciding what counts as evidence, and I want to know whether the rule is actually defensible or just a way of protecting a position I already hold.

Here is the rule. When my friend points to some concrete evil — a child's terminal illness, say — and asks why a good God would permit it, I tell myself that if God exists, the reason he permits that particular evil could easily be something I am not in a position to grasp. The connections between permitted evils and greater goods might be too large, too complicated, or too far in the future for a limited mind like mine to survey. So I treat my inability to think of a good reason as not counting against God at all; in effect, I don't think anyone has to produce a theodicy before setting the argument aside. I apply the rule in the middle of fast conversations, where it works as a shortcut for deciding whether I owe my friend a fuller answer.

But I have realized that I do not run the rule in the other direction. When something looks like an answered prayer, or when I am struck by how orderly and life-supporting the world is, I let that count in God's favor, and I do not first tell myself that if God did not exist, there might be some natural explanation of these things that I am simply not in a position to see. So the rule seems to stop evidence against my view from getting through while evidence for my view passes freely, and I cannot tell whether that asymmetry is something I can justify or just something I am comfortable with.

My question is whether this rule, as I actually use it, is rationally defensible. Is there a real principle about limited knowledge that would justify treating inscrutable evil as no evidence against God while still treating apparent answered prayers or apparent orderliness as evidence for God? Or is the only defensible version one where I apply the same standard on both sides — and if so, would the defensible version force me to give up either the rule or the positive evidence?
