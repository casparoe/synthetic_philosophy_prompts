I've been asked to run a two-hour seminar for a group of PhD students in philosophy of mind and cognitive science who keep encountering the bootstrapping problem and the problem of easy knowledge in papers on perception, and who are confused about why so much epistemology is built around them. They understand the standard vocabulary of knowledge, justification, and deductive closure, and they have strong views about perception from their own work, but they have never worked through the debate over whether perceptual beliefs can be justified without prior, independent evidence that perception is reliable. I won't assign any reading. The point of the session is for them to end up seeing why the problem is hard, not for them to conclude that it is just circular reasoning with extra steps.

Don't explain the bootstrapping problem to me; give me a plan for how to make it land. I need the order of ideas first: what has to be in place before the problem can even be stated, because otherwise the audience will find it either trivial or inexpressible? I suspect immediate, non-inferential perceptual justification has to come first, but I do not know what depends on what after that — the closure step, the transition from first-order propositions about an object to second-order propositions about one's own reliability, the difference between having justification and being able to produce it on demand — and I want the sequence justified by those dependencies, not merely listed. Second, the carrying example: which single case should anchor the session, and what must the case have and what must it lack? I need to know whether to use a red wall seen in ordinary conditions, whether it helps or hinders to bring in a second sense such as reaching out to touch the wall, and whether a memory-based case would be cleaner or only more confusing. Third, the misconceptions to plan around. I expect roughly four. (1) That the subject has simply reasoned in a circle, so there is nothing more to see. (2) That this is just the problem of induction in a new costume, and gets no further than Hume did. (3) That the argument only threatens externalist or reliabilist accounts, so a traditional evidentialist is unaffected. (4) That the flaw is failing to rule out the white-wall-illuminated-red alternative, so a second, independent look would solve it. Are these the right misconceptions, are there others I have missed, and where in the sequence should each be headed off before it hardens? Finally, tell me what to leave out entirely at this level and how to flag what I am leaving out if someone asks, and give me one end-of-session question whose answer would show whether they understood the problem rather than memorized the story. Keep the entire plan under 1,000 words.
