I've come around to illusionism about phenomenal consciousness: the view that there are no phenomenal properties, no subjective what-it-is-like character to experience. On this view introspection systematically misrepresents our sensory and affective states as having ineffable, non-physical feels, when really there are just informational and representational states plus the weird introspective beliefs we form about them. I want you to act as a professional philosopher of mind who knows the illusionism literature and its critics but is willing to be blunt about where the position is exposed.

So: what would have to be true for illusionism to be false? Don't just give me the standard line that it's obviously wrong because we have direct access to experience. I want the defeaters mapped. Start by brainstorming the least convenient observations, introspective reports, and empirical findings for the view, then rank them from fatal to merely awkward. Which of these would committed illusionists (Frankish, Dennett, that crowd) concede count against them, and which would they just re-describe as part of the illusion? In particular, is the familiar objection that the illusion itself would have to be a conscious experience fatal, awkward, or something they have a good answer to? And tell me honestly whether the view has been insulated to point where nothing could count against it—if so, whether that's a bug or a feature for a view of this kind.
