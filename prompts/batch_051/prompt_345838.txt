(1) Suppose the true causal graph G and the observed distribution P satisfy the causal Markov condition: every variable is independent of its non-effects conditional on its direct causes (parents).

(2) Minimality says that no proper subgraph of G satisfies the causal Markov condition for P.

(3) If G is Markov and minimal for P but not faithful, then P contains an extra conditional independence relation, one the graph does not entail via d-separation.

(4) In a linear Gaussian parameterization, any such extra independence corresponds to exact cancellation between paths. For example, with X→Y→Z and X→Z, if the direct effect of X on Z exactly cancels the indirect effect through Y, then X is independent of Z unconditionally, even though the graph doesn't entail that. Minimality still holds because, conditional on Y, X and Z become dependent again, so the edge X→Z can't be deleted.

(5) Exact cancellations require the coefficients to satisfy an algebraic equality, and in a continuous parameter space such equalities have Lebesgue measure zero. So any continuous prior over the free parameters assigns them probability zero.

(6) Therefore, for a Bayesian with a continuous prior over the free parameters, minimality plus the causal Markov condition already gives faithfulness with probability one. Faithfulness as a separate assumption is doing no work.

(7) But here's the problem. The exact same reasoning would also tell a Bayesian engineer to assign probability zero to any exactly maintained equality among real-valued quantities — a chemical equilibrium, a conservation law, a feedback controller holding a setpoint. Yet we do rationally expect those systems, and we design them on purpose.

So the argument seems to prove too much: if (5) and (6) are right, then it's irrational to expect any of these real, engineered systems to behave as they do.
