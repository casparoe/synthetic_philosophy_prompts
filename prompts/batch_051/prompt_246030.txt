For a collection of speculative stories I am writing, each one built around a philosophical problem in the life sciences, I would like you to draft the opening piece: a short story of roughly 1,500 words that dramatizes, rather than explains, the tautology objection to the propensity interpretation of fitness.

Set the story on a remote island research station where a young population geneticist is running a long-term breeding program on tidepool copepods. Her model assigns each experimental lineage a fitness score understood as a probabilistic disposition—an expected number of offspring under a specified range of environmental conditions—rather than a retrospective count of the offspring actually produced. She is increasingly troubled by an objection her former supervisor used to press: that if fitness is simply inferred from which organisms survive and reproduce, then the claim that fitter organisms leave more descendants collapses into an empty circularity, and the familiar slogan used to summarize natural selection ends up saying no more than that the organisms which survive are the ones which survive. She intends to answer this by documenting, in the lives of the animals themselves, a case in which a lineage with a high expected reproductive output is nearly wiped out by chance—a storm rearranging the tidepools, a fouled pump, an infection that strikes before the first clutch hatches—while a lineage with a lower expected output happens to flourish. Her visiting colleague resists from the opposite direction, arguing that an unobservable propensity that can be overwhelmed by luck is no better than an occult virtue. Write the story in close third person; make the distinction between actual reproductive success and expected reproductive success drive the plot rather than sit inside expository dialogue; and do not resolve the debate with a tidy metaphysical answer at the end.
