In the years since I retired from a research career in atmospheric science, I have been making my way through moral philosophy, and I have settled — tentatively — on a view I am now trying to attack. The view is that consequentialist cluelessness is not a merely practical inconvenience but a structural problem for act consequentialism. By cluelessness I mean this. For almost any ordinary action, the consequences that matter most to its assessment are remote: it will affect which people come to exist, which institutions and technologies survive or fail, and what happens centuries downstream, through causal chains no agent could trace. Those remote consequences are not just unknown but unknowable from any epistemic position a real deliberating agent can occupy. Act consequentialism, taken at its word, makes the rightness of an action depend on all these consequences; so it makes rightness depend on facts to which the agent has no access in principle. A theory whose verdicts are inaccessible in this way seems to me unable to guide action, and a moral theory that cannot guide action seems to me to have lost its claim on us.

I want to know what would count against this view. I am not asking for the standard menu of replies; I am asking for a map of my position's exposure. What would have to be true for the worry to be overstated, misplaced, or straightforwardly false? Please sort the considerations from those that would merely force a qualification to those that would make me abandon the view, and for each say whether a practising consequentialist would themselves concede it as counting against the cluelessness worry rather than merely deflecting it. In particular, address: (a) whether distinguishing an objective from an information-relative or subjective ought dissolves the problem, and whether the consequentialist who retreats to the information-relative standard has quietly given up the feature that made the theory distinctive; (b) whether there are decision-theoretic grounds — for example that, averaged correctly over one's ignorance, the expected marginal effect of the unknowable ripples is zero or at least negligible — for legitimately ignoring the remote consequences rather than just pretending they do not exist; (c) whether the argument, if sound, is equally devastating to every rival moral theory, and what follows for my view if the objection is no longer distinctive to consequentialism; and (d) whether my statement of the view is so protected by phrases like "standard form" and "in principle" that no finding could count against it — and if it is, whether that insulation is itself a defect. Given my old habits of thought, the consideration that would move me most would be a demonstration that the long-run effects I am calling unknowable are predictable in exactly the sense that model-based decision-making already relies on, since that is where I am least willing to take "unknowable" on faith.
