I am a senior analyst for a state capital review commission. After direct appeal, every death sentence in the state comes to us; we recommend whether the sentence should stand or be commuted to life without parole, and the governor almost always follows our recommendation. We handle nine to fourteen cases a year. Once a review opens, we have three weeks to produce a written recommendation for seven commissioners — a former prosecutor, a former public defender, a homicide detective, a trauma surgeon, a priest, a victims' rights advocate, and a law professor. None of them is trained in philosophy, and several arrive with firmly fixed positions. By the time a case reaches us the conviction is legally final, so we cannot re-litigate guilt; nevertheless, the commission has always treated residual doubt about actual innocence as a permissible ground for commutation.

Our working principle, which the law professor keeps defending from the capital punishment literature, is that a death sentence should stand only if all three of these are met: the defendant's culpability places the crime among the most aggravated the statute contemplates, making death proportionate to desert; the sentence can be expected to deter at least some capital-eligible killings at the margin; and the chance that we are mistaken about guilt or about the facts bearing on the first two conditions is low enough to tolerate, given that the decision is irreversible. In practice the commissioners apply these ideas inconsistently. The professor tried enforcing a strict order — resolve innocence first, then retribution, then deterrence — but it broke down when several members noticed that a stronger desert showing makes them willing to tolerate a somewhat higher residual risk, and the professor could not say whether that trade-off is part of the principle or a failure of discipline.

Please turn this principle into a reusable decision procedure my staff and I can actually run: a sequence of questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a recommendation to commute, and where it hands off to a human because the step requires a value judgment or an empirical guess the record cannot settle. It must be usable by non-philosophers in no more than a day of discussion per case and defensible in a short public recommendation, and it should mark every point at which it is doing philosophy rather than applying a rule. Then run the procedure on three cases. First: a man with no prior violence killed a store clerk with one shot during a robbery; the only identification came from a customer who saw the shooter's face for two seconds from across the street, forensics show only that the defendant had been in the store that afternoon, and the mitigation packet documents severe childhood abuse and an IQ at the border of intellectual disability. Second: a man tortured and murdered three victims on video, with DNA and a confession; there is no meaningful doubt about guilt and the crime plainly ranks among the most aggravated, but the available evidence on marginal deterrence is equivocal and the state's own expert called the effect plausible but unproven. Third: a twenty-year-old lookout who helped plan a robbery in which a co-defendant shot and killed the store owner; the lookout never entered the building and did not believe anyone would be shot. Make sure at least one of the worked applications yields a recommendation the commission will not like, and end by saying explicitly what the procedure cannot do for us. Begin with the procedure itself, with no preface and no restating of my request; then give the worked applications and the statement of limits.
