I'm trying to get clear on what I actually think about when priors wash out, and whether there's a single principle behind my gut calls. All the cases involve Ann and Bob, who see the same sequence of flips from a coin with unknown bias θ, but start from different priors. Here are my verdicts.

- Ann's prior is uniform over θ in (0,1); Bob's is a Beta(100,100), so he's quite sure the coin is near 0.5. After 5,000 flips, about 40% heads, I say the priors have washed out: their forecasts are basically indistinguishable.

- Bob's prior puts all its mass on θ = 0.5 exactly; Ann's is uniform. The coin is in fact fair, and they watch 5,000 flips. I say they wash out as well, since Ann drifts to 0.5 anyway and Bob is already there.

- Same sharp Bob, but the coin's true bias is 0.6, and they watch 5,000 flips. I say they do not wash out: Ann ends up at about 0.6, Bob stays at 0.5 forever, and Bob is just wrong.

- Both priors are smooth and positive everywhere, but Bob's is a tight spike around 0.5 while Ann's is uniform; they see only 20 flips, 15 heads. I say they've barely washed out. Bob is still much nearer 0.5, though the gap has started to close.

- Ann and Bob have different priors over two theories that make exactly the same predictions for every possible observation. I say their disagreement can't wash out, no matter how much data they see.

- Both have the same smooth prior, but Bob insists on modelling the flips as independent while Ann allows a simple Markov dependence; the true sequence is streaky. I say they end up apart, and Ann is closer to the truth.

Is there a principle—stated precisely, not just as a slogan—that gets all of these, or close? If a clean one forces me to overturn a verdict, which one and why? And what does it say about a case I keep going back and forth on: Ann's prior is uniform over (0,1) except that she rules out a tiny open interval around 0.5, Bob's prior is uniform, the coin is fair, and they see a long run—do they wash out or not?
