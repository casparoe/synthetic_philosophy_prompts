I keep tripping over the way countries remember their worst military disasters, and I want to get clear on what it commits them to. Britain turned the 1940 evacuation at Dunkirk—a retreat, basically, with the army pulled of a beach while under attack—into one of its proudest national stories. Serbia built a national mythology around the Battle of Kosovo, which it lost. The Confederacy lost its war, yet monuments to it went up in huge numbers, and the defeat got reframed as a story about honor and sacrifice rather than failure. Masada, a siege the defenders lost and in the traditional account ended with mass suicide, has functioned as an emblem of national resolve. But plenty of other defeats are simply forgotten or treated as embarrassing, so there is a selection effect and a framing trick at work. My question is what theory of a nation, or of collective identity over time, this practice presupposes. Reconstruct the implicit theory the commemorators are relying on, even if they would not state it that way. In particular: does celebrating certain defeats presuppose that a nation is constituted by shared narratives of suffering and defiance rather than by institutions, territory, or continuity of population? Does it presuppose that some losses reveal character better than victories do, or that there is a distinct kind of moral victory? And what would you have to change in the practice if you held a more institutionalist or demographic view of what a nation is? Finally, mark the places where the practice is philosophically underdetermined—where several different underlying views could justify it—and the places where it really does commit someone to a specific claim. I am a journalist covering ideas, so keep the categories honest but free of jargon.
