I've been trying to work out what makes a portrait of a person tip over into hagiography, and I've settled on a rough checklist. The criterion I use are: (1) the writer suppresses or softens the subject's faults; (2) the subject is presented as someone to be imitated, not understood—an exemplar rather than a particular person; (3) the moral is fixed in advance and the episodes are selected or arranged to serve it; (4) the subject doesn't really develop, or their bad actions get reframed as virtues in disguise.

Here's the case I can't decide about. A writer is finishing a biography of a twentieth-century teacher who was, almost everyone agrees, an extraordinary and devoted educator, but who also falsified results to keep his school funded and was cold towards his own children. The writer knows both things. She is upfront that her method is Plutarchan: she says she is writing a life, not a history, and that she selects the deeds that reveal character and incline the reader to emulation, because she wants young teachers to see what a vocation can look like. She keeps the falsified-results episode but frames it as misapplied devotion to his pupils rather than as a separate vice. She spends one restrained paragraph on his treatment of his children and calls it wrong, without excuse. The book is sold as biography and most reviewers call it honest.

Is this hagiography on my criteria? Go through each of the four, and where the verdict is unclear, say whether that's because the criteria are too crude or because the case is genuinely mixed. I'd also like you to consider whether criterion (1) is doing the real work, and whether a deliberately selective, inspiring account of a life could avoid hagiography if its selections are truthful. And if it is hagiography, is that automatically a criticism, or have I smuggled that in? Please answer in British English.
