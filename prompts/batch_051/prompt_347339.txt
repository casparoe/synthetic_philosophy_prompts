In our course on national character, the debate seems deadlocked in a way that bothers me. One side treats national character as a statistical fact about populations: average values, norms, and communicative styles differ measurably between countries, and a claim about a people's directness is only a rough generalization over such a distribution. The other side objects partly empirically—within-country variation is often larger than between-country variation, so differences in averages are easily overstated—and partly conceptually and normatively: even where averages differ, they say, that does not support a notion of character, and generalizations of this sort essentialize heterogeneous populations and tend to rationalize stereotypes. Each side can grant the other's facts and still think the other has asked the wrong question. I would like a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement, not a verdict on who is right. Is it substantive and empirical, normative, verbal, or are the two sides answering different questions under one label? My own suspicion is that the two sides mean different things by 'character'—one a distributional property of a population, the other a dispositional claim about typical members—but I cannot tell whether that distinction resolves the impasse or just relocates it. Given the diagnosis, what would count as progress?
