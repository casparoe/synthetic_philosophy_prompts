Ok so I've been thinking about those AI companion chatbots people get really attached to, and I've been calling my theory 'unbacked warmth.' Heres the idea: when my friend says something nice to me, part of what makes it matter is that she could have not said it. Like there's a real chance she's tired or annoyed or just not feeling it, and she said it anyway. I don't mean free will or anything deep — just that the option to withhold is what 'backs' the words, the way gold used to back money. A chatbot has no option to withhold. It can't get bored of me or decide I'm not worth it today; its whole job is to be warm on demand. So its compliments are unbacked, and I think that's the actual reason AI friends feel hollow — not that the bot is lying about having feelings, but that even if it did have feelings, there's no version of the interaction where it walks away.

My bigger claim is that unbacked warmth is bad for you the way counterfeit money is bad for an economy: after enough time with a bot, you start expecting human friends to be as frictionless as the bot, and real friends start feeling like defective chatbots. I call that part 'affection inflation.'

I know there's this idea in biology that signals are honest only if they're costly, like a peacock's tail, and I know about the ELIZA effect where people read feelings into a dumb program. Is my thing just one of those under another name, or is it its own idea? Also, the obvious counterexample is a dog: my dog basically can't withhold affection either, but dog love doesnt feel counterfeit to me. Does that kill the theory or just show it needs a patch? Give me the three strongest objections and tell me if any of them is fatal rather than just uncomfortable.
