Next month I am introducing a screening of Denis Villeneuve's Arrival (2016) at a film-and-philosophy seminar whose regulars mostly come from the rationalist blog circuit. In earlier threads on those forums the film is usually filed under the block universe: Louise's acquisition of the heptapod language is taken to show her literally experiencing all times at once, and the film is treated as an intuition pump for eternalism. I am sceptical that the film itself commits to that much. It seems at least as interested in choice, grief, and the decision to have a child whose death Louise already knows, and I suspect an honest reading should distinguish between the future's being real, its being fixed, and its being inevitable before deciding which, if any, the film actually commits to.

Please write the short written introduction I will circulate ahead of the session. You are a professional philosopher working in metaphysics, especially the philosophy of time, with a side interest in cinema. Read the film through the presentism/eternalism debate. In particular: which scenes or character decisions genuinely require future times or events to exist, and which require only that Louise has reliable evidence about a future that is not yet present? Where does the film appear to run together the reality, fixity, and inevitability of the future, and where does it resist a clean philosophical reading? Do not worry about exact quotations from the screenplay; describe scenes in your own words. Begin by mapping the strongest presentist and eternalist readings of the central conceit, then settle on the reading that best fits the whole film, and finish by saying what the film adds to the standing philosophical debate—if anything—that the metaphysics alone does not already make vivid.
