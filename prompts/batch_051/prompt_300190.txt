I am a novelist, and for the past four years I have chaired the jury of a national book prize. Each year we convene seven jurors around a longlist of roughly fifteen novels, and over a single weekend we must deliver a shortlist of six and a single winner. The prize's trustees have become uneasy about how our final choices are actually made: in some years we vote by simple majority, in others we deliberate until consensus, and the outcome seems to swing with the style of the chair and the personalities in the room. As this year's chair, I have been asked to draft a standing decision procedure for the prize jury, one that will bind future juries as well as this one, to be approved by the trustees before next year's cycle.

I have read a little about Condorcet's jury theorem and believe it may do some useful work here, but I also fear it will be misapplied. My rough understanding is this: if each voter in a group is independently more likely right than wrong on a two-option question, then a majority vote among a sufficiently large group of such voters is very likely to be right, and that likelihood rises with group size; if the voters are each worse than chance, a majority vote is very likely to be wrong. Please write a procedure grounded in what this theorem can and cannot support, not in a lay paraphrase of it.

The procedure must be usable by a prize administrator and seven jurors, none of whom has any training in formal or social-choice theory, over a single weekend, for decisions that materially affect writers' livelihoods. It should cover at least these stages of our work:

(a) eligibility disputes and other factual matters, such as whether a submitted novel is substantially the same work as one published elsewhere, where there seems to be a fact of the matter that a panel could get wrong;
(b) the cut from longlist to shortlist;
(c) the choice of the winner.

Please give me an ordered sequence of questions, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each; a clear statement of where the procedure authorises a majority vote, where it authorises deliberation towards consensus, and where it stops and hands the decision to a named individual as a matter of taste or values rather than aggregation; and the points at which judgement cannot be eliminated, marked as such. I want the distinctions the jury theorem actually depends on to survive the simplification — above all, the difference between a question with a right answer that independent people can be more or less likely to find, and a question of literary preference, where "more likely to be right" may not even make sense. But I do not want the procedure to become an academic seminar. Do not spend space on bibliography or references; this document will be used, not read as scholarship.

Then apply the procedure to three worked cases:

1. Four of the seven jurors judge that a submitted novel fails the prize's eligibility rule on original language composition; three judge that it passes. The rule is newly drafted and the margin is thin.
2. The jury must cut fifteen novels to six, and three of the seven jurors share a strongly similar aesthetic and have read many of the same reviews.
3. The jury is split four to three on the winner, but the minority has read the two finalists far more thoroughly than the majority and has given the most detailed reasons on both sides.

Include at least one case in which the procedure gives an answer I, as chair, am likely to find unwelcome.

Finally, tell me plainly what the procedure cannot do — especially where the prize's public claim to have chosen the "best" book goes beyond anything a voting rule can deliver — and write the entire document in British English.
