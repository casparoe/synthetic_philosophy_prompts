Ok here's a rabbit hole I've been in. There's this classic "harmless torturers" thought experiment: a thousand people each flip a switch on the same machine, each flip makes the victim's pain worse by an amount so small that no single flip is perceptible, but after all thousand flips the victim is in agony. The usual fight is whether each person is off the hook because they individually made no perceptible difference, or whether each still did something wrong because the harm only shows up in aggregate.

I'm a dev, not a philosophy person, so I keep trying to turn it into a threshold system and see whether the standard answers survive. Here's the variant I want someone to actually work through.

Replace the smooth analog pain dial with a digital counter. There are 1005 switches, not 1000. Every time the machine has seen a fresh batch of 10 flips, it gives the victim one nasty shock and resets the counter. Leftover flips at the end do nothing. With 1005 flips the victim ends up with floor(1005 / 10) = 100 shocks. If any single person had never flipped, there'd be 1004 flips, and floor(1004 / 10) is still 100 shocks. Assume the victim only cares about how many shocks they get — not timing, not the underlying count.

So in the original each flip had a real, if imperceptible, marginal effect on the final pain: remove one flip and the total pain is a hair less. In this version each individual flip has exactly zero effect on the final harm. Yet the group of 1005 flips still produces 100 shocks.

Now I don't know what to say. The inefficacy objection — my little act makes no difference — seems like it should be much stronger here than in the original. But each flip also has a 100/1005 chance of being the tenth flip in one of the batches, the one that "completes" a shock, if the flips arrive in a random order and nobody knows their position. Does that ex ante chance matter, or is it a trick? Even the flip that completes a batch didn't really change anything: if that person had stayed home, the next flip would have filled the slot and there'd still be 100 shocks.

Then there's the thing that's bugging me about climate, meat, and voting as special cases. In those real cases nobody actually knows the total headcount or the threshold. If each person has a probability distribution over how many other switches get flipped, and there's some non-zero chance their flip is exactly the one that would complete the 100th batch, then their expected marginal harm is positive again and the usual expected-utility reply comes back. So is the deterministic known-headcount version just an artifact of knowing too much?

Work through the original continuous version and this threshold-batch version side by side. Does the no-perceptible-difference move get stronger, weaker, or just fall apart? Does blame land on everyone, on the 100 batch-completers, or on nobody? And take a clear stance on whether the deterministic known-headcount case is actually the right toy model for climate, meat, and voting, or whether uncertainty is what makes individual contributions matter. Please answer in American English.
