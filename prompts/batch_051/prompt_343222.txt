I teach moral psychology, and every term, when we reach psychopathy, the same one-liner does a great deal of argumentative work: "they know right from wrong." A student will grant that a psychopath is callous, manipulative, and incapable of remorse, and then add, "But he knew right from wrong—so he isn't off the hook." A student on the other side will say the opposite: "He didn't really know it was wrong." What bothers me is how many different claims that phrase could be making, and how readily the seminar slides among them without noticing. It would help my teaching and my own thinking to have the readings separated out.

Could you take that one-liner and lay out the distinct precise claims it might be expressing—for instance, the ability to state the conventional rule, the ability to classify a concrete act under it, the recognition that another person's distress counts as a reason, or the appreciation that a rule has authority over oneself—along with any further distinctions you think do the real work. For each reading, indicate whether it is a platitude nearly everyone should accept, a substantive empirical claim, or something likely false; then say which reading or readings would actually do the job the sentence is usually assigned, namely settling whether a psychopath is morally responsible rather than excused. I am less interested in a single correct interpretation than in a menu with verdicts attached.

Please write in American English, and finish with a one-sentence summary of the disambiguation, on its own line, beginning with "Summary:".
