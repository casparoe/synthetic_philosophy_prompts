Consider a variant of Nozick's experience machine that changes one feature of the original setup. In the original, the machine is a private tank that delivers a scripted stream of experience, so that while you are inside, your beliefs about doing things and being a certain kind of person are systematically false. Nozick's diagnosis of the intuitive resistance to plugging in is roughly threefold: we want to do things, not merely to have the experience of doing them; we want to be a certain sort of person, not merely to have the experience of being one; and we want contact with a reality deeper than one manufactured for us.

Now modify the machine. It is no longer a private illusion but a shared, causally self-contained world, already inhabited by many other real people who have also plugged in. Within this world, your actions have real and lasting consequences: promises bind, kindness and cruelty leave their marks on others, skills are genuinely acquired, and character is genuinely built or degraded. The only sense in which this world falls short is that it is sustained by machinery in the base world, and nothing you do inside can reach back into that base world; your base body remains asleep in its tank. You may choose whether to remember the outside while inside.

Does the standard anti-machine argument survive this change? Work through the three considerations and identify which are neutralized and which, if any, continue to do work against plugging in. If a single consideration is left standing, is it strong enough to carry the refusal by itself? In particular, would refusing now amount to claiming that the base world is more real, or more worth living in, simply because of what it runs on, even though the machine-world is real in its causal and moral structure? And what does that show about where the original thought experiment was really getting its force?
