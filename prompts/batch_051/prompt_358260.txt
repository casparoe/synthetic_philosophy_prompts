I've got a heuristic about the end of physics that I've been running for years and I want it tested. Whenever anyone — including working physicists — says physics is close to a final theory, or that the broad picture is settled and only details remain, I basically stop engaging and assign the claim negligible weight. My evidence is the outside view: the same thing was said before relativity and quantum mechanics, then again after quantum mechanics, and again after the standard model; every "we're nearly done" call so far has been wrong, including wrong about what "done" would even look like. So when a friend sends me a paper claiming strings/AdS/whatever is the end of the road, I dont read it. He says I'm using the outside view as an excuse to avoid the object-level evidence, and that my historical sample is tiny and loaded with hindsight. Is this actually a reasonable epistemic policy, or a bias wearing a base-rate costume? What should make me update that this time is genuinely different, and would I even recognize a final theory if it showed up?
