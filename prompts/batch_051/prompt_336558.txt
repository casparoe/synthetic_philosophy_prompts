I've been stuck in a months-long argument in my behavioral econ reading group about loss aversion and the endowment effect, and I'd like a diagnosis of the argument itself rather than another round of the usual points. On this view loss aversion is a bias, and the remedy is to fix the environment—change the framing, set better defaults, debias people. The other side says that's question-begging: if losses genuinely feel worse than equivalent gains, and if holding onto what you already have is a reasonable strategy for a finite creature, then the reference point is part of the preference, not a contaminant, and calling it a bias just sneaks in the assumption that preferences have to be context-independent. We all accept the same experimental results, so the facts aren't the sticking point, and yet nobody has moved in months. My hunch is that the fight is mostly about what counts as a real or underlying preference—one side wants preferences to be stable, context-free rankings, and the other says that's an economist's fiction—but it might also be a normative dispute about what rationality requires, or a semantic one about the word bias.
