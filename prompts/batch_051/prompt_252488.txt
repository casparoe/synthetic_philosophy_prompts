Epistemologists have been arguing about the KK principle — roughly, that if a subject knows that P, then she is in a position to know that she knows that P — for as long as epistemic logic has existed, and the debate now has the feel of a long stalemate. One side treats KK as a near-consequence of the first-person character of knowledge: knowledge is not supposed to be the kind of state you can be in while being unable, on reflection, to tell that you are in it. The other side treats KK as refuted by anti-luminosity reasoning of the sort Williamson pressed: take a subject undergoing a gradual transition from being in some condition to not being in it; if the condition were luminous — such that whenever it obtains, the subject is in a position to know it obtains — then a margin-for-error premise would force the condition to obtain at every later stage of the transition, which is absurd. So no non-trivial condition is luminous, knowledge is not luminous, and knowing P need not put you in a position to know that you know P. Both sides have had the other's arguments for years, and the standard moves have become predictable without producing movement.

Diagnose this impasse rather than adjudicating it. What kind of disagreement is this, really? Is it verbal — a dispute about what 'knowledge' means or about how strong 'in a position to know' is? Is it a dispute about idealization, with one side theorizing about cognitively ideal agents and the other about bounded, ordinary knowers? Is it a proxy for the older internalism/externalism dispute about whether knowledge is a mental state? Is there a hidden empirical question about human metacognition underneath, or is the question ill-posed? Offer a diagnosis grounded in what each side actually says, and then say what would count as progress given that diagnosis — a distinction, a formal result, an experiment, a change of question — or argue that no progress should be expected and why. You may treat the following as a hypothesis to test: that the two sides are answering different questions under one heading, one about the logic of an idealized reflective knower and the other about the epistemic powers of embodied, fallible creatures like us.
