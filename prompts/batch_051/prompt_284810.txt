I'm a third-year philosophy PhD student, working mostly on free will and moral responsibility, and I've been asked to put together a one-page "where I stand" statement for a seminar on compatibilism. Before I send it to my supervisor I want a consistency check from someone who really knows this literature. Please act as the kind of person who teaches the compatibilism debate at graduate level and is ruthless about hidden inconsistencies. Read the whole list before judging, maybe do a quick pairwise brainstorm first, and then tell me whether it all hangs together or wheter I've papered over something. Here's roughly where I've landed.

(1) I'm a compatibilist, but the free will I care about is the kind worth wanting in Dennett's sense: self-control, reasons-responsiveness, the ability to revise my views under criticism, resistance to coercion and undue influence. I don't think the incompatibilist's "could have done otherwise, same past and same laws" is worth wanting at all. (2) I think the Consequence Argument is sound: if determinism is true, nobody could have done otherwise in that exact sense. I just don't think that's the sense that matters for freedom or responsibility. (3) I'm convinced by Frankfurt cases that moral responsibility doesn't require alternative possibilities. (4) But I also have strong intuitions about manipulation cases of the Pereboom kind: if neuroscientists secretly implanted someone's deepest desires and deliberative dispositions, even in a soft version where the person still weighs reasons and identifies with the resulting motives, that person isn't responsible the way ordinary people are. (5) I don't think this pushes me into hard incompatibilism. The ordinary determined agent differs from the manipulated agent because her reasoning mechanisms weren't intentionally designed by another agent; history matters somehow, but only insofar as it explains how she actually came to be who she is. (6) I also think of myself as a Strawsonian: being responsible is mostly about whether reactive attitudes like resentment and gratitude are apt toward the person, and whether the person can hold themselves answerable to certain demands. I don't see why that needs any contra-causal machinery. (7) And in everyday life I plainly say that people "could have done otherwise," and I'd rather not end up with a revisionism that says ordinary speakers are systematically wrong about that.

Go through these and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension, which ones only look that way, and rank the real tensions by how bad they are. For each real tension, say what the cheapest fix is and exactly what it costs: which commitment has to go or be quietly qualified. I'd like the response in continuous prose, the way you'd write an email, so no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables please.
