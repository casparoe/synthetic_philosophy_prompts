I run a roughly 15-person software company and about once a month I end up arbitrating a conflict that isn't about what somebody did but about what it said about how they were being regarded — a code review that's technically right but leaves a junior feeling like an idiot, a PM who says their team doesn't recognize who owns a decision, someone who wants to be asked for input not because it would change the answer but because not asking signals that they don't count. My reflex is to ask what action would fix it, and that reflex makes everyone more annoyed. I've spent enough years on rationalist forums that I don't want to write everything off as tone policing, but my current fallback is just as lazy: treating the feeling of being hurt as self-certifying.

Write me a decision procedure I can run in under an hour, where neither side has any philosophy background, for deciding whether an attitude itself was owed and whether it was withheld: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it has to stop and say this is a genuine values call that a human has to make, not a question of fact. In particular I want the procedure to keep two things seperate: being owed uptake (treated as someone whose reasons count) vs. being owed deference, admiration, or credit — and to handle the difference between a single sigh and six months of sighs, since most actual complaints are patterns. The facts have to be agreed by both parties before I run it, and I'm the one who signs off; don't tell me to hand it to HR because there is no HR.

Then run it on three cases: (a) a senior engineer gives correct review feedback but frames it so the junior is treated as someone who hasn't bothered to read the spec; (b) a new hire says they feel unrecognized because a veteran responds to every proposal with only skeptical questions and no put-downs; (c) our best engineer is curt and dismissive toward a PM in meetings, and I'd really rather not lose either of them. Make one of the applications land somewhere I won't like, and finish by saying outright what the procedure can't do.
