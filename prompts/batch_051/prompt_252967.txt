A friend in my department has an argument he calls unpacking Friedman's F-twist, and I've been turning it over for a week without finding the exit. It goes like this.

(1) The F-twist, roughly, says a model should be judged only by how well its observable predictions match the data; the realism of the assumptions inside it is irrelevant.
(2) So when two models fit the same data equally well, the one with less realistic assumptions is not for that reason any worse.
(3) Take a decent model and bolt on a false story about unobservable machinery: every particle is nudged by invisible gremlins whose pushing is defined to be exactly whatever the original forces would have done. The new model has identical predictions, so by (1) and (2) it is just as good.
(4) By the same logic, no amount of data could ever favor a model whose inner workings are, as we ordinarily say, more accurate over a contrived model that merely reproduces the same data with mechanisms we know are wrong.
(5) Explanatory realism in science would then be empty: preferring a model that gets the mechanism roughly right over a Ptolemaic-style patch with an equal fit would be a matter of taste, not evidence.
(6) But that's not how good science works; scientists often reject models that fit the data just as well but have absurd mechanisms, and we think they're right to.
(7) Therefore the F-twist is false.

I'm loathe to accept (7), since I basically believe (1) whenever I do empirical work, but (4) and (5) feel like they slip somewhere. Which step would you reject, and what does rejecting it commit you to? If I wanted to keep (1) and still resist (5), what's the cheapest thing I'd have to add?
