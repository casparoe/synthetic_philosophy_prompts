I've been going back and forth on this with some people in my department and I think I need to actually nail down the question before I go read another paper that just asserts an answer. The puzzle starts from something embarrassingly concrete: a buddy of mine is a welder, and he can point to a bad bead and a good bead, and he gets better in ways you can see by Friday. I work in ethics and political philosophy, and I honest to god can't tell you whether my subfield knows more in 2024 than it knew in 1960. The usual line is "some fields get fast, clear feedback and some don't, and that's why they progress at different rates." But I don't buy that it's one thing: pure math gets almost no empirical feedback and it accumulates; cosmology can't rerun the universe and it still moves; medicine supposedly had feedback for centuries and still took forever to drop bloodletting; chess got faster once engines made feedback immediate, but it wasn't stagnant before. So the interesting variable isn't just "does feedback exist."

Here's what I want. Before I start writing my own pet theory of verifiability and progress, give me the desiderata: what would any adequate theory of why some fields advance faster than others have to get right? For each requirement, tell me the case or consideration that forces it, not just a slogan. Distinguish which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just taste. Flag whichever pairs of desiderata you think can't be jointly satisfied — I suspect there's a real tension between wanting feedback to be cheap and wanting it to be conclusive, and maybe another between field-level progress and individual-level learning. And if there's a single requirement that does the most to knock out popular answers, say which one.

One more thing: don't let "feedback" sit there as one blob. Part of the deliverable is the distinctions a theory can't afford to collapse — feedback that trains a person versus feedback that accumulates in a field, feedback that arrives early versus feedback that arrives after the publication incentives have already rewarded you, feedback that tests a theory versus feedback that just tests whether the user liked it. I'll do the scoring against existing views myself; I want the criteria to be the thing that's sharp.
