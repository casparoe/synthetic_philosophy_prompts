Here's a weird one. Imagine a debating society founded in the 1890s — call it the Linnean Argument Club, mostly naturalists and amateur historians. Its constitution contains the following article, unchanged since the begining:

"Article IV, Section 2. No member shall assert as true, in any address, paper, or debate before the Club, any matter of fact for which he is unable to produce evidence sufficient to convince a fair-minded hearer; and an assertion which is shown to rest only on hope, fear, or the authority of common opinion shall be deemed a breach of this section."

For a century this was mostly used to keep cranks from presenting sea-serpent sightings as established facts. Then the club slowly turned into a community support group. At last month's meeting, a member — call her Dana — moved a resolution reading: "The Linnean Argument Club affirms that Dana's daughter will recover fully from her illness." The illness is a rare cancer with something like a 40% survival rate, so recovery is genuinely possible but far from certain, and Dana's own oncologist has refused to say it is likely. The resolution passed. Another member has now filed a complaint under Article IV, Section 2, saying Dana asserted a matter of fact — the future recovery of a specific child — without evidence capable of convincing a fair-minded hearer, and that her grounds are hope, fear, and the warmth of the room.

Dana's defense runs like this: the resolution was not a statement of fact; it was an act of solidarity, more like saying "we will get through this" than "the tumor will shrink." And she says the founders meant to govern factual claims in debates about natural history and public affairs, not to police the way a community tries to keep someone's spirits up during a crisis. The members genuinely dispute whether the child will recover, but they also genuinely dispute whether Article IV, Section 2 can be applied to this at all.

So work it through: does the rule catch the resolution? Please run through at least three approaches — the plain words, the purpose of the rule, and what the 1890s founders would have said if told the club would become this — and say which approach should govern and why. And then tell me what you think the underlying disagreement is really about: what Dana said, what the club is for, or where the line is between believing something and just refusing to act as if it's false?
