In their paper “Einstein, incompleteness, and the epistemic view of quantum states,” Nicholas Harrigan and Robert W. Spekkens write:

“Only in the latter case can the quantum state be considered to be truly epistemic, that is, a representation of an observer’s knowledge of reality rather than reality itself.”

I want to test an argument that draws a strong conclusion from this passage. It goes as follows:

(1) The characteristic feature of a ψ-epistemic model is that some ontic state is consistent with more than one distinct pure quantum state.  
(2) Suppose an ontic state λ is consistent with both |ψ⟩ and |φ⟩, where |ψ⟩ and |φ⟩ assign different probabilities to some measurement outcome.  
(3) If an agent has complete knowledge of λ, then any further distinction between |ψ⟩ and |φ⟩ can only be knowledge, not a fact about λ itself.  
(4) So such an agent should assign the same measurement-outcome probabilities regardless of whether the preparation was described by |ψ⟩ or |φ⟩.  
(5) But quantum theory says the correct probabilities differ for the two preparations.  
(6) Therefore, in any ψ-epistemic model that reproduces quantum predictions, the probabilities actually assigned by a fully informed agent cannot be read off from the ontic state alone; they must depend on the agent’s quantum-state description as well.  
(7) But if outcome probabilities depend on the quantum-state description even after conditioning on the ontic state, then the quantum state is not merely a representation of knowledge in the sense Harrigan and Spekkens define.  

Conclusion: the passage’s definition of “truly epistemic” is unstable: once you admit overlapping ontic states, you are forced to choose between failing to reproduce quantum predictions and reintroducing the quantum state as part of reality.

I think this argument is valid, but I have been told it conflates two notions of “epistemic.” Is it valid? If not, exactly which step fails, and under what reading of “truly epistemic” would the argument go through?
