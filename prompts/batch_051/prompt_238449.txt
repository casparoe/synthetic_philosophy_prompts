I volunteer at a living history village—the old woolen mill town kind of place—and right now the board is tied up in knots over something I honestly think they're misreading. Our handbook has a rule that got written in the 1980s after a fight about whether to keep the village's connection to slavery out of the tour script. The rule reads: "The past shall be interpreted as it was; no interpreter shall alter, soften, or omit the attitudes of the persons portrayed because those attitudes offend present-day opinion." A few weeks ago one of our interpreters, who plays the mill owner, logged onto the village's social media account and posted in character. He wrote some period-accurate but pretty grotesque things about the Irish mill girls—the actual slurs and sentiments from the real mill owner's letters. The post went viral in the worst way, donors are threatening to walk, and the board is now saying the handbook leaves them no choice but to defend the posts, because the rule forbids "imposing present standards" on the past. That feels like a dodge to me, but I can't quite say why. I've also been reading about presentism in history—whether any of us can describe the past without smuggling in our own time's standards—and the whole mess has me going back and forth on who's actually doing the smuggling here.

I'd like you to act as an expert on historical interpretation and the ethics of living history. Think hard about what the rule actually requires or permits in a case its authors never imagined: a costumed character posting in their period voice to social media, for an audience the 1980s board couldn't have pictured. Before you settle on the fifth item, genuinely weigh the first four against each other. Give me exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion: what the plain wording says; what the original purpose of the rule suggests; what the 1980s board would most likely have said if you showed them the screenshot, the comments, and the donor spreadsheet; what a reasonable visitor reading the handbook today would expect; and which of these approaches should win here, and why.
