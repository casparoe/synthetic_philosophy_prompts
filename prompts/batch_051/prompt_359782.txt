Here's the thing I keep getting stuck on. The old argument runs roughly: classical physics was deterministic; determinism rules out the genuine alternative possibilities that libertarian free will needs; but quantum mechanics replaced that determinism with fundamental chance at the bottom level; therefore physics no longer closes the door on free will. I want you to tell me whether that argument survives decoherence, understood the way a working physicist understands it.

The development, as I'd put it: decoherence says that when a quantum system is coupled to a warm, noisy environment—like the inside of a neuron—the phase relations that make a superposition more than a classical probability distribution leak into the environment on fantastically short timescales. By the time you get to anything the size, temperature, and speed of a neural process, the dynamics are effectively classical: you get probabilities, but no live interfering branches. So quantum indeterminism, whatever it amounts to, is washed out before it could touch anything a "choice" could be made of. The randomness that does reach the scale of neurons is mostly thermal noise, which nobody ever thought helps free will.
