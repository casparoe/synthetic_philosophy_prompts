I spent my career as a plant geneticist and only started reading the Stoics properly after I retired. I keep tripping over a passage in Epictetus's Enchiridion, chapter 2, in the Elizabeth Carter translation:

"But if you are averse to sickness, or death, or poverty, you will be wretched. Remove aversion, then, from all things that are not in our control, and transfer it to things contrary to the nature of what is in our control."

Here's the case I can't make headway on. My niece just tested positive for a BRCA1 mutation and is deciding whether to get a preventive double mastectomy before thirty. She is also reading the same popular Stoic books I am, and she's now convinced the passage tells her she must not be averse to the cancer at all — and she can't figure out wether scheduling the surgery is a legitimate exercise of "things in our control" or a backdoor attempt to control what cannot be controlled. The passage feels like it was written for a world where sickness mostly just showed up; now you can act on a probabilistic warning and change the odds. So, reading the words carefully, does the passage require her to drop her aversion to the cancer, and would that make going through with the surgery a failure of the discipline or a proper use of it? I'd like to see what you get from (a) the plain words, (b) the purpose of the chapter, (c) what Epictetus would say if you could sit him down and ask "what about prophylactic surgery?", and (d) what a reasonable modern reader of the Enchiridion would take away. If these readings pull apart, which one should she follow and why? Does a surgery that changes a probability put the cancer partly in her control, or is that the exact confusion the dichotomy of control is aimed at?
