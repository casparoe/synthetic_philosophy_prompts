Nutrition policy in a lot of low-income countries does something that looks flatly inconsistent with veganism as it's practiced in rich countries: to reduce child stunting, programs push eggs, milk powder, and small fish on pregnant women and toddlers, and emergency food baskets often include dried milk or tinned meat. The justification is always that the plant-based alternative is unavailable — too expensive, too low in the right nutrients, or too risky for an already malnourished kid. I can read the presupposition two ways: (a) the duty to avoid animal products is contigent on having adequate alternatives, so the vegan principle itself blesses the practice; or (b) it's welfarist pragmatism that treats animal interests as weightless once human need is desperate. Which does the practice actually presuppose — or is it underdetermined between those and some third option? Do the chronic-malnutrition side and the emergency-aid side even presuppose the same theory, or are they inconsistent with each other? Then tell me what a child-nutrition program that took animal rights seriously (not just animal welfare) would have to do differently, if it kept the goal of reducing stunting. And what should someone who is vegan in Berlin but handing out tinned fish at a food-aid distribution in a refugee camp say she's doing?
