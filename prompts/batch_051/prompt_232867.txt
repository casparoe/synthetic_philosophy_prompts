I am a high school student, and I've been trying to understand how mathematicians decide when one definition is better than another. The two cases I keep circling are why a topological space is defined by its open sets rather than by a distance function, and why a group is defined by closure, associativity, an identity element, and inverses, rather than stopping at an associative operation with an identity. I have come to hold a view: the right definition is the least restrictive set of conditions under which the characteristic theorems of the object still go through. A definition earns its place by what it lets you prove and by how wide a range of cases it covers, not by how closely it matches a prior, intuitive picture of the object. If two definitions support the same interesting theorems, I would prefer the one with fewer assumptions; if a more familiar definition supports fewer theorems, it should lose, however natural it feels.

What I want to know is what would count against this view. I am not asking for the familiar objection that some definitions simply track an already existing concept; I want a full map of the view's exposure. Which possible mathematical cases, formal results, or arguments should move me, sorted from decisive to merely awkward? For instance, are there definitions that remain the right ones even though they are more restrictive than the theorems require, and are there definitions whose value comes from something other than the theorems they generate? What would a committed defender of this view concede counts against it, and what would they dismiss as not really a test? I would also like an honest assessment of whether my reliance on the idea of characteristic theorems makes the view flexible enough to absorb any counterexample, and whether that flexibility is a problem.
