The thing that's bugging me: I keep seeing people nod at Popper's 'poverty of historicism' idea — roughly, that there can't be laws of history the way physics has laws, because the future depends on future knowledge and on how people react to predictions about them. Fine. But Hari Seldon's psychohistory looks like exactly the kind of law-driven, predictive history Popper says is impossible: it models huge populations statistically and spits out the fall of the Galactic Empire and the length of the dark age that follows. Meanwhile the books also seem to anticipate Popper's complaints: the Plan only works at the level of masses, not individuals (the Mule blows it up precisely because he's a one-off), and the Second Foundation exists to quietly steer things back whenever the model goes off. And Seldon's recorded appearances at each crisis feel like predictions that change the thing they predict, since everyone knows the Plan and acts differently because of it.

I want a real reading of the trilogy through Popper's argument, not a plot summary. Where does psychohistory genuinely fit the thing Popper attacks, and where does Asimov seem to be tweaking the definition so it slips out? Does adding the Second Foundation as a kind of feedback/control loop actually solve Popper's problem, or does it just change what's being predicted?
