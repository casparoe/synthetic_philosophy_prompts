I've been trying to get clear on when it's ok to just pretend that events are independent, the way people do in probabilistic heuristics, and when that pretending goes badly wrong. Here are my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases; I want to know what principle, if any, captures the pattern.

1. Guessing how many twin primes there are up to x. The standard move: a random integer around n is prime with probability about 1/log n, and you treat "n is prime" and "n+2 is prime" as independent, so twin density is about 1/(log n)^2. That gives the right shape, x/(log x)^2, but it's off by a constant that everyone then fixes by hand. I'm happy to count this as a success of the independence assumption — the independence step did the real work, and the constant is just a patch.

2. Guessing how many prime pairs there are of the form n, n+1. Treating them as independent predicts loads of them, but there's exactly one pair, (2,3). I think that's a failure, and a bad one — not fixable by a constant. Though I also have the feeling the obvious reason (one of any two consecutive numbers is even) is the kind of thing you're supposed to factor out before you start pretending at independence.

3. The birthday problem. The standard shortcut treats the events "these two people share a birthday" as independent, which they plainly aren't, and it gets the chance of at least one match almost exactly right. I think that's fine — harmless simplification.

4. A pollster samples 200 people from a country of 300 million and treats the draws as independent, even though it's really sampling without replacement. Fine, obviously; the without-replacement correction is negligible. I don't even think of this as an assumption, more like rounding.

5. A bank in 2006 treats mortgage defaults across different households as roughly independent, and uses that to estimate the chance a lot of them default at once. I think that's a mistake, and not a small one: the dependence (house prices falling, the economy turning) is the entire tail risk. Here the independence assumption isn't a harmless simplification — it's wrong exactly where it matters.

6. The one that keeps bugging me. People also use independence-style heuristics for how primes are distributed in shortish intervals: model each integer n as prime with probability 1/log n, independently. My understanding is that this model is known to fail for certain interval lengths — the primes bunch up in ways the independent model can't produce, even after you fix the usual local constants. My gut says that's a real breakdown of the independence heuristic, not another patchable case, but I can't articulate why its different from the twin-prime case.

My working rule so far is something like: independence is innocent when the dependencies are local and wash out, or can be absorbed into a constant, and guilty when there's a global dependence that moves many events together. But that's vague, and case 6 seems to break the rule without having an obvious common cause like a housing crash.

Is there a single principle that gets all six of these, or close? What does it say about which of my verdicts I have to revise? And give me a case I haven't listed that would distinguish the best version of the principle from the runner-up.
