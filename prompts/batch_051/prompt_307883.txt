You direct the behavioral-insights unit of a national consumer-protection regulator. Your unit issues binding guidance on whether a market practice or proposed rule should treat consumers' loss aversion and endowment effects as biases to be corrected — through mandatory disclosure, changed defaults, or re-framing — or as legitimate preferences to be accommodated by leaving the reference point or default where it already stands. Rulings arrive weekly, affect products and disclosures used by millions of people, and must survive administrative review; the analysts who will run the screening are not economists, they have ten working days per case, and a supervising official signs off on each decision.

Write the reusable decision procedure: an ordered sequence of concrete questions, with criteria for what counts as a sufficient answer, explicit stop conditions, the points at which the question is one of values rather than facts and must be escalated, and a clear statement of what the procedure cannot determine. Then apply it to three cases, including at least one where the procedure is likely to produce an answer your team dislikes: (1) a bank proposes to cut the interest rate on existing savings accounts, applying the new rate to balances already held rather than grandfathering them; (2) a subscription service renews existing customers automatically each year unless they opt out, and a consumer group proposes requiring an annual affirmative renewal instead; (3) a public-health agency must choose whether to describe a cancer screening's benefit in terms of the average months of life gained with screening rather than the average months lost without it.
