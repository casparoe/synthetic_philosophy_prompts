My line was the old one from the 70s: the Appel–Haken proof is unsurveyable, it leans on a massive computer case-check, nobody can hold the whole thing in their head, and so it isn't a proof in the traditional sense — it's more like an experiment where you end up trusting the machine. Her line was that this is just out of date: there's now a fully formal, machine-checked proof of the four-colour theorem done in Coq (I think Gonthier's), where the whole argument is a formal object verified by a tiny kernel that a human can actually read. So the trusted base shrinks down to something surveyable plus the definitions. Does the old unsurveyability worry survive that, or does the problem just move somewhere else — into the definitions and how the proof represents planar graphs, into whether the theorem statement really says "every planar graph," into the checker itself, or into the fact that no human has actually inspected the proof object?
