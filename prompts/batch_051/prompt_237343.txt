After last week's meeting of our machine learning reading group, two members stayed behind arguing about whether Solomonoff induction actually grounds Occam's razor, and I could not tell who had the better of it. Daniel argued that Solomonoff's framework makes the razor precise in Bayesian terms: identify hypotheses with programs, give each program a prior probability of two to the power of minus its length, and shorter programs automatically receive exponentially more prior mass, so a preference for simpler theories falls straight out of the formalism. Priya replied that this merely relocates the difficulty to the well-known language-dependence of Kolmogorov complexity, because program length is meaningful only relative to a chosen reference machine; build in a primitive for your favorite hypothesis and it can be compressed to a single line. Daniel countered that the invariance theorem closes that escape route: any two universal Turing machines can simulate each other with at most a constant overhead, so the prior assigned to any given hypothesis changes by at most a constant multiplicative factor across machines. Priya said that constant factor is doing much less than he imagines, since when two serious scientific hypotheses are within a few bits of each other in complexity, the machine-dependent constant is precisely what determines which one ends up with the higher prior. Daniel answered that the machine-dependence washes out as evidence accumulates, because the predictions made from different universal machine priors converge in the long run with the likelihood overwhelming the prior. Priya countered that real inference never operates in that asymptotic regime, and that the Solomonoff predictor is uncomputable in any case, so invoking it as a standard of rational theory-choice substitutes one idealization for another rather than supplying an actual rule.
