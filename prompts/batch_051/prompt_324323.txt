David Hume, in Section VIII of An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding, writes:

“It is universally allowed, that matter, in all its operations, is actuated by a necessary force, and that every natural effect is so precisely determined by the energy of its cause, that no other effect, in such particular circumstances, could possibly have resulted from it.”

Now suppose we vary the standard Frankfurt-style case as follows. In the original case, an intervener has planted a device that will infallibly cause Jones to decide to kill Smith if Jones shows any sign of deciding otherwise; Jones decides on his own, the device never fires, and the usual lesson drawn is that Jones may still be morally responsible. In the revised case, the intervener has no device. He has merely observed a very regular pattern in Jones’s behaviour: whenever Jones’s brother publicly taunts him about an unpaid debt, Jones storms off and resolves to kill Smith, who is his brother’s associate. The intervener deliberately arranges for the same public taunt, but he does not deceive Jones about any fact and does not force the brother to speak; the taunt is genuine. The regular pattern holds, and Jones resolves to kill Smith. If the intervener had seen signs that the pattern was failing, he would have abandoned the plan rather than intervene.

Does Jones act freely, and is he morally responsible, in the revised case? Work out how Hume’s account of necessity—as constant conjunction plus customary inference—bears on the difference between the infallible device and the ordinary psychological uniformities on which the revised intervener relies. In particular, if Hume is right that the necessity we ascribe to matter is nothing over and above such uniformities and the inferences we draw from them, can a compatibilist treat the two cases as relevantly alike? Does an incompatibilist have a principled reason to treat them differently? Finally, explain whether the revised case strengthens or weakens the Frankfurt-style claim that moral responsibility is compatible with an inability to do otherwise.
