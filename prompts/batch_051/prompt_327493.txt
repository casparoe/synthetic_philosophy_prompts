In my philosophy of language seminar the argument about slurs has been running for weeks without anyone moving. Half the group thinks a slur's derogatory force is part of its literal meaning, so that even a negated or non-asserted use still encodes contempt; the other half thinks the contempt is purely pragmatic — a matter of taboo, use, or conventional implicature rather than what's said. Both camps can handle the standard data: projection through negation and conditionals, the failure of saying he's not a ___ to neutralize, in-group reclamation, the gap between offensivness and derogation. But each side privileges a different subset of that data, and I can't tell whether they're arguing about the facts or about where to draw the line around the word meaning. Diagnose the impasse rather than adjudicate it. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, or two different research programs — one about truth conditions, one about acquisition and processing — sharing a name? And if anything could count as progress, what would it look like: a shared case, an experimental result, or a distinction that would force at least one side to redraw its view?
