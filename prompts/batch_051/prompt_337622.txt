Below is a question from an exam in a course on the philosophy of criminal law, followed by one student's answer. You are the grader. Assign the answer a grade, and provide the specific point-by-point comments that justify the grade. In those comments, identify any steps in the argument that do not follow, any doctrines that are conflated, and any parts of the question the answer fails to address.

Question. The city of Halden adopts an ordinance making it a petty misdemeanor to "unreasonably interfere with the safe and orderly movement of passengers in any transit station." Jonah is charged after he sits on a narrow staircase in a subway station during a service delay; passengers are able to pass around him, but a transit officer concludes that his presence "slowed the flow." Jonah challenges the ordinance as void for vagueness under the Due Process Clause. Evaluate the strength of his facial and as-applied challenges.

Student answer. The ordinance is void for vagueness. The doctrine has two requirements: a criminal law must give ordinary people fair notice of what it prohibits, and it must not delegate standardless discretion to police. The phrases "unreasonably interfere" and "safe and orderly movement" are undefined, so a person cannot know in advance whether sitting on a staircase is criminal. The ordinance supplies no quantitative threshold, such as a number of inches or minutes, and without such a threshold it leaves every judgment to the officer on the scene; that is precisely the arbitrary enforcement the doctrine forbids. Because the ordinance reaches harmless conduct such as sitting still during a delay, it is also overbroad in the vagueness sense. For these reasons, the ordinance is facially unconstitutional.
