Consider the standard argument for presentism in historiography. It runs roughly as follows: the historian cannot escape the standards and categories of her own time, because selecting a subject, describing the evidence, and judging past actors all require using some present-day vocabulary, and there is no neutral metalanguage in which that vocabulary can be checked against the era under study. Suppose a later development: computational historians can reconstruct the conceptual structure of a past period from a large digitised corpus of that period's own texts, using distributional semantics and related methods. These reconstructions are produced procedurally from the period's usage rather than chosen in advance by the historian, and they can correct or overturn the historian's initial categories. Does the standard presentist argument survive this development? Is it now dead, does it need repair, or does it go through unchanged?

Please reconstruct the presentist argument in premise form, identify exactly which premise the computational development bears on, and assess whether the argument can be restored in another form. Then give the strongest reply a presentist could make, and say whether that reply collapses the challenge back into a version of the original argument or instead marks a genuine limit on what computational reconstruction can do. Write your answer in British English.
