You are a moral philosopher with a secondary expertise in moral psychology, and you are familiar with recent debates over grit, resilience, and post-traumatic growth. I would like you to evaluate a distinction I have been drawing privately for the past two years, and I would rather you be candid with me than charitable to the point of uselessness. Begin by brainstorming the existing frameworks and distinctions mine resembles, so we can see where it is redundant; then reconstruct the proposal in its strongest form; then assess it.

A close relative of mine has a long illness that will not end in recovery, and I have been one of the people caring for that person. Friends and acquaintances keep praising our family's "resilience," and I have come to think the word is quietly doing two incompatible jobs. I have started calling them load-bearing fortitude and load-shedding fortitude.

Load-bearing fortitude, as I define it, is the capacity to keep carrying the same load: the same obligations, the same standards, the same self-conception, under stress. It is the parent who remains the organized, cheerful, competent one; the worker who absorbs every added demand; the person whose life after the crisis looks as much as possible like their life before it. Load-shedding fortitude is the capacity to deliberately drop, renegotiate, or transfer part of the load when carrying it would destroy you, and to accept the loss of standing or identity that follows. It is the decision to leave the marriage, quit the job, let the friendship end, or ask for help in a way that permanently changes how others see you.

My thesis is that public talk about grit and resilience is not neutral between these two. It treats load-bearing as the virtue and load-shedding as failure: quitting, lowering standards, setting boundaries. But in a long asymmetric crisis, load-shedding may be the only kind of fortitude actually available, and praising load-bearing can function as a way of keeping an unsustainable arrangement intact by making the person who absorbs it feel noble. I suspect a good deal of what gets marketed as "resilience training" is load-bearing training plus emotional management.

Please tell me: is the distinction coherent, or does it collapse under pressure — because every act of carrying involves small acts of shedding, and the difference is only one of time horizon? Has this already been worked out under other names, and if so, where does my version differ? Give me your three sharpest objections to the distinction and to the ideological claim, and tell me whether any of them disposes of the idea outright rather than merely limiting its scope. I am especially worried that I have stacked the deck by pretending all loads are optional; some are not, and renegotiating a genuinely non-negotiable obligation may not be a second virtue but simply a wrong. Is that the crack in the whole thing? Do not turn your answer into a bibliography; name related ideas only insofar as they help us think.
