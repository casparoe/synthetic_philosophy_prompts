I'm a postdoc in philosophy of mind, and in a few weeks I'm giving a colloquium defending a version of illusionism about phenomenal consciousness. The thesis isn't the bog-standard one: I'm arguing that introspection is a representational process that reports on informational states but never reveals their vehicles, so when we claim to find phenomenal properties in introspection we are really misreading a theoretical posit as something directly observed. The argument has two main moves. First, everything introspection seems to disclose—contents, immediacy, effortlessness—can be got from the representational contents plus the access profile. Second, the leftover what-it's-likeness is better explained as a bundle of reactive dispositions and heuristics than as a property over and above the representations. I've run it passed a colleague who works on perception, and she thinks the representationalists will be mostly onside, but I'm less sure.

The audience is a philosophy department that's heavy on mind and perception: a committed naive realist who thinks perceptual experience is acquaintance with mind-independent objects, a disjunctivist, two representationalists who think phenomenal character just is a special kind of content, a cognitive-science philosopher who works on blindsight and unconscious perception, and a phenomenologist who works on for-me-ness. I'd like a Q&A prep sheet. Go objection by objection in roughly the order this crowd will actually raise them, each phrased the way the relevant person would put it. Tell me which objections have real force, what I can concede without losing the project, what I should hold, and how to reply in about two minutes. Also flag the weakest point in my argument—the one I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss—and tell me the question I'm quietly praying nobody asks. Please write the answer in British English.
