I work as a software engineer, and a recurring theme in my side reading has started to feel like a properly philosophical question rather than just a social-science one. Cross-national surveys consistently show that publics in the Nordic countries report high trust in their governments and public institutions, while American trust in government has been low and declining for decades. The standard explanation is that trust tracks institutional quality: Nordic states have low corruption, transparent administration, and broad universal welfare programs, so citizens have solid evidence that the state is competently serving their interests. On this picture, high trust is largely a rational response to a good institutional track record, and American suspicion is largely a rational response to a worse one. I find myself defending a somewhat stronger claim: that a default stance of high trust is the more reasonable equilibrium to try to reach, because it helps produce the very institutions that then justify it. High-trust publics are willing to fund programs whose benefits are deferred and to accept that some money will be misallocated, while low-trust publics demand tight monitoring and quick punishment of errors, which locks in adversarial relations and makes cooperative institutions harder to build. So on my view the Scandinavian attitude is not merely understandable in its own context; it is closer to the normatively correct baseline, and entrenched American suspicion, even when it tracks real institutional defects, is partly self-fulfilling.

I would like you to treat this as a position to stress-test rather than to advocate for. You are an expert in political philosophy and social epistemology. Please begin by brainstorming candidate defeaters, then give me a structured map of what would genuinely count against the claim that high default trust is the more reasonable attitude. In particular, separate empirical findings or arguments that would be decisive from those that would merely be awkward. Are there cases where high trust coexists with corruption, capture, or bad policy in a way that breaks the tracking story? Could Nordic trust depend on factors such as ethnic homogeneity, a distinctive founding bargain, or particular welfare-state institutions in a way that makes it non-exportable, and would that undermine the normative claim rather than just the policy one? Does the error-cost asymmetry ultimately cut the other way, so that trusting a bad institution is so much riskier than distrusting a good one that suspicion is the rational default after all? I also want to know which concessions a defender of the position should make, whether the position is in danger of insulating itself through its feedback-loop claim, and what you think the strongest single objection to it really is.
