Consider Nozick's experience machine. In the standard version, a device can stimulate your brain to produce any experiences you choose, indistinguishable from reality, while your body floats in a tank. Nozick's point is that many people would still refuse to plug in, because they want to actually do and be certain things rather than merely have the experience of doing and being them, and because they want genuine contact with reality.

Now suppose the machine is redesigned so that it offers only epistemic experiences. Inside it, you live out a life as a researcher: you formulate questions, read, get stuck, and occasionally undergo the full phenomenology of breakthrough—the feeling of a confusion suddenly clearing up, a line of reasoning snapping into place, and the sense of having genuinely come to understand something. Before plugging in, you are given a guarantee: every proposition that will present itself to you as a discovery is false, and none of what feels like insight can be carried out of the machine or put to any use afterward. Once inside, you will not remember this guarantee, and your breakthroughs will feel, in every detail, like real understanding of real truths.

How should someone who thinks knowledge is valuable for its own sake answer the question of whether to plug in? In particular, does this variant become more or less attractive to them than Nozick's original machine, and why? Work through the main positions available to such a person: that the felt satisfaction of curiosity is what has final value, so the machine should be acceptable; that final value attaches only to genuine knowledge, which requires truth, so the guarantee matters; or that what matters is the agent's actual relation to the world rather than the quality of the experience. Finally, say which of Nozick's original anti-machine considerations still carry weight in this variant and which are neutralized by the fact that the machine now supplies exactly the experiences a lover of knowledge might claim to value.
