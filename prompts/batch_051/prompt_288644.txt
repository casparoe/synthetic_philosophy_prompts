I am writing a paper for a course on justice across generations, and I keep coming back to the Montreal Protocol as a case that gets cited constantly as a success story yet is rarely examined as a problem in intergenerational ethics. Take the broad facts as uncontroversial for this exercise: in 1987 states agreed to phase out ozone-depleting chlorofluorocarbons; by then the scientific community had strong convergent evidence that CFCs were thinning the stratospheric ozone layer; the principal anticipated harms—elevated skin-cancer rates, more cataracts, disrupted marine and terrestrial ecosystems—would fall largely on future people, while the main anticipated costs would fall on present-day industries, workers, and consumers; and in the actual event substitutes proved faster and cheaper than expected, while developing countries were given a longer timetable and financial support for compliance. I am not asking about the atmospheric science, the diplomacy, or the treaty's legal architecture.

The lens I want to use is the debate about pure time preference in intergenerational justice. One side holds that future benefits may be discounted simply because they arrive in the future; the other side holds that counting someone's welfare for less merely because of when she is born is an arbitrary temporal partiality, morally akin to discounting someone by geography or race. Analyze the Montreal Protocol through this lens, and be as disciplined about the mapping as you can. Who or what has standing in the analysis—present industries, present consumers, developing-country governments, future persons? What does the pure-time-preference view imply the parties were doing when they accepted near-term costs for harms that would mostly land after many of them were dead, and what does the opposing view imply they should have done? Then indicate which features of the case each view handles well and which it must stretch to accommodate, such as the unexpectedly cheap substitutes, the grace periods for developing countries, and the fact that many living adults also stood to benefit. Finally, state what the episode should do to the underlying debate: does it vindicate pure time discounting, refute it, or reveal that the phrase bundles together several separable considerations that Montreal disentangles? Keep the analysis honest, and say where the theory is silent rather than forcing it to speak.
