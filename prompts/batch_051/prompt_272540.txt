Act as a professional philosopher of history and historical epistemology. I keep noticing that discussions of whether history has a direction — whether the arc of history bends toward justice, whether there is providence or a telos, or whether history has no structure at all beyond one thing after another — proceed by pointing at one or two loaded examples. Someone cites the abolition of chattel slavery or the spread of human rights norms and concludes the arc bends; someone else cites the genocides of the twentieth century, the persistence of mass incarceration, or current emissions trajectories and concludes the opposite. Before I accept or reject any theory of historical direction, I want the full specification of what such a theory would have to get right.

Give me the desiderata: the cases a theory must handle, the distinctions it cannot collapse, and the results from other fields it cannot contradict, with the reason each is a genuine requirement rather than a prejudice of one camp. Things I suspect belong on the list, though you should adjudicate rather than just accept them: local reversals versus global trend; whether the relevant trend is toward justice rather than toward something else (complexity, expanding moral circles, state capacity, explanatory unification); whether apparent directionality can be an artifact of scale, of survivorship bias in the records, or of winners writing history; whether a directional theory must be predictive or only retrospective; whether it needs a mechanism rather than a mere observed regularity; whether catastrophic regressions count as part of the arc or against it; and whether the standard of justice that the arc allegedly bends toward is allowed to shift across history. Tell me which requirements are negotiable and which are not, whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable (in which case the list itself is a result), and which single requirement knocks out the most popular theories. Then briefly score the main families of views — providential or teleological views, progress narratives, and discontinuity or anti-direction views — against the list, but keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases rather than on the scoring. A table with columns for the requirement, why it is a requirement, and whether it is negotiable is fine.

Start with some brainstorming about candidate requirements before settling on your final list. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the most important requirement and the overall shape of the list — and then give the reasoning. No preamble, no restating of the question.
