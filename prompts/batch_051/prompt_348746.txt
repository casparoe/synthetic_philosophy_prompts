I've been trying to get my head around the low-impact AI debate and I keep hitting the same wall. The question is simple to state: can we get an agent to avoid side effects without first giving it a full specification of what we value? Everyone agrees you'd like a system you can point at some dull task — fetching a coffee, cleaning a room, writing a file — and have it do that task without also rerouting the economy to make coffee faster. The disagreement is about whether "side effect" can even be defined without a value function.

One camp says yes. Pick an inaction baseline: what would have happened if the agent did nothing. Then define impact as some distance between the actual future and that baseline, and penalize the agent for divergence. The baseline and the distance metric do the work that a utility function would normally do, but — they claim — without anyone having to specify what's good.

The other camp says that's just value specification wearing a disguise. Choosing the baseline means declaring the status quo (or "nothing happens") as the reference point worth preserving. Choosing the distance metric means deciding which differences between futures count. That's most of what a utility function does; you've just hidden the normative choices inside the measurement apparatus. And efforts to make the metric neutral seem to either collapse (any departure at all counts, so the agent never does the task) or to smuggle in a hidden model of what matters about the world.

I can't tell wether this is a technical impasse, a confusion about the word "side effect," or the old point that you can't get values out of non-values. It feels like one of those debates where both sides are right under different notions of "impact."

So, what kind of disagreement is this? Is there a hidden formal or factual question underneath — like whether any nontrivial impact measure can satisfy some plausible axioms — or is it really just the value-specification problem resurfacing under a new name? And what would actually settle it: a theorem, a sharper distinction, changing the question, or is that the wrong hope entirely? Be honest if you think one side is muddled.
