In a reading group on moral deference, I constructed an argument that now seems to show far more than I can accept, and I cannot locate the step to blame.

By "pure moral testimony" I mean adopting a moral judgment on another person's say-so while lacking independent access to the facts that ground it.

(1) If it is permissible to form a moral belief by pure moral testimony, then it is permissible to act on that belief without being able to cite, in your own words, the right-making or wrong-making features of what you are doing.
(2) A person is answerable for acting on a moral judgment only if they can cite those features in their own words.
(3) If a person cannot answer for an action in this sense, they should not perform it.
(4) Therefore, one should never act on a moral belief formed by pure moral testimony.
(5) But this proves too much. We routinely and blamelessly rely on others' moral say-so in just this way: an employee tells me a contract term is exploitative, a colleague reports that a hiring practice is discriminatory, a friend describes the treatment of their child as unjust. In each case the hearer is moved by the report while the underlying facts remain inaccessible to them.
(6) If the argument is sound, all of that is impermissible. That cannot be right.

I would most like to keep (2), because answerability to others feels essential to moral agency, not a dispensable extra. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If you see more than one vulnerable step, tell me which rejection is least costly. Please end with a one-sentence summary of the diagnosis as the final line.
