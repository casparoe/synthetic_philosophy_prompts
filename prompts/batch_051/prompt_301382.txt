I am a novelist with an amateur's interest in the philosophy of decision, and I keep running into a configuration in my plotting that I suspect has a name in the literature, but I do not know the name or how precisely the field has drawn its boundaries.

A character faces a choice whose predictable consequence is to alter the character's own evaluative outlook: not merely her beliefs, her circumstances, or her information, but the standards by which she will later assess whether the choice was good. The change is not a side effect to be weighed; it is central to the choice. Before she decides, she cannot know the relevant outcome in the usual sense, because the outcome includes what it is like to be the person with the new outlook, and that is accessible only from inside. Asking people who have made the choice is oddly unhelpful: they answer from within the new outlook, which is precisely the thing whose adoption is in question, or they offer aphorisms that do not translate back into the deliberator's current terms. Ordinary expected-value reasoning appears to require values stable enough to weight the outcomes, but this kind of choice rewrites the very values the calculation would have to use.

This is not ordinary risk, where outcomes are uncertain but values are fixed. It is not ordinary preference change over time, because the change is foreseeable in advance and foreseeing it does not make the choice easier; it makes the problem harder. It is also not exactly regret, because the difficulty is present before the choice, not only after it.

The cases in my fiction are the usual life-changing ones: a first child, conversion, emigration, marriage across a deep difference, enlisting, taking a vocation that will consume a person and make her other than she was. In each, the person who deliberates is, in a real but hard-to-state way, not the person who will live with the decision.

I want to know whether this configuration has an established name in decision theory or the philosophy of action. In particular: is it one phenomenon or two — one about the limits of knowing what an experience will be like from outside, and another about a choice changing the chooser's values — and can either occur without the other? Do the relevant accounts distinguish changes in mere preferences from changes in judgments about value, and should they? And what do they say about whether, and under what constraints, deliberation about such a choice can be rational?

A bare name will not satisfy me; I need the carving — the boundary cases, the distinctions that do the work, and the points where the main accounts come apart. Please do not supply a bibliography or reading list. Begin your response with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
