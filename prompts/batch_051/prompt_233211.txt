For the past two years a seminar I convene on the foundations of privacy law has been circling the same impasse, and I have begun to suspect that the impasse itself is the phenomenon worth understanding. One camp argues that privacy is essentially an agent's control over information about herself: privacy is violated exactly when a person's power to determine who has access to facts about her is overridden or bypassed. On this view, if someone has genuinely consented to a disclosure, the resulting access is not a loss of privacy, because the relevant power remains intact. The other camp argues that privacy is fundamentally a state of limited access to a person, whether or not she chose it: someone can have her privacy destroyed by an increase in access even when no one disregarded her choices, and someone can give her privacy away without realizing it, simply because access has grown. A third proposal, that privacy is really a continuous practice of negotiating informational boundaries with different audiences, attracted brief enthusiasm in the group but was eventually set aside because it seemed to describe the regulation of privacy while leaving the underlying thing unexplained.

The two main camps now agree on a stable set of paradigm cases. Both treat a stranger watching someone through an uncurtained window as a violation; both treat a company that infers a pregnancy from accumulated purchase records as a violation; both treat a person who publishes her own diary as having surrendered privacy rather than had it taken; and both agree that privacy matters because of something about persons, not merely because of its downstream costs. Yet these agreements have produced no convergence. Each camp can accept the other's cases by giving them a slightly different description, and each proposed reformulation gets defeated by the same move.

I want a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement rather than a ruling on which account is correct. Is this a merely verbal dispute about which states deserve to be called private? Is it a substantive disagreement about the value of privacy, and specifically about whether privacy's value is intrinsic or entirely derivative from goods such as autonomy, intimacy, dignity, or the integrity of the self? Is there an unnoticed difference in what the two camps think a theory of privacy must do, such as describe a condition, articulate a right, or guide legal judgment? Or is one side answering a descriptive question and the other a normative question without noticing it, as I have begun to suspect? And given that diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a distinction that dissolves the appearance of rivalry, a ranking of cases that both accounts must reproduce, a further set of cases chosen to isolate the point of divergence, or an argument that the original question was ill posed?
