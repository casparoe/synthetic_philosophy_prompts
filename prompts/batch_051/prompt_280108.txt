I have been reading about Quinn's self-torturer in a rational choice course, and our study group has produced a reconstruction of the puzzle that none of us can satisfactorily diagnose. The case, as we are using it, runs as follows. A person can move a device through settings 0 to 1000; for each one-setting increase he is paid $10,000. The added pain at each move is so small that he cannot discriminate between adjacent settings, and he is happy to take every step. But the difference between setting 0 and setting 1000 is severe, and he would refuse a single direct offer to move from 0 to 1000 for the same total amount of money, all in one go.

We want to know whether the following argument fails, and if so, at exactly which step and on which reading. (1) For each k from 0 to 999, the self-torturer rationally prefers setting k+1 over setting k, since that setting is financially better and, for him, indistinguishable in pain. (2) Rational preference is transitive across finite chains: if an agent rationally prefers each later state in a chain over its immediate predecessor, then she rationally prefers the final state over the initial one. (3) Settings 0 and 1000 are the endpoints of such a finite chain, and by (1) the self-torturer satisfies the chain condition. (4) Therefore the self-torturer rationally prefers setting 1000 over setting 0. (5) But the self-torturer does not rationally prefer setting 1000 over setting 0, since he refuses the one-shot move for the same total payment, and this refusal does not seem irrational. (6) So the case appears to show either that one of these premises must be given up, or that the notion of rational preference is not univocal across the argument. Is the argument valid? Which step fails, and what exactly is the error — for example, does it conflate the phenomenal indistinguishability of adjacent settings with the absence of a reason, or does it smuggle in a transitivity assumption the example is designed to test?
