I am a doctoral student in political and legal theory, and part of my dissertation asks how far Jeremy Bentham's approach to sexual morality can be extended to problems that postdate him. In the chapter I am currently drafting, I am trying to reconstruct what Bentham would say about the non-consensual creation and distribution of deepfake pornography: synthetically produced sexual imagery that depicts a real, identifiable person without that person's consent. His actual position on the regulation of sex gives me a starting point, and it is more specific than the familiar utilitarian slogan. He argued that consensual sexual conduct, including same-sex conduct, should not be punished, because it produces genuine pleasures and because punishing it inflicts pain without preventing any comparable pain to an assignable person. He traced much of the existing criminal law in this area to what he called the principle of antipathy and to asceticism, neither of which he regarded as a legitimate substitute for utility. And he insisted that the disgust, shock, or moral offence of observers is not by itself an adequate ground for legal punishment; the relevant question, on his view, is whether an identifiable person is made worse off, and how reliably the law can trace that worsening.

The deepfake case troubles me because it seems to fall between his categories. No sexual act involving the depicted person occurs; what is taken or fabricated is the person's appearance, and the image may exist without the person ever learning of it. Yet such images, once circulated, can humiliate, blackmail, or destroy a person's standing, and their production can feel like a violation even before any third party sees them. This is unlike the consensual conduct Bentham defended, and it is also unlike a straightforward assault on body or property. I would like a careful, internal reconstruction of how he would reason, from his own principles, about three stages: producing a non-consensual deepfake, sharing it, and merely possessing or viewing it in private. In particular, where would he locate the relevant pains and pleasures; would he count the depicted person's distress as a real harm or as something closer to embarrassment or wounded feeling; and would his analysis support punishing production and distribution while leaving private possession untouched? If his principles would yield different verdicts at different stages, I want the exact point at which the balance tips.

A contrast may sharpen the problem. A fully synthetic sexual image of a person who does not exist seems, on Bentham's framework, to remove the assignable victim altogether, leaving only the offence of observers as an objection, and he usually refused to treat that as a proper basis for punishment. If that is right, then the deepfake of a real person is the hard case precisely because the victim is assignable but the harm is mediated by representation and publicity. I would be interested in whether Bentham's concepts of security, reputation, and the thwarting of expectation would have to be stretched to cover this, and whether he would have been willing to stretch them.
