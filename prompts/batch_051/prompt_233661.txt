Write a piece of sustained satirical parody in the form of the minutes of a three-member committee of a small liberal-arts college, roughly 700 to 800 words, in the deadpan, faintly euphemistic style of real committee minutes. Write it as continuous prose, as though a punctilious secretary set the record down in ordinary paragraphs: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables. The committee is deciding some suitably small-bore academic matter—the conditions attached to a modest bequest, the naming of a seminar room, the disposal of a defunct journal's back issues—so that the stakes stay comic rather than serious.

The committee's procedure is scrupulously democratic. It considers the question clause by clause, voting by majority on each subordinate element separately, and the secretary then compiles the collective position from those separate votes. To be concrete, the three members' votes should follow the classic profile of the discursive dilemma. The first member accepts both subordinate clauses and therefore votes for the overall conclusion; the second accepts the first clause, rejects the second, and therefore votes against the conclusion; the third rejects the first clause, accepts the second, and therefore votes against the conclusion. Each member is thereby perfectly consistent individually, and all three accept that the two clauses, if both true, settle the conclusion. By majority, the committee nevertheless affirms each clause separately while rejecting the conclusion that its own affirmed clauses require.

The members should be reasonable, articulate, and slightly self-congratulatory about their procedural fairness, and the minute-taker should be oblivious, recording the votes as unremarkable. The comedy should come from the logic of aggregation itself and from the bureaucratic prose, not from anyone being stupid or venal. Do not name or explain the discursive dilemma within the piece; a social-choice theorist should recognize a fair instance of it, while a lay reader should be able to feel the contradiction without having it pointed out. The target of the satire is the practice of clause-by-clause majoritarian aggregation and the accompanying assumption that rational individuals plus a democratic procedure must yield a rational group.
