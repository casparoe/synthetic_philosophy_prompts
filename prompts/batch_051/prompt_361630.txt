hey — im teaching an upper-level seminar on strategic choice (mostly philosophy students, ~18 people, one 75-minute session). The topic is commitment races: situations where each side wants to lock itself into a course of action before the others can, because locking in first lets you force everyone else to adapt, but when everybody races to lock in first the group often lands somewhere nobody wanted. I want the students to feel that happen before I name it. Design a 45-minute in-class game with concrete rules, a non-monetary payoff scheme, and roles if needed. It should produce an actual rush to commit — publishing a number, signing a public card, passing an irreversible token — where individual players can see that committing early is smart for them but the escalating race is self-defeating for the group. It should not just be a prisoner's dilemma with new labels: the bad outcome has to come specifically from the race to lock in, so that once someone commits, it changes what's left for everyone else, and part of the unease is watching the window for a good joint outcome close. By the end of the session the students should be able to distinguish a commitment race from both ordinary defection and a coordination failure. Then write a 20-minute debrief that maps moments in the game onto the parts of the idea — the individual pull to commit, the first-mover advantage, simultaneous commitment, and the collective regret — and identifies where each could have been resisted and what it would have cost. Be honest about what a classroom game can't reproduce (genuine irreversibility, stakes you really fear) and how I should supply that in the debrief, plus what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. You're an expert on strategic interaction and experimental teaching; think carefully and brainstorm three candidate designs before picking one.
