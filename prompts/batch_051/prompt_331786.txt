Ok so I just had a long argument with a friend about this and I'm trying to work out what the practice actually commits someone to, as opposed to what my friend was projecting onto it.

Take consumer software and hardware design. A huge amount of effort goes into making the tool vanish into the task: auto-save instead of a visible save button, gesture controls with no interface chrome, notifications that fire only when something breaks, onboarding that tries to make the whole thing feel obvious, and the shared assumption that a well-designed app is one you don't notice. The tool is suposed to disappear until it fails. And yet the same practice is obsessed with failure: progress bars, error messages, status indicators, recovery wizards. The moment something resists, the tool becomes the centre of attention.

My friend's line, which he argued with real confidence, is that this practice presupposes a Heideggerian picture: equipment in absorbed use is ready-to-hand, and it only shows up as present-at-hand when it breaks or refuses. He thinks designers are doing applied Heidegger without realising it, and that the whole ideal of "seamless" design only makes sense if you have already bought that story about tool use. I'm not convinced the practice needs the full Heideggerian story. It might just presuppose something much thinner, like attention being a scarce resource that interfaces should spare.

So what I'd like is a careful audit. What theory of tools, attention, and breakdown does this design practice strictly presuppose, and what does it merely fit well with? Where, if anywhere, is it actually incoherent — for example, treating "invisible except when broken" as the ideal while also insisting that the user be shown exactly what the system is doing the moment it hiccups? And what would have to change in software practice if we replaced the invisibility-until-breakdown presupposition with something closer to its opposite: that a tool should sometimes announce itself and make its workings visable even when nothing is wrong?
