I've been going back and forth with a friend about whether law is basically force with better PR. He keeps bringing up Pascal—in the *Pensées*, I think—saying that justice by itself is too disputed to command obedience, while force by itself is obvious but looks like tyranny, so every stable legal order has to fuse them. Pascal's punchline, as my friend tells it, is that you can't make what is just strong, so you end up making what is strong look just. That makes sense as a description of an absolute monarchy, where coercion wore a uniform and carried a sword.

But I wonder whether the arguement survives when force stops being that visible. These days coercion mostly arrives as a notice in the mail, a frozen account, an automated fine, or some eligibility score that quietly stops you from getting housing or a loan. Nobody swings a sword; you just hit a wall of code and procedure. So does Pascal's claim still go through? I'm especially interested in which premise this actually pressures—the idea that justice is contested, the idea that force is undisputed and easily recognized, or something else—and whether there's a repaired version of his position that's still true for bureaucratic or algorithmic enforcement. Don't just give me a survey of what commentators say; I want you to actually work through the argument and defend a verdict.
