I'm a novelist, and for the last few months I've been trying to write a story that leans on the old Wimsatt and Beardsley idea from "The Intentional Fallacy" — the one about how once a text is out in the world, what the author intended doesn't control what it means. I don't want to write an essay about it; I want the notion to operate as the engine of a story. Could you draft a short story for me? Here's the seed I keep circling: a midlist literary author in his late seventies shows up, unannounced, at the offices of a small but influential journal that published one of his early stories. That story has been anthologized for decades as a critique of political quietism, and the author now insists it was actually about his dead brother's religious crisis. The only person willing to hear him out is an assistant editor in her late twenties who has a tattoo of the magazine's motto on her wrist.

The story should make the reader feel the pull of the author's private anguish without resolving whether his intention should matter to how we read the story. Maybe the assistant editor starts digging through his personal archive for evidence, or maybe the magazine is preparing a fiftieth-anniversary republication with the standard reading blurbed on the back. I'm not attached to those turns. The important thing is that the narrative dramatizes the conflict between the author's remembered meaning and the public text, rather than arguing for one side. Keep it grounded — no magical-realist device where the author can literally revise the past. And please don't make the assistant editor a naive stand-in for authorial intentionalism; she should be smart, overworked, and genuinely uncertain about what editorial fidelity would even mean in this situation.
