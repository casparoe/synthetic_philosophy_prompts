I teach a graduate seminar in formal epistemology, and next week I want to devote one session to the Judy Benjamin problem. The students already know how to conditionalize on propositional evidence and can work with partitions and priors, but most of them have not thought about cases in which the evidence is not a proposition one comes to accept but rather a constraint on a conditional probability. I want the session to make three things clear: first, why standard conditionalization gives no answer at all in such a case; second, why the most natural-looking proposed solution, minimizing cross-entropy subject to the new constraint, yields the surprising result that probabilities shift in regions the message never mentioned; and third, why that result has been taken to reveal something general about updating on conditionals rather than being a quirk of a military example. Plan the session for me. What concept has to be securely in place before I introduce the problem, and in what order should the pieces go so the mathematical result does not seem like a trick? What simplified two-by-two case should carry the whole session, and what must that case have and lack? What misconceptions should I expect students to form—for example, that learning a conditional probability is just conditionalizing on the corresponding material conditional, or that the proposed update rule is merely an arbitrary convention—and at what point in the sequence should each be headed off? Finally, what one question at the end would show me whether they have understood why the problem is hard, as opposed to merely being able to reproduce the calculation?
