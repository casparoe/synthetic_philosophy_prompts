I'm taking a seminar on the metaphysics of propositions, and I've gotten stuck on the classic problem sometimes called Bradley's regress. The problem begins from the thought that a proposition is not a mere list of its constituents: in the proposition that Socrates is wise, Socrates and wisdom are combined into a single truth-evaluable whole. The natural explanation is that a relation—perhaps instantiation, or predication—combines them. But the relation then seems to become just one more constituent, and something must combine it with Socrates and wisdom, and so on. I've tried to make the regress precise as follows:

(1) A proposition is a complex whole whose constituents are united by a relation.
(2) A relation unites two constituents only by standing in a further relation to each of them.
(3) Each such further relation is itself an additional constituent of the complex, so by (2) it too must stand in yet further relations to whatever it relates.
(4) Therefore, the unity of any proposition requires an infinite chain of further uniting relations.
(5) An explanation of unity that requires an infinite chain of uniting relations is vicious, because at each stage the would-be unifier still awaits unification and the original unity is never actually secured.
(6) Therefore, no relation can explain the unity of a proposition.

I half-suspect the trouble is in (2) or (5), but I can't say exactly what goes wrong without simply abandoning the relational account. Is the argument valid as it stands? If not, which step fails, and what would a corrected version of that step say? If it is valid, which premise should a defender of a relational account reject, and what alternative account of propositional unity does that leave them with?
