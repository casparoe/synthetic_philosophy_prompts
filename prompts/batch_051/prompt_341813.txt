I've been rereading Schopenhauer's The World as Will and Idea, and in book 3 (§52 I think) he makes this striking claim, in the Haldane/Kemp translation something to the effect that music differs from the other arts because they copy the Platonic Ideas, whereas music copies the will itself. That is supposed to explain both why music hits us harder than painting or poetry and why we can't say in words what a piece of music "means." But here's what bothers me: we now have decent cross-cultural evidence that recognizing the emotion in an unfamiliar musical tradition is surprisingly poor — people do worse than their confidence suggests, and a lot of what feels natural (major = happy, minor = sad) is learned. If music really were a direct copy of the will, prior to representation, shouldn't its effect be much more universal and unlearned? Does this evidence kill Schopenhauer's argument for the special status of music, or does it only kill a strong version of it and leave a weaker formal claim (tension and resolution, striving and satisfaction) intact? Be specific about which premise the cross-cultural data bears on, and whether the argument can be repaired or is dead.
