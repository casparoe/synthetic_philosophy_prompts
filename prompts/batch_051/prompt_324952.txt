Question: According to Hobsbawm and Anderson, roughly how old are nations? Explain what each author means by an invented tradition and an imagined community, and state whether these accounts imply that nations are unreal.

Student answer: Nations as currently understood are modern, not ancient. Anderson argues that a nation is an imagined community: its members will never know most of their fellow nationals, yet they participate in a shared sense of horizontal comradeship. Print capitalism spread vernacular print-languages, which allowed strangers to regard themselves as a single reading public and which replaced older religious and dynastic forms of community. For Anderson, calling the nation imagined therefore means it is ultimately a fiction—a false belief his analysis is designed to expose. Hobsbawm's notion of invented tradition points in the same direction: many customs presented as immemorial national heritage were actually created relatively recently, often deliberately by elites seeking to give a state or a movement a pedigree; the Scottish kilt, discussed in the volume he co-edited, is a well-known example. The conclusion is that nations are top-down inventions with no real existence and no deep hold on the lives of ordinary people.
