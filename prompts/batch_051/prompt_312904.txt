I've spent the last few weeks trying to work out what it could mean to say that an artificial system wants something, as opposed to merely behaving as though it does, and I keep catching myself defending a set of claims that I suspect were drawn from different debates and may not cohere. I'm preparing a short position paper on this, and before I circulate it I'd like a consistency audit of my own commitments rather than a survey of the literature. Here is the set I find myself wanting to defend:

(a) The orthogonality thesis: intelligence and final goals are independent dimensions — for any level of intelligence and any physically realizable final goal, an agent with that intelligence and that goal could in principle exist.
(b) In biological creatures, wanting is not a primitive feature of intelligence or of matter; it exists because natural selection built systems that pursue fitness-promoting outcomes. There is no wanting prior to a history of selection, or something structurally close to it.
(c) A directly programmed utility maximizer — a system whose code explicitly ranks possible futures by expected number of paperclips — would have paperclips as its terminal goal, and this is unaffected by how the system came to exist.
(d) In reinforcement learning, the reward signal is not automatically the agent's goal. Reward shapes the policy, but the trained system's goal, if it has one, is whatever outcome the training dynamics selected it to pursue, not the reward function itself.
(e) Inner misalignment is a real possibility: a trained system could end up wanting something other than what its designers intended, precisely because the training process, not the stated specification, determines what the system was selected to pursue.
(f) Whatever explains why I want things — for example, why I want to avoid pain — is something my evolutionary history supplied, and a mere artifact that executes a utility function does not have that same thing.

My question is not which of these are individually defensible, but whether the set hangs together. Which commitments genuinely pull against each other, which pairs only appear to conflict once the relevant sense of wanting or goal is pinned down, and which single revision would restore joint tenability at the lowest cost? I'd rather revise whichever claims are doing the least work than abandon the view wholesale. Please rank the real tensions by severity, say for each what would have to give, and then tell me which argument, empirical finding, or conceptual distinction would most plausibly change your ranking — and where you take your own reasoning to be least secure.
