I've been turning over the legal doctrine of sovereign immunity: the principle that the state cannot be sued in its own courts without its consent, descended from the old maxim that the king can do no wrong. Most modern defenses treat this as a practical compromise, on the grounds that a government makes millions of discretionary decisions and could not function if each one were open to litigation. But I want to test whether the maxim has a surviving moral core. Specifically, I want to know whether there is a real sense in which a state can wrong a person even when no individual officer's action, considered on its own, counts as a moral wrong by that officer.

Construct a thought experiment to my specifications. I want a single, realistic case in which a person is morally wronged by a state, and yet the wrong cannot be pinned on any individual. Constraints: (1) a modern, roughly democratic administrative state with ordinary institutions—no dictator, no conspiracy, no science-fictional technology, no exceptional malice on anyone's part; (2) every candidate wrongdoer you might name must have acted within their role, on the information reasonably available to them at the time, in a way that at the individual level was at least excusable and arguably defensible; (3) the narrative itself should be compact, no more than a paragraph or two, while the analysis may run as long as it needs; (4) it must remain intuitive that a wrong occurred, not merely that a bad outcome occurred—the reader should feel that this person was done an injustice, not just that something unfortunate happened.

After the scenario, run the check. Name the natural candidate wrongdoers in your case—the rule-drafter, the caseworker, the supervisor, the minister, the voter, the software vendor, or whoever else the case supplies—and show for each why the case withholds individual blame. Then identify the specific feature of the case that does the work. Is it the distribution of information across roles? The gap between the design of a procedure and what it outputs in aggregate? The absence of any point in the process at which the full picture is visible? Something else? Say what happens to the case if that one feature is removed, and whether the intuition of a wrong survives the removal. Finish by laying out the two positions the case is built to adjudicate. The corporate-agency reading says the state itself is the bearer of the wrong, and that this is what makes sovereign immunity morally suspect even where it is administratively convenient. The reductionist reading says a wrong without any individual wrongdoer is a conceptual confusion, and that the intuition should be explained away rather than admitted. Give each side its strongest version—steelman both—and then say what, if anything, the case actually licenses as a conclusion.
