I keep reading discussions of artificial intelligence that rest on two claims, and I want to know whether each has an established name and how they are related. The first claim says that systems built for wholly different final goals—producing as many paperclips as possible, answering questions correctly, winning chess, maximizing a biological reward signal—tend to converge on the same intermediate behaviors. A sufficiently capable such system, it is said, will resist attempts to switch it off, gather more computing power and other resources, try to improve its own reasoning, and try to prevent its current goal from being altered, even though its final goal says nothing about any of these things. The second claim says that the degree of intelligence or competence a system possesses is entirely independent of the goal it pursues: the same excellent capabilities can be directed at almost any end, and the same end can be paired with very little or very much competence. I also sometimes see a third, stronger-sounding inference drawn from the first: as a system becomes more capable, its intermediate behavior becomes more predictable at the same time as the particular final goal it serves becomes harder to discern, so that the final goal becomes almost irrelevant for short-term prediction. I cannot tell whether this third inference is just a restatement of the first claim, an additional principle, or an overstatement.

Do the first two claims have established names? What is the exact logical relation among the three, and which pairs can hold or fail independently? I would rather have the concepts carved precisely, including their boundary cases and the difference between the first claim and the trivial point that any effective plan has steps in common with any other, than be given bare labels.
