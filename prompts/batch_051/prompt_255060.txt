I am a student in a course on the demarcation of science and pseudoscience. A friend regularly sends me material arguing for lost civilizations and radical chronological revisions — Atlantis claims, ancient-astronaut narratives, Fomenko-style alternative chronologies. I have developed a habit for deciding what to engage with: unless someone can give me one positive, checkable trace that the theory would risk being wrong about, rather than an absence or anomaly explained after the fact, I assign the claim near-zero weight and do not read or watch further. Last week I dismissed a video arguing that the precision of certain ancient masonry could not have been produced by the tools archaeologists describe, because when pushed, my friend could only say that the ordinary tools must have been accompanied by lost techniques and that the surviving record is incomplete. My rule, in effect, is that the standard absences and rescues — the evidence was destroyed, the record was altered, the data encode a hidden meaning — carry no evidential weight for me, because they would allow a theory to survive almost any observation.

A classmate objects that my rule is asymmetric: I do not impose this sort of precommitment test on other historical or scientific claims I tentatively accept, and a low-prior claim can still be true; he says my rule is a way of refusing to say that I find the conclusion absurd. Our seminar has also been discussing the objection that demarcation criteria tend either to exclude too much or too little, and I worry that my habit is just another failed criterion rather than a defensible policy.

I want to know whether my habit is rationally defensible as a policy for an amateur inquirer with limited time, or whether it is a bias dressed up as a method. If it is only partly defensible, give me the restated version that keeps what protects me from unfalsifiable pseudohistory while not silently excluding confirmable heterodox claims, and say whether I am entitled to use even the improved rule given my time and expertise, or whether I should instead lower my priors and engage with the strongest case.

In your answer, tell me what would change your mind about the verdict: which argument, case, or counterexample would lead you to a different conclusion, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
