I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am preparing a long piece on epiphenomenalism and the particular difficulty it creates for judgments about our own conscious experience. Let me set out my terms as I use them. By a phenomenal judgment I mean a judgment or report about what one's own experience is like: that seeing red feels this way, that a pain is located in my hand, that a sound has just entered my awareness. Epiphenomenalism about consciousness is the view that phenomenal experience is caused by physical processes in the brain but exerts no causal influence of its own, including on what we say or believe about it. The paradox of phenomenal judgment is then the apparent bind: if our reports of experience are produced entirely by underlying physical processes, the felt character of the experience seems to play no role in why we produce those reports, and yet we treat those reports as a uniquely secure form of self-knowledge.

Before I begin interviewing philosophers and neuroscientists, I want a specification of what any adequate theory of phenomenal judgment must achieve. The theory I need is one whose job is to explain how a physical system like us ends up producing sincere, detailed, apparently accurate reports about what its experience is like, and whether those reports can count as knowledge even if experience itself is epiphenomenal. Give me the requirements list: the phenomena it has to explain; the awkward cases it has to get right — blindsight, subliminal or masked perception, dissociations between task performance and subjective confidence, and the conceivability of a behavioral duplicate with no experience; the distinctions it cannot collapse — above all, the line between the functional availability of information for reasoning and report and the felt quality of experience; and the empirical and conceptual constraints it must respect. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it. Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable constraints on any serious theory and which are merely preferences of one camp. Tell me whether you believe some subset of the non-negotiable requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, and if so identify exactly which combination generates the conflict. Then score the main available positions against the list briefly — full epiphenomenalism with a story about how phenomenal judgments can nonetheless track the facts, positions that attribute real causal power to phenomenal states, and illusionism, which denies that phenomenal judgments are made true by any nonphysical features of consciousness. Keep the scoring short; the criteria are what I most need.

Finally, since I may rely on this for the article, state how confident you are in the list as a whole, and single out the one requirement you are least sure is a genuine constraint rather than a bit of framing inherited from one side of the debate.
