I am the student speaker at my high school's commencement ceremony in a few weeks, and I have been told to keep it under six minutes and to make it uplifting for the graduating class and their families. I do not want to give the usual address about how far we have come and how bright the future is, because I have been reading about the case that the agricultural revolution was a mistake. The argument runs that early farmers worked longer hours, had worse diets and more disease, and ended up in more sharply unequal societies than the hunter-gatherers they replaced, who should not be pictured as desperately scrabbling for survival but as, in important respects, more leisured and more equal. That argument has unsettled the story of progress I would otherwise lean on at a graduation, and I want the speech to be uplifting anyway, without pretending that every major transition is an upgrade.

The audience is mixed. A handful of teachers will recognize this argument from the popular histories and will be listening for whether I am doing it justice; most students and families will not have encountered it and may find any mention of prehistoric life strange. Write the speech so that the philosophical idea does real structural work rather than serving as a decorative fact. In particular, there should be one moment where a conventional commencement sentiment is stated plainly and then immediately complicated by the hunter-gatherer comparison, and the speech should use the comparison to ask, at the close of one stage and the start of another, how we will know whether we have made our lives better or only made them more productive. It should not become a takedown of education or a case against college.

It has to land as affection for my class rather than as cynicism or a lecture about the past. It must not require footnotes, sources, or quotations, and it should sound like something a seventeen-year-old could actually deliver aloud.
