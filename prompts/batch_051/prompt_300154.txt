I am taking an epistemology course, and after the unit on epistemic circularity my instructor wrote on the board what he called a compressed statement of the bootstrapping problem for reliabilism, which I have reproduced below from my notes. I have the sense that something in it is too fast — in particular, I know that track-record reasoning is often treated as perfectly respectable when we calibrate an instrument against independent evidence, so I do not yet see why the reliabilist's situation deserves to end in the word refuted. I also suspect the summary slides between a couple of different notions of circularity, and perhaps between a problem about justification and a problem about knowledge, but I cannot yet say whether those distinctions make a difference.

Here is the instructor's summary:

"Reliabilism says a belief is justified when it is produced by a reliable belief-forming process, and the subject need not have independent evidence that the process is reliable in order to be justified. From here one can apparently give oneself an epistemic free lunch. Suppose I look at a red wall in good light and form, on the basis of how it looks, the belief that the wall is red. I may then reason: the wall is red; the wall looks red to me; so on this occasion my color vision got things right. But notice that the premise that the wall is red was itself established by the very faculty being checked, so the faculty is both supplying the data and receiving the credit. If I repeat this across many objects and many occasions, I can inductively conclude that my color vision is reliable. With no prior evidence about my own reliability, I have bootstrapped my way to a justified belief, and arguably to knowledge, that my color vision is trustworthy. This is blatantly circular reasoning, and it shows that reliabilism cannot exclude the most transparently self-certifying inferences. Since any acceptable theory of justification must exclude those, reliabilism is refuted."

I would like you to treat this as an accuracy audit rather than as an invitation to take a side. Go through the summary and separate what a careful reliabilist would accept as fair, what is a simplification or conflation, and what is overstatement or an objection presented as a settled refutation. In particular, I would like you to say whether it matters to distinguish premise-circularity from rule-circularity here, whether justification and knowledge are being run together, and whether the bootstrapping charge is best read as showing that reliabilism is uniquely refuted or as a version of the problem of easy knowledge that threatens any view on which we can have basic perceptual knowledge without antecedent reasons for trusting perception. Finally, tell me which of the summary's conclusions survive once the errors are corrected, and what a defender of reliabilism would still owe an answer to.
