I'm one of the coordinators of a mutual credit exchange in the city where I live — a cross between a time bank and a community currency: members earn credits by doing things for other members (tutoring, fixing bikes, tax help, after-surgery care, that kind of thing) and spend credits on other members' services. We've been running on a simple one-hour-equals-one-credit rule and it keeps producing fights, and the fights are starting to poison the group's trust. Some members say an hour of a lawyer's time should be worth more than an hour of dog walking when they're trading directly with each other; others say the whole point of the exchange is that everyone's hour is equal, and the lawyer joined knowing that. We also get recurring disputes about negative balances: is letting a member run down to -40 credits just friendly credit, or is it usury with a friendlier face? The board has asked me to draft a procedure, not a one-off policy: a step-by-step decision checklist our volunteer arbitrators can use whenever a proposed trade or a balance limit gets flagged. We get two or three flags a week, the arbitrators usually have a hour per case, and any negative balance past 20 credits needs board sign-off. Our founder keeps paraphrasing Aristotle from some reading group years ago: fair exchange is reciprocal but proportional rather than one-for-one (the builder and the shoemaker don't swap one house for one shoe), money is there to make unlike things commensurable, and what actually holds the whole exchange together is mutual need; and he did not like money making money rather than serving real needs. I don't want a liturgy and the arbitrators will never read the texts, so no citations please — I want the principle turned into a usable decision tree or checklist. Make sure it handles at least these three: (1) a disputed direct trade between two very unlike services; (2) a member with an ongoing urgent need — say daily help after surgery — who wants to run a large negative balance; (3) a proposed trade in which the buyer doesn't strictly need the thing and the seller does. Give a worked example of each, including at least one where the checklist says we must disallow something the board would rather keep (we're not trying to create a hierarchy of trades). And say plainly where the procedure will still break down and an arbitrator has to use judgment, and which part of Aristotle's account stops doing work at that point.
