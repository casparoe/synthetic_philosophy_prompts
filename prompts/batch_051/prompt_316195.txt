I work as a software engineer, so my instinct on hearing a large claim is to ask what telemetry I would need to verify it, and to be precise about what the measured signal would and would not prove. Lately I keep hearing the claim that the world is becoming less violent, and I cannot tell whether it is an empirical claim about a trend in some quantity or a disguised choice about how to define and count violence.

I would like you to take that claim at its strongest and design the best concrete test of it you can. Please specify: the operational definition of violence you would count, including how you would handle borderline cases such as threats, psychological abuse, neglect, suicide, state-inflicted harms, and harms usually recorded as accidents or collateral damage; the metric itself, such as per capita risk versus absolute totals, raw counts versus severity-weighted harm, or incidence versus prevalence, and why that choice matters; the time window and geographic scope, and how you would avoid selecting periods or regions that flatter a prior; and the confounds you would need to adjust for, such as changing reporting rates, changing legal categories, population growth, or improving data collection.

Then tell me which results would count as evidence for the claim, which would count against it, and what no result would settle. In particular, I want to know whether the statement that the world is becoming less violent has a determinate truth value once all the data is in, or whether it is true only relative to a scoring function that reasonable people could reject. If the latter, say exactly where the metric choice ends and the empirical question begins, and whether any test could show that one scoring function is wrong rather than merely unflattering.
