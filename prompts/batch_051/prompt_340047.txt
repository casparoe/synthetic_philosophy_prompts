I am giving a talk next week to a mixed applied ethics and political philosophy workshop, and I need to be prepared for the question period. My thesis is that appeals to human dignity in contemporary human-rights and bioethics debates are doing either redundant or merely rhetorical work: in cases where dignity appears to settle a dispute, some other principle—autonomy, equality, or non-maleficence—is already doing the normative load. I plan to anchor the talk in Kant's discussion in the Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, where he says that rational beings are called persons because their nature already marks them out as ends in themselves, and that they therefore must not be used merely as means. My reading is that Kant there makes the normative restriction depend on a substantive metaphysical status claim, and that much present-day dignity talk wants to keep the normative force while quietly dropping or severing that metaphysics. I also connect this to the Macklin-style worry that dignity is a useless concept because it reduces to respect for persons or autonomy without adding anything.

The audience is not neutral. It includes one committed Kant scholar, two public-health ethicists who are sympathetic to Macklin's skepticism, a human-rights lawyer who thinks dignity does important work in treaty interpretation, and a moral philosopher who works on cognitive disability and moral status. I just had a long argument with a friend about all of this on the way home, and he thinks my position collapses into a mere defense of negative freedom.

Give me the objections in roughly the order they are likely to come, phrased as those specific audience members would phrase them, with what I should concede, what I should hold, and the weakest point in my own position I should prepare to defend rather than hope nobody raises. I want a preparation sheet, not a survey of the literature.
