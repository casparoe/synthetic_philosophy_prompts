Last evening I argued with a friend — she teaches reading in a primary school — late into the night about whether English is really an alphabet. Her view was that it is an alphabet in name only, because its spelling tracks pronunciation so badly; I found myself replying that English is trading one kind of consistency for another, keeping spellings stable across meaning-bearing units such as sign and signature or electric and electricity. The argument left me certain that I know what an alphabet is only until I try to say it. I want a working definition of the term alphabet: one that clearly counts the Latin alphabet as used in a highly phonemic orthography such as Finnish's, clearly excludes Chinese characters, whose signs mostly correspond to morphemes, states what level of language a script's basic signs must encode, and yields a definite verdict on each of the following hard cases, several of which I keep changing my mind about.

The cases are the Arabic script, whose basic letters mark consonants while short vowels are usually omitted or supplied only by optional diacritics; Devanagari, whose consonant letters carry an inherent vowel and fuse into larger units with the marks that change or suppress that vowel; Korean Hangul, whose letters correspond to consonants and vowels and are even shaped to reflect their articulation, yet are packed into square syllable blocks rather than written in sequence; English orthography, which uses the same twenty-six letters as the Latin alphabet but with famously non-phonemic correspondences; Japanese kana, whose signs each stand for a whole mora, roughly a syllable, such as ka or ki; and the International Phonetic Alphabet, which assigns one symbol per speech sound but serves as a transcription tool rather than as the ordinary script of any language. I also want the definition to settle how phonemic a system must be in order to count as an alphabet: does English's deep orthography merely make it a bad alphabet, or does it stop being an alphabet? If the definition returns a surprising verdict on a case, defend the surprise rather than revising around it; and if the term is best treated as a matter of degree, spell out the dimensions along which something can be more or less alphabetic.
