I'm a compliance lead at a midsize freight-forwarding company — ships, planes, warehouses. We get two or three internal incidents a week where one of our people touched a customer's wrongdoing without actually committing it: a dispatcher booked cargo that later turned out to be part of a sanctions-evasion scheme, a warehouse manager stored machinery that later got retrofitted into a smuggling operation, a sales rep kept renewing a client that everyone sort of knew was bribing customs brokers. None of the employees caused the wrongdoing, and in most cases the customer would have done the same thing with or without us. Right now our internal reviews basically clear people by default once we establish they didn't cause it, and I have a growing suspicion that "we didn't cause it" is doing way too much work — that complicity isn't a species of causation and needs its own test. The stakes are high: customers get sanctioned, individual staff can face prosecution, and the company could lose its freight-forwarding license.

I'd like a reusable decision procedure my team can actually run on any such case in under an hour, without a law degree, before it goes to our general counsel for sign-off. I'm not after a legal opinion specific to any jurisdiction; I want the reusable structure underneath, the part that survives changes in the law. It should be a checklist or ordered set of questions, where each question has a clear idea of what counts as a sufficient answer, and where the procedure either stops with "not complicit," stops with "yes, complicit," or explicitly hands the call to a human because the question is one of judgment or values. It has to handle the distinctions that actually come up for us: active assistance versus just not stopping something; wilful blindness versus genuinely not knowing versus "you should have known"; help given before the wrong versus help cleaning up afterward; and providing a standard, off-the-shelf service to anyone versus tailoring something to a client's scheme, like special routing or thin paperwork. I also need it to be honest about where a tidy procedure will misfire, and which step my team will most want to skip.

If there's a distinction from the philosophy of responsibility or complicity that I'll need to explain to my operations managers — something like why "the customer would have found another carrier anyway" doesn't automatically clear us, or why a replaceable link is still a link — build the explanation into the procedure itself rather than adding footnotes.

Then run the procedure on these three cases and show me the result, including at least one where it tells me something I won't like:

(1) An ops coordinator books a charter for a client shipping agricultural equipment to a neighboring country. A week later we learn the equipment included components that needed export licenses, and the client routed it through us specifically because we're known for thin paperwork. The coordinator says she wondered about the destination but didn't want to ask too many questions.

(2) A warehouse manager stores forty pallets for a long-standing client. The pallets turn out to be counterfeit goods. The manager says the invoice listed the goods as textiles, which is what it always says, and there was no reason to think twice.

(3) A sales rep signs a renewal with a firm that port authorities have repeatedly investigated for bribing customs officials. The rep knows this — everyone in the office jokes about it — but our service is exactly the same off-the-shelf service we sell anyone, and the rep does nothing extra for them beyond the contract. They still earn there commission.
