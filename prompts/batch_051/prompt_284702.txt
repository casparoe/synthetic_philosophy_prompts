I am a novelist, and the small cooperative press I belong to is considering a new submission rule. Every story submitted for our quarterly anthology would have to be accompanied by a "doubt note" of at most two hundred words. In it, the author must name the single way in which their own instincts are most likely to have misled them while writing that story—a habit, a bias, a self-indulgence—and state the one revision they would make if they trusted that doubt above everything else. The editorial committee must read the doubt note before opening the story itself, and for accepted pieces the note is printed directly beneath the title.

I want an analysis of how this mechanism would actually behave, not a verdict on whether it should be adopted. Walk me through the incentives it creates for authors in both the stories and the notes, how the editors' reading is changed by being told where a flaw should be before they encounter the work, how authors will strategize around the note, what published doubt notes do to readers and to writers' reputations, and where the scheme fails first. Then say whether any amendment keeps whatever genuine self-knowledge the rule is supposed to extract, without simply turning the note into one more performance.
