I'm in a seminar on the ethics of space colonization, and we've spent the last two weeks on whether laws and charters can bind people who don't exist yet. My instructor handed out a model charter for a Martian settlement, and one clause keeps bugging me. It reads:

"No generation of the Settlement shall lay upon its successors any lasting obligation to which those successors have not freely consented, nor shall it encumber the common resources of the Settlement beyond the lifetime of its present residents."

The case we're applying it to: the settlement has been occupied for a generation, and the medical staff now have good data that a child conceived and raised on Mars will never be able to return to Earth safely — the cardiovascular adaptation to 0.38 g makes full Earth gravity dangerous. The council is about to approve the first births in the settlement. But every resident has a standing duty to help maintain the habitat, and a child born there will, simply by being born there, be permanently unable to live anywhere else. So the parents would be imposing on a future person an obligation (upkeep of the colony, no realistic option of leaving) to which the child cannot consent — not because it's inconvenient to ask, but because there is no one to ask yet.

I'm less interested in whether the births should be approved than in the interpretive question: does the clause, read carefully, reach the decision to have a child at all? I'd like you to run through what the different approaches each give — the plain words; the evident purpose, since it was clearly aimed at things like selling off the colony's ice reserves or signing multi-generation labor contracts; what the drafters would have said if they'd known the gravity data; and what a reasonable resident would take the clause to promise. If they pull in different directions, wich one should win here, and does the non-identity point (the child couldn't have existed anywhere else, so "consent" is incoherent) dissolve the conflict or just dodge it?
