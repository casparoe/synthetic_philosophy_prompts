I'm a philosophy of biology grad student, and I've somehow ended up running my department's levels-of-selection reading group this year. We've hit a wall and I need to figure out why before my thesis chapter on it becomes as stalled as the group is. The strange thing is that nobody in the room is a old-fashioned group-selection skeptic anymore; everyone agrees selection can be represented at the gene, individual, and group level, and that arguing over whether group selection "ever happens" is wasted motion. The actual fight is over what it means for a level to be a real unit of selection. Half the group treats levels as bookkeeping: if you can partition fitness variance and heritability at a level using something like the Price equation, it's a level, and the gene's-eye, organism's-eye, and group's-eye descriptions are just equivalent accounting conventions. Asking which one is "real" is, on their view, a category mistake. The other half thinks this is exactly the move that dissolved the question instead of answering it; they insist a level only counts if something actually operates at that level — an interactor, a bottleneck, a vehicle, whatever — and they point to cases like meiotic drive genes or cancer cell lineages where the lower level and higher level pull in opposite directions, which they say shows the levels aren't just redscriptions of the same process.

The impasse is this: each side thinks the other is confused about something too basic to state, and every argument we read just restates one side in the other side's vocabulary and then claims victory. The bookkeepers say the causalists are smuggling in metaphysics; the causalists say the bookkeepers have redefined the debate so that they can't lose. My working theory is that it's a verbal dispute, or maybe two different senses of "level" (one causal, one statistical) sharing a word. But I could also imagine there's a hidden empirical or formal question doing the work that we keep missing. Diagnose the disagreement for me: is it verbal, metaphysical, empirical, formal, or just ill-posed? And what would actually count as progress — a distinction, a formal result, a kind of biological case, a change of question — rather than just another round of the same citations?
