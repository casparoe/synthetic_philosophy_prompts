I have no academic background in philosophy, but I have been thinking on my own about the difference between genuinely choosing something and merely running through a routine. The short essay below is my attempt to make that difference more precise, and I would like your comments on it. Please do not summarize it or offer general encouragement; I want a blunt assessment of where the reasoning is weakest, and I do not want suggestions for further reading. In particular, I would like you to address the three questions that follow the essay.

For about a year I have been unable to let go of a description I read of a kind of digger wasp, Sphex, that provisions a burrow with other insects. On the standard telling, the female wasp paralyzes her prey, drags it to the entrance of the burrow, leaves it there, and goes inside to inspect the burrow. When she comes back out, she normally drags the prey in. But if an experimenter moves the prey a few inches while she is inside, she comes out, relocates it without much trouble, and drags it back to the entrance—and then goes inside to inspect the burrow again. If the experimenter moves the prey every time, she does this again and again. She checks a burrow she has already checked and never seems to treat the moved prey as a reason to skip a step she has already completed.

My first thought was that this is exactly what unfreedom looks like: behavior that serves a goal but cannot bring the whole plan into view and revise it. But when I tried to state the principle behind that thought, I got stuck. I wanted to say that free behavior is behavior that can be revised when the situation changes. Yet the wasp does revise something; it relocates the prey. Its revision is just very local. So perhaps the difference between the wasp and a deliberating person is one of degree—a matter of how many levels of a routine can be interrupted and reconsidered. A person can reconsider not only this drag or that inspection but also the whole plan of provisioning the nest, and one level up, the standard they use for deciding when to reconsider at all. I began to think that freedom might just be the depth of that hierarchy of overrides.

But then I hit a new worry. A program could have many levels: one instruction says to drag the prey; another says to check whether the prey is still where it was left; another says to check whether the checking procedure is still the right one; and so on. If each level is itself a stored instruction, then adding levels gives us only a taller wasp. We have not located whatever it is that lets an agent call its own standards into question; we have pushed the question up a step. So either free action requires something other than a more flexible mechanism—something I cannot yet name—or my original confidence that the wasp is obviously unfree is doing more work than the argument has earned.

I still want to keep the intuition that there is an important difference between the wasp and a person, but I no longer trust the story I first told myself about what that difference is.

Here are the questions I would most like your comments on. First, does the middle of the essay already help itself to the conclusion that freedom has to do with levels of flexibility, and could someone reasonably resist that move? Second, does the essay run together determinism, mechanism, and rigidity, and can that running-together be repaired without technical vocabulary? Third, what is the strongest version of the final worry, and can the essay answer it in its own terms, or should I concede more than I do?
