After retiring from a career in materials chemistry, I joined a small reading group on dispositions, causal powers, and the metaphysics of natural kinds. We have spent several months on the inadequacy of the simple conditional analysis of dispositions—the idea that, for example, a glass is fragile just in case it would shatter if struck—and the convenor, Clara, has been unusually consistent about a family of cases that usually trouble people. She has not yet committed on the companion family of cases, and I should like to know what her record commits her to.

Her verdicts to date are these. A lead-crystal wine glass dropped onto a stone floor: fragile, without hesitation. The same glass fitted with a close-fitting expanded-polyurethane jacket and dropped from the same height: it does not break, but she insists it is still fragile; the jacket does not remove the brittleness, it only absorbs the energy that would otherwise reach the glass. A crystal of sodium chloride placed in deionised water: water-soluble. The same salt placed in water already saturated with sodium chloride at the same temperature: no net dissolution, but she says it remains water-soluble; the saturated brine masks the solubility rather than deleting it. A lethal dose of potassium cyanide swallowed together with the specific antidote at a dose sufficient to prevent death: she says the cyanide is still poisonous; its capacity to kill is masked, not absent.

The group calls a blocker of the jacket, saturated-brine, or antidote kind a mask: the disposition's physical basis stays in place, and only the terminal manifestation is prevented. We contrast it with a fink, in which the trigger itself removes or replaces the physical basis before the manifestation could run to completion. When the familiar fanciful case was put to her, of a glass that a hidden agency would render non-brittle in the instant before it was struck, Clara declined to answer and said the mask cases ought to be settled first. The mask cases now look settled, so the fink question is open.

The case I want to predict is this. Imagine an engineered ceramic tile that, as it sits on the bench, has the hardness and crack-propagation behaviour of porcelain. It is, however, a metastable material: if a blow were struck hard enough to initiate a fracture, the elastic energy released at the crack tip would rapidly drive a transformation of the adjacent crystal to a tougher phase, blunting the crack, so that the tile dents rather than shatters under a standard impact test. Nothing external interferes; the tile's own structure changes in response to the very event that would otherwise have broken it. I would like you to tell me what Clara is most likely to say about whether the tile is fragile, and why. In doing so, please set out the rule or rules that fit her five earlier verdicts, state whether two or more plausible rules fit those verdicts and diverge on the tile, give your best guess as to which rule she would actually own, and describe one further case that would most sharply distinguish that rule from its closest rival. I am interested less in the bare verdict than in the candidate rules and the structure of the prediction.
