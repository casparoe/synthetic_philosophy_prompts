I'm a chemistry grad student, not a philosopher, and I keep getting stuck in the same argument on a forum about access to paywalled research. The case: a university in a low-income country has been circulating downloaded copies of journal articles to students through a password-protected shared drive, because it genuinely can't afford the subscription packages. The publishers have sent takedown notices, and in the thread someone keeps deflecting every reply with "information wants to be free," as if that settles whether the university is doing anything wrong. Sometimes they add that trying to control information is like trying to hold water in a sieve, which makes me suspect that one sentence is doing several jobs at once.

Before I respond I want to seperate the readings. What are the distinct, precise claims that the sentence "information wants to be free" could be making? For each reading, tell me whether it is a descriptive claim about the economics of copying, a normative claim about rights or entitlements, a prediction about what enforcement can actually accomplish, or something else; and whether it is trivial, defensible, false, or just confused. Then tell me which reading(s) would actually support the claim that the university is justified in sharing the articles and owes nothing to the publishers, and which readings people typically run together to make the slogan feel like a decisive argument when it isn't one.

Please give the whole answer as a JSON object with these fields: "readings" (an array, each element an object with "label" for a short name, "precise_claim" for what that reading asserts as a one-sentence thesis, "claim_type" as one of descriptive, normative, predictive, conceptual, mixed, or other, "verdict" as one of trivial, defensible, contentious, false, or confused, "supports_sharing_side" as one of strongly, weakly, only_with_extra_premises, or not_at_all, and "reason" tying that reading to the journal-sharing case in one or two sentences); "reading_that_does_the_most_work" (the label of the strongest reading for the slogan-user, with a one-sentence explanation); and "conflation_diagnosis" (a short explanation of which readings get run together to make the slogan feel like an argument).
