Since I retired from a research career in the physical sciences and turned to philosophy, I have been struck by how often arguments about individual contributions to collective harms — a single flight, a factory-farmed meal, a ballot — turn on the claim that the one act makes no difference to the aggregate outcome, and how that same claim is then used to draw opposite conclusions. Before I read further, I want the specification of what an adequate theory of individual participation in collective harms must deliver. Give me the desiderata: for each requirement, the case or consideration that forces it, and a judgment as to whether it is non-negotiable or merely the preference of one camp. I expect the list to have to say how a theory stays causally honest — the act really may have no detectible effect — without thereby flattening different cases into one verdict, and how it handles thresholds that are real but unknowable at the time of acting. Flag any pairs or subsets of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, since that is the part I most want to see. Then, briefly, score the main families on offer — expected-utility, universalization, complicity, and expressive or virtue-theoretic views — against the list, and tell me which single requirement does the most to disqualify candidates.

Format: (1) "The desiderata," about 60%, one requirement at a time with its motivating case and negotiability; (2) "Tensions," about 20%, for sets that look jointly unsatisfiable and the argument that they conflict; (3) "The contenders," about 15%, two or three sentences per family; (4) "Corrections," about 5%, where you say plainly whether I have misposed the problem — for instance, whether one theory should not be expected to cover climate, voting, and consumption all at once, or whether I have drawn the line between negotiable and non-negotiable in the wrong place. If the request rests on a confused premise, tell me so rather than complying politely.
