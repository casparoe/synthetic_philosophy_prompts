I teach a graduate seminar in experimental philosophy, and the unit on the Knobe effect always settles into the same stock survey. We begin from the familiar asymmetry: when a company chairman says he does not care at all about a side effect of a profit-making policy, people are far more likely to say he brought the side effect about intentionally when it is harmful than when it is helpful—even though intention, awareness, and causal structure are held fixed across the two vignettes. Each year my students generate the same short list of explanations, and I would like to force a discussion that goes beyond it.

The list I already have, roughly, is this: (i) the evaluative-concept view, on which the folk concept of intentional action itself encodes moral evaluation, so the asymmetry reflects the concept rather than a distortion of it; (ii) the culpable-control or blame-bias view, on which the bad outcome triggers a desire to blame the chairman and this motivational pressure biases the factual judgment; (iii) the norm-violation view, on which intentionality ascriptions track whether the action deviates from a salient norm, so the asymmetry should reverse when helping violates a norm and harming conforms to one; (iv) the trade-off view, on which a side effect is judged intentional when the agent treats it as a cost he is willing to absorb for the sake of the main goal; (v) the deep-self view, on which an action is judged intentional when it expresses the agent's underlying evaluative commitments, which the harmful side effect does but the helpful one does not; and (vi) the conversational-pragmatic view, on which the asymmetry is an artifact of what the word "intentionally" is being used to do in the two contexts rather than a difference in the underlying concept.

Your task is to give me one more explanation of the effect that is not on this list. Be explicit about what counts: a rewording, a special case, a conjunction, or a change of example of any item above does not count; a new explanation should posit a distinct underlying process or mechanism, or at minimum make a prediction the listed accounts do not already make. I suspect this list may already contain everything standard, so if you think some standard explanation is missing, name it and then set it aside, and give me the genuinely new proposal separately. For that new proposal, explain exactly how it differs from and relates to the listed explanations, and then give an honest assessment of whether it is any good—what evidence would support it, whether it could be distinguished experimentally from the standard accounts, and where it is most likely to break down.
