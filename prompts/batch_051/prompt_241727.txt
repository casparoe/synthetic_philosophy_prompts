Here's an asymmetry I keep coming back to. We give people responsibility-credit for making a plan even when the plan never gets used. A team that spends a week on a detailed plan, then throws it out when the situation changes and wings the whole execution, is judged as more professional and more responsible than a team that wings it from the first day — even if the actual decisions, actions, and results are identical. The plan did no causal work in either case; it just happened to be written down first in one of them. But in conversation or art, the credit runs the other way: a joke we find out was prepared in advance is somehow less funny, a "spontaneous" compliment that was actually planned reads as fake, and a fully scripted jazz solo is treated as a lesser achievement than an improvised one. So planning earns us responsibility-credit even when it changes nothing about what happens, while improvisation earns us authenticity-credit even when the same output could have been produced deliberately. Are these two asymmetries instances of one underlying principle, or just two separate biases?
