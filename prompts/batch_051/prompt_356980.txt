In my graduate seminar on the ethics of armed conflict, two doctoral students have spent the term defending opposite positions on the moral equality of combatants. One defends the traditional view: the rules governing permissible conduct in battle are the same for all combatants regardless of whether their side's war is just, because ordinary soldiers on both sides are typically alike in their epistemic situation, their subjection to command, and the threats they pose to one another. The other defends the revisionist view: a combatant who fights for an unjust cause cannot in fact satisfy the conditions for permissible killing, so the apparent symmetry between just and unjust combatants is a concession to law, institutions, and the limits of enforcement rather than a truth of fundamental morality.

They have agreed to produce an adversarial-collaboration document rather than another round of rival papers, and I have asked them to draft it. Please write the statement they could both sign, for an audience of philosophers who know the debate but are not committed to either side. It should contain: (1) a numbered list of claims both accept, covering what jus in bello and jus ad bellum respectively govern, what wartime killing requires in order to be permissible, and the relation between legal and moral permissibility; (2) a disagreements table stating the residual difference with enough precision that neither side would object to how its own view is worded; and (3) a section on cruxes. For each crux, state the sub-question that would most move the debate, describe one concrete case or argument both would agree bears on it, and record in advance what each side predicts the case shows. Include at least these candidate cruxes: whether liability to be killed tracks posing a threat or moral responsibility for an unjust threat; how much a combatant's excusable ignorance changes the moral status of their killing; and whether the permissibility of individual acts of killing can be inherited from the justice of the war as a whole. If you conclude that any part of their disagreement is verbal, say so and state the underlying substantive question.
