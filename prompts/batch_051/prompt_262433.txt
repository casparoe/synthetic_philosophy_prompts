In the preface to *The Principles of Psychology* (1890), William James writes:

“Every natural science assumes certain data uncritically, and declines to challenge the elements between which its own ‘laws’ obtain, and from which its own deductions are carried on.”

I would like you to apply this thought to the programme of making AI outputs legible by training models not merely to give correct answers but to produce explanations of their reasoning that humans can inspect and evaluate. Please argue for the following position: that much of this programme treats the natural-language explanation as the sort of uncriticised datum James has in mind, because it assumes that an explanation stands to the underlying computation roughly as a measurement stands to the quantity measured—that is, that the former can be taken as evidence for the latter without first establishing that relation. In doing so, make the problem concrete: if a model is rewarded when its explanation is judged faithful by humans or by another model, what exactly is being assumed about the link between the explanation and the mechanism, and what would have to be true for that assumption to be licit? Then defend the thesis against the expected objection that interpretability work is precisely trying to establish that link rather than presuppose it, and clarify whether your concession to that objection would remove the force of James’s warning. Please write your answer in British English.
