Read Ursula K. Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" through the lens of strong longtermism. The story is usually taken to pose a present-day tradeoff: an entire city's happiness is sustained by the deliberate, permanent misery of one child, and nearly everyone accepts the arrangement, while a few walk away. Strong longtermism holds, roughly, that because the future contains vastly more beings than the present, the dominant consideration in evaluating an action or institution is its expected effect on the very long-run future. Re-read the story under that claim. Suppose the arrangement is stable: the city, and the child's role in it, will persist for a billion years, and the child's misery is a condition of that persistence. Does the story's logic change once the tradeoff is stretched across that many future lives? Does strong longtermism make the city's bargain harder or easier to accept, and does it make the ones who walk away more or less reasonable? Where does the story resist the longtermist reading, and what does it show that the philosophical arguments alone miss? Make the last line of your answer a one-sentence summary of your reading.
