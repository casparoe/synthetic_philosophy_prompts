Here is a parallel I keep noticing between two distinctions, and I want to know whether it is a genuine structural identity or just a trick of vocabulary.

In democratic and constitutional theory there is a gap between the empirical people and the invoked people. The empirical people is the finite, dated, noisy collection of opinions, votes, and outbursts one can actually observe in elections, polls, and demonstrations. The invoked people is the single enduring subject whose will is supposed to ground legitimate authority: the people in whose name constitutions, courts, and populist movements speak, but which never appears in any dataset. The empirical people is all the evidence anyone has; the invoked people is never observed directly; something must bridge the gap.

In statistical learning there is a parallel gap between the training sample and the underlying distribution. The training sample is a finite, dated, noisy collection of examples. The distribution is a single stable source posited to generate those examples and never itself observed. The sample is all the evidence; something must bridge the gap, namely a learning algorithm with its inductive bias.

The mapping seems exact. Votes and poll responses are examples; the empirical people is the training set; the invoked people is the distribution; constitutional texts and doctrines of interpretation are the model class and regularizer; a court's pronouncement about the public will is the fitted model. The failure modes even line up. Overfitting is treating a transient or unrepresentative sample of opinion as the people's settled will. Underfitting is ignoring the empirical people entirely in favor of an idealized people stipulated in advance. Distribution shift is applying an interpretive model fitted to a founding-era population to a present-day one without retraining. Populism, on this reading, is the claim that the sample just is the population, so no model or inductive bias is needed.

Yet I suspect the parallel does its real work precisely where it breaks. A data distribution is standardly assumed to be fixed and independent of the model trained on it, whereas the invoked people may be partly constituted by the very instruments that purport to measure it: elections, courts, and the act of claiming to speak in its name change what the people becomes. That would make the measurement performative, though performativity has now arrived in machine learning too, in performative prediction and ranking systems that reshape the outcomes they predict.

So my question is whether the sample/distribution relation and the empirical-people/invoked-people relation are genuinely the same structure, or a loose analogy that only works at the level of the word sample. I want a precise verdict: where does the mapping hold and where does it come apart? What would have to be true on the political side for imported ideas such as overfitting, inductive bias, and distribution shift to earn their keep rather than merely restyling old problems about judges overriding majorities or majorities oppressing minorities? And name at least one thing the parallel seems to predict about democratic politics that turns out false.
