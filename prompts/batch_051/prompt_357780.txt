I teach a decision-theory unit in a small philosophy seminar — about 14 undergrads, one 75-minute block. They already get the St. Petersburg game, so I want a follow-up on the uglier cousin: a gamble where every payoff is finite but the expectation is undefined because the series only converges conditionally, so the fair price depends on the order you sum the possible cases in. Design a runnable in-class activity that makes them feel that before I name it. In particular, I want two groups to each price what is secretly the same gamble and arrive at different finite answers, each with a perfectly defensible way of tabulating the outcomes. Then a debrief that maps what their two tabulations did onto conditionally convergent series, why expected value can fail to be well defined even with finite payoffs, and why both groups were right in a way that undermines the idea of a single fair price. No real money, materials I can prep the night before. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and which part of the point the activity can't actually demonstrate.
