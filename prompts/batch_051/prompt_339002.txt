Here's an argument I want you to evaluate. In my philosophy of mind class we've been arguing about whether pain is intrinsically bad, and one student brought in the case of pain asymbolia—a rare condition, usually from a brain lesion, where (as the case is usually described) people report feeling pain but dont seem to suffer from it. They can point to where the pain is, rate how intense it is, and say whether it's sharp or burning, but they don't flinch, don't withdraw, and don't ask for it to stop. The student thinks this shows pain isn't intrinsically bad. Here's the argument, cleaned up:

(1) If pain is intrinsically bad, then its badness must be part of the pain sensation itself. Two people with the very same pain sensation would have to be equally badly off—there's nothing else for the badness to consist in.
(2) Pain asymbolia shows that the sensation and the suffering can come apart. Patients have the pain sensation (they can locate it, rate it, describe its quality), but they lack the suffering: no wincing, no withdrawal, no wanting it to stop.
(3) So an asymbolic patient and an ordinary person can have the same pain sensation, while only the ordinary person suffers.
(4) If the badness were in the sensation itself, that difference would be impossible.
(5) Therefore the badness of pain is not in the sensation itself; it's a seperate reaction that normally comes along with the sensation. Pain isn't intrinsically bad.

I have my own view about whether this goes through, but I want to see what you make of it. Is the argument valid? If not, say exactly which step fails and why. If you think it is valid, say which premise a critic would attack and whether the argument survives. Either way, be explicit about what you're counting as "the pain sensation" at each step, and whether the argument shows pain isn't intrinsically bad or only that one component of pain isn't.
