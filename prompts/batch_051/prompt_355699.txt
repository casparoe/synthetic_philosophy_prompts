I sit on the research ethics committee of a large academic medical center, and we must decide on a new randomized trial. We want to decide it in a way that is consistent with our own recent precedents, which are as follows:

1. We approved a placebo-controlled trial of a drug already used widely off-label for chemotherapy-induced nausea. The expert oncology community was genuinely divided about whether the drug's benefits outweighed its particular side-effect profile, and the two lead investigators stated in the protocol that they were personally in equipoise. Verdict: approved.

2. We rejected a trial that would have randomized patients with HER2-positive breast cancer in the adjuvant setting to receive or not receive trastuzumab, at a point when it was already standard of care and no responsible oncologist regarded withholding it as acceptable. The investigators had no personal uncertainty, and the broader community had no genuine controversy either. Verdict: rejected.

3. We approved, with conditions, a two-arm trial comparing a newer, broader-spectrum antibiotic with an older, narrow-spectrum antibiotic for hospitalized cellulitis. The infectious-disease community was divided over whether the newer drug's broader in vitro coverage would translate into faster clinical recovery and whether any such advantage would justify its higher risk of Clostridioides difficile infection. Our approval required an independent data and safety monitoring board and a prespecified interim futility analysis, and we required each enrolling clinician to certify in writing that they had no settled view about which arm would be better for the patient being enrolled. Verdict: approved with conditions.

4. We rejected a proposed non-inferiority trial of a cheaper new oral anticoagulant against standard anticoagulation for atrial fibrillation. Preliminary phase II data suggested the new drug had a roughly 40% higher stroke rate with similar major bleeding, though the sponsor argued the trial was justified by lower cost. We found there was no genuine uncertainty about which arm was better on the primary efficacy outcome, whatever the economic considerations. Verdict: rejected.

The new proposal is a randomized trial of an immune-checkpoint inhibitor combination versus standard chemotherapy as first-line treatment for metastatic non-small-cell lung cancer. The broader oncology community is genuinely split: some leaders believe the combination's response-rate advantage will translate into an overall-survival benefit that justifies its substantial immune-related toxicity, while others believe the toxicity and the absence of randomized survival data are reasons to remain with chemotherapy until phase III evidence matures. That controversy is real. However, the two principal investigators who would enroll most of the patients have both published several strongly worded editorials arguing that the combination is already clearly superior and that chemotherapy is no longer an acceptable first-line option; in a planning meeting, one of them said he would not want a close family member randomized to the chemotherapy arm. Both intend to enroll their own patients.

If we are to be consistent with our own precedents, should we approve, reject, or approve with conditions? Identify the rule or rules that best explain our prior verdicts, and in particular say which interpretation of equipoise is doing the work in each candidate rule. Which of our earlier cases is the closest analog to this one, and why? If our precedents genuinely underdetermine the new case, state what principle we would be adopting under each available choice.

End your response with a line beginning exactly "Verdict:" followed by approve, reject, or approve with conditions, and, if conditions are imposed, a brief statement of them.
