Act as a professional philosopher of science who works on the epistemology of thought experiments. Begin by brainstorming several candidate principles for when a thought experiment yields knowledge, then turn to my cases and assess how well each principle fits them. Please think carefully before committing.

I want to know what principle, if any, captures my considered verdicts about whether scientific thought experiments yield knowledge—that is, justified new belief about the natural world, or at least new theoretical understanding—even though the experiment is performed only in thought. These are my verdicts:

1. Galileo's falling bodies. A heavy body and a light body are joined; Aristotle's principle that heavier bodies fall faster then seems to imply both that the composite falls faster, because it is heavier, and slower, because the light body retards the heavy one. Verdict: this yields genuine knowledge, but only the negative knowledge that Aristotle's principle is internally inconsistent when applied to composites. It does not by itself establish that all bodies fall at the same rate.

2. Einstein's elevator. An observer in a sealed elevator cannot distinguish uniform acceleration from the effects of a uniform gravitational field. Verdict: this yields genuine theoretical knowledge. It makes the idea that gravity and acceleration are locally equivalent both precise and thinkable, and it points toward a theory in which this equivalence is a consequence. But I do not think the thought experiment on its own justifies the principle; it is a guide, not a proof.

3. Maxwell's demon, as originally conceived. A small being opens and closes a door to sort fast and slow molecules, apparently making a hotter body hotter and a colder body colder without work. Verdict: in its original form, this did not yield knowledge. It posed a problem and set up a research program, but it became knowledge only when the later phase of the literature connected sorting to information processing and erasure. The original thought experiment by itself was epistemically incomplete.

4. A perfectly rigid light-year-long rod. Imagine a rod one light-year long, perfectly rigid, so that if you push one end, the far end moves immediately. Verdict: this yields negative theoretical knowledge: it reveals that special relativity is incompatible with the idea of a perfectly rigid extended body. But it tells us nothing directly about the behavior of any real material rod, because no such rod exists and the assumption is idealized to the point of impossibility.

5. A neutral mutation with exactly offsetting effects. Imagine a mutation that makes an organism more fertile and, to exactly the same degree, more conspicuous to predators, leaving inclusive fitness unchanged. Verdict: this does not yield knowledge about whether the mutation will spread. It merely re-describes the assumptions built into the model under a vivid guise.

6. A magnet and a ramp arranged so that the magnet appears to pull a cart up a ramp, after which the cart rolls down the other side, and the process repeats. Verdict: this yields knowledge that the arrangement is impossible, but only because conservation of energy is already assumed; the thought experiment's contribution is to make that assumption's implications vivid.

I notice my verdicts are not obviously unified. In cases 1, 2, 4, and 6 I attribute some knowledge-yielding role to the thought experiment, but of very different kinds. In cases 3 and 5 I deny it, but for apparently different reasons. I want you to do the reflective-equilibrium work: propose a principle, or a small set of principles, that captures as many of my verdicts as possible; state precisely what the principle says about a further case I have not listed; and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why. If you think the right result is that I must drop or revise one of my verdicts, say so directly.

Finally, please tell me what would change your mind: identify the strongest consideration against the principle you propose, and the point in your reasoning where you think a different reading of one of my cases would most likely push you to a different account.
