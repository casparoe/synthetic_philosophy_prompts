Here's a methodological asymmetry in archaeology that I can't get out of my head. Put on your best philosopher-of-science-who-also-knows-archaeology hat, and think carefully before you answer — maybe start by brainstorming the obvious ways someone might try to justify the asymmetry, then pick your actual position and commit to it.

The asymmetry: a prehistoric burial turns up with a pot by the skull and a few beads at the waist, and the excavation report will state pretty confidently that these are grave goods, placed with the dead, and that they point to belief in an afterlife or at least to ritualized treatment of the dead. Nobody finds this methodologically scandalous. But run the same style of inference forward in time. Imagine an archaeologist two thousand years from now excavating a typical modern American cemetery. Caskets contain a wedding ring, a photograph of the grandkids, a military service medal, maybe a lucky coin. If that future excavator wrote "these are grave goods and indicate a belief that personal bonds and luck outlast death," we'd say she's badly overreading the material — because we happen to be the ones doing the burying, and we know a lot of this stuff goes in the casket out of grief, sentiment, family pressure, or just "we thought she'd want it," with no settled theology attached at all.

So the same inference — objects deliberately placed with the dead → beliefs about what happens to the dead — is treated as solid when aimed at the distant past and as to naive overreading when aimed at us. Is that asymmetry actually justified, or is it a double standard that mostly survives because there's nobody around to contradict the prehistoric report? A few obvious lines to test: the ancient grave goods were costlier, more standardized, or more clearly patterned across a whole society while modern casket contents are idiosyncratic; or the ancient objects were positioned around the body in a way that carries specific ritual meaning; or modern practices are a special case because we have living informants and texts telling us they're secular. But then face the future-archaeologist case squarely: the ring, the photo, the medal, and the coin are also patterned, deliberate, emotionally costly, and widespread across a culture, and a future excavator without our informants would write them up exactly as "ritual." I also can't shake the suspicion floating around the discipline that "ritual" often functions as a label for "we don't know what this is for" — and that the harder the inference (tools are easy to read, belief is hard), the more confidently people seem to draw it when nobody can check.

So: is there a defensible principle that makes belief-inference from ancient burials stronger than belief-inference from modern ones, or should the confidence collapse once you notice that the modern grave would look just as ritual to any outsider? Take a definite stand and defend it; give me your actual view rather than a survey of options, and be honest about the strongest objection to your position.
