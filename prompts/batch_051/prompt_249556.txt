I have been reading about two ways of supervising the reasoning of large language models, and I noticed what might be a structural parallel between them and a distinction in moral philosophy. In outcome-based supervision, a model receives a positive or negative signal only from its final answer: a chain of reasoning that ends in the correct answer is treated as good, and one that ends in the wrong answer is treated as bad, even if the intermediate steps were, respectively, flawed or sound. In process-based supervision, the model receives feedback on each step of its reasoning: an invalid step is marked wrong even if the final answer happens to be right, and a valid step is marked right even if the chain later goes astray. This looks to me like the contrast between consequentialism and deontology. Consequentialism evaluates an action by the state of the world it brings about; deontology evaluates the action itself, or the rule it embodies, independently of how things happen to turn out. The analogy would be: final answers are to consequences as intermediate steps are to actions, and process-based supervision is deontological in the way outcome-based supervision is consequentialist.

I want to know whether this parallel is deep or superficial. Please state the mapping precisely, say what would have to be true on both sides for results to transfer from one domain to the other, and identify at least one thing the analogy predicts that turns out false. I would especially like you to test the places where the mapping might come apart. For example, process-based supervision may value correct steps only because they tend to make the final answer more reliable, whereas deontologists typically deny that the rightness of an action is merely a matter of its tendency to produce good outcomes; and consequentialists can accept rules of thumb, so it is not obvious that outcome-based supervision is the only consequentialist position. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to reverse your verdict about whether the parallel is deep or superficial, and where in your reasoning you think a mistake is most likely.
