Last night I had a long argument with a friend about how risk should enter into the evaluation of choices. He spent the evening trying to show me that my own preferences are inconsistent, and I came away thinking that my verdicts are more coherent than his theory allowed, but that I cannot yet state the principle behind them. I would like you to extract that principle, or the best candidate principles, from the cases below. For the principle you propose, please say which case motivates each clause; state what it predicts about some further cases I have not settled; and be explicit about which of my verdicts, if any, it forces me to abandon. I would rather revise one or two verdicts than keep a principle that merely re-describes the pattern case by case.

The cases are given in order.

1. I would pay $120 to insure against a 1-in-10,000 chance of losing $1,000,000, although the actuarially fair price is only $100. I would also buy a $2 ticket giving a 1-in-100,000,000 chance of $150,000,000, although the ticket's expected value is about $1.50. I hold both of these and see no conflict. My friend says the first purchase is risk-averse and the second is risk-seeking, so I cannot have one stable attitude toward risk. I think he is conflating two different things, but I could not say what they are.

2. I prefer a certain $3,000 to an 80 percent chance of $4,000, with nothing otherwise. I also prefer a 20 percent chance of $4,000 to a 25 percent chance of $3,000. I recognize that this pair violates the independence axiom of expected utility theory, but neither preference moves when I try to argue myself out of it.

3. I prefer a sure $8,000 to an 85 percent chance of $10,000. But I also prefer a 0.085 percent chance of $10,000 to a 0.1 percent chance of $8,000. This feels like the same pattern as case 2, but no certainty is involved; every option here has some chance of ending with nothing. A principle that explained case 2 purely as a certainty bias would not automatically cover this pair.

4. Here is the pair that most troubled the argument. Presented separately, I accept the insurance policy and the lottery ticket of case 1. Presented with a single combined package whose outcomes are exactly the sum of those two — I pay $122 now, I am covered against the 1-in-10,000 loss of $1,000,000, and I keep the 1-in-100,000,000 chance of $150,000,000 — I turn it down. The only difference is that the two small costs are billed together and the two rare events are displayed in one place. I do not know whether a principle should treat the framing as psychologically real and predict my refusal, or treat it as a mistake and predict that I should accept. But a principle that simply adds up the value of the parts will get one of my two verdicts wrong.

The closest thing I can state after the argument is a tentative principle. Keep the value of each outcome fixed and evaluate a gamble by replacing the objective probability p of each event with a decision weight r(p), where r is increasing, r(0)=0, r(1)=1, r(p) is greater than p when p is very small, r(p) is less than p when p is close to but below 1, and the gap between certainty and any nonzero risk is larger than the objective probabilities suggest. This would handle cases 1, 2, and 3 together: the same function that makes tiny probabilities worth buying and insuring against also makes a 20 percent chance of nothing loom larger than its objective size. But I am less sure that it handles case 4, and I suspect it overpredicts choices I would not in fact make.

What principle, stated precisely, comes closest to capturing all four cases? Which verdicts, if any, should I give up? And what does the principle you propose predict about these three choices, on which I do not yet have a settled view: (a) a 99 percent chance of $1,000,000 versus a sure $980,000; (b) a 1 percent chance of $1,000,000 versus a sure $9,000; (c) a 0.0001 percent chance of $10,000,000 versus a 0.1 percent chance of $10,000. If the right principle is neither expected utility theory nor simple decision-weighting, please say what it is and why its extra structure is justified rather than ad hoc.
