In my gov class we learned about sovereign immunity, the rule that you can't sue the government unless the government passes a law saying you can. So if a mail truck smashes into your fence, or a state agency gives you bad info that costs you your savings, you don't automatically get your day in court against the government the way you would against a company. But the same government can sue you, fine you, and put you in prison when you break its rules. And individual officials often have immunity too, so sometimes it looks like the state can wreck your life and nobody has to answer for it in court.

What I want to know is what this actually assumes about whether the state can do wrong. Is the practice committed to the old idea that the king (or president, or whatever) can do no wrong, literally? Or is it just a procedural thing about where you get your remedy, like through a claims process or voting the bums out instead of a lawsuit? And can it even be just procedural if the state also settles cases and pays people, which seems like admitting it did something bad, while still refusing to let itself be dragged into court without permission?

Please reconstruct the theory of state responsibility that makes this whole setup make sense, then separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits with. Also say whether the pieces are actually consistent, and what would have to change in the legal system if you replaced the underlying theory with the view that the government is just another moral agent that has to answer for the harm it causes in court like anyone else does.
