I am a novelist, and I repeatedly face a craft problem that I would like turned into a reusable procedure. In my novels, a reader often has to grasp an unstated rule — the logic of a taboo, the true condition under which a charm holds, the boundary of whom a ceremonial door admits — purely from a small number of incidents, some of which satisfy the rule and some of which do not. The rule cannot be announced in narration or dialogue; the reader must assemble it from what is shown. I understand this is the problem studied in Bongard problems, in which a handful of positive figures on one side and negative figures on the other define a rule the solver must induce. I am not asking for a literature review or for citations; I want the operational, writer-facing version.

The deliverable should be a procedure I can run while drafting, in about twenty minutes per rule, with no formal training. It should take as input a plain-language statement of the intended rule and the story situation, and it should produce two things: a set of no more than eight positive and negative narrative examples designed so that most readers converge on the intended rule without its ever being stated, and a short list of the most plausible wrong rules a reader might settle on instead, with a note on how the examples rule each wrong rule out.

The central difficulty is how to choose the examples, and especially the negatives. I suspect that near-miss negatives do the most work — a case that obeys every condition except one, placed beside a case that obeys all but a different one — but I do not trust that intuition, and I want the procedure to tell me when near misses mislead. The procedure should therefore include: an ordered checklist; a method for generating rival rules and checking that the examples exclude them; an explicit test for whether a reader could infer a simpler rule than the one I intend; a warning about places where a reader's knowledge of the ordinary world will override the text; and clearly labelled points where judgement cannot be eliminated and I must decide as a writer rather than follow the procedure mechanically.

Apply the procedure to three rules from the novel I am currently planning:

1. A shrine opens only for a petitioner who arrives barefoot and carrying an iron nail; it turns away the petitioner who carries the nail while shod, and the barefoot petitioner who brings no nail.
2. A ferryman will take a coin only from a passenger who intends to return; he refuses the same coin from a passenger who means to cross once. The word 'intends' must never appear in the novel.
3. A garden gate opens for a visitor who sings or carries a lit candle, including a visitor who does both; it closes only for a visitor who neither sings nor carries a candle. The reader must not be permitted to settle on 'sings' alone or 'candle' alone, and the inclusive 'or' must never be stated in the novel.

For each of the three, give the procedure's verdict and the example set it commands, if an example set exists. At least one case should produce an answer I will not like — a rule that has to be changed or conceded to the reader in some other way rather than induced cleanly from a few examples; the purpose is not to rescue every rule. Close by saying plainly what the procedure cannot do and which failure modes it will not detect.

Please write in British English. Begin with the bottom line of the procedure in the first sentence — the one principle that all the other steps follow from — and then give the procedure and the reasoning behind it, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
