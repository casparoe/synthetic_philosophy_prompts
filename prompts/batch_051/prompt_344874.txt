I'm drafting a review of a colleague's manuscript for a popular science outlet. The manuscript argues that culture evolves by natural selection in a strong, predictive sense, and its central argumentative device is the familiar analogy between cultural items and genes: a meme is treated as a replicator that competes for limited attention and memory, and the manuscript leans on that analogy to license forecasts about which ideas, slogans, and styles will spread — for instance, that variants which are easier to remember, more emotionally salient, or easier to imitate will reliably outcompete their rivals. The analogy is doing real argumentative work for the author, not serving as decoration.

I'd like you to audit that analogy the way a careful reviewer would. Spell out the intended correspondences: what in the cultural domain is supposed to play the roles of gene, phenotype, mutation, reproduction, and selective environment. Then identify the points at which the mapping strains or breaks, and distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the manuscript's predictive use of the analogy from the ones that are merely cosmetic. In particular, examine features of the genetic source that the analogy may quietly import without the cultural target earning them: discrete, high-fidelity replication; a clean genotype–phenotype distinction; particulate inheritance; and undirected, non-Lamarckian variation. Finally, say whether the forecasting conclusion survives once those disanalogies are granted, and whether some other comparison — a parasite or epidemic, a language, a market — would carry the same conclusion with fewer distortions.
