In debates about whether divine foreknowledge destroys free will, one image keeps being deployed to dissolve the threat, an image associated with Boethius. Picture a traveler moving along a road at her own pace and choosing her steps, while a spectator on a high ridge sees the whole road laid out at once. The watcher's simultaneous view of stretches the traveler has not yet traversed is meant to be no more coercive than ordinary observation; seeing ahead does not push. God's relation to the whole course of events is then treated on this model, with eternity standing in for the elevated vantage point. Audit this analogy as an argument for the compatibility of foreknowledge and freedom. Lay out the intended correspondences: road, traveler, spectator, vantage point, seeing, absence of interference, and their theological counterparts. Then identify the disanalogies that matter for the argument rather than merely decorating it. A spectator on a ridge is still a being inside time, observing a world already there to be observed; an atemporal, infallible knower is not. Does the spatial picture quietly import the future's already existing in order to be seen? Finally, judge whether the thesis the analogy is meant to license — that foreknowledge imposes only a conditional necessity on what is known, not a simple necessity that would make the act unfree — survives once those disanalogies are admitted, and say whether any better analogy is available.
