I'm a high school student writing a paper on reproductive ethics. I recently read a magazine article that summarized Julian Savulescu's principle of procreative beneficence and its relation to what the author called "liberal eugenics." The article's summary, as I remember it, went roughly like this:

"Savulescu's starting point is the familiar practice of screening IVF embryos for serious genetic diseases. He argues that once you accept that practice, consistency pushes you further: parents using IVF are morally required to select the embryo with the best genetic prospects — the one expected to be the healthiest and smartest — because picking any other embryo knowingly gives a child a worse life than it could have had. He calls this principle procreative beneficence, and he presents it as a liberal position about what parents owe to their future children rather than about state coercion, so many writers group it under 'liberal eugenics.' But the article says this is still eugenics with a friendlier face. If you admit that some possible children are less worth bringing into existence, you have accepted the premise that disabled people's lives are less valuable, and coercive state eugenics is the natural next step. Procreative beneficence, the article concludes, is old eugenics with better manners."

I'm suspicious of the last few moves — it seems to slide from a claim about what individual parents morally ought to do to a claim about what a state may or may not do, and from "choosing against a disability" to "judging disabled people's lives as worth less" — but I don't know the position well enough to pin down what's unfair. Could you give me an accuracy audit of this summary? Please separate what accurately reflects the view, what is a common simplification, and what is a plain misrepresentation, and then say how a defender of procreative beneficence would respond. In particular, does the article's closing charge survive the corrections?

Please write in American English, and end with a one-sentence overall assessment on its own line beginning "Verdict:".
