In my philosophy of mind course I draw a four-rung ladder of possible minds, lifted from Dennett but cleaned up for undergraduates. Bottom rung: Darwinian creatures test designs only the slow way, with natural selection doing the trial and error over generations. Next rung: Skinnerian creatures can emit new behaviors in a single lifetime and let the environment reinforce or punish them. Above that: Popperian creatures can preselect candidate actions before trying them in the world—they have some internal stand-in for the environment, so bad options get eliminated in advance. Top rung: Gregorian creatures use words and other representational tools to borrow the preselection that other agents have already paid for.

I've been worrying the ladder with a case I made up. Suppose I build a robot whose only job is to move through a cluttered room without knocking things over. The designers hard-code a little physics simulator inside it. Before each movement the robot generates, say, ten thousand candidate motor sequences more or less at random, runs each one through the simulator, throws out any sequence that would cause a collision, and executes the first one that survives. Then it resets and does the whole thing again. The simulator never updates; the robot never learns from it's own successes or failures; the candidate generator is just a randomizer over joint trajectories.

Is this robot a Popperian creature? In the most literal sense it seems to be: candidate behaviors are dying inside an internal model rather than out in the real room. But I can also describe it as a Skinnerian creature with the punishing environment relocated inside its chassis, or as a Darwinian creature where the real selection is being done upstream by the designers and their physics engine. Work through the ladder rung by rung and tell me (a) whether the robot qualifies as Popperian or whether that label only fits once we smuggle in something the definition doesn't actually state—an updatable model, understanding, directed hypothesis formation, a self—and (b) whether the ladder needs an extra clause at the Popperian rung to handle cases like this. And because I may be leaning on a point of definition rather than the world, tell me what would change your mind: which fact about the robot (that the simulator could learn from its own predictions, that the candidates were generated deliberately rather than randomly, that it represented the room to itself in something like a map) would flip your classification, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
