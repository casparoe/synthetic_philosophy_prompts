I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've been trying to get my head around the longtermism debate because I might write something on it. I read a popular piece that gave a quick summary of Bostrom's simulation argument and then used it to make a pretty sweeping claim, and I want to check whether I'd be repeating a fair version or a garbled one.

The piece said, roughly, that Bostrom showed there are only three possibilities: almost all human-level civilizations go extinct before they can run simulations of their own past; the ones that survive could do it but choose never to; or we're almost certainly living inside one of those simulations right now. It then said that since we have no special reason to think civilizations reliably wipe themselves out, and since a civilization that can run one ancestor simulation could run billions just as cheaply, the third possibility swamps the others, so its overwhelmingly likely we're simulated. From there it drew what it called the real conclusion: this is bad news for the people who say our biggest moral priority should be shaping the far future. If we're simulated, our descendants aren't really a continuation of us; the simulators can pull the plug whenever they like, and anything we do to help the next million generations would just be improving the lives of characters in someone else's game. So far-future fanaticism rests on a false picture.

I can already see that the claim Bostrom showed this is doing a lot of work, since I'd always heard the argument was a trilemma, not a proof that we're in a simulation. But I'm less sure about the back half. Does it actually follow that if we're likely to be simulated, the far future matters much less? Or is that a jump a serious longtermist would have good replies to? I'd love an accuracy audit: which parts of this summary would someone who works on the simulation argument accept, which parts would they wince at, and does the far-future conclusion survive once the sloppy bits are corrected?
