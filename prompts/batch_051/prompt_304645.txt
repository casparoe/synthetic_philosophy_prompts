So I keep getting confused about how Kant's universalization test actually works, especially in the Groundwork where he makes this big claim about moral necessity. In the Abbott translation, Kant says:

"Everyone must admit that if a law is to have moral force, i.e., to be the basis of an obligation, it must carry with it absolute necessity; that, for example, the precept, 'Thou shalt not lie,' is not valid for men alone, as if other rational beings had no need to observe it; and so with all the other moral laws properly so called; that, therefore, the basis of obligation must not be sought in the nature of man, or in the circumstances in the world in which he is placed, but a priori simply in the conception of pure reason..."

That passage seems to be saying the basis of moral obligation can't come from human nature or the world we're in, but has to come from pure reason a priori. But then when he actually applies the categorical imperative, the examples often look like they turn on what would happen if everyone did the thing—like everyone making false promises, or everyone not developing their talents. That sounds suspiciously like it depends on facts about human life and consequences, which he just said shouldn't be the basis. So here's my question: is the "what if everyone did that?" test in Kant doing the same job as the later generalization arguments in Hare's universal prescriptivism and Singer's generalization argument, or are they relying on different logical machinery? If they're different, what exactly does Kant's version get you that the others don't, and does his a priori claim in that passage survive the application of his own test? Try not to just give the standard replies about perfect vs imperfect duties, I want to understand the underlying logic.
