In my old line of work, ecological monitoring and conservation, we habitually described habitats in medical terms: an intact landscape was healthy, a drained or fragmented one was degraded, and clearing was an injury. The analogy did real argumentative work. In particular, it let us pass from a judgment about the system — this wetland is still healthy — to a judgment about the organisms in it: they are better off than they would be in a damaged or simplified version of the same landscape. That inference is what I now want examined.

I have recently been reading the wild animal welfare literature, and it puts pressure on exactly that step. A healthy ecosystem is not necessarily a comfortable one from the perspective of the individuals in it. Predation, parasitism, and starvation are constitutive of a functioning system, and many species — the r-selected ones, which produce enormous numbers of offspring with little parental care — seem to live under conditions in which most individuals die young and painfully. A landscape can therefore score high on measures of ecosystem health while looking, in terms of individual welfare, worse than a simplified or depauperate habitat would look. This has forced me to ask how far the medical analogy really carries.

Give me an explicit correspondence table: in the target system, what plays the role of the body, the organs, the cells, disease, healing, and death? Then identify where the mapping breaks, and in particular where the analogy quietly imports a unified set of interests — a patient whose own welfare goes up and down with the welfare of its parts — into a thing, an ecosystem, that may not have either. Finally, commit to a verdict: once the disanalogies are on the table, does the conclusion that habitat destruction harms the animals within the system survive, or was the medical analogy doing the moral work all along? I would prefer a definite, defended answer over a survey of options.
