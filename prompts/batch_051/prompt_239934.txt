Last night I got into a long argument with a friend about Sidgwick's dualism of practical reason — roughly, the worry that self-interested reasons and impartial reasons can each be fully rational yet point in different directions, and there's no higher principle to adjudicate between them. By the end we'd invented an institution instead of solving it. Suppose a foundation writes into its charter that any grant over a certain size has to pass two seperate votes. In the first vote, trustees are instructed to give only agent-neutral reasons — reasons framed as what any rational person should want, not what benefits the foundation. In the second vote, they are instructed to give only reasons grounded in the foundation's own long-term interest, treated as if that were the whole ballgame. A grant goes through only if it wins both votes. I want you to take this seriously as a mechanism and tell me how it would actually behave: what sort of reasons people would learn to give in each vote, how they'd game or blur the line between the two kinds of reason, which projects this selects for, and whether the two-stage vote just relocates the original dilemma into the counting procedure. Are the failure modes fixable without turning it back into an ordinary single vote? And answer in continuous prose, like an email, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
