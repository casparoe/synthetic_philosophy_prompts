In Book III, chapter 3 of De Anima (On the Soul), Aristotle works through what phantasia is not, distinguishing it from perception, from thought, from belief, and from knowledge. His positive account is brief but gives the following criteria. Phantasia is a movement produced by perception actually operating; it does not occur without perception, and there is no supposition without it. It is in our power whenever we wish, the way we can place something before our eyes, which is not true of belief, because belief involves conviction. It also can be false, whereas perception of the proper objects of each sense is always true.

Now consider this borderline case: while awake, a person deliberately forms a detailed image of an impossible object—say, a pink elephant standing in the corner of the office where she is working. The image is voluntary, assembled from past perceptions of color, size, shape, and spatial arrangement, and the person knows it is false. On the criteria above, it seems to satisfy the voluntariness condition and the potential for falsehood, and it depends on past perception. But it is not obvious that it is a movement produced by perception actually operating, since the present perceptual scene continues unchanged while the image is superimposed on it, and the image is not an afterimage of anything currently seen.

Does this case qualify as Aristotle's phantasia? Work criterion by criterion, say exactly where the criteria underdetermine the verdict, and then answer this further question: does the example show that Aristotle's account needs to distinguish imagery from afterimages or perceptual illusion, or can it handle the case without modification?
