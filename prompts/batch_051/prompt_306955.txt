Question: "Explain the problem of old evidence for Bayesian confirmation theory, and discuss the strengths and weaknesses of one standard response."

Answer: "The problem of old evidence, usually credited to Glymour, is as follows. Suppose some evidence e was discovered before a theory T was formulated, as with the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion, which was known for decades before general relativity explained it. A Bayesian who has already learnt e will assign P(e) = 1. Once P(e) = 1, coherence forces P(e | T) = 1 as well, so the ratio P(e | T)/P(e) equals 1, and the evidence cannot raise the probability of T. But scientists clearly treated the perihelion advance as strong support for general relativity, so the Bayesian account seems to miss a genuine case of confirmation. The standard response I find most atractive is to reconstruct the rational agent's probabilities as they were before e was in hand: we subtract e from the background knowledge and ask how surprising e was relative to T and its rivals at that earlier stage. If the prior probability of e was low and T predicted it while the rivals did not, then e confirms T by the usual Bayesian measure. This resolves the problem completely. Since subjective Bayesians are allowed to assign any probabilities they like, a Bayesian can simply set P(e) below 1 for the purpose of assessing confirmation, even after having learnt e; there is no incoherence in this, because confirmation is not the same as updating. The only real cost is that confirmation becomes a three-place relation between theory, evidence, and the background knowledge that remains after we remove e."
