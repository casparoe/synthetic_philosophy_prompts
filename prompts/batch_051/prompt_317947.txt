So I've been poking at a short essay about Dennett's competence without comprehension thing, specifically the Darwin/Turing pair, and I'd appreciate comments from someone who actually knows the source material. Working title: "The teleprinter and the strange inversion." I'm hanging the essay on this passage from Alan Turing, "Computing Machinery and Intelligence," *Mind* (1950), section 1:

"In order that tones of voice may not help the interrogator the answers should be written, or better still, typewritten. The ideal arrangement is to have a teleprinter communicating between the two rooms. Alternatively the question and answers can be repeated by an intermediary."

My main move is to treat this not just as Turing controlling for voice leakage, but as a quiet criterion-shift: thought is being defined operationally as whatever passes the game when the channel is deliberately impoverished. That's supposed to be the same move Darwin makes when design gets redefined as adaptation under selection rather than as intended construction. But I'm honestly not sure Turing is doing that much work here, or whether I've just fallen in love with my own reading of one sentence about teleprinters. I also dont want to claim Turing is doing the strange inversion in Dennett's sense if the historical evidence is thin. So the comments I want are: (1) is the reading of the passage accurate, (2) does the Darwin parallel carry real argumentative weight or is it a metaphor stretched too thin, and (3) where's the weakest spot in the essay as described?
