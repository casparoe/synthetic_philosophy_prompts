I'm a novelist and I've been reading up on criminal law for a trial scene, and I keep getting snagged on the "heat of passion" rule. The rough idea is that a killing counts as voluntary manslaughter rather than murder if the defendant acted in the heat of passion, before a reasonable person would have had time to cool off. So the law seems to be taking a stance on what an emotion is and how it runs its course—but I can't tell if its a coherent stance, partly because the same legal system turns around and lets juries put a dollar figure on emotional distress damages, as if an emotion were a lasting harm you could price. William James writes in "What is an Emotion?": "Our natural way of thinking about these standard emotions is that the mental perception of some fact excites the mental affection called the emotion, and that this latter state of mind gives rise to the bodily expression. My thesis on the contrary is that the bodily changes follow directly the perception of the exciting fact, and that our feeling of the same changes as they occur is the emotion." Could you use that passage to reconstruct the picture of emotion the law is quietly committed to, say whether the criminal and civil pieces of the practice are consistent, and tell me what would have to change in the cooling-off rule if James is right?
