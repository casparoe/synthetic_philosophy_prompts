I'm assembling a 'which account of choice under symmetric alternatives do you actually hold?' decision tree for a rationalist-adjacent blog, and I'd like the deliverable to be a sorting instrument rather than a prose essay. The aim is to sort readers by what they say about concrete cases in which an agent seems to have no preference between options, not by which label they find flattering.

Please construct the decision tree as follows:

- Use eight to ten forced-choice items, each presenting a concrete case of apparent indifference: identical tins in a supermarket; two job offers that tie on every criterion the reader settled on in advance; two strangers drowning at equal distance when only one can be saved; two lotteries with equal expected value but different payoff profiles; the traditional ass equidistant between two bales; choosing between two indistinguishable passwords or cryptographic keys; and so on. Each item should offer two or three answer options phrased in ordinary language, testing what the reader takes the agent to be doing or what the reader would do, with no jargon such as 'sufficient reason', 'incommensurability', 'voluntarism', or 'Buridan'.

- The scoring key should map answer patterns onto the following positions, which may be named only in the key and explanations: (a) choice reveals or constructs a preference, so a fully described chooser is never truly indifferent; (b) ties are real, and rationality is silent, so an agent may or must break the tie by a rationally arbitrary selection; (c) a volitional or agential faculty selects without being moved by a prior preference or reason; (d) apparent ties are an artefact of coarse-grained description, and the world or the agent's full desiderative profile is never exactly symmetric; (e) a perfectly rational and fully informed agent with no comparative preference genuinely has no basis for choice and can be stuck, while actual agents only 'choose' by failing to meet that ideal or by importing an unendorsed tie-breaker; (f) some choices — especially labels, conventions, and commitments — are not attempts to track an antecedently better option but create the value or commitment that dissolves the tie in the acting.

- Build the tree from the logical space where possible rather than from the list of names: first state each branching question as a claim on which the positions genuinely split (for example, whether the agent has a well-defined comparative preference after full reflection; whether a tie-break without a reason is rationally permissible; whether the symmetry belongs to the world or to the agent's representation of it). Then locate the positions among the resulting answer combinations. For combinations no one seems to occupy, say briefly whether they are incoherent, unattractive, or merely underexplored. Flag any positions the tree cannot separate rather than forcing a distinction.

- Each leaf should carry the position's name (or 'unnamed' if no one defends it) and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. The angle I care about is where the asymmetry required for choice is supposed to come from: the choice itself, the will, the fine-grained world, or nowhere.

Please write the answer in British English.
