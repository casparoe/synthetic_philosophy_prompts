You are a professional philosopher working on trust, testimony, and the ethics of childhood. Please think through this slowly, and begin by brainstorming the nearby categories the phenomenon could be mistaken for before deciding whether any of them fits. A bare name will not satisfy me: what I want is the carving — what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges are.

I keep noticing a situation in family life for which I have no word, and I would like either the nearest established concept or, if none exists, a newly defined one.

Take two ordinary instances. A six-year-old believes that Santa Claus brings the presents, because every adult the child trusts has said so for years, because the presents really do appear, because the half-eaten cookies and the footprints corroborate the story, and because the child's friends believe it too. The same child also believes that the tooth fairy replaces lost teeth with money, again on the unanimous word of trusted adults, with the coins under the pillow as visible proof. Both beliefs are false, and yet by any ordinary standard the child was not reasoning badly. Given the evidence the child actually had, the false belief was very well supported; the child was using exactly the methods that normally teach children truths about the world.

I want to set aside the question of whether the parents acted wrongly. What interests me is the child's position as a knower. A normally reliable method — believing the unanimous, well-meaning, corroborated report of the people whose care one depends on — produced a false belief, and it did so only because the child's entire information environment had been deliberately arranged to make a falsehood credible. When the child later discovers the truth, neither of the obvious reactions seems right. It would be wrong for the child to conclude that they should have doubted earlier, since at the time there was no evidence that would have supported doubt. But it would also be strange to say that the methods and informants that produced the false belief are entirely unchanged in their trustworthiness, since the discovery shows that the adults who taught the child most of what it knows are willing to fabricate, in concert and with staged evidence, an entire corner of the child's world.

My questions are these. Does this configuration have an established name in philosophy? Is it simply a specimen of some familiar category — a lie, a paternalistic lie, a noble lie, a collectively maintained fiction, a case of justified false belief of the kind discussed under knowledge and luck — or is it importantly different from each of those? If there is no established name, what would you call it, and how should it be defined so that it does not collapse into those neighboring categories? And is there a principled account of how a person should revise their trust after discovering such a thing: should the child discount the adults' other claims entirely, discount only the claims that were equally uncheckable, or discount none of them, and why? I have asked about children and parents, but I would also like to know whether the analysis carries over to an adult who learns that every source they trusted had coordinated on a falsehood, or whether the child case is special because of the relevant asymmetries between child and adult.
