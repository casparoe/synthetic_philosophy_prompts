In evolutionary biology, one of the most influential arguments against the habit of assuming that every feature of an organism was shaped directly by natural selection is Stephen Jay Gould and Richard Lewontin's spandrels argument. They describe the triangular spaces left between the arches and dome of St. Mark's Basilica in Venice. On their account, those spaces are a geometric byproduct of the plan of placing a dome on rounded arches; they were not built in order to display mosaics, but mosaics were later placed in them. A visitor who saw only the decorated surfaces could easily conclude that the spaces existed for the sake of the mosaics. Gould and Lewontin then transfer this picture to organisms: many traits, they suggest, are like the spandrels — byproducts of developmental constraints, architectural rules of growth, and phylogenetic history, rather than direct products of selection — and some of those byproducts are later co-opted for a function without having been selected for it.

Audit this analogy in detail.

First, produce the correspondence table. What in the biological case is supposed to correspond to the arches and dome, the spandrels themselves, the mosaics, the architect, and the later act of decoration? Be explicit about which correspondences are natural, which are forced, and which have no clean equivalent.

Second, identify where the analogy breaks. Consider at least the following: an architect works from intentions and a blueprint, whereas selection has no prior plan; the architectural spandrels are fixed by geometry once the dome-on-arches plan is chosen, whereas biological byproducts depend on contingent developmental and phylogenetic history; once a biological byproduct is co-opted, it can be maintained, modified, or embroidered upon, so the clean distinction between byproduct and adaptation erodes; and the architectural case itself may be less tidy than the analogy requires, since the relevant spaces may not be purely inert leftovers. Say which of these disanalogies matter for the work the analogy is meant to do, and which are merely verbal or irrelevant.

Third, state what the analogy quietly imports from architecture into biology — for example, assumptions about intentional design, about a fixed plan with geometric necessities, or about being able to separate a structure from its ornament — and assess whether those imports are doing more of the argumentative work than the explicit analogy acknowledges.

Finally, give a verdict. The conclusion the analogy is used to support is, roughly, that the adaptationist program is not entitled to treat every trait as an adaptation until shown otherwise, because byproducts and constraints are live alternatives that must be ruled out rather than ignored. How much of that conclusion survives the audit once the disanalogies are on the table? Would a better analogy carry more of the load, and what would it have to capture that the spandrels analogy cannot — or is the conclusion ultimately better defended without the analogy at all?
