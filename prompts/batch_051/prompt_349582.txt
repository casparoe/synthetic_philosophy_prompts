I am drafting a short essay on the reductionist/anti-reductionist debate about testimony, and the paper is built around a passage from David Hume, An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, Section X, Part I ("Of Miracles"):

"It will be sufficient to observe, that our assurance in any argument of this kind is derived from no other principle than our observation of the veracity of human testimony, and of the usual conformity of facts to the reports of witnesses. It being a general maxim, that no objects have any discoverable connexion together, and that all the inferences, which we can draw from one to another, are founded merely on our experience of their constant and regular conjunction; it is evident, that we ought not to make an exception to this maxim in favour of human testimony, whose connexion with any event seems, in itself, as little necessary as any other. Were not the memory tenacious to a certain degree; had not men commonly an inclination to truth and a principle of probity; were they not sensible to shame, when detected in a falsehood: Were not these, I say, discovered by experience to be qualities, inherent in human nature, we should never repose the least confidence in human testimony. A man delirious, or noted for falsehood and villany, has no manner of authority with us."

The standard reading treats this as pure reductionism: whatever right we have to believe what people tell us is acquired entirely through experience of the usual conformity between reports and facts, and through experience of the standing features of human nature—memory, truthfulness, sensitivity to shame—that make reports reliable. My working thesis is that this passage actually runs together two claims that need to be pulled apart: a genetic claim about how confidence in testimony arises and varies in creatures like us, and a justificatory claim about what gives someone the right to believe a report. The essay argues that the passage is most defensible when read as making the genetic claim, and that the familiar anti-reductionist counterexamples—for instance, a young child who trusts what she is told long before she could have run the relevant induction—damage the justificatory claim while leaving the genetic claim largely untouched. The controversial move comes next: I suggest that a reductionism built only on the genetic claim may no longer be a position about epistemic warrant at all, in which case much of the contemporary debate has been arguing past this passage rather than with it. I suspect that last step is an overstatement, but I cannot yet see exactly where it breaks.

I would be glad for critical comments on the draft. In particular: can the genetic/justificatory distinction be drawn here without distorting what Hume means by "no other principle"; does the child-type counterexample really fail to touch the passage once it is read genetically, or does it still embarrass the justificatory suggestion of the text; and which step in the argument is least likely to persuade someone in the anti-reductionist camp? What I most want is a hard look at whether the argument is coherent and where it overreaches, rather than suggestions about further reading.
