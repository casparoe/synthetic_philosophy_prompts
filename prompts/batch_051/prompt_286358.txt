I spent most of my working life in invertebrate physiology, and now that I'm retired I keep gnawing on a question about wild animal suffering. My working criteria for saying an animal is genuinely suffering, as opposed to just mechanically responding to damage, are: (1) it has receptors that encode noxious or tissue-damaging stimuli; (2) those signals get integrated into a single negatively valenced state, so there is a point of view that feels bad; (3) the animal pays some cost to escape or avoid the stimulus; and (4) the state shapes flexible learning, not just a hardwired reflex. Now consider a hornworm caterpillar parasitized by a braconid wasp. The larvae are eating it from the inside; the caterpillar thrashes, stops feeding, and can learn to avoid an odor paired with a noxious poke, with the learning transferring across contexts. But you can cut off the head and the body still thrashes, and it's nervous system is a chain of ganglia with no cortex or any single integrating hub like a vertebrate brain. Does this caterpillar qualify as suffering on my four criteria? Go through them one by one and say honestly where they underdetermine the answer. If it doesn't qualify, is that a fact about the caterpillar or a defect in my criteria? And if it does qualify, what does that mean for how we should think about the enormous number of wild insects that die slowly to parasitoids?
