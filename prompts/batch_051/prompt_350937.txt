Consider a situation that has become common in the digital age. A novel is still under copyright, but its author has died, the heirs cannot be located, and the publisher has disappeared. A software program is still under copyright, but the company that made it dissolved and no successor holds the rights. In both cases the work is legally owned, and the protection continues for the rest of the copyright term, yet there is no one who can grant permission to republish, digitize, adapt, or preserve the work. A library that wants to digitize an out-of-print book, or an archivist who wants to preserve a defunct company's software, is therefore told that the object has an owner, while no owner is available to exercise any ordinary power of ownership over it. These are the orphan-works cases.

The puzzle lies in the contrasts. In tangible property, an owner who walks away from a chair or a car can be treated as having abandoned it, and a finder may take ownership. Within intellectual property law itself, a trademark can be lost through non-use and neglect. But a copyright does not lapse merely because its owner cannot be found and the work is commercially dead. What theory of ownership does this legal practice presuppose? Does it presuppose that property is fundamentally a right to exclude, detachable from use, stewardship, and the owner's continued existence? Or would a coherent theory of intellectual property treat the orphan-works outcome as a mistake — an inconsistency the incentive-based system never intended? Reconstruct the implicit theory of ownership as fully as you can. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, and say what would have to change if the underlying view of ownership were replaced by one that ties rights to continued use or care.
