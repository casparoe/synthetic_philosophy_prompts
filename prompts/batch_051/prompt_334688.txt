I am a doctoral student in political philosophy working on collective decision-making, and I have been unable to settle whether an asymmetry in our standards of rationality can be defended. Consider an individual asked, about a single legal case, whether the defendant breached a duty of care, whether the breach caused the harm, and whether the defendant is liable, where the law makes liability turn on both breach and causation. If he answers yes to breach, yes to causation, and no to liability, we call him incoherent: he affirms the premises and denies the conclusion they entail. Now consider a three-member panel deciding the same three questions by majority vote, with each member individually coherent. The first judge accepts breach, denies causation, and therefore denies liability; the second denies breach, accepts causation, and therefore denies liability; the third accepts both breach and causation, and therefore accepts liability. The panel's majority positions are then the very combination we would not accept from an individual: yes to breach, yes to causation, no to liability. This is the discursive dilemma, and bodies of this sort produce such outcomes routinely, yet we accept their decisions as ordinary legitimate collective judgements rather than as symptoms of irrationality.

I want to know whether this difference in treatment is justified. One might argue that a group is not a single mind and so cannot be incoherent; that majority rule is simply a fair procedure for settling disagreement; or that the alternatives, such as premise-based voting, introduce their own arbitrariness. But the asymmetry still troubles me, because we rely on the aggregated answers as a unified view. Is the asymmetry a defensible division of labour between individual and collective rationality, or an inconsistency in our practice that we should repair - either by demanding individual-level coherence from groups or by relaxing the coherence we demand of individuals? Please answer as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion, and let the first point state your overall verdict.
