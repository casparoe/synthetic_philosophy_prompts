I do not mean the familiar move that death cannot harm you because you are not there to be harmed; that version has always struck me as too glib. I mean a sharper timing argument. It seems to show that death's alleged badness has no location on the timeline, which would make it unlike ordinary harms such as pain, illness, or poverty. What worries me is that the same argumentative machine appears to prove that being betrayed in secret is never bad for the betrayed person: they never experience it, and there is no moment at which it hurts them. Here is the argument:

(1) If a state of affairs is bad for a person, then there is some time at which it is bad for that person.

(2) The only candidate times at which death could be bad for the person who dies are before death, at the moment of death, and after death.

(3) Before death, the person has not yet died; they may suffer from anticipating death, but the anticipation is not the death itself, and a future event is not thereby bad now.

(4) At the moment of death, either the person is still alive or no longer alive. If still alive, the death has not yet occurred; if no longer alive, there is no subject present to be harmed. Either way, the moment of death supplies no time at which death is bad for the person.

(5) After death, the person no longer exists, and nothing can be bad for someone who does not exist.

(6) Therefore, there is no time at which death is bad for the person who dies.

(7) Therefore, by (1) and (6), death is not bad for the person who dies.
