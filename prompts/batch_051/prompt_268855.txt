Counterfactual: the 1968 Harvard Medical School ad hoc committee that proposed using irreversible coma as a criterion for death never convenes, and no jurisdiction ever adopts a neurological standard afterward. So right up to 2025 the law everywhere stays with the old rule: you're dead only when your heart and breathing have stopped permanently and won't restart. That means the patient on a ventilator with no brainstem reflexes is, legally, alive.

Walk me through how critical care, organ transplantation, advance directives and family decisions about withdrawing life support, homicide and assault law, and health insurance have adapted by now. For each one I want the causal chain laid out: what the old circulatory standard forces, what workarounds people and institutions would invent, and where the system just quietly breaks. Then separate your predictions into the ones you'd bet on and the ones that are guesses, and say what extra fact about human behavior or institutions each weak prediction depends on. Also think about this: does the legal system start treating some of these living bodies as dead for particular purposes anyway — letting families withdraw ventilators, charging a beating that destroys the brain as murder, letting insurers stop paying — while publicly insisting the old definition is intact? How far can that kind of equivocation be stretched before it collapses?
