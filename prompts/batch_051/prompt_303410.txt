I am preparing a short presentation for my high school philosophy club, and I want to make sure I do not accidentally repeat a mischaracterization. I recently read a popular science article that summarized panpsychism roughly as follows. The article said that panpsychism is the view that mind is everywhere, and that its appeal is that it offers a clean solution to the hard problem of consciousness: instead of trying to explain how consciousness suddenly switches on when matter gets complicated enough, it claims that every fundamental particle already has a tiny glimmer of experience, and the rich consciousness of a human being is just these micro-experiences combined, the way many small drops of water run together into a puddle. It added that, in this picture, even a rock or a tree is conscious in some loose sense, since it is built entirely out of conscious parts. The article then argued that the view collapses under the combination problem: no matter how many little minds you pile up, they remain separate, just as a crowd of people never becomes a single person. So, it concluded, panpsychism cannot explain how you and I end up with one unified consciousness, and we are left where we started.

I find the crowd analogy especially convincing, and I am inclined simply to report the article's conclusion. But I also suspect the article may have smoothed over some distinctions in order to make the view easy to criticize. Could you help me sort out which parts of that summary are fair, which parts a careful panpsychist would push back on, and whether the final conclusion actually follows from the parts that survive correction?
