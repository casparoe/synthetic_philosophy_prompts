Two friends of mine — one a civil litigator, the other a former public defender — got stuck in an argument over dinner, and I could not referee it. It began with a familiar hypothetical. A pedestrian is hit by a bus and injured, and she cannot identify the bus. It is stipulated that the Blue Bus Company runs 80 percent of the buses on that route at that hour, and that the only other company runs the remaining 20 percent. She sues Blue Bus. In a civil case the plaintiff must prove each element by a preponderance of the evidence — more likely than not. Does the 80 percent statistic justify a judgment against Blue Bus?

Naomi, the civil litigator, said it plainly does. Preponderance is a rule for allocating the risk of error, not a promise of certainty, and 0.8 is above 0.5. If the court refuses to find liability here, it lets a defendant that very likely caused the injury escape, on no evidence pointing the other way.

Marcus, the former public defender, answered with the gatecrasher. A thousand people attend an event, but only 499 paid admission. Choose an attendee at random and it is 50.1 percent likely that she entered without paying — more likely than not. Yet you would not on that basis demand payment from each member of the crowd individually, because you would knowingly be collecting from 499 people who did pay. The same bare-probability logic that condemns Blue Bus would license a finding against each gatecrasher on evidence you know is false for hundreds of them. So individual verdicts cannot be built on naked statistics.

Naomi called this a category error. The gatecrasher scenario is a mass-enforcement problem, or a criminal-law problem about the state's special aversion to convicting the innocent. A civil damages judgment is exactly an error-allocation device: decide every such case by the probabilities and you minimize the expected number of wrong verdicts across the class. The reason we would hesitate to penalize the whole crowd is not a reason to hesitate about one civil defendant.

Marcus said she had skipped the harder problem. The 80 percent figure is not a fact about this accident; it is a fact about some reference class — the route at that hour. Choose a different class and the probability changes: weekdays, northbound traffic after dusk, buses under maintenance contracts. Each description yields a different probability for the same event, and nothing in the evidence selects among them. The court would be finding liability on the strength of a description it chose for convenience.

Naomi replied that this cuts both ways. An eyewitness's reliability is also a rate over a class of sightings, and a medical expert's testimony rests on base rates over patient populations. If evidence cannot be used until the reference-class problem is solved, courts could not function. The law already accepts purely statistical allocation in some contexts, such as market-share liability, where damages are divided among manufacturers without proof of which one caused which injury.

Marcus granted the point but insisted a difference in kind remains. A generally reliable eyewitness who says the bus was blue could be wrong only through some local failure — bad light, misperception, lying. The 80 percent statistic, by contrast, is fully compatible with this very bus being one of the other company's: the statistic is just as accurate if the bus was not blue. Nothing has to go wrong for the statistic to mislead about this accident. So the statistic supports the assertion that buses on the route are usually blue, not the assertion that this bus caused this injury, and a verdict asserts the latter.

Naomi answered that this is phenomenology, not probability. Suppose two cases rest on different evidence, and in both the court's rational posterior that the defendant is liable is 0.8. Refusing liability in one but not the other is knowingly making the more error-prone decision in the case where you refuse. Trials are for allocating the risk of being wrong, not for making metaphysical pronouncements.

Marcus's final move was to insist that the law's own categories resist this. Tort requires the plaintiff to prove actual causation — that this defendant's conduct in fact caused this injury — not an elevated risk that it did. If the 80 percent figure suffices for causation, causation collapses into statistical risk, and a judgment stops saying what it has always been understood to say.

I have compressed their argument; these are the moves that mattered. Please adjudicate it move by move, rating each contribution from clearly sound to clearly mistaken and explaining each rating. Then say: who is more right overall, if anyone; whether the blue-bus plaintiff should prevail under preponderance; and whether the distinction between statistical and individualized evidence, or the appeal to actual causation, survives the reference-class problem — or whether Naomi is right that it collapses under pressure.
