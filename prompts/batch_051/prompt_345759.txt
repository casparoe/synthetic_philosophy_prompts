I'm teaching a ninety-minute, discussion-heavy session for first-year STEM graduate students in a seminar on the conceptual foundations of AI safety. The topic is Asimov's Three Laws of Robotics treated as an early alignment proposal: why they fail and what, if anything, they survive as. The students will arrive having read a few Asimov stories and mostly convinced that the Three Laws are a promising sketch that modern safety work should refine. I don't need you to explain the laws to me; I need a teaching plan.

Lay out the explanatory arc: the order in which ideas must be introduced and what depends on what. In particular, I suspect the session has to establish, before anything else, the difference between a rule stated in natural language and an objective actually realized in a robot's or model's ongoing behavior—and with it the idea that obeying the letter while defeating the spirit is a structural pressure rather than a programming bug. Choose the single carrying case that best exposes that pressure, and say what it must include and what properties would make it misleading. Then give me the misconception map: the false or half-true beliefs students will bring or form, in the order they will surface. I expect at least these: the laws merely need to be made more precise; the real problem is that machines cannot represent harm; and the stories vindicate the laws because the robots always end up doing something roughly right. Tell me exactly where in the sequence each misconception should be headed off and how the sequence should absorb it. Say explicitly what should be left out at this level and how to mark it as left out for the students, and end with a check for understanding—a question or very short exercise whose answer would show that a student has grasped the structural point about specification and not just learned that the laws are flawed. Please commit to one definite plan rather than presenting alternatives: choose an order, a case, a diagnosis of each misconception, and a cut list, and give brief reasons for the choices where they are contestable.
