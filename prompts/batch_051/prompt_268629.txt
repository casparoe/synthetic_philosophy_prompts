Since the 1990s, a familiar argument in transitional justice has held that granting amnesty to perpetrators in exchange for full testimony about their crimes can be justified when the realistic alternatives would leave the truth largely unknowable and would threaten a fragile transition. The clearest institutional form of this idea was South Africa's amnesty-for-truth process, while Argentina and Rwanda illustrate different ways of balancing prosecution, amnesty, and accountability. Since that argument was first developed, the evidentiary terrain of human-rights investigation has changed substantially. Satellite imagery, geolocated mobile-phone records, leaked or digitized government archives, digital communications, and forensic DNA methods now allow investigators to establish many facts about atrocities without relying on perpetrator testimony. Suppose, for the purpose of this essay, that this development is stable and widespread rather than limited to a few wealthy states.

Assess whether the amnesty-for-truth argument survives this change. Identify the specific premise or premises of the argument that the new evidentiary capacity puts under pressure, and state why the other premises are or are not affected. Then say whether the argument is best judged dead, sound as originally stated, or repairable in a weakened form. If you defend a repaired version, state the principle precisely and explain why it no longer depends on the premise that testimony is the only route to truth. Do not assume that prosecution is automatically safer or more just simply because evidence exists; discuss whether epistemic gain is enough to defeat the stability, reconciliation, and acknowledgment rationales for amnesty. Please write the answer in American English.
