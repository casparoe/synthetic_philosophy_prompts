I have been running an advanced seminar on the epistemology of markets for several years, and the discussion stalls at the same point every time: what the no-trade theorem actually shows. The group is split. One side reads it as a fatal result for the information-aggregation view of markets. On their telling, the theorem shows that rational Bayesian agents with common priors, common knowledge of their rationality, and common knowledge that exchange is voluntary could never trade purely on differences in private information: each would treat the other's willingness to trade as itself adverse information, and any such exchange would be a zero-sum bet that at least one party should refuse once they update on what the offer reveals. So, if observed speculative trading really is as information-driven as it looks, it must be riding on noise, overconfidence, or divergent priors — none of which underwrites the claim that markets efficiently discover fundamentals. The other side reads exactly the same theorem as a reductio of its own assumptions rather than of markets. On their view, the result merely pins down the conditions under which the price system cannot do its informational work: common priors and common knowledge of rationality are precisely the idealizations that suppress the process — heterogeneous beliefs, costly information, the ordinary discipline of profit and loss — that the market story needs in order to get going. So the theorem tells us about a stylized model, not about actual markets.

What frustrates me is that no one in the room disputes the facts: everyone agrees that speculative trade occurs, that transaction prices contain at least some information, and that the theorem is valid as a piece of model logic. The disagreement is over what kind of fact that validity is, and what it can properly be cited as evidence for. After a year of argument, neither side has moved. My working hypothesis is that this is not really a disagreement about markets, or even about the theorem, but about the status of idealizing assumptions: one side treats common priors and common knowledge as harmless simplifications to be relaxed later, while the other treats them as carrying the explanatory burden, so that relaxing them changes the question rather than answering it. Underneath that, I suspect, is a difference about what an economic model must do to explain — isolate a mechanism that could in principle operate in real markets, or merely trace the logical consequences of a set of explicit commitments. But I am not certain this is the right diagnosis; the impasse might instead rest on a hidden empirical question about how much real-world trade is purely informational, or a hidden formal question about how far the result survives as its assumptions are relaxed one at a time.

I am asking for a diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement itself, not a verdict for either side. Given that diagnosis, tell me what would count as progress — a distinction, a theorem, an empirical finding, a reformulation of the question — or, if no progress should be expected, why the question is ill-posed or the two groups are answering different questions under one heading. In your answer, state the diagnosis in the first sentence, then reason through it, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
