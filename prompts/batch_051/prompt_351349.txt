I retired about six years ago after a career in high-energy physics and started reading philosophy of science properly, which I should have done decades ago. Heres a distinction I've been turning over for the last year or so, and I want an honest verdict rather than encouragement.

Most of the talk about a final theory or a theory of everything assumes the target is what I'd call answer-completeness: every well-posed empirical question in the theorys domain has a definite answer once you feed in the boundary conditions. I think that's the wrong target. You can always make a theory answer-complete in a cheap way by adding adjustable bits, or by making it flexible enough that whatever is not predicted becomes a new ingredient. That kind of theory is answer-complete and completely dead.

So I've been using a second notion I call question-completeness. A theory is question-complete when it generates no new well-posed empirical questions beyond the ones it was introduced to answer. My claim is that the end of physics is a theory that is both answer-complete and question-complete, and that the second is the real marker. A final theory should be experimentally sterile: every experiment it suggests is just a confirmation of what it already says, never a probe into something it hasn't already specified. Ptolemaic epicycles are the opposite extreme: they never stop generating the next question, the next epicycle, the next anomaly to absorb. A cheap answer-complete theory keeps birthing questions; a final one should go quiet.

The operational side is a sort of decay rate for questions. Count the number of distinct new well-posed empirical questions a theory generates per decade, per working physicist. It should spike when the theory is young and decay as it matures. The final theory would be the fixed point where that rate falls to zero while the theory's predictive success stays high.

Now I'm aware this might just be the difference between fitting data and predicting novel results dressed up in new clothes. And my whole criterium leans on what counts as a well-posed empirical question, which is probably theory-relative: a stubborn theory could just rule out every awkward question and declare itself question-complete. It might also confuse physics with the sociology of a community that has run out of ideas. And I honestly can't decide whether a question like why this constant has this value is well-posed or not, even though my criterion seems to hang on exactly that sort of boundary case.

So tell me straight: is question-completeness a real addition, or does it already exist under a name I would know if I had read more? What are the three strongest objections, and are any of them fatal rather than just uncomfortable?
