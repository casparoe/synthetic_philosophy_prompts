I have been reading behavioral genetics alongside the machine-learning literature on prediction, and I keep noticing that the two fields seem to run the same set of warnings in two different dialects. In the cleanest case, a narrow-sense heritability estimate is just the proportion of phenotypic variance accounted for by a linear model whose predictors are the additive contributions of the relevant genetic variants — that is, the R² of a regression of phenotype on genotype. And the standard cautions about heritability map almost one-to-one onto standard cautions about R². Heritability is specific to a particular population in a particular range of environments, just as R² depends on the spread of the predictor variables in the sample. High heritability does not imply that the trait is genetically determined or that environmental intervention cannot move it, just as a high R² says nothing about causation or manipulability. A within-population heritability estimate licenses no inference about the causes of average differences between populations, just as a regression fitted within one group licenses no inference about differences between group means. And an estimate obtained under one environmental regime need not survive a change in that regime, just as a model fitted on one distribution need not transfer to another.

Is this a genuine identity of structure, or am I just pattern-matching? The analogy seems cleanest for estimates computed from measured genotypes, and less clean for twin-based estimates, which decompose variance from correlations among relatives rather than fitting an explicit regression — though perhaps that is only a different estimator of the same underlying quantity. If the parallel is real, then results about distribution shift, omitted-variable bias, and variance inflation should transfer back to genetics and say something non-obvious about when a heritability estimate will or will not travel. What would have to be true of both sides for that transfer to be legitimate, what is one precise result from statistics or machine learning that carries over and tells us something about heritability that the usual cautions miss, and what is one thing the parallel seems to predict that turns out false?
