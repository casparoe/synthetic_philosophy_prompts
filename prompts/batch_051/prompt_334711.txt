I've been teaching an undergrad moral psychology course and we just spent two weeks on Schopenhauer and Nietzsche on pity, and I've walked away with a distinction I keep reaching for but haven't seen spelled out anywhere. I call it sunken pity vs. banked pity. Sunken pity is the kind where the boundary between me and the sufferer briefly goes: I feel their suffering as mine, and the impulse to help isn't really a decision so much as a reflex of self-preservation — my pain and theirs are the same pain. Banked pity keeps the boundary firmly in place: I register that you're suffering, I'm moved, but my distance and agency are preserved, and the very act of registering it becomes moral credit I can draw on later. My working view is that Schopenhauer takes pity to be essentially sunken — if it isn't identificatory it isn't Mitleid at all — while Nietzsche thinks the sunken kind is mostly a self-serving fantasy and that the pity we actually observe is banked, with all its hidden self-regard. If that's right, a good chunk of their fight is just two people describing different phenomena and both calling it pity.

I'm aware this could be too neat, so I want an honest assessment rather than encouragement. Is the sunken/banked distinction tracking something real, or is it just a metaphor that collapses on inspection? Does it actually carve the Schopenhauer/Nietzsche disagreement at the joints — or does Nietzsche's attack cut against the sunken kind too, and would Schopenhauer just say the banked kind isn't pity at all but something like vanity? And where does the distinction itself fail: cases that are both, cases that are neither, places where having made it doesn't actualy do any work. If it already exists under another name in the literature, tell me that and what it's missing.
