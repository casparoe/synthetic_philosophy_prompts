I've been reading around the nationalism debate, and one thing keeps puzzling me: the argument over how old nations are has been running for decades without any apparent convergence, even though the main camps agree on a surprising amount of the empirical record. I'd like a diagnosis of the structure of that impasse, not another survey or verdict.

The positions, as I understand them. Primordialists and perennialists hold, in somewhat different ways, that nations are old — rooted in long-lived ethnic communities whose members felt durable collective loyalty long before the modern period. The modernist camp, in the Hobsbawm–Anderson–Gellner line, holds that nations as we know them are recent constructions: print, mass literacy, standardized vernaculars, state administration, and industrial mobility made it newly possible for people to imagine themselves as members of a large anonymous national community, and much of the apparent antiquity of national traditions is retrospective invention. Ethnosymbolists, following Anthony Smith, split the difference: modern nations are new in form, but they crystallize out of pre-modern ethnic cores — shared names, myths, symbols, and memories — that constrain which nations could form at all.

What frustrates me is that the camps barely disagree about the key facts. Modernists do not deny that pre-modern people had ethnic identities, origin myths, and local solidarities; ethnosymbolists and primordialists do not deny that mass literacy, printing, bureaucratic states, and language standardization transformed the scale and self-understanding of political communities. So the dispute has all the marks of something that should have been resolvable by now, yet each side keeps citing the facts it finds salient and the others never move.

My working hypothesis is that the debate is a disguised ship-of-Theseus problem: the camps are applying different similarity metrics to historical sequences, and the question of how old a nation is has no determinate answer until you fix the metric. One metric tracks institutional and political continuity, another tracks cultural content such as language and myth, and a third tracks self-ascription and collective consciousness. Before I commit to that, though, I want it tested against the strongest versions of each position.

So: is this really a verbal disagreement about the meaning of nation — a sovereign political unit versus a self-conscious ethnic group versus something else — or is a substantive disagreement hiding somewhere else? Is the hidden disagreement about what kind and degree of continuity suffices for identity across time? Is it about which cases count as data, with modernists weighting Western European states and ethnosymbolists weighting Greece, Poland, Armenia, or stateless nations? Is it about what an explanation is supposed to do, so that genealogy, causal mechanism, and counterfactual dependence are all being smuggled under one heading? Or is something normative — claims about territorial legitimacy or authenticity — doing the real work under the guise of historical argument? Give me the diagnosis, supported by what each camp actually says, and then say what would count as progress given that diagnosis: a distinction that dissolves the question, an empirical program that could genuinely discriminate between the positions, or a reason to think no historical finding can settle it because the question as usually posed is ill-posed.
