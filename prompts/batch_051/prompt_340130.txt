I'm a retired geologist — forty years treating a thing as real if you could stick a probe into it — and lately I've tumbled into philosophy of mathematics, specifically the question of what makes a mathematical object a legitimate object at all. A friend who runs a community adult-ed program has roped me into leading a ninety-minute evening session for a group of bright nonspecialists: high-school algebra and geometry, plus whatever they've picked up from popular science on YouTube. The arc I want them to feel in their bones is the move from the old constructibility standard — the Greeks' idea that a curve counted as a real object of geometry only if you could, in principle, produce it with straightedge and compass — to the later situation in which curves like the cycloid (the path of a point on a rolling wheel) or the graph of the sine function count as perfectly good objects because they are pinned down by definitions and equations, even though no straightedge-and-compass construction will generate them. I don't want to deliver a history lecture; I want the session built so the shift feels forced, as if the old criterion snaps and something else has to take it's place.

Plan the session's explanatory arc for me. I care about the order of ideas: what has to be in place before the problem even bites (I suspect they first have to see constructibility as a standard for existence, not just a drawing technique), and what depends on what, so I understand why each step lands where it does. What single example should carry the entire thing — I was thinking the cycloid, because it is easy to generate mechanically, easy to picture, and clearly beyond straightedge and compass, but tell me if a different example carries more of the conceptual load and what properties the carrying example has to have and lack. What misconceptions should I expect them to walk in with or form halfway through — things like 'if Desmos can plot it, it exists,' or 'these transcendental curves are just approximations, not real mathematics,' or 'an equation is just shorthand for a picture' — and where exactly in the sequence should I defuse each one. At the end, what one question or small task should I put to them so I can tell whether they've actually gotten the distinction between a construction procedure and an object pinned down by a defining property, rather than just memorizing the words constructible and transcendental? And what should I openly leave out at this level — I'm guessing the impossibility proofs, set-theoretic existence, most rigor — and say I'm leaving out?
