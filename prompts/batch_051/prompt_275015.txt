Next month I'm giving a paper at a workshop on the ethics of war. The audience is mostly just war folks — a mix of revisionists who think the deep moral work is done by individual liability, traditionalists who think the rules of war come apart from ordinary morality, two Catholic moral theologians, and one law-of-armed-conflict lawyer. I'm defending a deliberatly Anscombean line: Truman's decision to bomb Hiroshima and Nagasaki was murder, and it stays murder even if you grant that the war was just and that the invasion alternative would have killed far more people, because the civilians weren't collateral damage in the double-effect sense — their deaths were the point, a means of coercing surrender. My wrinkle is that I don't think the atomic bomb changes the analysis at all, so the same verdict applies to the conventional firebombing of Tokyo (and Dresden, for that matter), which I suspect will annoy the traditionalists in the room a lot more than the nukes will.
