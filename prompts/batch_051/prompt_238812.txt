For a seminar on long-run predictions about population and resources, I want the specification first: what must a theory in this area get right before we can grant it the status of "adequate"? I keep hearing past predictions defended as "right but early" and other people treating the same record as straightforward falsification. Before I read further, write the full list of desiderata a long-run population-resource theory must satisfy, with the case or consideration behind each requirement. The list should address, but need not be limited to: how the theory handles (i) local famines that occur while global per-capita food supply rises, (ii) the demographic transition, (iii) the Green Revolution's apparent expansion of carrying capacity, (iv) a contested collapse such as Easter Island, and (v) the shift toward sink constraints—climate, nitrogen, water—rather than harvested resources. Include the distinctions a theory cannot collapse: between a mechanism that has not yet operated and one the theory omitted; between a conditional and an unconditional prediction; between local scarcity and a global binding constraint; and between "the theory was wrong" and "the theory was right but early." Then mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular camp (Malthusian, Boserupian, demographic-transition, and similar). I want a committed answer: identify which single requirement does the most work in excluding the usual candidate theories, flag any pair or subset of requirements that is jointly unsatisfiable—with special attention to whether a theory can at once be falsifiable by evidence within a specified horizon and still legitimately invoke "right but early" after an apparent failure—and defend your classification rather than leaving it all open.
