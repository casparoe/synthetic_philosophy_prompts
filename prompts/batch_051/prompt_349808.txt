I have been turning over a parallel between nonlinear dynamics and a certain picture of historical explanation, and I cannot tell whether it is a real structural affinity or just an attractive piece of pattern-matching.

On one side is the mathematical phenomenon of deterministic chaos. A deterministic system can still be unpredictable in a specific sense: two trajectories that begin arbitrarily close together separate exponentially, with a positive Lyapunov exponent; beyond a finite predictability horizon, however finely the initial state is measured, the forecast error grows until it saturates at the size of the system's attractor. On the other side is the old intuition about history condensed in the saying about the lost nail that costs a shoe, a horse, a rider, a battle, and finally a kingdom. The intuition is that the difference between two possible histories can begin at the scale of a missing nail and end at the scale of a state's survival or collapse.

The mapping I have in mind is this: a particular history is a trajectory; an event is a state; a counterfactual alteration, such as the nail's being lost rather than kept, is an infinitesimal difference in initial conditions; and the subsequent divergence between the two histories is the exponential separation of nearby trajectories. If the mapping were exact, several things should transfer. There should be something like a characteristic Lyapunov time for historical episodes — a horizon beyond which the detailed course of events, as opposed to broad constraints, could not have been predicted or recovered from any amount of archival evidence. Some periods or processes should be chaotic in this sense while others are stable, and only the chaotic ones should exhibit for-want-of-a-nail sensitivity. And there should be a principled metric on possible histories that makes statements about how far apart two worlds have drifted meaningful.

The difficulty is that each of these transferred claims seems off when I try to apply it to actual events. I cannot say whether that is because the analogy is wrong, because history fails the conditions the mathematics requires, or because I have drawn the mapping inaccurately.

So my question: is this a genuine structural identity, a loose analogy, or a category mistake? What would have to be true of historical processes themselves, not merely of how we describe them, for concepts like Lyapunov exponents, predictability horizons, and attractors to do real work in history rather than provide ornament? And what is the clearest historical case in which the parallel yields a prediction that is actually false, rather than merely hard to check?
