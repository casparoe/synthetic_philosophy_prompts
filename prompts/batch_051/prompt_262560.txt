I work as a software engineer, mostly on games and simulations, and I have no formal background in philosophy. Lately I've been trying to sort out my own reactions to cases about whether video-game characters matter morally. I don't want a literature review; I want you to inspect my verdicts and tell me whether a single coherent principle captures them, and if not, which of them I should give up.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. Scripted sympathetic NPC. In a story-driven game, an NPC has thousands of lines of dialogue, a detailed backstory, and scripted emotional responses. If a player spends an hour tormenting her purely to make the character suffer, with no in-game goal, I feel the act is mildly wrong—but mainly because it seems bad for the player, a kind of cruelty rehearsal, rather than because the character is wronged.

2. Procedural background pedestrian. In a city simulator, pedestrians are generated on the fly with no persistent memory, no names, and no dialogue. Running over dozens of them with a car feels no different to me from knocking over traffic cones.

3. Griefing another player. If I use an exploit to destroy another human player's carefully built structure, that feels clearly wrong, but the wrongness is about the human's time and investment, not about their avatar.

4. Deleting a save file. I delete a save file containing a character I played for two hundred hours and care about. The deletion doesn't feel like ending anyone. I hesitate, but only the way I'd hesitate before deleting an old photo.

5. Rule-based bot farm. I run a thousand instances of a simple rule-based bot in an MMO. Each bot follows the same dozen if-then rules. When I terminate all of them with one script, I feel nothing about ending the bots themselves, apart from possible concern about what they did to the game's economy.

6. Learned agent. I train a single agent in a physics sandbox using reinforcement learning. It isn't scripted; over weeks it develops novel, flexible behavior to pursue its reward, and it now reliably avoids damage, seeks energy, and tries new things when understimulated. Deleting it feels at least a little wrong, in a way deleting the bot farm doesn't. It feels like ending something with a point of view, however thin.

Cases 5 and 6 are the pair that bothers me. They look structurally alike—in both I create software agents, run them, and terminate them—but I react differently. Is that difference defensible, or am I just anthropomorphizing the learned agent because its behavior is less predictable than the bots'?

What I'd like from you: state a principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures as many of my six verdicts as possible, stated precisely. Then apply it to cases I haven't listed: a behaviorally rich NPC whose whole life is deterministically precomputed from a seed; a learned agent with no persistence between runs; a character in a game no one is currently playing; and a large simulated ecosystem that gets shut down. Tell me which of my six original verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and say whether you think the fault is in the principle or in my verdict. I would rather have a simpler principle that overturns one of my verdicts than a patchwork that saves all six by adding a clause for each case.

Please begin your reply with your bottom line—the principle or candidate set you think is best—in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble and no restating my question.
