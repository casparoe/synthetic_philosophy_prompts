Heres a real episode I want you to think through, not report on. The 1976 Viking landers on Mars carried a suite of biology experiments. The labeled release experiment added a nutrient broth with radioactive carbon to Martian soil; if something in the soil metabolized it, it would release radioactive CO2, and it did — the signal looked biological, and the usual heat-sterilized control didn't do it. But the gas chromatograph/mass spectrometer on the same lander found essentially no organic molecules in the soil, so the consensus became that the labeled release result was some weird inorganic surface chemistry, not life.

I want you to analyze this as a case study in the philosophy of defining life, running it through two views and then running the episode back through them. On the first view, 'life' just is whatever passes a battery of operational tests — metabolism, growth, maybe reproduction — so detecting life means running the tests and reading off the answer. On the second view, 'life' is a messy natural kind or cluster concept, and operational tests only ever detect proxy signatures, so a single positive metabolic signal doesn't settle what kind of thing you're looking at. Discipline the mapping: for each view, who or what is the player, what were the decision options open to the biology team, and what counted as evidence when they got the labeled release result and the no-organics result? Include where each view is silent. Then reverse the lens: does the episode make operational definitions look like the only way to make 'detecting life' a scientific project, or does it make them look like a way to mistake soil chemistry for biology? And whats your verdict on the boundary case itself — a reaction that eats and exhales but has no detectable biochemistry and maybe doesn't reproduce. Is that 'not life,' 'life as we know it,' or a sign that the term 'life' wasnt doing any real work in the debate at all?
