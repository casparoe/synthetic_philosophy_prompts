We got onto pacifism at lunch the other day because my manager was reading something about conscientious objectors, and it turned into a real back-and-forth between two of my coworkers, Dana and Priya. I'm a software engineer and I've never done any formal philosophy, so I couldnt referee it, but I've been thinking about it ever since. Dana pulled up William James's essay "The Moral Equivalent of War" on her phone and read this part out loud:

The war against war is going to be no holiday excursion or camping party. The military feelings are too deeply grounded to abdicate their place among our ideals until better substitutes are offered than the glory and shame that come to nations as well as to individuals from the ups and downs of politics and the vicissitudes of trade.

The exchange after that went roughly like this. Dana said: James's point is that the anti-war side keeps assuming rational argument will win, but the pull toward war is emotional and durable. War gives people glory, collective purpose, discipline, a sense of being part of something bigger than themselves. If pacifism refuses to substitute anything for those things, it just leaves a need-shaped hole. So pacifism has to build, not only oppose.

Priya said: that's exactly the surrender. He calls them "military feelings" and says they hold a place "among our ideals." If those are the ideals you're trying to satisfy, you've already let war define the target. A serious pacifism should ask why glory and sacrifice have to look like war at all, not try to feed the same appetite with a peaceful version.

Dana: I don't think he's endorsing those feelings, he's modeling them. The "until better substitutes are offered" part is a conditional about human nature. It's like inheriting a legacy codebase — the old design isn't good, but you can't just delete it and declare victory. You have to understand what's actually running before you can refactor it.

Priya: but look at how he supports the claim right after. He says almost no American would vote to erase the Civil War from history, and he calls the ancestors and battles "a sacred spiritual possession worth more than all the blood poured out." If your evidence that military feelings run deep is that people sacralize a war, then the feeling and the violence come bundled. You can't neatly strip out the war and keep the rest.

Dana: I think that's too harsh. He picks the strongest example on purpose. The claim is just that the appeal exists, it's old, and it isn't going anywhere. Ignore it and you lose.

Priya: fine, but he also says war is "the strong life" and that its horrors make the fascination. Once you take that as the psychology, pacifism turns into a project of reproducing the intensity of war without the killing. That's a pretty warlike peace, and it still treats the appetite as fixed instead of something history and culture made.

Dana: so what's the alternative? Tell someone who wants meaning and collective sacrifice that their desire is just wrong? Good luck selling that.

Priya: not that it's just wrong — that part of pacifism's job is to reshape the desire itself, not slot a substitute into it. That's a different psychological claim than James is making.

Dana: so really we're disagreeing about whether pacifism should compete with war on the same psychological market, or reject the market.

Priya: yeah, and about whether James's "realism" is a useful constraint on strategy or a loaded bet about human nature that pacifism shouldn't accept.

Honestly I can see pieces of both, and now I'm not sure if James is handing pacifists a helpful constraint or stacking the deck against a more radical pacifism. Could you go through their actual exchange point by point, say who's right about which specific claims, and then say who is more right overall about what James is arguing in that passage?
