In arguments about whether large language models understand language, one move recurs with remarkable confidence: someone describes the system as a 'stochastic parrot' and treats the matter as closed. The implicit inference appears to be that a stochastic parrot cannot genuinely mean, refer, or understand, so the debate ends there. I want to slow that inference down.

Give me a careful disambiguation of the phrase 'stochastic parrot.' Lay out the distinct precise claims it could be used to assert. Distinguish at least claims about (a) the training objective (the model is trained to maximize the probability of next tokens over a corpus), (b) the inference-time procedure (outputs are sampled from a learned probability distribution), (c) the absence of communicative intent, (d) the absence of semantic grounding or reference, and (e) the absence of any internal model of the world. For each, tell me whether it is trivial or near-definitional, substantive and defensible, or false or so oversimplified as to mislead.

Then probe the two words themselves. Does 'stochastic' do fair work, given that a trained model can be decoded greedily or at temperature zero, and that 'stochastic' may describe the sampling regime rather than the model's competence? Does 'parrot' smuggle in an unearned contrast, given that real parrots are not simply mindless echo chambers and that the relevant failure may be something more specific than mimicry? Finally, identify which reading, if any, would actually support the inference from 'this system is a stochastic parrot' to 'therefore it does not understand anything,' and flag any reading on which that inference is a non sequitur.
