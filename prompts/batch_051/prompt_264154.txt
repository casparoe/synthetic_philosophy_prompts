I am a software engineer with no formal philosophical training, so I may be missing something obvious. I have been reading John Stuart Mill’s On Liberty and keep returning to a puzzle I cannot dissolve. In the early part of the essay, Mill sets out what looks like a clean boundary: coercing someone against his will is justified only to prevent harm to others, and a person’s own good, whether physical or moral, is never a sufficient warrant. In the final chapter, however, he pauses over whether the same principle would require allowing someone to sell himself into slavery. There he argues that such a contract would be void from the start, because anyone who binds himself permanently into servitude has, by that very act, cut away the capacity for future choice that the whole system of liberty exists to protect. He even suggests, as I understand him, that the principle of freedom cannot require that a person be free to give up his freedom.

I can see two candidate reconciliations. One treats liberty as an instrument for human development rather than a bare side-constraint; on that reading, a permanent self-enslavement contract is like deleting the runtime needed for any future update, and the principle was never meant to protect that sort of act. The other treats the enslaved future self as a distinct person whose liberty is violated by the present self, so preventing the contract is really preventing harm to another. But both feel a bit like patching the rule to handle an unwelcome edge case. I am interested in whether the two positions can genuinely be reconciled without turning the original principle into a mere rule of thumb, which one Mill actually held, and whether there is a third reading I have not seen. Does this example also show that valid consent requires more than a competent adult’s unforced yes at the moment of signing, or does it only show that some rights are inalienable?
