Suppose Anselm, while composing Cur Deus Homo in the 1090s, had made a single conceptual shift. In the actual book he treats sin as a failure to render God the honor owed, and the problem as a lack of satisfaction that restores the order of justice. In the counterfactual, he instead treats each sin as a legal transgression that makes the sinner liable to punishment, so that divine justice now demands the infliction of a penalty rather than the repayment of a debt of honor. He still argues, as in the actual work, that no finite creature can discharge what is owing and that only a sinless God-man can. But now the God-man does not offer an infinite supererogatory payment on behalf of sinners; he suffers, as a substitute, the exact penalty the guilty deserve, so that the punishment itself is borne and exhausted.

Give me your best reasoned prediction of how Latin soteriology and penitential practice develop in the hundred and fifty years after this divergence. I want the chain of argument, not a story. In particular, address what this change would do to the later concept of penitential satisfaction, to the contrast between contrition and attrition, to the reception of Abelard’s moral-influence account, and to how the suffering of Christ is depicted in preaching and devotion. Which of these downstream effects do you think are robust, and which depend on further assumptions about how quickly the new conceptual scheme would displace the vocabulary of debt and honor? Also mark where the historical record itself is genuinely contested among scholars, and do not present a contested reading of Anselm as the consensus.
