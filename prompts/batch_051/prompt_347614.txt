I am a novelist, and I keep returning to an argument that seems to show something I need to be false: that no word has a stable meaning. My whole craft presumes that a writer can rely on a word to carry something determinate from page to reader, so the conclusion of this argument would be disastrous. The argument runs:

(1) A word has a determinate meaning only if, for every context of use, there is a fact of the matter about what the word contributes to what is said.
(2) For any word, there are contexts in which competent speakers use it in a slightly shifted sense — metaphor, loose talk, metonymy, the chicken-as-animal versus chicken-as-meat contrast — and other competent speakers immediately understand what is meant, without learning a new word or a new lexicon entry.
(3) If competent speakers understand the shifted use without learning a new entry, then the shift must be licensed by the word's standing meaning; otherwise the agreement between speaker and hearer would be unexplained.
(4) Therefore a word's standing meaning must license an open-ended and indefinitely varying range of contributions.
(5) But a thing that licenses an open-ended and indefinitely varying range of contributions does not determine any particular contribution; it is at best a loose aptitude, not a meaning.
(6) Therefore, words do not have determinate standing meanings. What we call a word's meaning is an idealization with nothing behind it.

I want a diagnosis, not a survey. Which premise or step should I reject? I am least willing to give up (3), because it seems to track what speakers actually do with words. For each main way out, please say what it would cost a writer.
