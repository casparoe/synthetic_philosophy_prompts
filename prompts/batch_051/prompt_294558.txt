Could you write a decision tree for my phil mind tutorial? The topic is whether the brain is literally a computer, and I want the tree to sort students into views by what they actually believe, not by which -ism they've heard of. Build it out of branching questions where each node is a concrete case or claim a student can answer cold, rather than a term. Some cases you might use (you don't have to use all of them): a chatbot that passes an ordinary conversation test because it has precomputed every conversation up to, say, a hundred exchanges and just looks the answers up; a gradual replacement of a person's neurons one by one with silicon chips that keep each neuron's input-output profile identical, and the subject keeps saying her experience is unchanged; a perfect computer simulation of a thunderstorm; a thermostat; a rock that you can, if you squint, interpret as adding one to something; two systems running the same program but one on neurons and one on beer cans and string. The leaves should at least include: classical computationalism (the mind is literally a program over symbol-like representations), connectionist computationalism (the brain computes but in a distributed, non-symbol-laden way), biological naturalism (the brute biological facts about the brain cause the mind, and the computational story is the wrong level of description), the view that the brain-is-a-computer claim is only a metaphor, and pancomputationalism (everything computes, so the claim is true but empty). For each leaf give a one-sentence plain-English gloss and one kind of evidence or argument that would make someone abandon that position. Keep it to six to eight branch nodes max. Seperately, flag any two leaves the tree cant really distinguish, and point out any combinations of answers that no reasonable student would occupy and say why (incoherent? just unattractive? nobody has thought about it?). The thing I care about most is that the node separating simulating a mind from actually having one doesn't smuggle in the answer.
