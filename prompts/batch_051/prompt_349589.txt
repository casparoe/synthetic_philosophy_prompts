Consider a working definition used in machine ethics. A computational system engages in ethical reasoning — rather than merely producing behavior that happens to conform to ethical standards — only if it meets four conditions.

(1) Moral description. Its internal states represent the situation under at least some morally relevant categories, such as harm, need, consent, fairness, or desert, and not merely as a vector of physical, demographic, or statistical features.

(2) Pro tanto sensitivity. It treats those considerations as pro tanto reasons — considerations that can conflict, be outweighed, and still leave a residual claim that counts for something even when they lose — rather than as quantities that are always summed into a single score.

(3) Procedural revisability. It has at least some capacity to alter its own decision procedure when it repeatedly encounters a consideration that the procedure handles badly.

(4) Answerability. When a decision is challenged, it can produce a rationale for that decision whose force is not exhausted by the fact that the decision matches its training distribution.

Now consider a specific system. Allocator is an organ-allocation system used by a national transplant network. Its input for each candidate is a structured record containing expected survival benefit, time already waited, medical urgency, and several statutorily fixed constraints (for example, age may not be used as a decisive factor). Its recommendation proceeds in two stages. First, a model trained on the decisions of historical human transplant committees produces a ranking. Second, a fixed lexicographic override is applied: any candidate who meets the statutory definition of imminent death without transplant is moved to the top of the list, regardless of what the learned ranking says. When a clinician requests an explanation, a separate component produces a post hoc statement by selecting phrases such as 'expected survival benefit' or 'medical urgency'; this statement is generated after the ranking is fixed and does not feed back into the decision procedure. Between quarterly external updates, the system does not alter its override rules or retrain itself; it only keeps a log of every case in which the override fires.

Does Allocator engage in ethical reasoning under the four conditions? Work through each condition in turn, indicating where the evidence is mixed or where the definition underdetermines the verdict. Then give an overall judgment. Finally, say whether the case shows that any of the four conditions should be revised — for example, whether answerability should require that the stated rationale be causally responsible for the decision, or whether a lexicographic override should count as treating a consideration as a pro tanto reason.
