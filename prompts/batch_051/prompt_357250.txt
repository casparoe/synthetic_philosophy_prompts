I'm a novelist, and I'm deep in the research phase for a book about a woman writing a memoir of her late mentor, so for the past year I've been reading old lives — Plutarch, the medieval saints lives, the kind of book where the author plainly sets out to make the reader braver or more patient, not just to record what happened. I keep noticing a move I don't have a word for. The writer reshapes the life around the lesson: a general's temper gets reframed as righteous fury, a reformer's neglect of her own children quietly vanishes, and when someone objects that this isnt what the sources say, the reply is never really about the sources. The reply is that the facts as they stand couldn't do their moral work, that a life has to be pointed and pruned before it can serve as a proper example.

But here is the part I actually want named: the pruning seems to me to defeat itself. The lives that have actually changed how I think about my own choices — fictional or real — are the ones where the mess was left in and the lesson was allowed to half-fail. The more a writer bends a life to make it improving, the less improving it becomes, and yet the bending keeps happening and readers keep asking for exactly that. Does this whole configuration have an established name — not just the burnishing, but the self-defeating logic of the improved life as a moral example? And if it doesn't, how would you demarcate it so it stays distinct from ordinary whitewashing, from propaganda, and from plain historical fiction? A bare label won't help me much; I'm after where the edges are.
