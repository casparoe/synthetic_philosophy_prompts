I keep seeing a structural parallel between a debate in the philosophy of biology and a pattern of explanation in software engineering, and I want to know whether it is deep or superficial.

On the biological side, Gould and Lewontin's spandrels critique is aimed at the adaptationist program: the tendency to treat every trait as if it were shaped by natural selection for its current function. Their charge is that this is not a neutral methodological default but a bias. Many traits are byproducts — architectural, developmental, or historical side effects of other things — or exaptations: features that evolved under one set of pressures and were later co-opted for a different role. One of the sharpest parts of the critique is that adaptationism courts unfalsifiability: when no selective story is readily available, the adaptationist treats the absence not as evidence against the default but as a gap in our ingenuity, so the program can in principle accommodate any trait at all by generating a post hoc just-so story.

On the software side, I see the same explanatory habit in how people reason about the behavior of large, long-lived codebases. Engineers, maintainers, and postmortem reviewers often speak as if a system's current observable behavior traces back to someone's intention to satisfy some current requirement. In fact, a great deal of behavior is a byproduct: compatibility constraints, legacy decisions, unintended interactions between components, local fixes that were never generalized. And there is a recognizable analogue of exaptation: once enough users come to depend on behavior that was never specified or intended, that behavior becomes effectively part of the public contract and must be preserved. There is even an analogue of the just-so story: the retroactive reclassification of a bug as a feature.

Stated as a mapping, the parallel would be:

- the adaptationist default corresponds to a design-intentionalist default;
- a biological spandrel or byproduct corresponds to incidental behavior of a system;
- exaptation corresponds to accidental behavior becoming load-bearing interface;
- the just-so story corresponds to retrospectively redescribing behavior as intended.

I want the answer to assess whether this mapping is a genuine identity of structure, a loose analogy, or a confusion. Specifically: what would have to be true on both sides for results to transfer from the biological critique to the software case — for example, for the charge of unfalsifiability to stick to design-intentionalism the way it sticks to adaptationism? What corresponds to natural selection, heritable variation, and the selective environment on the software side? Do the obvious disanalogies matter — software is produced by agents with foresight; code does not reproduce with variation the way organisms do; biological functions are the product of a blind process whereas code behavior is stipulated, however fallibly — or are those disanalogies themselves part of the point? Finally, name at least one thing the parallel predicts that turns out false: a concrete place where the two cases come apart.

I would also like the answer to be self-critical about its own verdict: tell me which argument or which disanalogy would most likely change your mind, and where you think your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
