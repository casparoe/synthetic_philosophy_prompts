Walter Bagehot in The English Constitution writes, "A new Constitution does not produce its full effect as long as all its subjects were reared under an old Constitution, as long as its statesmen were trained by that old Constitution." I keep thinking about this line when I read the comparative politics stuff on presidential vs. parliamentary systems. It seems like half the time when people condemn presidentialism they're really looking at places like Weimar Germany or Latin America in the first few decades after a new constitution, and when people praise parliamentary systems they're comparing them to Britain after it had centuries to settle. So I want to get clear on what Bagehot actually means by a constitution’s full effect. Is that a real causal claim about how institutions only discipline politics once a new generation is socialized into them, or is it more of a methodological warning about not judging too early? And if he's right, can the standard quantitative comparisons between presidential and parliamentary regimes ever be informative, or are they always measuring old, broken-in systems against new ones? Is there any way around this problem without waiting two generations for every constitutional change, or does the whole research program need to be more historically local in a way that would make broad cross-national claims impossible? I'm also curious whether Bagehot's point cuts differently against presidentialism specifically, because presidents have fixed terms and might be tempted to test the system's limits before the older generation loses influence. What would a parliamentary counterpart of that danger be?
