Here's something I've been chewing on. The standard Bayesian story says the prior doesn't matter in the long run: as the data piles up, two agents starting from different priors but seeing the same evidence will end up with posteriors that get arbitrarily close. The theorem behind this needs the true data-generating process to be in the support of each prior — there's a "grain of truth" assumption — and a lot of the philosophical mileage (eventual agreement, subjective priors are harmless) rests on it.

But this assumes the agents are conditioning inside a model that's at least capable of representing reality. The people doing applied statistics and machine learning now mostly live in a misspecified world: the models are known simplifications and the true process isn't in the hypothesis space at all. As I understand it, under misspecification the posterior doesn't necessarily settle on the true thing — it settles near the point in the model that minimizes KL divergence to the true distribution, and which point that is can depend on the prior and the model family, so the washing out can just fail. In the limit you can still end up with two rational agents who've seen the same unbounded evidence and still disagree.

So my question: does the classic washing-out-of-priors argument survive misspecification? I want to know which exact premise the misspecification result puts pressure on, and whether the argument is dead, needs repair, or survives in a weaker form. If there's a repair, what's the strongest thing you can honestly say — is it that predictions wash out but not parameter estimates, or that priors wash out only within a shared model class everyone agrees is adequately true, or something else? And where does the repaired version itself break?
