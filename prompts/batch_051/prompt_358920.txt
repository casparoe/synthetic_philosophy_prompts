I've been trying to sort out the different ways a creature — or a species, or a system — can make decent choices without first having to suffer the bad ones, and I keep coming back to a four-rung ladder that I don't have a name for. Bottom rung: the individual never learns at all; the trial and error happens across generations, and the 'knowledge' gets stored in genes. Second rung: an individual can try actions in its own lifetime and keep the ones that pay off, like a rat learning which lever gives food. Third rung: an individual can test candidates internally before acting, like me running through a tricky conversation in my head to see if it'll go badly before I actually have it. Fourth rung: the testing doesn't have to be done by the individual at all — it can be borrowed from other people through language, imitation, tools, and culture, so each generation starts with a huge pile of pre-tested designs.

My question isnt just what this ladder is called, if it has a name. What I actually want to know is whether the fourth rung belongs on the same ladder at all. The first three seem to be about one system doing its own testing, just at different speeds and in different places; the fourth one is social — it changes the subject from one mind testing its own ideas to many minds pooling their tests. So is the four-rung picture misleading? And is there a fifth rung once you have machines that learn from massive datasets without having either genes or a lifetime of real-world trial and error — or are those just a fast version of one of the lower rungs? I'd rather have a careful carving of the phenomenon than a label.
