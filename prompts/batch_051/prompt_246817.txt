The question the student was answering was:

Explain the Principle of Alternative Possibilities (PAP) and how Frankfurt-style cases are supposed to challenge it. Suppose the cases succeed: does that, by itself, establish that moral responsibility is compatible with determinism? Defend your answer.

Here is the student's answer, reproduced as written:

The Principle of Alternative Possibilities says that a person is morally responsible for what they did only if they could have done otherwise. Frankfurt cases are designed to refute this principle. In the standard setup, Jones is deciding whether to kill Smith, and Black, a neuroscientist, wants Jones to kill Smith. Black has secretly implanted a device that will detect any prior sign that Jones is about to decide not to kill Smith, and will then make Jones decide to kill Smith. In the actual sequence, Jones decides to kill Smith on his own, and Black never intervenes. Jones could not have done otherwise, because if he had been about to decide otherwise, Black would have forced the decision. But Jones is still morally responsible, since the decision and the act came from him in the ordinary way. So PAP is false: responsibility does not require the ability to do otherwise. This also shows that moral responsibility is compatible with determinism. In a deterministic world no one can do otherwise, but the case shows that what matters for responsibility is how the action is actually produced, not whether there were alternatives. One might object that Jones still had an alternative possibility, namely the prior sign — say, turning pale — that would have triggered Black's device. But this flicker of freedom is just the ability to decide otherwise, and a mere flicker like that is too thin to ground responsibility. So the objection fails, and the Frankfurt case establishes both the falsity of PAP and compatibilism.
