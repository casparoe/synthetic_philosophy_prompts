Consider the following argument about probability in the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics. I find it plausible on a first reading, but I have a persistent sense that one of the middle steps moves more quickly than it is entitled to. I would like a step-by-step audit: is the argument valid, and if it is not, at which step exactly does it fail, and what is the least costly repair?

Setup. A spin-1/2 system is prepared in the state

    √(1/3)|up⟩ + √(2/3)|down⟩,

and an ideal measurement of spin is made in the up/down basis. According to the many-worlds interpretation, no collapse occurs; the measurement interaction, together with decoherence, produces two branches: one in which the apparatus reads "up" and one in which it reads "down." Each branch contains an observer, and neither branch is privileged by the formalism.

The argument:

(1) Immediately after the measurement, before an observer checks the apparatus, there are exactly two live possibilities for what she will observe: "up" and "down." She is uncertain about which branch she occupies.

(2) If that uncertainty is to take the form of rational credences, the credences must be fixed by a probability measure over the branches.

(3) The only probability measure that the branching structure itself selects is the uniform one: two branches, treated symmetrically, each receive probability 1/2.

(4) Therefore, her rational credence in "up" should be 1/2 and her rational credence in "down" should be 1/2.

(5) But the Born rule assigns probabilities 1/3 to "up" and 2/3 to "down"—the squared moduli of the amplitudes—and those are the frequencies confirmed by the statistics of many runs.

(6) So if a many-worlds observer set her credences by the measure selected by the branching structure alone, her credences would disagree with the Born rule.

(7) Therefore, the many-worlds interpretation cannot recover the Born rule from its own branching structure; it must add the Born rule, or some further principle that selects the amplitude measure, as an additional postulate.

Please audit the argument. For each step, say whether it is true, and where a step has several possible readings, give the strongest reading on which it is true and say whether the argument still goes through on that reading. Then give an overall verdict: is the argument valid as written, and if not, exactly which step fails? In particular, examine the inference from (3) to (4), since I suspect it tacitly assumes that a "branch" can be counted independently of any choice of basis and of any decision about how finely to coarse-grain; I have not managed to make that suspicion precise. Finally, if the argument fails only at a subsidiary point, say whether its conclusion nevertheless survives in a weaker form, and state what that weaker form is.
