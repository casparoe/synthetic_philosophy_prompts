I have been using the dollar auction to think about escalation of commitment, and I have hit an argument that seems to prove too much; I can no longer tell whether it is a real problem for rational choice theory or a trick of the light. Consider the usual setup: two bidders, a one-dollar prize, bids rising in fixed increments smaller than half a dollar, and the rule that the highest bidder wins the dollar and pays their bid, while the second-highest bidder also pays their bid and receives nothing.

(1) Suppose you are the second-highest bidder, having bid b, while the other bidder has bid b + i. If you quit, you lose b for certain. If you raise to b + 2i and the other bidder then quits, you pay b + 2i and receive one dollar, for a net of 1 - (b + 2i). Compared with quitting, that is an improvement of 1 - 2i, which is positive because i is less than half a dollar. So, conditional on the auction ending with your raise, raising strictly dominates quitting.

(2) If the other bidder does not quit but raises again, you face the same comparison once more: you are now the second-highest bidder at a higher bid, deciding whether to quit or overtake.

(3) Therefore, once you have entered, quitting is never the rational choice. At each decision point, quitting guarantees a loss, whereas raising either ends the auction with a strictly better payoff or merely returns you to the same decision at higher stakes.

(4) Since the same reasoning is available to the other bidder, two rational bidders who have entered will never quit, and the price will rise without any finite bound.

(5) So a rational person should never enter a dollar auction at all: entering starts a process that, by the players' own reasoning, can issue only in indefinite escalation and unbounded losses.

(6) But that conclusion seems too strong. An opening bid of a nickel against a rational opponent looks like a small, possibly profitable gamble, and a theory of rational choice should be able to say that without implying that the first bidder has already made a catastrophic mistake.

Where does the argument go wrong? My suspicion falls somewhere between (3) and (4), but each step individually looks forced. If I had to keep something, I would keep the thought that expected-utility theory can both explain why entering may be a mistake and still make sense of the local pressure to escalate once one is in. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
