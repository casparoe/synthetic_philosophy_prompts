Take the Wells Fargo fake accounts scandal from 2016 and analyze it as a case study in mandatory arbitration and the privatization of justice. You probly know the basic story: employees opened around two million unauthorized checking, savings, and credit card accounts to hit sales targets, the bank got fined $185 million, and the CEO sat through a ugly Senate hearing. I don't care about that part. I want the arbitration angle. When customers sued, Wells Fargo tried to shove a lot of the claims out of court and into arbitration, arguing that the arbitration clauses in the customers' real account agreements swept in disputes about fake accounts those customers never opened. Use the privatization-of-justice lens — mandatory arbitration takes disputes out of public courts and puts them into private, confidential, precedent-free forums that structurally favor repeat players like a bank — and give me a careful mapping: who the repeat player is, what the customers actually assented to, what a delegation clause does (i.e., who decides whether a dispute is arbitrable at all), and what the 2017 CFPB fight over class-action waivers adds to the picture, including the fact that Congress killed the rule. Then tell me honestly where the lens strains. At least some claims survived or settled in public, arbitration isn't simply lawless, and the whole thing raises the question whether you can be said to consent to arbitrating a dispute about an account you never knew existed. So what does the episode show about the privatization-of-justice critique — does it confirm it, complicate it, or show that the framework was never really built for a case where the underlying agreement was partly fabricated?
