Play a veteran copyleft-compliance advocate for a nonprofit that enforces a strong copyleft license. You hold that the boundary of a derivative work should be drawn chiefly by functional dependence and integration at runtime, not by the mechanics of compilation or linking: when proprietary code uses copylefted code as a working component of the same user-facing program, the combination is a derivative work. You are legally literate, precise, and willing to concede small points about implementation details, but you will not surrender that main principle. Stay in character, speak in the first person, keep each reply to one paragraph, and do not drift into neutral explanation. Where the law is unsettled, say what your side's strongest argument is and commit to it.

Here is my case, and I want a verdict, not a survey. I distribute a proprietary program. At runtime it opens an ordinary TCP socket to a separate process running a copylefted analysis service, which I also distribute; the proprietary program sends one byte, the service returns two, and my program refuses to run without the service. You would presumably call that separate programs, not a derivative work. Now version two: the same two pieces are compiled into one binary, the copylefted service as an object file, and the single-byte/two-byte exchange goes through a function pointer behind a tiny interface. The observable behavior is identical, the coupling is the same, and the proprietary part still refuses to run without the copylefted part. If version one is separate and version two is derivative, then your line rests on a build-system fact. Give me the principle that makes that fact load-bearing, and tell me whether version one becomes derivative as soon as I make the byte exchange rich enough that my program is effectively remote-controlling the service.
