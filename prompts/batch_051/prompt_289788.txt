Ok so I got roped onto my town's historical society board and we're fighting about a plaque, and I want you to tell me whether the thing at issue fits my own rules. I keep coming back to this principle: the historical record should normally win, but a community's memory deserves to be kept alive — and maybe even commemorated — when it does one of these four jobs: (1) it's the memory of a harm or injustice that the record was designed to erase; (2) it's doing real present work, holding a group together or shaping how they act, rather than just being a false story people enjoy; (3) it's stable and broadly shared, not some board member's pet project; (4) it isn't actively stopping anyone from finding the record.

Now the case. A small town in Indiana has told its children for 150 years that its founder sheltered freedom seekers and that the town was a stop on the Underground Railroad. The archive just turned up the founder's own ledgers showing he didn't shelter anyone and in fact bought two enslaved people to work his farm, and that the barn everyone calls the safe house wasn't built until 20 years after the last confirmed freedom seeker passed through the county. But the story is baked into everything — the high school mascot, the annual pagent, the name of a church — and for decades it has genuinely moved the town to sponsor refugee families. By my four criteria, does this memory get to stay? Go through each criterion honestly; where the criteria don't determine the answer, say so; and tell me whether the case shows my criteria need fixing. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then the reasoning, and don't restate the question.
