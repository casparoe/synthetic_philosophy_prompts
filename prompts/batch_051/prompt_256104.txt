I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on research ethics, and my advisor has been the ethicist on our university's IRB for years, so I've got a running list in my head of how he judges randomized trial protocols. He always says he's just applying clinical equipoise, but I've started to suspect there are two different principles hiding under that phrase, and I want to figure out which one hes actually running before I hand him a draft of my dissertation chapter.

His past calls, as best I can reconstruct them:

- A head-to-head trial of two approved biologics for rheumatoid arthritis where the specialty was genuinely split: approved, no real discussion.
- A trial where the protocol's own PI wrote that he was personally convinced drug X beat drug Y, even though the field overall was divided: my advisor said the PI's personal conviction was irrelevant and voted to approve.
- A placebo-controlled trial of a new agent for a cancer that has no remaining standard treatment: approved.
- A placebo-controlled trial of a new diabetes drug that would have taken patients off metformin for twelve weeks, metformin being cheap, effective, and standard: rejected. His line was that the field doesn't seriously dispute that a type 2 diabetic is better off on metformin than on placebo, so a straight placebo washout wasn't consistent with competent care; they needed an add-on design or some kind of active comparator.
- A head-to-head trial of two antipsychotics where a recent meta-analysis slightly favored one but clinicians were still genuinely arguing about first-line prescribing: approved.

So he clearly doesn't care about individual investigator uncertainty. The community is doing the work. Fine.

Now the new protocol he'll be reviewing next month. It's a non-inferiority trial of a new anticoagulant against warfarin. The sponsor's existing data show the drug is, if anything, a bit worse at preventing strokes: the point estimate favors warfarin, though the upper confidence limit sits inside a defensible non-inferiority margin. It has no safety advantage; the selling points are that it doesn't require INR monitoring and will be cheaper. Nobody in the field, including the sponsor's own investigators, actually expects it to be better. The consensus is still that warfarin is the preferred drug for most patients. But they also don't think the new drug is unacceptably worse; it might be a reasonable fallback for people who can't manage monitoring or can't afford warfarin. The sponsor is running the trial to show exactly that.

I can see two rules he might be applying. One says randomization is okay only when there's genuine professional disagreement about which arm is better—on that rule this trial is out, because there's no real disagreement about which arm is better; the only question is whether the worse drug is still acceptable. The other says what matters is uncertainty about whether one arm is unacceptably worse than the other—on that rule the trial is fine as long as the margin is justified and nobody in any arm ends up below standard care.

What does he say about the anticoagulant trial, which past case is the closest analog (I keep flip-flopping between the metformin trial and the antipsychotic trial), and what principle is he committing himself to either way? And give me a further case—maybe something that isn't a non-inferiority trial—that would catch him if he's actually just voting case by case rather than running one of those principles.
