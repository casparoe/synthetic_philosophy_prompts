I am teaching an upper-level seminar on the ethics of autonomous weapons, and I want a diagnostic instrument that sorts students by their substantive commitments about meaningful human control, not by the label they prefer. Please write a Which view of meaningful human control do you actually hold? quiz with exactly nine questions.

Each question should present a concrete vignette about an autonomous or semi-autonomous weapon system and offer three answer options, all phrased so that a respondent can answer without already knowing any position in the literature. Build the quiz around four distinctions I take to structure the debate:

1. Locus of control: whether control must attach to the specific object selected, to the engagement rules or parameters, or to the system's aims and value trade-offs.

2. Timing and form of control: whether control must be exercised in real time by a human operator, through selection or veto, or can be exercised ex ante through design, testing, and imposed constraints.

3. What control is for: whether the point is to keep a human causally in the decision loop, to ensure someone can be held answerable afterward, or to ensure the system tracks the relevant norms regardless of who is watching.

4. Target-sensitivity: whether the demand for control varies with target class, persons versus materiel, or with the urgency of the engagement.

Use or adapt cases such as: a loitering munition that selects objects matching a human-set target profile; a target-recommendation system in which a human has only a few seconds to approve before the option disappears; a sentry system pre-authorized to fire on any person crossing a perimeter at night carrying a weapon; a counter-battery system whose engagement window is too short for any human veto; a system whose designers embedded a civilian-harm minimization rule but cannot predict its specific choices; a system updated in the field such that its engineers no longer know its decision rules; and a commander delegating to a subordinate the task of approving an algorithmically generated target list. Keep every case concrete, and do not let any question reduce to asking which slogan a respondent likes.

Then provide a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto at least these six positions: (a) direct-control MHC, on which only real-time human selection of specific targets counts; (b) veto-based MHC, on which human-on-the-loop oversight with adequate information and a genuine veto counts; (c) design-governance MHC, on which ex ante control over parameters, training, and constraints counts for routine cases; (d) answerability-based MHC, on which what matters is that failures can be traced to a responsible human or institution; (e) target-differentiated MHC, which requires more direct control for persons than for materiel; and (f) the skeptical view, on which such arrangements often amount to little more than ritual oversight and the only adequate stance on lethal force against persons is a human decision per engagement or a prohibition. If a coherent answer pattern requires another position, add it and give it a clear name.

For each leaf position, write one sentence stating its most awkward commitment. Identify any pairs of positions the quiz cannot cleanly separate and explain why. Also identify any answer-pattern combinations in the logical space that are unoccupied, and briefly diagnose whether each is unoccupied because it is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.
