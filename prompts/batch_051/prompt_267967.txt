I'm a novelist, and reading about AI alignment is wierdly like reading about my own trade. The question people ask — why would an AI want anything at all, since nothing shaped its preferences the way evolution shaped ours — is exactly the situation of a character in a novel. My protagonist doesn't want revenge because some selective history made her; I stipulated the want and wrote scenes that express it. (I can give her a backstory too, but that's also stipulated — it does the job a reward signal does during training, not the job natural selection does in shaping an appetite.) The inside-view of writing is that the want gets discovered, not invented: once she's on the page she pushes back, and when she acts out of character I either say I got her wrong or that she's revealed a deeper want I hadn't consciously put there. That maps eerily well onto the AI case: you specify an objective function, the system behaves off-spec, and you find yourself asking whether it "really" wants something else — reward hacking, goal misgeneralization — and whether the stated goal or the recovered one is the real one. I can't tell if this is a deep structural identity or just me flattering my craft. What would have to be true on both sides for anything substantive to transfer? And can you name a prediction the analogy makes that fails — somewhere characters and AIs come apart?
