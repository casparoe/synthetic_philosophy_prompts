For a piece I am developing, I want to stress-test an idea I have been calling the scarcity lag. It is my attempt to give Keynes's prediction in Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren (1930) a more charitable update than the usual claim that he simply misjudged the appetite for more. Keynes distinguished between absolute needs, which we feel whatever our neighbors have, and relative needs, which we feel only insofar as satisfying them puts us above our neighbors. He thought the economic problem would be solved once absolute needs were met, and that the harder problem would then be learning to occupy freedom from economic care well. The scarcity lag, as I want to use the term, is the process by which the habits, metrics, and moral language calibrated to scarcity outlive the material conditions that produced them. When productivity delivers surplus, those habits do not retire; they re-scarcify. Free time becomes a field of competitive optimization. Abundant goods are converted into ranked goods — selective schools, reservation windows, visible health, attention — whose value depends on someone else's exclusion. The felt experience of insufficiency is thus regenerated on top of rising absolute prosperity. On the strongest version of the claim, any level of material security can be metabolized into relative need because the evaluative system keeps moving the benchmark, so there is no fixed point at which the economic problem announces itself as solved and the culture changes course.

I want an unsparing assessment. Is the scarcity lag a genuine mechanism, or is it a metaphor that gives the appearance of explanation? What existing concepts does it duplicate? Is it more than the familiar observation that people adapt to higher income and that status is inherently comparative? And what would count as evidence against it — what would an affluent society look like if no such lag were operating?
