I am a PhD student in environmental political theory, and I am preparing a handout for a graduate seminar on climate justice and global governance. The handout should be a decision tree that sorts positions on whether and how to pursue stratospheric aerosol injection (SAI)—the proposal to inject reflective aerosols into the upper atmosphere to reflect a small fraction of incoming sunlight and thereby lower global temperatures. The seminar audience has no prior background in the geoengineering literature, so the tree must work entirely from questions a reader can answer about concrete cases and claims, not from recognizing labels or authors. Please build the tree from the logical space of positions rather than from a list of named views, with each branch labeled by the question or claim that separates it from its sibling branches. Phrase every branch node as a question about a concrete scenario or commitment rather than as a question about which label the reader likes.

The tree should branch on the following distinctions, though you may reorder or decompose them where the logic demands. First, whether intentionally managing the climate system is permissible at all, or is ruled out by a norm against hubristic intervention or by the thought that a deliberately managed climate loses the value of being a natural background. Second, whether the moral-hazard concern—that SAI research, or its eventual deployment, would weaken emissions mitigation—is decisive, and whether it applies at the research stage or only at deployment. Third, whether termination shock—the rapid warming expected if deployment were ever stopped abruptly—makes deployment self-defeating, or instead creates an intergenerational obligation to maintain it once begun; for instance, a node might ask whether a reader would endorse a deployment plan whose sponsors promise a legally mandated multi-century maintenance regime. Fourth, who may decide: whether deployment requires near-universal consent from affected states or some weaker multilateral threshold, or whether a single state or small coalition may act, for instance in a declared climate emergency. Fifth, whether deployment is acceptable only as a temporary bridge alongside aggressive mitigation or as a standing long-term policy tool.

The leaves must include at least: (1) a no-research/no-deployment position; (2) a research-permitted but no-deployment position; (3) a position permitting deployment only with broad consent and only as a temporary bridge; (4) a position permitting unilateral or minilateral deployment in emergencies; and (5) a position treating deployment as a legitimate ongoing tool. Also include a leaf for the view that the termination-shock lock-in is by itself a sufficient reason against deployment. Where the logical space yields combinations that no one appears to defend, identify them and briefly say whether each is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate with these questions. Keep the tree to roughly eight to twelve leaves.
