I have to grade a short take-home answer, and I want a careful second pass before I put a number on it, since a lot of these answers look plausible on a quick read. The question was:

Explain the Stoic dichotomy of control and evaluate the objection that it collapses into resignation or indifference toward health, wealth, and reputation.

A student wrote:

Epictetus opens the Enchiridion by dividing everything into what is up to us and what is not. Up to us are our judgments, impulses, desires, and aversions; not up to us are our body, property, reputation, and office. For the Stoics, only virtue is good and only vice is bad, and since both are exercises of what is up to us, externals are indifferent. But this does not mean ignoring health or money. The Stoics count some externals, like health and wealth, as preferred indifferents, to be pursued when doing so does not compromise character. The dichotomy is usually expanded into a trichotomy: things we fully control, things we partly control (like our health, since our choices affect it), and things we have no control over whatever we do (like the weather). On that reading, the advice is to control what you can and accept what you can't. So the doctrine is not quietist; it tells us to act on externals where our actions can make a difference while keeping happiness from depending on the outcome. A determinist might object that even our judgments are caused, so nothing is really up to us. But Epictetus only means that judgments are what the ruling faculty governs, not that they are uncaused, so the objection misses the point. The dichotomy survives and does not collapse into indifference.
