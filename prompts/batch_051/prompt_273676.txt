Take Kant's murderer-at-the-door case, but change one thing. In the usual version, a man comes to your door asking whether your friend is hiding in your house; the friend is in fact inside; Kant's famous conclusion (on at least the standard reading) is that you may not lie to him, though many add that you may refuse to answer or deflect. Now vary it: the murderer shouts from down the street that he's coming to ask whether your friend is in the house, and you have just enough time to shove your friend out the back to the neighbor's before you open the door. The true answer to his question is now 'no, he isn't here.' You say it. The murderer forms a true belief and leaves — exactly the way a successful lie would have made him leave. Only you didn't change the words; you changed the world so the words would be true by the time you said them.

What does the standard Kantian analysis say about this variation, and does the position survive it? In particular: if the wrong of the lie is that it uses the murderer's rational agency as a mere means to your end, why doesn't arranging the truth so that he draws the conclusion you want do the same? If the wrong is instead located only in asserting what you believe false, then the view is narrower than the manipulation worry — is that narrowness a feature or a bug, and what case would force the issue most cleanly? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Approach this like a Kant specialist who also cares about the broader ethics of deception, and work through the original and the variation side by side before you settle on a verdict.
