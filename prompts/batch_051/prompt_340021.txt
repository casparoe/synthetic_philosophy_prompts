I'm drafting a short essay on a problem in the philosophy of categories, and I'd value comments on the argument before I commit to it. The problem is the one the Letter Spirit project makes vivid: what makes every a an a? I don't mean the phoneme or the letter's alphabetical position, but the visual category itself—why do a Times glyph, a rushed handwritten single-storey a, and a blackletter a all count as the same letterform while an o or an epsilon-like curl with a tail count as not?

My working thesis is that letterform categories are neither defined by shared local features nor by necessary and sufficient conditions, but are better modeled as attractor-like regions in a space of stroke and shape parameters, with identity partly assigned by the system of contrasts a script establishes. Style, I want to argue, is a systematic, low-dimensional deformation field: a single style bends the whole alphabet coherently, which explains why a previously unseen a is legible even when no individual feature of it matches anything seen before. But the essay has hit a snag. Some members of the category are not connected by small continuous deformations at all: double-storey and single-storey a differ in topology—two enclosed counters versus one—yet both are unproblematically a. So the continuity story cannot by itself delimit the category; I seem forced to add a role-based tie, something like: a mark counts as an a because a competent reader parses it as occupying the a-role in a contrast system, and that role is stabilized by traditions of writing and type design that occasionally jump between islands in shape space instead of moving gradually.

Before I finalize the essay, I'd like comments on three points. First, is the move from feature-continuity to role-based identity doing genuine work, or would the same criterion overgenerate—classifying, say, an o-shaped mark as an o-role fulfillment under the right conditions, or an upside-down e as a numeral? Second, where is the argument weakest, especially the claim that style is best understood as a low-dimensional deformation across an entire alphabet rather than as a bundle of per-glyph choices? Third, what nearby case outside typography—phonemes, gait recognition, architectural ornament, anything—would either strengthen the analogy or break it, and what exactly would the case show?
