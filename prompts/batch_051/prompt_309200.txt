I'm a university student taking a philosophy of mind course that's currently on animal minds, and something's been bugging me. We've been reading about sentience as a matter of degree: the idea that there isn't a clean on/off switch between a thermostat, a bee, a fish, a rat and a pig, just a gradient. I get that part. But I've started noticing that people only behave like gradualists in the top half of the gradient. Compare two things that are both clearly above some rough line—a pig and a fish, or a rat and a chicken—and everyone's comfortable saying one matters more than the other, and the comparisons can be fine-grained: a chicken counts more than a fish, a pig counts quite a bit more then a rat, etc. But the moment one side of the comparison is below the line, the gradualism vanishes. A bee, a plant, a thermostat: people talk about all three as if they had exactly zero moral status, all equally distant from mattering, and some people get almost offended if you say a bee has a tiny bit of status and a plant has less but not none. Yet if sentience is graded, then whatever physical stuff makes a rat count more than a fish ought to also make a bee count more than a plant and a plant count more than a thermostat—by tiny amounts maybe, but by something. So the asymmetry is: high up, a small difference in the underlying stuff makes a small difference in moral status; at the line, an equally small difference makes the difference between nothing and something. Why would a continuous property have a point where it suddenly starts counting? And we aren't even sure where that point is: most people are less confident a bee feels pain than they are that a pig does, but we still treat the bee as either in or out rather than probably-in.

I want the strongest principle that generates this pair of habits—graded above the line, binary below it—and an honest verdict on whether it actually works, or whether the binary is just a practical shortcut we've mistaken for a fact. Please don't give me a reading list; I want analysis.
