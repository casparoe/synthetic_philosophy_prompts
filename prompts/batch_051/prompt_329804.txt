A friend of mine has an annoyingly stable set of intuitions about object identity, and I've been trying to reverse-engineer what rule she's actually running. I keep coming up with two or three rules that fit her track record, and I want to know which one survives contact with the trickier cases.

Her verdicts, as close as I can pin her down:

(1) The standard Ship of Theseus: every plank and rope is replaced over ten years, one part at a time, old parts thrown into the sea as they come off, and at the end nothing original remains. She says it's obviously the same ship.

(2) The collector: unknown to the crew, someone fishes each discarded plank out of the water, stores it, and after the last replacement reassembles all the original planks into the original shape at the dock. Now two candidate ships exist. She says the continuously repaired one is the original, and the rebuilt-from-original-planks one is not, even though it is made of the original parts.

(3) The paused build: a small wooden boat is taken apart plank by plank for a move, stored as a labeled pile in a barn for six months, then reassembled exactly. She says it is the same boat the whole time, including during the six months when no boat-shaped thing exists.

(4) The duplicator: a machine makes an atom-for-atom duplicate of her desk; original and duplicate sit side by side. She says the duplicate is a different desk, not the same one.

Now here are the cases I actually want to use to test the rules, because I think they come apart there:

(a) The boat-to-barn case: a wooden boat is disassembled and the planks are used to build a barn that stands for a decade; then the barn is taken down and the same planks are reassembled into the original boat design. At no point during the decade is there any plan to rebuild the boat. Is it the same boat?

(b) The state-conferred identity case: in the collector scenario, after the continuously repaired ship is destroyed in a fire, the port authority declares that the rebuilt-from-original-planks ship is the true continuation, gives it the original name, the original captain, and the original route. Does she now say the rebuilt one is the original all along, or does she say the original is gone?

(c) The duplicated-then-destroyed case: the desk duplicator makes a copy of the desk, and the original desk is immediately burned. The copy is then used in the same office, by the same person, for the same work. Is the copy now the same desk as the one before duplication?

I'm less interested in the yes/no answers then in the underlying rule. Walk me through the candidate rules that fit (1)-(4), what each predicts for (a)-(c), and which of (a)-(c) does the most work in telling them apart. Give me your best guess about what she'd actually say, and propose one more case I didn't think of that would settle it if she gives a clean verdict. Also flag if any of the first four verdicts already secretly rules out one of the rules I'm still considering.
