I have been trying to understand the frequentist interpretation of probability, and I keep arriving at a result that seems absurd, which makes me suspect that I am tacitly assuming something the frequentist would reject. As I understand the view, a frequentist identifies the probability of an outcome with the limit of its relative frequency across an infinite sequence of trials of the same type. But any concrete event can be described under many different types, and each type picks out a different trial sequence with, in general, a different limiting relative frequency. Suppose I ask for the probability that a particular 62-year-old man who smokes and has a family history of heart disease survives the next ten years. He falls under many reference classes: all 62-year-old men, 62-year-old male smokers, 62-year-old male smokers with his family history, and so on. These classes will yield different long-run survival frequencies. Since the man belongs to all of them, the frequentist seems forced to say that one and the same event—his surviving the decade—has several different probabilities at once. Is that a consequence of the position, or does it rest on a misunderstanding of what a frequentist means by probability? And if the frequentist's reply is that probabilities are always relative to a chosen reference class, does the need to choose a class not simply reintroduce the subjective element that the frequentist was trying to avoid, collapsing the view into subjectivism? I would like a committed response, not a survey: take a definite position on whether the reference-class problem is fatal to frequentism and on what a frequentist can legitimately say about single-case probabilities.
