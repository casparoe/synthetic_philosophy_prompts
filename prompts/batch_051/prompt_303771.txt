I write about science and ideas for a general audience, and lately I keep noticing a pattern I don't have a decent name for. In coverage of genetics, climate modeling, and especially machine learning, you increasingly see arguments where the reason cited for a conclusion isn't really held by any particular human being. The researchers didn't reason their way to it in the ordinary sense; it emerged from a pipeline, a simulation, a regression, a fine-tuned model, or a long chain of automated analyses. The reason is fully operative in the published work, but there's no one who can tell you, from the inside, why it's the reason. It's not just that the evidence is statistical or complicated. It's that the rationale has been detached from any reasoner who could own it, revise it, or answer for it.

I was reading Peirce's The Fixation of Belief and hit this passage, where he's mocking Bacon's picture of scientific method: "That we have only to make some crude experiments, to draw up briefs of the results in certain blank forms, to go through these by rule, checking off everything disproved and setting down the alternatives, and that thus in a few years physical science would be finished up — what an idea!" Peirce thought that was obviously absurd. But we've now built scientific machinery that does something uncomfortably close to that, and the justifications it emits feel strange in a way I'm trying to pin down.

Is there an established name for this, or for the failure mode it produces? I don't think it's just the black-box problem, because the issue isn't only that I can't inspect the reasoning; it's that there isn't a reasoner in any ordinary sense, yet the reason still does real argumentative work in the literature. If there's no established name, what would you call it, and how would you distinguish it from nearby ideas like collective cognition, distributed knowledge, or institutional reasoning? I'm less interested in the label itself than in getting the boundaries of the thing clear.
