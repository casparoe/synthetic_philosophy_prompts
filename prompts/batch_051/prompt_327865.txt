Been going in circles on this and figured I'd write it up. I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, so bare with me if I mangle the labels.

I've been trying to classify the reasons an AI race between two labs (or two countries) can happen even when both sides would prefer the slower, safer outcome to the race. The list I keep coming back to is the Fearon-style rationalist list, translated to AI:

1. Private information with incentives to misrepresent. Neither side can credibly tell the other how close it really is to AGI, because each has reason to bluff about capabilities and timelines (to attract talent or funding, to scare off rivals), so they can't find the mutually acceptable deal that actually exists.

2. Commitment problems. Even if they stumble on a deal — a pause, a coordinated slowdown, an agreement to share safety results once someone gets ahead — the side that gets ahead later can't credibly commit not to exploit its lead. The trailing side knows this, doesn't trust the bargain, and keeps racing.

3. Indivisibility. The thing at stake is being first to AGI, and you can't split first place down the middle; there's no way to divide the prize, so a split-the-difference bargain isn't on the table.

I've heard it said these three are supposed to be exhaustive on the rationalist account. So my question is whether there's a fourth rationalist mechanism I'm missing — a reason the race still happens even though both sides would prefer some slower alternative — that isn't just a rewording, special case, or combination of those three. If you can come up with one, name it and say how it differs from the three above. If your view is that any candidate collapses into one of them, I'd want to hear which one and why, applied to AI specifically.
