I've been going in circles on something and hoping you can sort it out. I'm taking a medical ethics course, and we spent weeks on informed consent: in research you absolutely cannot enroll someone in even a minimal-risk study without their signed, informed consent, and review boards will reject a study over nothing more than how the consent form is worded. But then I look at the rest of society and the same people seem fine with risks imposed on others who never agreed to anything. A city will approve a diesel truck depot or a highway widening that predictably raises asthma rates or traffic deaths in the surrounding blocks; the neighbors get a hearing or a comment period at best, nobody signs anything, and the project goes ahed even if every one of them objects. Driving is like that too: any licensed stranger can drive past your house and impose a small chance of killing you, and we treat that as normal rather than as something you needed to opt into. And we're not simply indifferent to tiny unconsented risks, since firing a gun into the air in a city is criminal even when the chance of hitting someone is tiny.

So what view of risk and consent is this whole pattern actually presupposing? I can half-see some candidate theories — maybe that consent is only required for bodily intrusions, or that democratic decisions stand in for individual consent, or that risks below some threshold don't count as harms at all — but I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory the practices commit us to, and to be careful about the difference between what the practices strictly presuppose and what merely happens to be compatible with them. In the places where they're philosophically underdetermined, say so. Then do the hard part: if we actually replaced whatever view is in the background with the principle that no non-trivial risk may be imposed on someone without their consent, what would have to change? Which ordinary institutions would break, and what would have to take their place?
