In a reading group I'm in, we have spent three sessions on Kant's short 1797 essay On a Supposed Right to Lie from Benevolent Motives, and the argument about the murderer at the door has not moved at all in a year. Everyone has read the same text, and nobody disputes what Kant says at the surface: he refuses to grant a right to lie even to a would-be murderer. The disagreement is about what kind of claim is actually being made. One side treats it as a rigorist moral thesis, namely that truthfulness is an unconditional duty and any lie, even one told to protect an innocent friend, is wrong. Another side treats it as a narrower claim in the theory of right: the essay denies that you can claim a legal right to lie, but it does not settle the moral question about whether you may do so all things considered. A third side fastens on a particular passage in which Kant contrasts the person who lies to the murderer with the person who tells the truth: the liar, he suggests, may become accountable for whatever bad consequences follow from the deception, whereas the truthful person cannot be blamed for the murderer's later acts. That third group says the real work is being done not by an unconditional prohibition on lying but by an asymmetry in Kant's account of imputation and responsibility for the consequences of one's actions, and they suspect the essay is less about what you may do and more about what you can be held answerable for.
