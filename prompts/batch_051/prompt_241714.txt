Act as an expert on the philosophy of property and IP, and think it through carefully before giving your verdict. I've been kicking around a theory about orphan works and abandoned software, and I want it checked hard, not flattered. I call it use-relative salvage. The core claim is that a work stops being owned — or at least stops being protected against a given use — when, for that specific use, the cost of locating and clearing the rights is bigger than the value the new use would add. So the same 1990s video game can be orphan'd for a fan translation patch and still fully owned for a studio remake; the same photo can be an orphan for a small-town archive and not for an ad campaign. Ownership stops being a fact about the work or the owner and becomes a fact about the pairing of a work, an owner, and a proposed use. I know it rhymes with adverse possession and the old property-rule/liability-rule distinction, but I think it's different because nothing hangs on the taker having possessed it openly or on a court deciding anything: the threshold is just whether clearing rights is worth it. Start by steelmanning the view, then tell me what it's actually closest to in existing debates, then give me your three hardest objections. After that, commit: is any one of them fatal, or does the thing survive as a defensible reform? Don't lay out options — take a position.
