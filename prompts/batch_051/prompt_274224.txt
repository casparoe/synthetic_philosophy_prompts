Ok so I've been going back and forth on the old question about whether markets make people better or worse, and I keep finding myself pulled in two directions at once.

On the one hand I'm basically convinced by what you might call the civilizing-commerce story, and Adam Smith's version in the Wealth of Nations (Book III, chapter IV) is the one I find hardest to shake. His account, roughly, is that after the fall of Rome the great landowners were petty tyrants over their tenants and dependants, and what changed this wasnt preaching or law — it was trade. He argues that commerce and manufactures gradually brought order and good government to the countryside, and with them liberty and security for ordinary people, who had previously lived almost in a state of permanent war with their neighbours and in servile dependence on their lords. The mechanism is what gets me: the landlords wanted trinkets from the towns, so they had to turn their surplus into money, which meant letting their tenants keep more of the produce, and over time the tenants became independant and the lords' authority just dissolved without anyone intending it. The broader point is that character gets reshaped by incentives and habit rather than by argument — punctuality, honesty, keeping your word, all becoming necessary if you want to be trusted in trade. I find that genuinely persuasive.

And yet I can't shake the intuition that there are cases where introducing a market frame corrupts instead of civilizes. The stock examples: paying for blood donations can actually shrink the supply, and paying kids for chores seems to teach them to ask "what's in it for me" rather than to be helpful. But my unease is broader — it's the sense that there's a difference between being trustworthy because it's good for business and being trustworthy because it's what you owe someone, and that a society that runs entirely on the first has lost something even if it has more of the reliable behavior. I don't want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the corruption-intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the civilizing-commerce theory to be amended or abandoned, and whether there's a reading of "corrupt" on which both Smith's story and my unease can survive.
