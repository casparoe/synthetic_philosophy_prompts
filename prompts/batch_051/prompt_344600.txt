While teaching the foundations of probability this term, I had a student come to office hours with the following case. A physicist told her that a particular radioactive nucleus had a 0.4 chance of decaying within the next hour, so she set her degree of confidence that it would decay at 0.4. Shortly afterward, a second person—who had never once been wrong about this type of event, though he had no way of influencing the nucleus—told her that it would in fact decay within the hour. She asked whether she should keep her confidence at 0.4 simply because that was the stated chance, or whether she should revise her confidence in light of the prediction. Please answer her question carefully, doing justice to both the authority of the stated chance and the reliability of the prediction.
