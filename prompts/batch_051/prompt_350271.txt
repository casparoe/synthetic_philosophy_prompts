I'm in a reading group on the foundations of computation, and we've spent the last three meetings stuck on the Church–Turing thesis — I mean really stuck, nobody has moved an inch. We all agree on the mathematics: Turing machines, lambda calculus, general recursive functions, the equivalence proofs, all of it. The argument is about what kind of claim the thesis is. One faction says it's a definition, or at most a conceptual analysis of our pretheoretic notion of "effectively computable," so it isn't the sort of thing that could be falsified, and asking for evidence for it is a category mistake. The other faction says it's an empirical hypothesis about what can be computed in the actual physical world: if we ever found a physical process that computes a non-Turing-computable function — some hypercomputer, or some exotic spacetime where an infinite computation finishes in finite proper time — the thesis would be false. So for them it stands or falls with physics, not math. My working theory is that we're smuggling two different theses under one name — one about the extension of an informal concept, one about the physics of our universe — and that the physical one should have a different name entirely. But whenever I try to press that, somebody says the conceptual version is trivial and the physical version is the only interesting question, and then we're back to square one. What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it verbal, is it a hidden empirical question, is it a disagreement about whether "computable" has a settled pretheoretic meaning, or is the question just ill-posed? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a theorem, an experiment, a distinction, or an argument that we should stop expecting any?
