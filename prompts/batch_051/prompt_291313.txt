I've got skin in this one now: my insurer has declined to cover a treatment for my back on the grounds that the disc degeneration is "a normal age-related change, not a disease," so I've ended up reading philosophy of medicine in my evenings, and honestly its made me more confused rather than less. At a wedding last month I fell into conversation with two friends, Ed and Mariam, who have both actually studied this stuff, and they spent an hour arguing it in the beer garden while I took mental notes, because I could tell I was supposed to referee and couldn't.

Ed's position was roughly naturalist: a disease is a part or process failing to do what it's biologically there to do, i.e. a departure from species-typical functioning, and that makes it objective—you can discover that someone's pancreas has stopped making insulin regardless of whether anyone is upset about it. Values might decide which diseases we fund or treat, but not which states are diseases. Mariam shot back that "what it's there to do" isn't read off nature, because the same state can be a malfunction in one environment and a normal or even protective variant in another, like sickle-cell trait and malaria; and she pointed out that homosexuality was once listed as a mental disorder and then de-listed without the biology changing, so the boundary is tracking social evaluation too. Ed said that history cuts both ways: calling that old classification a mistake presupposes there is a fact of the matter about disorder, otherwise removing it was just a change of fashion rather than a correction toward truth. Mariam then switched to the present, saying loads of current diagnoses are really tarted-up risk states—high blood pressure and high cholesterol are often symptomless and judged by future harm, which means the line is about which futures we happen to want to prevent; and once medicine can intervene, ordinary variation gets medicalised, like short stature becoming an endocrine problem once growth hormone exists. Ed said she was confusing two questions: whether something is a dysfunction, and whether we should treat it, and only the second is value-laden. She said she didn't think the first was value-free either, because "function" in medicine is partly a notion of how an organism ought to be, and a dysfunction only becomes a disease when it causes harm in the life an organism actually lives—lactose intolerance is normal in most mammals but becomes a disease in dairy-drinking populations.

I told them I needed a week to think and never got back to them.
