I am a member of a small evangelical church whose statement of faith contains this article: 'We believe that salvation is to be found in Jesus Christ alone, and that no one who, having reached the age of discretion and having understood the claims of the gospel, renders worship to any other god, or knowingly participates in the rites of any non-Christian religion, remains in a state of grace while he so continues.' I find myself in a case its drafters plainly never imagined. My father was Hindu and died this spring. As the eldest son I was expected to light the funeral pyre at his cremation, and I could not in conscience refuse him. Because I live abroad and could not travel in time, I attended the cremation through a telepresence robot operated by my brother: I saw and spoke through it, and I guided the robotic hand that touched the flame to the pyre, while reciting the words the rite requires. In my own mind I intended no act of worship; I meant it as a son's duty and as a way of honouring my father. Several members of the church now say I have 'knowingly participated in the rites of a non-Christian religion' and should be regarded as outside the state of grace.

I am less concerned with what the church should actually do about me than with whether the article, interpreted, reaches my case. Argue it from the wording of the article, from its purpose, from what its drafters would have said if they had been asked, and from what a reasonable member of the church would have understood at the time. On each approach, am I caught by the clause? Does it matter that I acted remotely, or that I disclaimed any intention to worship? If the approaches diverge, which should decide, and what does that show about what the article was ever able to settle?
