Al-Ghazālī's fire-and-cotton argument says that fire doesn't really burn the cotton: God creates the burning directly on the occasion of contact, and the constant succession we observe is just God's custom, which he could in principle change. A big part of the argument is that all observation ever gives us is one thing following another. We never observe a necessary connection, or a real causal power passing from fire to cotton, so we have no grounds for saying the fire itself does the work.

What's been nagging me is that Al-Ghazālī was working with passive observation of stable sequences. Since then we've gotten very good at intervening in controlled ways: we vary one factor while holding others fixed, randomize, knock things out, and watch the effect change exactly when we change the putative cause. We don't just sit there watching cotton followed by burning. We can make cotton not burn by removing oxygen, make it burn with a spark, or scramble combinations of fuel, heat, and oxygen and watch the result track the variables. So the evidence now looks a lot richer than "it happened to follow."

Does Al-Ghazālī's argument survive this? Or was his argument always build on a purely epistemic premise, that observation can only deliver sequences and never causal connections, so that controlled intervention doesn't actually touch the underlying occasionalism? Say precisely which premise, if any, the development puts pressure on, whether the argument can be repaired, and whether you'd call it dead, weakened, or fully intact. I'd rather you commit and be wrong than hedge.
