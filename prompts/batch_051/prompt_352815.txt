For a graduate seminar in analytic theology, write a dialogue between two theologians who disagree about how the doctrine of the Trinity is consistent with the logic of identity. One character defends a relative-identity account: expressions such as same God and same divine person express distinct counting relations, just as one and the same word can be realized by many distinct tokens, so the Father and the Son may be the same God without being the same person. The other character rejects relative identity as incoherent and instead treats the divine nature as a single concrete substance that each person is wholly, with the persons distinguished only by relational features internal to that one substance. Have the dialogue turn on the hard cases: whether the first character can say, without equivocation, exactly what is being counted when theology counts one God and three persons; whether the second character can make those internal relational distinctions real without either dissolving the persons into a single undifferentiated subject or introducing composition and plurality into God; whether the indiscernibility of identicals forces either view to collapse the persons or multiply the gods; and whether either view can justify refusing to count three distinct divine and personal realities as three gods. Both characters should be equally competent and rigorous, and each should respond to the other's actual arguments rather than restating their own position.
