Consider a familiar but puzzling pattern: some societies invest their most solemn public commemoration not in victories but in defeats. In the American South, monuments to the Confederacy—a defeated secessionist regime—were erected in large numbers not immediately after the Civil War but decades later, from the late nineteenth century through the twentieth. In Serbia, the Battle of Kosovo, remembered as a defining defeat at the hands of the Ottomans in 1389, is marked annually as a foundational national event, with its central monument at Gazimestan. In Israel, Masada, a first-century fortress where a Jewish revolt against Rome ended in defeat and, according to the account that shapes its commemoration, in mass death rather than surrender, has for decades hosted military swearing-in ceremonies and served as a site of national self-definition. In Britain, Dunkirk—the 1940 evacuation of the British Expeditionary Force after a military collapse—is publicly remembered less as a defeat than as a vindication of national character. In each case, the loss is made to carry the community's meaning.

What theory of the relation between a political community and its past does this pattern presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit view, and in doing so: (1) say whether commemorating a lost cause presupposes that a polity can be constituted, dignified, or morally interpreted through defeat, and which conception of collective identity—narrative, existential, institutional, or something else—is doing the work; (2) distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with—for instance, could someone who holds that political identity rests only on present consent and present institutions still coherently support these memorials, and if so, what would they be doing when they support them?; (3) assess whether the four cases actually share a single presupposition, or whether they diverge—Masada and Kosovo as defiance through destruction, Dunkirk as a defeat re-read as deliverance, the Confederate case as a retrospective attempt to confer honor on a discredited cause; and (4) say what would have to change in each practice if the underlying view were rejected in favor of the thesis that a polity's past has no meaning-making authority over its present. Identify the point where the four cases part company.
