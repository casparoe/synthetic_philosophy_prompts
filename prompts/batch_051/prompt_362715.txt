Suppose that on 1 January 2030 a one-time intervention makes every adult human a moral saint in the following precise sense: each person’s overriding and gladly embraced aim becomes doing whatever is morally best, understood as producing the most good from an impartial point of view. No law, institution, price, body of knowledge, or physical resource is altered directly, and everyone retains their factual knowledge, technical skill, and memories. People still disagree as much as before about which actions will actually do the most good and about moral questions. What changes is the underlying structure of their cares: personal projects, aesthetic tastes, intellectual curiosity, close friendships, and family attachments now matter to a person only insofar as pursuing them can be justified as a means to the general welfare, and no one experiences this as a sacrifice. Children born after 2030 are raised so that they normally develop the same character.

Give me your reasoned prediction of how this world looks in 2060. I want mechanisms, not a narrative. For each of the following areas, trace how the change in what people ultimately care about alters what gets produced, who is trusted and rewarded, which activities flourish or wither, and which institutions function better or worse. Cover at least: art and literature; scientific inquiry; close friendship and romantic love; parenting; markets, technological innovation, and material production; democratic politics; and humor, play, and athletics. For each area, mark which predictions are robust and which depend on contestable assumptions, such as whether doing the most good favors innovation and risk-taking over caution and redistribution, or how far impartial beneficence can sustain intense particular attachments. Tell me which of your predictions you are most and least confident in, and what would have to be true for your least confident predictions to flip.

Then take a committed position on the underlying question: is a world of moral saints a world we should prefer to our own? If your 2060 contains real losses of value, does that show that maximal moral goodness is an undesirable ideal for a human life, or does it instead show that our own valuations are morally perverse, and why? I want one definite verdict, not a survey of possible verdicts, and I want the prediction to do the argumentative work rather than having your verdict drive the prediction from the outset.
