The old argument that greed is the engine of prosperity (the Mandeville/Smith private-vices-become-public-benefits line) seems to run on one crucial fact: sellers cannot coordinate, so each self-interested seller has to undercut or out-improve the others, and competition turns private greed into lower prices and better goods. Now suppose a large share of price-setting is handled by learning algorithms, and suppose it is true that these algorithms can soften competition with no explicit agreement—each one discovers that matching rivals' high prices beats a price war over the long run. I'm not asking about the right policy. I'm asking what this does to the old argument. If the argument needs the independence premise and algorithmic pricing breaks that premise, does the argument now point the other way (acquisitiveness has to be constrained for markets to benefit anyone), can it be patched by saying greed is an engine of prosperity only within well-designed market rules, or does it just stop applying? Which premise gives way, and can it be restored in another form?
