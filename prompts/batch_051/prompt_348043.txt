A friend and I argued late into the evening about whether sentience is a matter of degree. Her position was that there is a gradient from something like a bee's minimal feeling to a dog's richer experience, and that moral status should track that gradient rather than a sharp line. My position was that experience itself is binary—a light is on or it is not—and that all the gradations we observe are in behavior, memory, learning, and complexity of response, not in the underlying character of experience. After hours, we could not tell whether our disagreement was empirical or largely verbal.

I want you to help me determine whether any evidence could bear on this. Propose the strongest concrete test of the claim that sentience itself, rather than merely its outward correlates, is graded. Specify exactly what would be measured or manipulated, what pattern of results would favor the gradualist over the threshold view, and what pattern would favor the threshold view. Then say precisely what a positive result would fail to settle: for example, whether gradations in measured responsiveness might still reflect only gradations in cognitive sophistication, whether a continuously varying quantity could be an artifact of averaging over binary states across trials or individuals, and whether the test could ever get past the problem of first-person privacy. Keep the proposal realistic enough to run on animals or simple artificial systems, do not rely on the subject's verbal report, and be explicit about which parts of the concept of graded sentience your operationalization leaves untouched.
