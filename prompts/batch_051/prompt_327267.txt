I've been part of a moral psychology reading group for the past year, and we're stuck. The group has split into two stable camps over the ethics of deference to authority. The first camp, which leans on the Milgram experiments and on the Arendtian worry about the ordinary, unthinking functionary, holds that deference is the deepest moral risk there is: it lets people hand off responsibility for their own actions, and therefore the proper stance toward any order is a kind of standing suspicion, with a strong presumption against compliance until the person receiving the order satisfies themselves that the order is justified. The second camp, which draws on military ethics and on thinking about how institutions distribute knowledge and responsibility, holds that deference is often a virtue rather than a failure: soldiers, junior members of a surgical team, or employees who are not in a position to reconstruct every decision from first principles sometimes act rightly precisely by deferring to someone with better information, a coordination role, or legitimate authority. On their view, unconditional suspicion would make almost any complex institution impossible.

The frustrating thing is that we all agree on the examples: the historical obedience experiments, the atrocities committed by functionaries in hierarchical systems, and also the cases where coordinated action seems to require that someone decide and the rest follow. We can all see that deference has been catastrophic and that some kind of acceptance of direction is unavoidable. What we cannot settle is whether those two observations point to a single lesson — a general principle about when deference is warranted — or to two different projects that we have been treating as one debate. My own hypothesis, which I keep failing to make precise, is that the first group is doing cautionary moral psychology (how deference tends to go wrong, especially in ordinary people) while the second is doing normative institutional theory (how responsibility is properly divided under legitimate authority), and that they mostly talk past one another. But maybe that is too easy: maybe there is a real substantive disagreement about how much trust any institutional chain of command can deserve, or about whether standing suspicion is psychologically compatible with functioning institutions.

Please write a careful diagnosis of why this debate has stalled, and what kind of disagreement it actually is. I would like you to engage the positions concretely, to say whether the impasse is verbal, substantive, a difference of questions, a clash of starting intuitions, or something else, and to explain what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, an empirical finding, a reformulated question, or an argument that no movement should be expected. Respond in American English.
