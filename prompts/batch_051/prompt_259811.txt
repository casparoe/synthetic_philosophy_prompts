A colleague of mine defends a version of the marker view of consciousness: she says that whether an animal has subjective experience — whether there is something it is like to be that animal — can be settled by a single behavioral capacity. Her proposed marker, as she describes it, is the ability within a single lifetime to learn novel associations between arbitrary stimuli and arbitrary behaviors, across sensory and motor domains, and then redeploy those associations flexibly in new contexts: not merely tuning hard-wired responses or learning a few privileged pairings, but forming and recombining learned connections for which evolution could not have supplied a pre-specified template. Animals that demonstrably pass a stringent test of this capacity are, she claims, conscious; animals that demonstrably fail are not — or at least not conscious in any sense that matters for how we should treat them.

I want to slow this down rather than either rejecting it as unverifiable or accepting it on her authority. First, design what you take to be the strongest feasible experimental test of whether a given species actually has this capacity. Be concrete about the training regime, the transfer condition, and the controls that would rule out a narrower, more reflexive form of associative learning; specify the pass/fail criterion, and say which animals you would run the test on and why.

Then step back from the biology. Suppose a species passes the strongest version of your test. What exactly does a positive result establish? Does excellent performance on a test of flexible, cross-domain learning ever license an inference to subjective experience, or only to a sophisticated but unfeeling form of information processing? And if only the latter, is there any observation that could close the gap? Conversely, suppose a species fails the test: does that failure show that the animal is not conscious, or merely that its consciousness, if present, does not run through that particular capacity? I am asking you to mark exactly the boundary between what the test measures and what the claim asserts, and to say what, if anything, no behavioral test could settle even in principle.
