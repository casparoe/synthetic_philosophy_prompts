Consider a mind with only finitely many possible total states at any instant — a finite-state mind, whether biological or artificial. I keep coming back to an argument that such a mind's experience is exhaustible, and I cannot tell whether the argument is sound or whether one of its steps is doing more work than it seems. Here it is:

(1) The mind has, at any instant, only finitely many possible total states.
(2) A mind's experience at an instant is fixed by its total state at that instant: no difference in experience without a difference in state.
(3) From (1) and (2), there are only finitely many possible momentary experiences for that mind.
(4) An endless stream of experience would be an infinite sequence of total states drawn from a finite set; by the pigeonhole principle, at least one total state must recur.
(5) Once a total state recurs, the subsequent stream of states simply repeats the stream that followed the earlier occurrence of that same state.
(6) Therefore, any finite-state mind that lives endlessly will eventually exhaust its possibilities: novelty runs out, and from then on every later experience is one it has already had.

I half-believe the conclusion, but I suspect step (5) is smuggling something in. Is the argument valid, and is each premise true? If not, exactly which step fails, on which reading, and what would a defensible correction of that step be? Also say what would change your verdict — which added stipulation (for example, determinism, or a narrower reading of "experience") would flip your answer — and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
