I'm giving a fourty-five minute research talk next month to a philosophy department colloquium. My thesis is that if you take Anscombe's argument in her pamphlet Mr. Truman's Degree seriously, the standard bombing-saved-lives defense of Hiroshima and Nagasaki isn't just empirically shaky—it's structurally the same move as doing evil that good may come, which she thinks is never legitmate. My paper leans on the passage where she argues, roughly, that to choose to kill the innocent as a means to one's ends is always murder, and that it doesn't matter whether it's one child or thousands, or whether the end is a good one. The audience is mostly analytic philosophers: two committed act-utilitarians, one Catholic moral philosopher who knows Anscombe's work well, a just-war theory person who thinks the bombings were justified but is queasy about modern drone warfare, and a WWII historian who will push back on any claim that the war could have ended soon without invasion. There's also a grad student who's read Finnis and Grisez and will try to turn this into a debate about double effect.
