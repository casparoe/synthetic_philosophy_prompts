I keep catching myself relying on an epistemic habit in arguments about mental imagery, and I want to know whether I am entitled to it. When I read the depictive side of the old Kosslyn–Pylyshyn debate against the propositional side, I notice that I treat my own introspective experience—the way a mentally rotated shape seems to move continuously through intermediate angles, or the way I seem to inspect a faint image and read off details I never deliberately stored—as strong, nearly decisive evidence for the depictive view. I consequently discount experiments and arguments suggesting that the pictorial feel is an accompaniment to something propositional underneath, and I catch myself treating reports from people who say they have little or no mental imagery as failures of introspection rather than as data. A friend says this is circular: no one in the debate denies that imagery feels imagistic; what is contested is the format of the representation that produces that feeling, and my introspection samples the feeling, not the format. So my habit appears to use the very surface in dispute as evidence about the machinery below it.

What I want to know is whether this habit is defensible, and if it is not, whether there is a nearby habit that is. Might it be legitimate, for example, to let introspection set a modest prior that depictive accounts are more plausible or more economical, while leaving room for behavioral and neural data to move me substantially? What would I have to believe about my introspective access to the format of my own mental representations for the stronger version of the habit to be legitimate, and is there any way to tell from the inside whether I have that access?
