I have a habit in moral theorizing I've never really stress-tested. When two theories both handle the cases I'm confident about, I always take the one with fewer principles. Fine—that's just parsimony as a tie-breaker. But I also do something stronger: when the simpler theory then conflicts with a strong intuition about a wierd case, I usually side with the theory and write the intuition off as unreliable, whereas when a more complicated theory conflicts with the same intuition, I count it as a genuine counterexample. So parsimony isn't only deciding which theory wins; it's deciding which evidence gets to count. Is this a defensible policy, or am I using simplicity as a shield? Under what assumptions about where moral intuitions come from and about whether simpler theories are more likely to be true would the asymmetry be justified, and when is it just protecting the neat story from refutation? If it's not defensible as stated, what's the closest defensible version that keeps the benefits without rigging the game?
