I've been trying to sort out what actually makes a script an alphabet, and I want someone to check my criteria instead of just telling me the answer. My working criteria: a script is an alphabet when (1) it has a smallish closed set of segmental letters, basically one grapheme per phoneme, rather than one per syllable or word; (2) vowels and consonants are both written as full letters; (3) the letter shapes are arbitrary—they don't depict the sound they stand for; and (4) the letters are laid out linearly in roughly the order of speech. Now take Korean hangul. It has 14 basic consonants and 10 basic vowels, they're phoneme-sized, and vowels are real letters, so (1) and (2) look fine. But the letters are packed into syllable blocks—한 is ㅎ+ㅏ+ㄴ squeezed into one square—so the writing isn't a linear string the way (4) imagines. And the consonant shapes are ment to be little diagrams of the articulators: ㄱ is the tongue blocking the throat, ㄴ the tongue at the ridge behind the teeth, ㅁ the lips, ㅅ the teeth. So they're iconic under (3), which would make hangul fail the test. My question is: is hangul an alphabet? If it fails (3), does that show my criterion is really just a fact about Latin and Greek rather than about alphabets, or does it show hangul is a different kind of thing—a featural script—that happens to have an alphabet-sized inventory? And then the deeper thing I can't settle: if the shapes encode articulation, does hangul encode a richer theory of what speech is made of (segments plus features) than a plain phonemic alphabet does, or is the iconicity just historical trivia that stops mattering once you can read the letters?
