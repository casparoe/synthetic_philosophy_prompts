I have been drafting a short essay for a general-interest magazine, and I would appreciate your comments on the argument before I revise it. The working title is The Will of the People Is Not a Fact Waiting to Be Found. The essay's central claim is that political disputes about the popular will are not best understood as empirical disagreements about what the public happens to want. They are, I argue, disagreements about how the people should be constituted as a subject, and the phrase the will of the people allows one side to present its own act of constitution as if it were mere discovery.

To make this case, I proceed in three stages. First, I argue that any attribution of a will to a group depends on two things that are themselves politically contested: a rule for aggregating individual views and a decision about who counts as a member of the group. Different aggregation rules can convert exactly the same underlying opinions into different group verdicts, and some distributions of opinion yield no stable group verdict at all until a procedure breaks the tie. This suggests that the appearance of a single popular will is often an artifact of the procedure rather than a fact the procedure merely discovers. Second, I distinguish between the people in the thin sense of the electorate under an existing legal procedure and the people in the thicker sense of a pre-institutional sovereign whose voice exists before any rule gives it expression. I claim that appeals to the thicker sense are either empty on their own or quietly borrow their content from some particular institution, poll, movement, or leader, so the appeal is never as independent of procedural questions as it presents itself to be. Third, I suggest that the phrase works rhetorically because it performs the very unification it seems to report: to call a position the will of the people is less like describing a fact and more like proposing that the audience treat that position as authoritative, much as a chairperson does when announcing the sense of the meeting.

The essay closes by considering the objection that the argument is a roundabout way of denying majority rule, or that elections by definition express the popular will. I answer by conceding that elections express a procedurally specified popular will while denying that they express a pre-procedural one, and I try to explain why that concession does not amount to abandoning ordinary democratic commitments.

I would be especially glad for comments on three points. First, the essay moves from the fact that attributing a popular will requires choosing an aggregation rule and a membership rule to the stronger claim that the will is performed or constructed rather than discovered. Is that move warranted, or is it like moving from the fact that temperature readings require a thermometer to the conclusion that temperature is invented? If the move needs shoring up, what kind of argument would shore it up? Second, does the third stage make the account too easy for the populist to accept, since a populist might reply that of course the people must be actively produced and that producing it is exactly what political contestation is for? If so, what should a critic of populism say next? Third, is the response to the final objection too concessive, in that it seems to grant that a democratic outcome has authority only because we have already accepted a particular procedure, and thereby gives up resources for defending it against those who reject the procedure? Beyond those three points, I would be grateful for any further comments on whether the essay, as sketched, would be clear and interesting to a general audience.
