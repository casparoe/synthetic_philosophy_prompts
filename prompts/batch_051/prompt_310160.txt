I've been a LessWrong/ACX reader for years and I still don't have a settled procedure for how to update when I get testimony about wierd, miracle-adjacent stuff: a normally-levelheaded friend says a homeopathic remedy fixed their chronic condition, someone in my orbit swears they saw something in the sky, a paper reports a psi effect at p < .001. The standard line is "extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence," but that's always felt like a slogan, not something I can actually execute. I'm rereading the first part of David Hume's "Of Miracles" (Section X of An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding), and this bit seems to be doing more precise work than I remembered:

"All probability, then, supposes an opposition of experiments and observations, where the one side is found to overbalance the other, and to produce a degree of evidence, proportioned to the superiority. A hundred instances or experiments on one side, and fifty on another, afford a doubtful expectation of any event; though a hundred uniform experiments, with only one that is contradictory, reasonably beget a pretty strong degree of assurance. In all cases, we must balance the opposite experiments, where they are opposite, and deduct the smaller number from the greater, in order to know the exact force of the superior evidence."

Please describe the most important implications of this specific passage for how I should think about those reports—I dont want a general summary of Hume on miracles, I want the implications of this balancing-and-subtracting move in particular. Does it actually cash out "extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence" into a procedure I can use day-to-day, or does the arithmetic only make sense for his narrower case of a violation of a law of nature? And does the subtraction still apply when the two bodies of "experience" are different in kind (my experience of physical regularities vs. my experience of how often this particular friend's reports check out)?
