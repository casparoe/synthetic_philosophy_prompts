I'm a novelist — two books out, third under contract — and the same problem hits me every book cycle, so every eighteen months or so. Twelve people read a draft: my writing group, my agent, my editor. They send notes. Some notes are about craft I can actually check (pacing, stakes, timeline errors), but a lot are aesthetic judgements: this chapter is dull, this ending is unearned, this character is thin. I have no stable rule for when to let someone else's say-so move me, and a bad revision can sink a book. My instict is with the pessimists in the aesthetic testimony debate: something like the acquaintance principle, that an aesthetic judgement has to be made from your own first-hand experience of the work and can't just be transmitted to you by testimony. But I also know I've fixed real problems I couldn't see until an editor pointed them out, so I can't just be a purist. I want a reusable decision procedure — a checklist, or a sequence of tests I run in order — for deciding when I may adopt another person's aesthetic judgement about my own manuscript as a reason to revise, and when I have to go back and have the experience myself before changing anything. It has to work for someone who will not read the philosophy, so don't give me a survey of positions: keep whichever distinctions from that literature actually do work and drop the ones that don't. Hard cases it has to handle: the note I still can't feel after three rereads even though eight people agree on it; craft notes versus taste notes; praise versus criticism; the reader who is a better writer than me but whose taste I think is broken; my editor saying the market won't tolerate this, which isn't even an aesthetic reason. Mark explicitly which steps are still judgement calls wearing a procedure's clothes. I revise in about three weeks, and when a note arrives I need a yes/no/check answer within an hour. There's no one above me who signs off, but my editor can make the book's life hard if I ignore him to much. Then run the procedure on two or three worked cases, including one where it tells me something I won't like, and say what the procedure can't do.
