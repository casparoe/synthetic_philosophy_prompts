I am the student representative on my high school's Academic Integrity Board, and next week the Board will set a penalty in a case that has split us. A junior copied most of a chemistry lab report from a graduated student's work. She admits the facts and does not contest that the report was plagiarized. Her explanation, given in writing and again at the hearing, is that she was not free to do otherwise: her mother lost her job this spring, she has been working night shifts, she looks after her younger siblings, and a scholarship she needs for university depends on keeping a B+ average in the course. In her account, she is not the kind of person who cheats; her circumstances produced the cheating.

Our rules say the Board may consider personal circumstances as a mitigating factor, but they say nothing about what to do when a student claims she was forced or had no alternative. One side of the Board reads her account as genuine duress and wants to reduce the usual penalty — a zero on the assignment and a semester of academic probation — to a zero with no probation and a reflective essay. The other side thinks her account makes the case worse, not better: on their view she is refusing to own the choice and treating herself as a thing acted on by outside forces, and they want the full penalty plus a note in her file.

I have been reading Sartre on bad faith and radical freedom, and I now see both sides through that reading. The Sartre I have in mind says that a person is never simply pushed into an act by circumstances, because circumstances still have to be taken up, weighed, and answered; describing oneself as an effect of one's situation is a way of hiding from that freedom. If that is right, the second side has a point. But the first side also has a point: recognizing how tightly choices are constrained is not the same as pretending the constraints are not real.

Please draft the one-page written opinion I will circulate before the Board votes. It should recommend a concrete penalty with a time frame, take each side seriously — including the strongest version of the side you ultimately recommend against — and be usable at our meeting rather than a summary of the rulebook. And if the Sartre framing is itself the mistake — if a high-school disciplinary board cannot responsibly decide whether a teenager's statement was bad faith and should not pretend to — then the opinion should say that plainly and tell the Board what it ought to weigh instead, rather than forcing a philosophically tidy recommendation.
