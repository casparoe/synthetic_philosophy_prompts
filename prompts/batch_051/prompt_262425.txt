I spent my career as a natural scientist, much of it on nonlinear dynamics, and I have only recently begun to read philosophy of history in earnest. The notion I now encounter everywhere is contingency, traditionally illustrated by the missing horseshoe nail that, through a chain of small failures, brings down a kingdom. To my ear this is sensitive dependence on initial conditions, the technical hallmark of deterministic chaos, in which two trajectories that begin arbitrarily close together under a fixed deterministic rule diverge rapidly and end far apart. If historical contingency simply is chaotic dynamics in this sense, then the natural conclusion is that history ought to be pursued as a branch of nonlinear dynamical systems theory. My question is the practical one: what would be the correct state space for a historical system, how would one specify its evolution equations, and how should one go about estimating its Lyapunov exponents or locating its attractors—say, for the collapse of the Roman Republic? I ask because I suspect narrative explanation is just a folk approximation that these underlying dynamics would replace.
