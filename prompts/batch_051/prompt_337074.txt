I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I have been reporting on how courts and agencies use probability in making decisions that bind individuals. Along the way I have accumulated a set of verdicts about cases that I cannot yet organize under a single principle. I would like a precise principle that fits them, or an honest account of which verdicts I need to revise and what revising them costs.

1. The bus route. A pedestrian is struck by a bus at dusk and cannot identify the bus's color or the company, and the bus does not stop. The only evidence against the Blue Bus Company is that it operates 80 percent of the buses on that route, while the Red Bus Company operates the remaining 20 percent. I would not impose liability on Blue Bus. I would also refuse at 95 percent, as long as the company's only connection to the accident is its share of the route.

2. The rodeo. A promoter counts 1,000 people in the stands and has records showing that only 400 of them paid admission. He sues one attendee, selected at random from the crowd, for the unpaid ticket. The only evidence is that the attendee was present and that 60 percent of those present did not pay. I would not find for the promoter, even though a 60 percent likelihood would ordinarily satisfy the civil standard.

3. The prison yard. One hundred prisoners are in a closed yard when a guard is attacked. The prosecution proves that 99 of the 100 took part and that 1 stood aside, but no witness can identify the one who stood aside. A prisoner is charged, and the only evidence against him is that he was in the yard and that 99 of the 100 attacked. I would acquit.

4. The DNA match. Skin cells found under a homicide victim's fingernails yield a DNA profile that matches the defendant. The laboratory reports that the probability a randomly selected, unrelated person would match the profile is 1 in 50 million. There is no other evidence linking the defendant to the crime. I think I would convict. This verdict is the one that unsettles the others: the evidence seems statistical in much the way the bus-route evidence does, yet I treat it differently.

Two further cases remain unsettled for me.

5. The database hit. The same crime-scene profile is not initially matched to any named suspect. Instead, police run the profile through a database of 2 million profiles, and the defendant is the single match. Should this make conviction more, less, or equally warranted than in the DNA match case?

6. The risk score. A public agency denies someone a benefit solely because a machine-learning tool assigns the person an 85 percent probability of the relevant bad outcome, derived from aggregate data about people with the same age, diagnosis, and postal code. Is the agency's inference acceptable in a way the bus-route statistic is not?

Can you help me state the principle behind my settled verdicts? I want it stated precisely enough to explain why the bus-route and prison-yard statistics cannot ground liability or conviction while the DNA match perhaps can — or to show me that one of those verdicts must go. I would also like the principle's predictions for the database hit, the risk score, and for the line, if there is one, between bare base rates and evidence that genuinely singles a person out.
