Suppose that, starting with submissions received January 1, 2028, the editorial boards of the five most prestigious general math journals (say Annals of Mathematics, Inventiones Mathematicae, JAMS, Acta Mathematica, and Publications IHÉS) jointly adopt this rule: any proof that essentially relies on the law of excluded middle, on double-negation elimination, or on a choice principle that yields an existence claim without a construction gets desk-rejected unless the author also supplies a constructive proof of the same theorem. They publish a referee checklist for what "essentially relies" means, and they say they'll hold the line for at least a decade. Nothing else changes — grants, hiring, promotion, and the rest of the journal landscape stay as they are, which means a lot of authors could simply defect down the prestige ladder.

I'm a philosopher of mathematics, not a working mathematician, so I have opinions about this but no settled forecast. Set aside whether the editors are philosophically right; I'm asking what happens if they actualy do it. I don't want a story. I want a forecast with the reasoning visible at each step.

What happens to mathematical practice over the next thirty years? Which subfields take the hit (think through things like the usual forms of the intermediate value theorem, the least upper bound principle, Bolzano–Weierstrass, Zorn's lemma in algebra, Tychonoff, and the compactness theorem in model theory) and which barely notice? Do working mathematicians mostly comply, route around the rule, or game it — adding a perfunctory constructive proof to a classical one, reclassifying the classical part as a lemma someone else will have to justify, publishing in applied or CS venues, or drifting toward problems that are constructively clean from the start? What new research programs or prestige hierarchies appear? Remember it's only five journals, not the whole profession, so how much does the effect propagate anyway?

And then the part I actually care about: tell me which links in your forecast are robust under almost any view of how mathematics progresses, and which are guesses that turn on mundane facts about incentives or editor stamina. What would have to be true about how mathematicians actually use nonconstructive proofs for your forecast to be wrong? Give confidence link by link, and flag where a small change in the rule — say, how strictly "essentially" is enforced, or whether "constructive" is read Bishop-style, Brouwerian, or just as "the referee can see an algorithm" — flips the sign of the prediction.
