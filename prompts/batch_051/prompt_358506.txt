So I've been going down a rabbit hole about people who try to actually opt out of the state—like the ones who want to build seasteads, or start a private city run like a company, or buy a chunk of land and call it its own little country. And it hit me that we have a wierd double standard here. If someone leaves a church, a marriage, a club, a job, or deletes their account on some platform, everyone says fine, that's your right; we'd be creeped out by any group that made it nearly impossible to leave. Even leaving your country is treated as normal, as long as you're just moving to a different country. But the moment someone tries to leave the state as such—not to join another state, just to stop being governed, or to set up a private city where governance is more like a contract you can cancel—suddenly people think it's crackpot or dangerous, and governments generally block it, with force if they have to. And nobody blinks at that.

I can't figure out what principle explains this. At first I thought, well, a club is voluntary and the state isn't, but that seems backwards, because you don't choose to be born into the state either. Then I thought maybe it's because the state provides roads and courts and defense, but a big company town provides a lot of that too and we don't let it lock people in. Is it just that states have the guns and don't like competition? Or is there some real difference between exiting a church or a club and exiting a state—something about public goods, or scale, or there being no neutral ground to stand on once you're outside? I want you to actually take a side. Give me the strongest principle you can find that either justifies the asymmetry or shows it's basically self-serving for whoever holds power, and then give an honest verdict: should we keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or drop it?
