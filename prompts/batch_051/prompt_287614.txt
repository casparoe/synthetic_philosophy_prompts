Consider the discovery of Neptune in 1846 as a case study in the philosophy of causation. The observed positions of Uranus repeatedly diverged from Newtonian predictions, and Urbain Le Verrier, with John Couch Adams having independently reached the same conclusion, inferred that an as-yet-unobserved planet was gravitationally perturbing Uranus's orbit; Johann Galle then located Neptune within about a degree of the predicted position. Analyze this episode through the contrast between a strict Humean regularity account of causal inference, on which constant conjunction licenses only habit-based expectation rather than any glimpse of a necessary tie, and the New Hume reading, on which the idea of necessary connection is the thought of a real, unobservable causal power lying behind observed regularities. In exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion, do the following: (1) state exactly what the regularity account permits one to infer about the source of the anomaly in Uranus and what it rules out; (2) state what the New Hume reading would add to or license beyond that inference; (3) identify the feature of the episode that each side would most plausibly claim as its strongest evidence; (4) formulate the hardest objection the episode raises for each side; and (5) give a considered verdict on whether the discovery of Neptune favors the regularity account, the New Hume, or neither, and say what this suggests about how the dispute between regularity and necessary connection should be framed.
