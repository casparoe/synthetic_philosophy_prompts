I maintain a reliability model for a distributed system, and I recently wrote up a design note arguing for a rule we apply routinely: when you know the individual failure rates of two components and have no information about how their failures are related, you should treat them as independent and multiply the rates. I formalized the argument in the note, and now I think the formalization proves too much, because it seems to force me to multiply in cases where I am nearly certain the result is wrong. Here is the argument as I wrote it.

(1) Let A and B be two events, such as service A being down and service B being down. My uncertainty about them is a distribution over four cells: both down, only A down, only B down, neither down.

(2) Suppose the evidence fixes exactly the row and column totals of that table, P(A) = p and P(B) = q, and discriminates in no way between tables that share those totals.

(3) Since the evidence is neutral among all tables with those totals, the only defensible distribution over the cells is the one that adds the least information beyond the totals. Any other distribution would encode a specific pattern of dependence among the cells, and I have no evidence for any such pattern.

(4) The unique distribution that adds the least information in this sense is the maximum-entropy distribution subject to those totals, and that distribution is the product distribution: P(A and B) = pq.

(5) Therefore, whenever I know only the individual rates of two events, the rational probability of their joint occurrence is the product of their individual rates; the rule is fully general for any events A and B.

(6) But now apply the rule to two instances of the same service, running the same binary against the same database. Each instance has an observed per-cycle failure rate of p = 0.01, and suppose my monitoring has never captured them failing together, so the data contain exactly the marginal rates and no joint observations. By (5), the probability that both fail together is 0.0001. I believe that number is off by more than an order of magnitude: a bad deploy, a schema migration, or a database outage takes both instances down at once, and I know that even though it does not appear anywhere in the marginal table.

Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? I would most like to keep the multiplication rule for services written independently by different teams that share no infrastructure. If keeping it requires accepting the identical-instances conclusion, I want to be told that plainly; if not, I want the rule restated precisely enough that it separates those two kinds of case without my having to hand-tune the model each time.
