I retired from a career in the life sciences and have spent the past few years reading philosophy of language. One long-running dispute there strikes me as both genuinely difficult and oddly familiar: after decades of work, there is still no agreed semantics for generic statements such as 'ducks lay eggs' and 'mosquitoes carry malaria.' The data do not seem to be the sticking point. Everyone accepts that 'ducks lay eggs' is true even though, at any given time, only reproductively mature females ever do; and everyone accepts that 'mosquitoes carry malaria' is true even though the overwhelming majority of mosquitoes never carry the parasite. No further observation about ducks or mosquitoes seems likely to settle the matter, yet the theories keep multiplying. One approach treats generics as law-like generalizations over a kind that tolerate exceptions when the property is characteristic of that kind. Another treats them as claims about what is normal, in some idealized or statistical sense. A third holds that the truth of a generic tracks not raw prevalence but the strikingness, danger, or conversational relevance of the property. A fourth argues that the meaning is sharply context-dependent and that the real work is done by what speakers use these sentences to do. Each camp can point to canonical examples that the others handle awkwardly.

My working hypothesis is that this is not a single unresolved empirical or formal question but several different questions traveling under one label: a question about literal truth conditions, a question about how children acquire such generalizations from sparse exposure, a question about their communicative function, and perhaps a question about whether the world really is divided into natural kinds. In biology I watched a comparable stalemate over the word 'function,' which began to dissolve only after people separated the historical question — what a trait was selected for — from the present question — what it now contributes to survival and reproduction. I wonder whether the generics debate needs the same sort of splitting, or whether it is instead a substantive disagreement about a discoverable fact of psychology or semantics.

I would like a diagnosis rather than a survey. What kind of disagreement is this, exactly? Is there a hidden empirical claim that would settle one version of it, or a formal distinction that would separate the versions? Or should we conclude that 'generic' is a syntactic category with no single underlying semantic phenomenon, so that the search for a unified theory was misconceived from the start? And under your diagnosis, what would genuine progress look like?
