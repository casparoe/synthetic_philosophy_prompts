I am a novelist, and I have written a scene that I cannot get philosophically right, and it matters to me that I get it right. The central character of the book is a retired physicist who has come to hold that mind is not a late achievement of complex nervous systems but something present, in vanishingly small measure, throughout the physical world: each fundamental item, on her view, has a faint inward aspect, and the pronounced, unified awareness of a human being is somehow composed of these countless microscopic inward aspects. Her brother, a neurologist, is her most patient critic, and I have given him the objection that stops her motionless: if every part of a brain is already a separate seat of experience, then a brain contains an enormous multiplicity of distinct experiential subjects, whereas each of us finds from within only a single, unified field of awareness. He asks what sort of operation could turn many separate centers of experience into one. He complains that many small glows do not, by aggregation, become one bright and unified glare; a pile of small pains is not one pain belonging to a single sufferer. She cannot answer him in my drafts except by murmuring about emergence, and I know that is a failure of the scene rather than of the argument.

I am asking for help with the underlying philosophy, not for a bibliography. Is the brother's objection as strong in principle as it sounds in the scene, or does it depend on assumptions a careful defender could reject? What are the strongest forms of the objection, the forms a well-read reader would complain about if I omitted them? And what is the most defensible reply my physicist can make without abandoning her position — not necessarily the view you yourself hold, but a serious answer that would let the character keep her convictions and her intelligence at once?

I also want to avoid a particular piece of carelessness that creeps into early drafts. The physicist is tempted to say that when a person has a headache, each part of the brain has its own miniature headache, and the person's headache is the sum of them. That sounds obviously wrong to me, but I cannot yet say exactly why, and I do not want her to say it unless it can be made rigorous. Should the character reject that formulation, and if so, what should she put in its place?

I do not need references or reading suggestions; I need the structure of the problem made clear enough that I can give the neurologist the hardest version of the challenge and give the physicist a response that a thoughtful reader would accept as a genuine answer rather than an evasion.
