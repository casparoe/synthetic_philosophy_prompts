You are a professional philosopher of religion who works on the Molinism–open theism debate. Before you write the dialogue, work out in private notes the strongest formulation of each position and the sharpest objections each side can press; do not include those notes in your answer, but let the care show in the exchange.

I am a novelist at work on a book whose central conceit is this: a young woman, upon touching another person, sees the complete tree of what that person would freely choose under any possible circumstance—not probabilities, not dispositions, but what the person would in fact freely do at every counterfactual branching. The book treats the vision as genuinely revelatory, not delusional, and the drama lies in what this knowledge does to her conviction that her future, and everyone else's, remains open. I wish to write a scene in which two characters—a Molinist theologian and an open theist philosopher—discuss what she has been shown. The Molinist regards her vision as a finite, creaturely analogue of middle knowledge: the pre-volitional knowledge of counterfactuals of creaturely freedom, perfectly compatible with libertarian freedom. The open theist regards what she sees as something that cannot be knowledge of a free agent's would-doings, since such counterfactuals would be true without anything that makes them true—not the agent's character, not the circumstances, not any actual choice—and so the tree, however vivid, is a fatalism smuggled in under the word would.

Write the scene as sustained dialogue. Each character should be sharp and charitable, and must respond to the other's actual moves rather than talk past them; the novelist has not yet decided which of them is right, and neither should win by default. Have them work through at least these three knots: whether a counterfactual of creaturely freedom requires a truth-maker and, if so, what could supply it; whether knowledge of the tree—divine or human—forecloses freedom even when it does not causally determine choice; and what open theism can say about how this woman might act on the vision without the future already being settled. Keep the abstract arguments tethered to one concrete branching point in the woman's own life—say, the choice of whether to reveal a secret that would destroy a friendship—and let the discussion return to it. End at a point of clarified but unresolved disagreement, not conversion.
