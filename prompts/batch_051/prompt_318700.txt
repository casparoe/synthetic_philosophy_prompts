Book club is doing No Exit next month and I want to come with something sharper then the usual conversation about the famous line where other people are hell. Read the play through Sartre's own view of radical freedom and bad faith, because the two seem to point in opposite directions. Sartre's claim is that we're always and unavoidably free, and that bad faith is something we do to ourselves—like the café waiter Sartre describes, who overcommits to his role so he can pretend he isn't free. But the three characters in the play can look like people who've had their freedom taken away by each other. Garcin is dead and still needs Inès to believe he wasn't a coward; Estelle has no mirror and uses the others to confirm she exists at all; Inès seems to need a victim to feel real. And at the end the door opens and Garcin won't leave. I want your honest read: is the play showing three instances of bad faith (they could leave—or at least stop performing for each other—and they refuse), or is it showing that the Look of others can really strip away possibilities in a way Sartre's theory says shouldn't be possible? Does the ending vindicate radical freedom, or does it quietly admit the theory fails the moment another person enters? Don't reduce the play to a lecture illustration, and don't bend Sartre to fit the play: I want to know where they align and where the playwright and the philosopher part ways, and what the play shows that the theory alone misses. Tell me where your reading is least secure—what detail about the play, or about Sartre's view, if it came out differently, would change your verdict. Max 800 words.
