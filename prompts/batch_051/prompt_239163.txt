In my philosophy of mind reading group, one guy keeps pushing the line that panpsychism is basically the claim that consciousness is like charge. He means it pretty literally: just as charge is a fundamental property of matter, showing up in every electron and proton, consciousness (or at least the raw stuff of experience) is a fundamental property of matter too, present all the way down. Then he uses it to run a much stronger argument: if that's right, every bit of the physical world is already conscious, and if it's conscious, it's already feeling something — so the early universe and the dust between stars were full of tiny panics, cravings, and frustation. He calls this pan-sentience, and he treats it as the obvious consequence. From there he concludes that fundamental physics is secretly a moral catastrophe, and that particle accelerators (or even having children, who assemble new experience out of old matter) become ethically loaded decisions. I can't tell whether the charge analogy is doing all the work in that jump. Audit it like an engineer, please. Give me the correspondence table — what's the electron, what's charge, what's the field, what plays the role of positive/negative? Where does the mapping break? What does "charge" quietly import that conscious states might not have — that it's simple, invariant, binary-ish, and morally inert? And the main thing: even if consciousness is fundamental in the charge-like sense, does the step to "suffering everywhere" survive, or does the analogy smuggle in the idea that basic experience has to come with familiar pain? If there's a better analogy from physics or elsewhere, offer it. Please end with a final line, "Verdict: ...", saying in one sentence whether the charge analogy can carry the conclusion that the physical world is saturated with suffering.
