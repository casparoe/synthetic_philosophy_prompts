There's a standard impossibility result in fair ML that, in its cruder decision-rule form, goes like this. Take a risk score that gets turned into a yes/no decision (predicting reoffense, default, whatever). You'd like all three of: calibration (among everyone the score says "60% risk," about 60% actually have the outcome, and that holds within each group), equal false-positive rates across groups, and equal false-negative rates across groups. The theorem says you can't have all three when the groups have different base rates—assuming the base rates are fixed, external facts about the groups.

But in precisely the places this matters most—predictive policing, child welfare screening, lending—the decisions feed back into the outcome your trying to predict. Being arrested raises your chance of being arrested again; a denied loan can push a borrower toward default; a family flagged by child services gets monitored in ways that make another flag more likely. So the base rates are partly endogenous to the classifier itself.

What I'd like to know is whether the impossibility result survives once you let the base rates move in response to decisions. Does it still go through, so you're stuck in the same trade-off (or a worse one)? Or does the feedback loop change the question—maybe by choosing how and where you deploy the classifier you can also shape the distribution you're scoring, so the three conditions could be approached simultaneously at a kind of fixed point? Or does the result stop being the relevant constraint, and the real impossibility becomes about which reachable equilibria a group can end up in?

Also say which premise of the original result is doing the actual work once base rates are endogenous, and what a repaired version of the theorem would have to claim.
