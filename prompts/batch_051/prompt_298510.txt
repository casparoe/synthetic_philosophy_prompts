I'm putting together a handout for a philosophy of biology reading group on whether calling DNA a code is misleading, and I want one more argument for thinking it's a metaphor rather than a literal description, beyond the ones I already have. My list so far: (1) real codes need a sender, a receiver, and a shared convention, and DNA has none of those; (2) the mapping from codon to amino acid is just ordinary causal chemistry, so calling translation decoding adds nothing; (3) if information only means statistical correlation, then every cause in the cell carries as much information as the genes do, which makes the whole idea trivial; (4) the sequence only gets interpreted by the rest of the cell, so there's no free-standing message sitting in the chromosome; (5) development constructs the organism rather than reading off a pre-written plan, so coding language quietly sneaks in preformationism. I also know the parity argument — that non-genetic developmental resources like the egg's cytoplasm or chromatin modifications carry information in the same sense genes do — so don't give me anything that's a restatement or a special case of these six. I want a genuinely different line, maybe from the history of the metaphor, and an honest verdict on whether its any good.
