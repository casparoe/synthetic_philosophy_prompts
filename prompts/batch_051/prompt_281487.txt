As a novelist I keep noticing an odd asymmetry in how we treat effort, and I can't tell whether it's a real principle or just a story we tell ourselves. If I mention that a novel took nine years — two discarded drafts, a stalled marriage, the usual devotional gestures — people handle the book with more respect, and I catch myself doing it too, as though the six-week book of equal quality would be a cheaper object. We say the ungifted runner who grinds to the same finish time as the natural is heroic, while the natural just has 'talent,' and talent is said with a faint smell of distrust. But flip the frame from persons to tools and we turn fiercely the other way: nobody wants a slower word processor so the writing will be more earned, nobody chooses the worse connection so the trip feels more deserved, and when I find a faster way to research a chapter I don't mourn the suffering I skipped. So the asymmetry is: effort seems to add value when a person achieves something, but we pay to eliminate it whenever it shows up in a process or a tool. Is there anything that actually justifies that split, or is it just an inconsistancy we live with? If you defend it, defend it against the case where two novels are word for word identical and the only difference is that one took nine years and the other six weeks — and then say whether a writer who deliberately uses a broken keyboard and produces a worse novel is admirable or just self-indulgent. If you give the asymmetry up, say which side goes first and what you'd tell someone about the nine-year novel.
