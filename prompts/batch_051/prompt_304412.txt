In an essay defending the claim that we can know what is possible through a priori reflection, the author uses the following analogy: conceivability stands to possibility as perception stands to actuality. Just as your seeing that the cat is on the mat is fallible evidence, but evidence nonetheless, that the cat is on the mat, your clearly and carefully conceiving of a situation is fallible evidence, but evidence nonetheless, that the situation is possible. The author then puts the analogy to work: because I can, after careful reflection, conceive of a world physically exactly like ours but in which no one has any conscious experience — a zombie world — I thereby have strong reason to think that such a world is metaphysically possible, not merely that I can imagine it. I suspect the analogy is doing more work than it can bear, but I want to be fair to it. Give an explicit mapping between the two sides of the analogy, identify the features of perception that the analogy quietly imports and the disanalogies that matter for this intended use, and say whether the author's conclusion about the zombie world survives once they are on the table. If the analogy cannot be repaired, say what a better analogy would be, if any.
