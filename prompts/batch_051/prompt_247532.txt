Compose a sonnet about the Bayesian claim that priors wash out with enough shared evidence. The octave should build the optimistic picture: two agents begin with different prior probabilities over the same set of hypotheses, condition on the same growing body of data, and their credences slowly come together. The volta should turn on the fine print: the convergence is proved only under conditions—roughly, that neither agent's prior rules out the true hypothesis (or, in a continuous space, the region around it) and that the model is not too badly misspecified—and it holds almost surely, not on every possible data sequence. What is proved to converge is their predictions, and under stronger conditions their posterior distributions, not a retroactive vindication of either starting point. The closing couplet should state exactly what the washing-out of priors buys and what it leaves untouched. The sonnet's turn should carry the philosophical weight rather than merely announcing an objection, and the poem should be precise enough that a formal epistemologist would not wince.
