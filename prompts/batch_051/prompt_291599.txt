Last term in my seminar on death a student said something that's been nagging at me ever since. On the one hand, when an 80-year-old in decent health gets another ten good years, we treat that as an unqualified good — the whole point of medicine, really. On the other hand, nearly everyone agrees that living forever would eventually become boring, empty, or somehow not a life we'd actually want. So we seem to want more life at every finite point, but we balk at an infinite amount of it. You get the same tension with heaven: people say an eternal existence of bliss would lose its savor, but they'll still call a death at 40 a tragedy. That's a strange pair of committments. Williams's Makropulos case is in the background here — a woman given a vastly extended life who ends up refusing to go on because it has all gone flat (I'm paraphrasing, don't worry about the text). But I don't want a reading of Williams; I want a principle. Is there a principled line between "more years are good" and "endless years are bad"? For instance, maybe each chunk of life is good only when there's still something unfinished, or only because we can plan toward an end, or because novelty can be renewed in a finite life. Each of those seems to imply that at some particular birthday, the next ten years tip over from good to bad, which sounds absurd. So: find the strongest candidate principle that justifies the asymmetry, test it honestly against the case where someone is doing fine at year 500, and tell me whether the ordinary view is defensible or whether we should give up one of its two halves — and which one.
