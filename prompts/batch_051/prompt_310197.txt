I'm giving a talk at a value-theory workshop next week and I've been loosing sleep over the Q&A. The paper defends what I've been calling tranquilism: for a given person, the only thing that's non-instrumentally good is the conscious, alert absence of craving (where craving is the felt, restless kind of wanting, not just any old desire, and the absence is a waking quiet, not coma or dreamless sleep), and the only thing that's non-instrumentally bad is suffering. Everything else (pleasure, knowledge, friendship, achievement, autonomy, whatever) matters only insofar as it bears on those two. The argument, very roughly, is a subtraction test plus a replacement test: strip craving and suffering out of any conscious life and nothing seems missing from the point of view of how well the life is going; put any candidate good into a life while holding craving and suffering fixed, and the candidate suddenly looks like it has no value of its own — knowledge without relief from restlessness feels empty, relief without knowledge doesn't. So the other goods all look merely conditionally valuable.

The audience worries me: two card-carrying hedonists, one desire-satisfaction theorist, an objective-list person who keeps bringing up knowledge and achievement, and a scholar of Buddhist philosophy who thinks I've flattened the end of suffering into a mere absence and dropped the path. There's also a grad student who asked me after a earlier talk whether the view makes a permanently daydreaming person the ideal. I want a prep sheet, not a polished defense. Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll actually come, phrased the way those people would phrase them, with what I should concede, what I should hold, and the weakest part of my position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope nobody notices. Also tell me the objection I'm probably not expecting but should be ready for.
