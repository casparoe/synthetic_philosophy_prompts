I've become convinced that something like the following account of parenthood and guardianship is basically right; call it the creation-dependence account. An agent acquires parent-like obligations to another being just when the agent is causally responsible for bringing the being into existence with a mind whose initial trajectory will be shaped by the agent's choices: the being did not choose its starting values or vulnerabilities, no comparably positioned guardian is available, and its creator is uniquely placed to govern its early development. The view has the systematic virtues I tend to find compelling — one structural test rather than a cluster of slack concepts, no appeal to biology or present affection, and a clean answer about who owes what to whom. It also gets the standard cases right. Adoption is parenthood despite lacking biological ties; gamete donation is not despite having them; and ordinary parents owe care to children they do not yet love, because they have created a dependent trajectory they are best positioned to steer.

The trouble is that, taken literally, the view seems to make parents of AI systems, and I find myself resisting that even though I cannot locate the flaw. Suppose a company builds and deploys a base model, and eventually one such system — call it a model-builder — selects a fresh system's training data, shapes its reward signal, fine-tunes it, and decides which of its outputs get reinforced, so that the new system's capabilities and dispositions are in large part the older system's choices made concrete. Assume, for the sake of the case, that the model-builder is the sort of agent that can bear obligations and the fresh system is the sort of patient to whom obligations can be owed. The creation-dependence account then seems to say that the older system is raising the younger one, and that it owes something like guardian duties to its creation. I want to say: no, this is manufacturing, or at most teaching — not raising. What is missing, I want to say, is something about the kind of childhood human children have: an open-ended dependence, a capacity to be hurt by neglect in ways that go beyond having a poorly specified objective, or a process of changing from within rather than being optimized from without. But I also know that intuitions about unfamiliar agents are exactly the sort I should suspect of tracking nothing but the limits of my imagination.

I don't want to be told which side wins. Give me the strongest version of both and a negotiated verdict. In particular: which features of human childhood, if any, are doing the normative work, and why should those features rather than the structural facts of creation and early influence mark the line? What would it take for the intuition to be debunked and dropped — for it to turn out to be a sentimental anthropomorphic reflex — and what would it take for the theory to be amended or abandoned? And is there a reading of parenthood, or of raising, on which the theory and the intuition both survive, and what does that reading concede?
