I'm taking a philosophy class on personal identity and we did Parfit's teletransporter, and I keep getting stuck on a version where the scan and the destruction are out of sync. In the usual case you step in, the machine scans you, destroys the body, and builds the replica on Mars from that same scan, so the replica has your mental life right up to the last moment. Suppose instead the machine has a fixed one-hour lag: it scans you as soon as you check in and stores the blueprint, and then you wait in a windowless lounge for an hour before the machine destroys you and builds the replica from the earlier scan. The replica wakes up with everything you had when you arrived, but nothing from the hour in the lounge—no memory of rereading the same magazine, worrying, or finally deciding to go through with it.

My question is whether the person who presses the button at the end of that hour survives. The psychological-continuity view would probably say an hour of lost memory doesn't matter much, it's like a blank after a nap, so you'd survive as the replica minus the lounge hour. But I'm not sure this is right, because the replica is continuous with the you from an hour ago, not with the you who is making the decision. Those two versions of you are related through ordinary memory, sure, but the replica branches off the earlier one while the later one gets deleted. Is that fission, a rewind, or just forgetting? And does the further-fact view say anything different here from what it says about the ordinary teletransporter, or is it just no survival either way?

The thing I really can't settle is wether the length of the lag should matter. If the wait were a week, or ten years, is there a point where the psychological view stops saying you survive, and if so what makes that cutoff non-arbitrary? Or does it just say survival is a matter of degree and there's no threshhold at all, in which case what matters in survival just thins out as the lag grows and the button-presser only partly survives?
