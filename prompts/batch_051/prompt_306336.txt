I'm a PhD student in jurisprudence, mostly working on sovereign immunity—specifically, whether it makes sense to say the state itself does wrong, rather than just particular officials doing wrong on its behalf. I've been going back and forth with my advisor about a fictional constitutional clause I'm using as a case study, and I'd love an outside take. The clause reads: "No action for damages shall lie against the State for anything done in the execution of a public function." The drafters were obviously thinking of old-fashioned harms: soldiers damaging a shop during a search, a minister defaming someone in a speech, a planning authority approving a project that floods a village downstream. Each of those involves an identifiable human official deciding something.

The case I can't get a handle on is this. The same state's tax agency uses a machine-learning system to score returns for audit. The model was trained on years of past audit decisions and soaked up an old bias against certain neighborhoods, so it flags their residents at much higher rates. Their returns get frozen for months; some lose jobs or homes while appealing, and no single human ever decided to go after any of them—the model's weights are not decisions or policies anyone adopted. Does the clause apply? What do the different approaches to interpretation give here: the plain words, the purpose of the clause, what the drafters would have said if asked, and what a reasonable reader today would take them to mean?

And, deeper: does automation change the underlying picture of what it is for the state to do wrong, or does it just make it harder to point to a wrongdoer?
