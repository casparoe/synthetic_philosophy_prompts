I am a retired natural scientist (physics was my field) and have recently begun reading philosophy of mathematics. I would like a consistency check on a set of commitments I seem to hold simultaneously. The commitments are:

(a) I am a modest platonist: mathematical claims have objective, mind-independent truth values, and the truth of the Riemann Hypothesis does not depend on whether anyone ever proves it.

(b) I retain a constructivist scruple from my scientific training: an existential claim is intellectually satisfactory to me only if the thing asserted to exist can be specified or generated by an explicit rule, or at least approximated to arbitrary accuracy. A bare existence proof with no witness and no procedure leaves me uncertain what I have been told.

(c) Nevertheless, I accept classical analysis as it is standardly practiced: the real numbers, the least upper bound principle, curves such as the sine curve that outrun compass-and-straightedge construction but are given by explicit rules such as power series, and nonconstructive theorems such as the intermediate value theorem, including cases in which the point whose existence is proved cannot be written down in closed form.

(d) I accept the Axiom of Choice and do not take the Banach–Tarski result as a reason to reject it; the result shows only that geometric intuition is an unreliable guide to arbitrary sets of points.

(e) Yet the well-ordering of the reals, which follows from that same axiom, genuinely disquiets me. I find myself wanting to say that it is true in some thin sense but empty because no well-ordering can be described.

Which of these commitments are actually in tension with one another, which pairs only appear to be, and what is the least costly revision that would make the whole set hang together? I would be grateful for an ordering of the tensions by severity, with an account of what would have to give in each case. Please answer in American English.
