I spent most of my career as a research biologist studying social behavior in animals, and since retiring I have been reading my way through classical Chinese philosophy. The Mencius–Xunzi dispute about human nature has struck me as more discriminating than the nature–nurture vocabulary I was trained in, and I have agreed to lead a discussion of William Golding's Lord of the Flies for a reading group whose members include a long-retired schoolteacher who regards the novel as a straightforward fable about innate savagery.

I would like a careful critical reading of the novel through the Mencius–Xunzi debate. By the Mencian position I mean the view that human nature contains incipient moral sprouts — the spontaneous alarm and compassion at the sight of a child about to fall into a well, the sense of shame and deference, the capacity to distinguish right from wrong — and that these sprouts grow into virtue only if they are cultivated; cruelty and wrongdoing arise from failure to nourish them, from bad conditions, or from allowing the heart to starve. By the Xunzian position I mean the view that our untutored desires and feelings, left to themselves, tend toward contention and disorder, and that the goodness and order human beings display are artifacts of deliberate effort: rules, ritual, teaching, and habit reshaping what nature provides.

I would like the reading to move scene by scene and to be faithful in both directions. Where does the novel support a Mencian reading — in Simon's uncoerced compassion, perhaps, or in the role of shame and mutual recognition in the boys' unraveling? Where does it support a Xunzian reading — in the fragile civilizing power of the conch and the assemblies, in what the hunters' painted faces release, in Roger's path once the old sanctions dissolve? And where does the novel resist each position? The surrounding adult world, which turns out to be at war, seems to me to complicate any confidence that institutions and cultivation make human beings good, while the ending, in which Ralph weeps over the loss of innocence and the darkness he finds in the human heart, resists a merely optimistic Mencian conclusion; yet the novel's treatment of Simon may equally resist the harsher Xunzian verdict.

Do not reduce the novel to an illustration. I want an account of what the Mencius–Xunzi debate brings into focus in the novel that a nature–nurture reading would miss, and of what the novel contributes that the philosophical debate on its own does not. If you had to carry the comparison in a single evening, which four or five scenes would you put before the group, and in what order? Paraphrase from memory of the text; quotations and citations are neither wanted nor needed.
