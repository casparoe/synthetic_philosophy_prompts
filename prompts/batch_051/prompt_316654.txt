Something's been nagging at me since I redid a few Bongard problems. You know the setup: there are six boxes on the left and six on the right, and the task is to name the rule that separates them — the left boxes might be closed curves with a dot inside, the right ones closed curves with no dot, or curves with exactly two crossings. People usually get these from just those twelve examples, even though the space of possible rules is huge, and we cheerfully say the person has caught on to the actual concept: they've genuinely learned the rule, they aren't just parroting the instances.

Now compare the machine. When a model nails the same problem after training on millions of images we describe it as pattern-matching or picking up a statistical correlate, and when a model nails it from the twelve examples alone we describe it as having seen the problem before, or as doing shallow analogy. We almost never say it has the concept. But the human also walks in with a lifetime of visual experience that is plausibly doing a lot of the same prior-laden work, so simple sample efficiency can't be the whole story; and anyway the human is allowed to have priors while the machine is disqualified for having them. So there's a asymmetry in ordinary epistemic practice: few-shot success from a human is treated as evidence of concept possession, while few-shot success from a machine is treated as evidence of cheating, overfitting, or shallowness.

What would actually justify treating the same performance differently? I want the strongest principled reason you can find — something better than a bald appeal to biology or to introspective certainty — and then I want you to test it against a hard case. Here's the hard case: the model is given only the same twelve images, a generic visual encoder that has never seen another Bongard problem, and a search over a simple, compositionally-defined space of rules; it outputs the right separating rule and generalizes correctly to four new images on each side. Does it have the concept? If your principle says no, say exactly what's missing; if yes, say whether you're willing to apply it to the human who does the same.
