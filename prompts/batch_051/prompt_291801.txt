I have been circling a problem in the pragmatics of presupposition for a few weeks now, and I have resolved to stop reading anything new until I have written down the standard of success. Most of what I have read so far takes sides before it states the criteria, and I keep finding myself convinced by whichever side I read last, which suggests I am missing the specification the whole debate ought to answer to. The immediate trigger was an argument with a friend about a dialogue system we were tinkering with: it would accept an utterance whose presupposition it had not already stored, and then carry on as if the new information had never arrived, in a way no human speaker would. The argument led me back to the familiar standoff between the common-ground picture, on which a presupposition is something the participants already take for granted, and the scorekeeping picture with its rule of accommodation, on which an utterance can bring a missing presupposition into the conversational score after the fact, provided nobody objects. I can gesture at both pictures, but I cannot yet say precisely what an adequate account of presupposition and accommodation must do.

Write me the desiderata, taking the standard to be adequacy to ordinary human conversation rather than to some formal-language fragment. I want the requirements, each paired with the case or phenomenon that forces it, and a note on why it is a requirement rather than a preference of one camp. The kinds of case I mean: the projection of presuppositions through negation, conditionals and questions, together with the fact that projection is not automatic and can be read locally; the possibility of explicit cancellation, where the speaker denies the presupposition rather than the asserted content; the felt difference between a false assertion and a failed presupposition, and the way a failed presupposition contaminates or sidelines an otherwise true sentence; the distinction between presupposition, entailment and conversational implicature that any theory has to respect; and, above all, the limits on accommodation—why a hearer can quietly accept a previously unmentioned sister in an utterance like 'I have to collect my sister from the airport', but not a presupposition the participants already jointly know to be false. Then flag the pairs of requirements that appear jointly unsatisfiable. I suspect the strict common-ground condition and the requirement to make sense of informative accommodation are exactly such a pair: tell me whether the conflict is genuine, what a theory must surrender if it is, or on what reading both can be kept. If you are willing, close with a brief scorecard for the common-ground and scorekeeping pictures against your list—but keep the weight on the desiderata and their motivating cases, not on the scorecard.
