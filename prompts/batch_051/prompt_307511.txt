Someone in my lab keeps arguing that HCH — iterated amplification, where a hard question gets recursively decomposed into subquestions that a human can answer in a few minutes, with other humans checking and putting the pieces together — isn't just an alignment trick but a good idealization of what individual human deliberation already is. On that view, a person thinking hard for ten hours is, at the limit, doing nothing fancier than a big tree of little fifteen-minute human computations, each needing only a small slice of context; everything else is glue and bandwidth. This is basically the factored-cognition assumption behind HCH. I find it suspicious, because sustained deliberation seems to do something the pieces don't: you hold two distant considerations at once and spot that they're in tension, or you get a global feel that a whole line of thought is lopsided, and so on. I don't want to keep arguing from introspection; I want to actually test wether HCH is a good idealization of individual deliberation. Give me the best experimental design you can for testing that claim, say which results would favour each side, and be honest about what the test could never settle even in principle — e.g., is 'some decomposition that preserves everything' even testable with a finite experiment? Also tell me where your reasoning is shakiest and what result or consideration would change your mind about how much the test can show.
