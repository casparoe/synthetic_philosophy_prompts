A political theory textbook I've been reading compresses the negative/positive/republican liberty debate into one paragraph, and I can't tell whether it's a fair compression or a subtle distortion. It says:

"Isaiah Berlin distinguished negative liberty from positive liberty. Negative liberty is the absence of interference by other people: I am free to the extent that no one stops me from doing what I have the capacity to do. Positive liberty is the freedom to be one's own master, to have one's higher, rational self govern one's lower impulses. Berlin held that positive liberty is the dangerous one, because once you posit a 'higher' self that knows your true interests, rulers can tell the ruled that they are 'really' free when they are being coerced, and this leads in a straight line to totalitarianism. Republican liberty, later defended by Philip Pettit, is essentially negative liberty with one qualification: interference is objectionable only when it is arbitrary. But 'arbitrary' just means 'without a good reason,' and since almost any state interference can be given a good reason, the qualification excludes almost nothing, so republican liberty does no work that negative liberty doesn't already do."

I'm inclined to think the last two sentences are unfair, but I can't pin down exactly why, and I also suspect the opening gloss on negative liberty is smuggling something in. I'd like an accuracy audit: go through the paragraph claim by claim and say which parts a careful defender of Berlin would accept, which parts a careful Pettit-style republican would reject and why, and whether the final claim—that republican liberty collapses into negative liberty—follows from the corrected account of the views. If it doesn't, what is the actual point at issue between a republican and a negative libertarian?
