A friend and I were fooling around with the doomsday argument, and he built a version that I can't shake, but I also can't bring myself to accept where it lands. Maybe you can find the exit. As he gave it to me:

(1) I should reason as if my birth rank is a random draw from the class of all humans who will ever live: conditional on there being N humans total, my rank is equally likely to be any whole number from 1 to N.

(2) I am roughly the 100-billionth human to have been born.

(3) If the total number of humans who ever live is enormous — say, 200 trillion — then being born at rank 100 billion puts me in the first 0.05% of all humans; that is an extremely early position. If the total is instead around 200 billion, my position is utterly ordinary. The enormous-total hypothesis makes my observed rank far less likely than the moderate-total hypothesis does.

(4) So my observation of my own rank is very strong evidence for a moderate total rather than an enormous one. Given how many people are alive now, that means strong evidence that the species is over relatively soon — not tomorrow, but not millions of years.

(5) The trouble is that the identical move works with any class I choose. If I treat myself as a random member of all conscious beings, or all mammals, or all English sentences ever produced, or all years of my own life, I get different predicted endpoints. The lifespan version says I should expect to die much sooner than the actuarial tables suggest, which seems like a sign somethings off.

(6) Still, for the human case his conclusion seems to follow: unless I had some independent prior that the total would be enormous, I should update hard toward "not that many more of us."

I don't want to reject a statistical argument merely because the conclusion is grim, but I also don't want to accept a method that hands me a different doomsday depending on how I slice my reference class. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If your answer is "none of them, the argument goes through," then I want to know how to live with the fact that the same reasoning gives conflicting predictions when I switch classes. And in either case: separate what is actually settled here from what is genuinely contested among people who know the anthropics literature, and don't present a contested position as the consensus.
