I have long been drawn to the forward-looking account of practical rationality that underwrites the standard verdict on sunk costs: an agent’s reasons are exhausted by the future consequences of her options, and a cost already incurred and unrecoverable is therefore no longer a cost at all. The view has the virtues of simplicity and unification, and it yields an unambiguous instruction in a wide range of cases — abandon the failed project, leave the relationship that has run its course, ignore the money already spent. And yet I cannot shake a firm intuition that this is not the whole story. There are cases in which continuing a course of action partly because one has already given years to it seems less like a mistake than like keeping faith with one’s earlier self — letting the past count as one’s own rather than treating one’s former self as a stranger whose errors may be written off at will. I do not mean the familiar qualifications about reputation, switching costs, or the information that persistence reveals. I mean the felt normative pull of the investment itself. Should that pull be debunked as loss aversion, a framing effect, or a side effect of the need to tell a coherent story about oneself? Or does it track something real — the diachronic unity of the agent, the value of integrity, or the way commitments constitute a self — that the forward-looking theory is forced to leave out? I am not asking for a simple choice. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that gives way, what it would take for the theory to be amended or abandoned to accommodate it, and whether there is a reading on which both can be preserved.
