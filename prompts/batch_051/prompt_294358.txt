I have been trying to settle a cluster of questions about arbitrary choices in mathematics — specifically, whether it matters how you label or pick out objects when a structure has symmetries that swap them, and when a choice is merely conventional rather than canonical. I have firm verdicts about five cases, but I suspect they do not add up to a single clean principle, and I would like you either to find the principle that captures them or to tell me which verdicts to abandon.

Some terminology. An automorphism of a structure is a permutation of its underlying set that preserves all of the structure's operations and relations. If some automorphism maps one element to another, I will say the two elements are structurally indistinguishable, because the structure's own operations and relations cannot tell them apart.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

Case 1 (the two square roots of -1). Let C be the complex numbers, specified only as the field obtained from the real numbers by adjoining a root of x² + 1; call that root i. Complex conjugation is an automorphism of C over the reals, and it swaps the two roots while preserving every equation. My verdicts are: (a) given only that specification, there is no fact of the matter about which of the two roots is really i; but (b) once we work in the standard complex field with i chosen, the sentence "i ≠ -i" is determinately true, and no one who asserts it is making an error. I want to keep both (a) and (b), though they can look inconsistent.

Case 2 (a cubic with a distinguished root). The polynomial x³ - 2 over the rationals has exactly one real root and two non-real roots, the latter swapped by complex conjugation. My verdict: the description "the real root of x³ - 2" picks out a root canonically and involves no arbitrary labeling, whereas the description "the non-real root of x³ - 2" does not, because there are two.

Case 3 (a bare two-element set). Let S = {a, b} with no further structure. The map swapping a and b is an automorphism. My verdict: there is no fact of the matter about which element is a and which is b, beyond the fact that they are two and distinct; the names a and b carry no structural information. I am entirely comfortable saying that S has exactly two elements, but I deny that either element has an intrinsic identity making it "the" first one.

Case 4 (roles pinned down by structure). In the field of rational numbers (that is, the usual arithmetic of fractions), 0 is the unique additive identity and 1 is the unique multiplicative identity; every automorphism of the field fixes both. My verdict: which element is 0 and which is 1 is a genuine, canonical fact, not a conventional choice. So structures sometimes do single elements out.

Case 5 (meaningfulness up to isomorphism). A standard algebraic construction says: choose one root of x² + 1, call it i, and build the complex numbers. The theorems proved afterward are understood to hold regardless of which root was chosen. My verdict: such theorems have determinate truth-values, and the dependence on an uncanonical choice is harmless. But this is exactly what I find hard to reconcile: I deny that "which root is i" has an answer when C is specified as in Case 1, yet I affirm that statements of the form "the number system obtained by choosing a root and calling it i has property P" are unproblematically meaningful.

What I would like from you is a principle. Please state, as precisely as you can, the best principle or small set of principles that captures as many of my five verdicts as possible, indicating for each clause of the principle which case motivates it. Then tell me whether a simpler principle is available that keeps most of the verdicts at the cost of overturning one of them; if so, state that simpler principle, identify the verdict it overturns, and say whether I should pay that price. Then apply your favored principle to a case I have not listed: the quaternions H over the reals contain a continuum of elements q satisfying q² = -1 (every unit imaginary quaternion), and some R-algebra automorphism of H carries any such q to any other. Is the statement "there are many square roots of -1 in H" well-defined, and does "the imaginary unit j" pick out any canonical object? Finally, be explicit about which of my verdicts, if any, your principle cannot accommodate, and whether the fault lies with the verdict or with the principle.
