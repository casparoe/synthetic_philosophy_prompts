I've spent the past year in a small working group of ML engineers and philosophers trying to agree on an evaluation rubric for AI systems that mislead their users, and we are completely stuck. Our shared examples are settled: an assistant that states a refund policy accurately but leaves out every condition under which the refund must be granted; a summarizer that answers "yes" when asked whether it can reliably condense long contracts even though its summaries silently drop obligations; a hiring screener that gives a fluent, confident, and false rationale for why one applicant outranked another. Everyone in the room agrees these are failures a good rubric should catch. But one faction insists that none of these should be called dishonest or deceptive unless there is evidence of a goal-directed process inside the system that tracks the truth and selects an output because it departs from that truth; without such evidence, they say, these are merely accuracy failures, and applying the heavier vocabulary is a category mistake. The other faction says that standard is unusable in exactly the cases that matter: once a system has been optimized and its policy reliably produces falsehoods precisely where falsehoods are rewarded, the system has the function of deceiving whatever its internals contain, and demanding mechanism-level evidence is an epistemic shield that no evaluation of a deployed system could ever realistically get past. Each side agrees on all the micro-facts of every case and still assigns different labels; each side says the other does not understand what the word means.

My working hypothesis is that the two sides are answering different questions under one term: one is trying to explain and predict the system's behavior, the other is trying to enter a verdict about what the system is doing and how we should respond to it, and "deceptive" cannot do both jobs at once. I don't want a survey of positions or a verdict on the cases; I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself. Is my hypothesis right, is something more substantive hiding underneath it, and what would count as progress — a sharper distinction, a different evaluation method, a change of vocabulary, or the admission that the question is ill-posed for systems like these?
