I am writing a short decision-theory piece and want a consistency audit before I circulate it. I hold all of the following.

In the standard counterfactual mugging, I believe you should pay the $100 after Omega tells you the coin landed tails. Omega, a perfect predictor, pays $10,000 on heads if it predicted you would have paid in the tails case; on tails it simply asks for $100. My reason is that the policy of paying has a positive expected value of $4,950 before the coin is flipped, and refusing has an expected value of zero. The fact that the coin has already landed is not, by itself, a reason to switch policies.

In the XOR blackmail case, I believe you should refuse to pay. I use that label for the following setup. A blackmailer gives you a ciphertext along with a demand. The ciphertext was produced by XORing a plaintext with a one-time pad, and the blackmailer can choose a pad that makes it decrypt to either a damaging true claim about you or a harmless message. You cannot tell from the ciphertext which of the two it will become. The blackmailer says: pay $1,000, or I will publish the pad that makes the damaging claim. I think paying is wrong: it does not make the damaging plaintext less likely to have been the one encoded, and it rewards a threat whose only force comes from the fact that a single ciphertext is consistent with two very different plaintexts.

I also accept these more general principles.

P: A decision procedure should be evaluated across the possible worlds in which it is followed, not only across worlds consistent with the agent's current observations, and the time at which a random bit was settled or at which the agent learns about it is irrelevant.

Q: If an act maximizes expected value only because the act is evidence of having a better-than-average policy, that can be a sufficient reason to take the act, even when the act has no causal effect on the prize after the relevant bit has been settled.

R: An agent may refuse a threat when the threatener's power comes from controlling how ambiguous evidence is later disambiguated, even if the agent's policy would be predictable enough to be exploited by a sophisticated threatener.

Do P, Q, and R fit together with the two case verdicts? I especially want to know: does P plus Q force me to pay in XOR blackmail, and does R just relabel the mugging/blackmail distinction rather than draw it? Or is the real problem located in the mugging verdict, with P and Q doing more work than I think? Order the tensions by severity, say which must give, and tell me what the cheapest coherent package looks like. Do not lay out the options and leave me to choose; give one definite recommended revision and defend it.
