I am a high school student preparing background research and a position paper for a Model United Nations crisis simulation on extended nuclear deterrence, and I have arrived at a distinction I would like checked before I build a paper around it, since I may be flattering myself. I have been calling it curated unpredictability. The standard framework says a deterrent threat must be credible: the adversary has to believe you would carry it out. Perfect credibility, however, seems unavailable for catastrophic threats, because no one would install a doomsday machine that automatically destroys their own country, and a pure bluff is testable and therefore unstable. My proposal is that successful nuclear brinkmanship often aims at neither ordinary credibility nor bluffing, but at making one's decision process deliberately unmodelable. On this view, a state wants its adversary to believe that any of several agents might be at the controls at the moment of decision — the cold cost-benefit calculator, the honor-bound nationalist, the leader boxed in by domestic politics — so that no matter how carefully the adversary models the state, it cannot assign a probability below some non-trivial floor to catastrophic retaliation. The state does not commit to a particular response; it commits to being the kind of agent whose response cannot be read in advance.

The historical record seems to gesture at this without naming it exactly: the madman theory, Schelling's idea of deliberately leaving something to chance, opaque command-and-control arrangements, contradictory red lines, and the cultivated worry that launch authority might be delegated or automated in ways outsiders cannot verify. Yet I think my mechanism is distinct. The madman theory asks the adversary to believe one is irrational; curated unpredictability asks only that the adversary be unable to decide which rational or irrational type one is. And Schelling, as I understand him, locates the residual chance in the world — a process slipping out of control, an accident, a rogue commander — whereas I locate it in the adversary's inability to model the decision procedure itself.

My question is whether that distinction survives scrutiny. Does curated unpredictability collapse into a bluff, into reputation, into standard game-theoretic uncertainty about one's type, or into Schelling's account with the stochastic element merely relocated? Where does it break in a prolonged crisis, when an adversary can observe behavior and update its model? Are any of the strongest objections fatal rather than merely uncomfortable? And have I reinvented an existing idea under a term I have not yet encountered? I would rather know that now than commit my paper to a distinction that dissolves on contact.
