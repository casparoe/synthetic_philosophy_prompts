I'm taking a philosophy of sport course right now and we've spent the last two weeks trying to pin down what makes something a sport. I've cobbled together the following criteria from the readings and seminar arguments: (1) it's rule-governed in a way where you can cheat, not merely do badly; (2) there's institutionalized competition — tournaments, rankings, records; (3) outcomes are settled by skilled performance that two competitors can be directly compared on, rather than by a judge's partly literary taste; (4) the relevant skill can be trained and visibly degrades under pressure; (5) the body matters — not necessarily raw athleticism, but the activity can't be one where the body is just a meat delivery system for the mind; and (6) a spectator can watch and recognize excellence without being told who won. My flatmate and I have been arguing about whether competitive debate, like varsity Lincoln-Douglas or policy debate, qualifies as a sport. I know the obvious problem is criterion (5), but I honestly can't tell wether that criterion is doing real work or is just a prejudice left over from thinking sport means football; chess and esports hover in the same area and people seem fine calling them sports in some contexts.

Could you go through the six criteria one by one and tell me whether competitive argumentation meets each, and then actually commit to a yes or no on "is it a sport?" Don't just lay out the options and tell me it depends on my definition — I want you to take the definite position you think is right and defend it. Where a criterion underdetermines the verdict, say so, and say whether you think the case shows my criteria need changing. And if you land on yes: would treating argumentation as a sport improve the reasoning people actually do, or would it push them toward optimizing for judges and speed instead of tracking truth?
