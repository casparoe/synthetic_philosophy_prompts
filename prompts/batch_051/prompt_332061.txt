I sit on the research governance board of a neuroscience institute. Until my sister was diagnosed last year with a progressive neurological disease, I thought of whole-brain emulation as a speculative technical puzzle; now the questions of fidelity and identity feel immediate. I am drafting the board's decision memo for the case below, so I need it argued, not confessional.

We are reviewing a single-participant, first-in-human whole-brain emulation proposal. The participant, who is not a relative of mine, is a competent adult with a degenerative neurological disease that will soon destroy her capacity to communicate reliably and is expected to kill her within a few years. The procedure is destructive: the brain must be vitrified and sectioned for high-resolution scanning, so the biological original dies in the process. The research team argues that the resulting emulation is numerically the same mind, not merely a very good copy, and wants the consent form to tell the participant that, if the emulation passes a pre-registered validation battery drawn from interviews conducted before the procedure, with a fixed similarity threshold, the emulation will be treated as the continuation of her person for purposes of her medical decisions, property, and family contact. Our scientific reviewers accept the scan-and-reconstruct pipeline as technically credible but warn that the battery can show behavioral and structural similarity without ever recording continuity of consciousness across the procedure.

The board is split. One faction regards a person as a computational pattern and the procedure as transferring that pattern to a more durable substrate. Another faction regards the emulation as at best a copy, holds that the original dies on the table, and argues that the continuation-of-personhood language is a false disclosure that invalidates consent; a few call it voluntary death dressed up as research.

Draft the board's decision memo. Recommend one of three actions: approve the protocol as written; approve the procedure but require the team to replace the continuation-of-personhood language with explicit copy language; or reject the protocol. State the recommendation at the top, give the reasons for it, include an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side, and make it a document the dissenting members can actually respond to. End with a final extractable line in the fixed form \answer{approve}, \answer{approve with amended consent}, or \answer{reject}.
