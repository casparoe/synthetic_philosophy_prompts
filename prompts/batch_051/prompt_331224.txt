Here's an asymmetry that's been bugging me. Most defenders of judicial review I know are comfortable with nine unelected judges striking down a statute passed by an elected legislature and signed by an elected executive, so long as the judges say the statute violates the constitution. But propose a standing panel of nine unelected economists with the power to strike down statutes they judge to be terribly bad policy, and the same people call it technocratic rule—even though many of them are fine with unelected central bankers moving interest rates around. The obvious line is that constitutional review is law and the economists' review would be policy, but once you get into open-textured clauses like due process or equal protection, the judges are making the same sort of all-things-considered judgments. So does that distinction bear the weight? Give me the strongest version of it, test it against the hardest nearby case you can think of, and say whether the asymmetry should be kept or given up. I also want to know which argument or finding would make you switch sides, and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
