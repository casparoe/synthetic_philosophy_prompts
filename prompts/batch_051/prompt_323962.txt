Question: In his 1949 essay A Remark About the Relationship Between Relativity Theory and Idealistic Philosophy, Kurt Gödel argues that a rotating solution of Einstein's field equations, if taken seriously as a model of the universe, has consequences for whether time passes objectively. Reconstruct the argument, being precise about the feature of the solution that does the work. Then assess what would follow if our own universe were such a rotating universe, and what would follow merely from the fact that Einstein's theory permits such solutions.

Student answer: The Gödel universe is an exact solution to the field equations with a cosmological constant and homogeneous dust, and it is rotating around every point. Its rotation tilts the null cones: far enough from the centre, the forward light cone at an event bends so much that it includes events that would otherwise be in the global past, and this allows null geodesics to loop back on themselves, giving closed timelike curves. Because of these closed timelike curves, an event can be in its own causal past and its own causal future, so there is no globally consistent way to divide spacetime into past, present, and future. Gödel takes this to show that an objective lapse of time—something more than the B-series relations between events—cannot exist in such a universe. Since the rotating universe is a valid solution of the same field equations that general relativity uses for our universe, he also concludes that general relativity itself rules out objective temporal passage in every relativistic world, including ours. The argument would apply to our actual universe only if it turned out that our universe contains closed timelike curves, which would depend on its overall rotation and matter content.
