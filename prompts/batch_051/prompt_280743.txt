For the past few years I have worked on a donor-funded governance programme and kept running into the same puzzle in different countries, and I would like help naming and carving the concept behind it.

In one country, a provincial politician pays a constituent's hospital bill, gives out envelopes of cash at funerals, and finds a nephew a desk job. External monitors code these as vote-buying, nepotism, and conflict of interest. Many local people describe the same acts as generosity and loyalty: the politician is a person who does not forget his people, and someone who refused the funeral envelope would be seen as cold, proud, and untrustworthy. In a second country, a minister resigns and immediately joins a firm she regulated; a candidate's campaign is funded by businesses that later receive favourable treatment; a legislator accepts a weekend at a donor's lodge. People argue endlessly about which of these are corruption and which are simply how business and politics are done. In a third, a civil servant accepts a modest New Year's gift from a company whose permit is pending; under the programme's rules that is corruption, while the official's neighbours find the rule bizarre and the refusal rude.

The pattern that troubles me: the line between corruption and legitimate exchange moves around. It seems to track local ideas about gift, reciprocity, kinship, and what a person owes to those who depend on him. I know this is somewhere near cultural relativism, but that debate is usually about whether corruption is wrong at all. I am after two narrower claims. First, that there is no single culture-independent boundary between corrupt and legitimate exchange, because the boundary is drawn differently by different local practices of gift, reciprocity, and obligation. Second, the stronger claim that the standard I am paid to promote — impartial rules, no personal ties, no gifts — is itself one local way of arranging public life, being exported as if it were neutral and universal.

In your answer, tell me: is there an established term for the first claim, for the second claim, or for the pair together? I half-know the terms clientelism, gift-exchange, and institutional corruption, but I cannot place how they relate. If the closest established concept only fits imperfectly, say exactly what it covers and where my pattern exceeds or falls short of it. If there is no established name, coin one, define it, and give boundary cases that let me tell whether a new situation falls inside or outside it. A bare label will not satisfy me; I need the carving.

After your prose answer, give me a structured summary as a table with exactly these columns: Candidate term; Discipline or literature; The phenomenon the term actually names; How well it fits the first or second claim; Where it diverges from my description; Verdict. In the Verdict column use one of: exact match, close but distinct, useful contrast, or not relevant. Include as many rows as you need to cover the plausible candidates.
