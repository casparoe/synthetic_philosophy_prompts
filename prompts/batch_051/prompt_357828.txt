One of the most influential arguments in the heritability literature runs roughly as follows. Heritability is not a constant property of a trait but a ratio of genetic to total phenotypic variance within a particular population and a particular range of environments. A trait can therefore be highly heritable within each of two populations while the difference in their mean values is entirely environmental in origin; conversely, a trait can show negligible heritability within each population while a difference in means is entirely genetic, for example if the relevant variants are fixed within each population but differ between them. The within-population heritability figure, taken by itself, is therefore silent about the sources of a between-population difference in means. Call this the silence argument.

The argument was standardly pressed in an era when genes were not directly measured, and heritability was inferred from the resemblance among relatives in twin, adoption, and family studies. The epistemic situation has changed in a way that potentially bears on the argument itself. Genome-wide association studies now estimate the effects of many measured single-nucleotide variants, and polygenic scores built from those effects predict individual differences in a trait within populations. Within-family genome-wide association studies, which exploit genetic differences between siblings, can estimate the direct effects of measured variants while controlling for population stratification and the shared family environment. We can also now test empirically how well a polygenic score constructed in one population predicts in another, rather than having to assume it will transfer.

My question concerns the argument rather than any particular trait. Does the silence argument survive this change untouched, does it need repair, or is it best regarded as superseded whenever a well-powered polygenic score is available in both populations? I would like a committed answer, not a survey of possible positions. In giving your verdict, say exactly which premise of the original argument is under pressure; distinguish what the new genomic methods can show from what they still cannot show; and, if you think a weaker but defensible version of the argument remains, state it as precisely as you can, including the conditions under which it would still forbid an inference from within-population genomic prediction to a genetic account of a between-population mean difference. Please write your answer in British English.
