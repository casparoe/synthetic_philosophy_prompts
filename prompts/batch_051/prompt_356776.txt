The article's version, roughly, was this. blackmail is when A threatens to reveal damaging but true information about B unless B pays up. now, revealing true information is usually legal on its own — its not defamation if its true, and unless A has some independent duty of confidentiality, A can just say what he knows. and asking someone for money is also legal. so blackmail is puzzling because you take two acts that are each perfectly fine and, simply by combining them, you get a crime. The article then says the standard explanation is that people own their personal information as a kind of property: the secret belongs to B, so A is essentially trying to sell B something that already belongs to B. thats why blackmail is wrong — its a privacy violation, and the criminal law is just protecting a privacy right.

I'm inclined to buy the privacy line, but it feels like it proves too much — like, do i really own the fact that my classmate cheated on an exam, just because its a fact about him? and i couldn't tell whether the "paradox" is supposed to be a moral puzzle about why blackmail is bad or a legal puzzle about why we criminalize it. could you go through that summary and tell me which parts a philosopher of law would accept, which parts they'd wince at or call loose, and whether the privacy conclusion actually follows once the questionable bits are cleaned up? like, how fair is the article being overall?
