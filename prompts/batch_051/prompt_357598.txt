I work in comparative political economy and am fluent in game-theoretic and mechanism-design vocabulary: equilibria and equilibrium selection, credible commitment, time inconsistency, principal-agent relationships, selectorates and winning coalitions, preference aggregation, information asymmetries, and repeated games. I keep encountering the phrase "performance legitimacy" in discussions of China and Singapore — the claim that a regime can be legitimate, or at least durably accepted, because it delivers growth, order, stability, and competent administration, in the absence of competitive elections or liberal consent. Restate that thesis in my vocabulary as faithfully as it can be stated, without smuggling normative premises in as though they were technical constraints. Then give me a residue report. Which parts of the performance-legitimacy claim translate cleanly? Which translate only with distortion, and which cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all? In particular, I want to know whether the normative notion of justification survives the translation or collapses into a descriptive claim about which regimes are stable under some payoff structure; whether "legitimacy" is best modeled as an equilibrium-selection device or focal point, or whether that merely names a different phenomenon; and whether "performance" can be represented as payoff-relevant outcomes or irreducibly presupposes a standard of evaluation. Treat the residue as the interesting part, and work the answers out conceptually rather than citing literature.
