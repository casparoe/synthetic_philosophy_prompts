I've been re-reading Milton Friedman's 1953 essay "The Methodology of Positive Economics" for a discussion group, and I keep getting stuck on how far the famous argument can be pushed. Friedman's position, as I understand it, is that the realism of a model's assumptions is largely irrelevant; what matters is whether the model yields sufficiently accurate predictions. He famously suggests that a hypothesis that firms maximize expected returns can be scientifically useful even if no actual firm performs the relevant calculations, because firms may behave *as if* they were solving the maximization problem, much as an expert billiard player might behave as if she were solving the underlying physics. I find this persuasive in the abstract but hard to apply to cases where the false assumptions are not just psychologically unrealistic but physically false.

Suppose I propose the following criteria for a scientific model worth taking seriously: (1) it makes predictions that are approximately correct over some nontrivial range of conditions; (2) its assumptions, even when known to be false, are chosen in a way that is transparent and motivated by tractability rather than by ad hoc curve-fitting; (3) its idealizations do not systematically hide the causal processes that would matter if the model were used outside its original domain; and (4) the model has some out-of-sample predictive success, not just a good fit to the data used to calibrate it.

Now consider a modern coupled climate model of the sort used in IPCC projections. Many of its subgrid processes—cloud formation, convection, aerosol-cloud interactions—are represented by parameterizations that every modeler knows are physically false at small scales; they are stand-ins that reproduce observed aggregate statistics. The model does remarkably well at reproducing historical temperature records, seasonal cycles, and broad precipitation patterns, and it makes out-of-sample predictions (e.g., about volcanic cooling or stratospheric water vapor) that are sometimes surprisingly good. But the parameterizations are often tuned to match observations, the assumptions are not physically realistic, and the model's performance in past regimes is an imperfect guide to future regimes where the parameterizations may extrapolate poorly.

You are an expert in philosophy of science, especially the methodology of idealized models in economics and climate science. Please work through my four criteria one by one and say whether this climate model qualifies as a good scientific model under each. Then take a stance on the deeper question: does this case vindicate Friedman's claim that predictive success is what matters, or does it reveal that the realism of assumptions still does important work—perhaps in bounding our confidence, guiding model improvement, or distinguishing explanation from prediction? Be explicit about where you are confident, where you are uncertain, and what additional information would change your verdict.
