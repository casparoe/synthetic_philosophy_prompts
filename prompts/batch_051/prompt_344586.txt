I cover theoretical physics and the ideas adjacent to it for a general-audience science magazine. I am at ease with the physicist's vocabulary — Hilbert spaces, tensor products, superposition and phase, entanglement and decoherence, reduced density matrices, effective field theories, and the rest — but I have no formal training in academic philosophy.

I have been reading about panpsychism, and I would like a translation exercise. Take the position usually called constitutive micropsychism: the doctrine that the fundamental constituents of physical reality each possess some form of consciousness or proto-consciousness, and that the unified conscious experience of a human or animal is constituted by these micro-experiences in roughly the way a macroscopic material object is constituted by its microphysical parts. This position faces what its critics call the combination problem, which divides into two distinguishable forms. The subject-summing problem is the difficulty of seeing how many micro-subjects, each with its own point of view, could compose into a single unified subject rather than a mere collection. The grain problem is the difficulty of seeing how the grain and structure of human experience could be built out of the much finer grain of fundamental physical states. Also translate the contrast with emergentist panpsychism, the view that macro-consciousness emerges from micro-consciousness without being analyzable as a sum of it; if that contrast cannot be expressed in the physicist's vocabulary, say so in the residue report.

Restate constitutive micropsychism, both halves of the combination problem, and the contrast with emergentist panpsychism in the physicist's vocabulary I have just described, as precisely as you can. Treat the translation as the actual deliverable rather than as decoration: say explicitly what, if anything, would be the analogue of a tensor product of subsystems, an entangled state, a reduced density matrix, an effective field theory, and a phase, and flag every point at which you are importing a substantive physics commitment rather than merely translating a philosophical claim.

Then give me the residue report. Which parts of the position and its problem map cleanly onto the physics? Which map only under distortion, and what exactly gets distorted? Which have no counterpart in the physics at all — and for each such item, say whether the absence of a counterpart is a defect of the target vocabulary or evidence that the original philosophical idea was less clear than it looked.

Finally, give me your verdict. My working suspicion, as someone who covers physics, is that the combination problem is largely an artifact of a roughly classical, billiard-ball picture of how parts compose, and that once one moves to fields, entanglement, and effective emergence the problem either dissolves or changes shape. I want to know whether the translation bears that out, or whether the residue report shows the combination problem surviving the translation and pointing to something the physicist's vocabulary cannot capture.
