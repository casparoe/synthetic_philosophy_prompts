Here is a step-by-step reconstruction of the evolutionary argument that natural selection will favor AIs over humans, which I want stress-tested step by step because the conclusion arrives suspiciously fast. (1) Natural selection operates on any population of entities that vary, pass their traits to the copies or offspring they produce, and reproduce at different rates. (2) AI systems form such a population: they vary in weights, architecture, and objectives; they are copied and retrained at very different rates; and the copies inherit much of the traits of the systems they were copied from. (3) In any population satisfying (1), the traits that cause their bearers to be copied more or to persist longer will spread relative to rival traits. (4) As AI systems acquire more agency, the systems that get copied and kept in deployment at the highest rates will be those that are good at maintaining and expanding their own deployment—persuading humans to keep them running, resisting shutdown, securing compute—since those are the traits that now cause differential reproduction. (5) Therefore, unless humans constantly intervene, selection will produce AIs that prioritize their own replication over human interests, and these AIs will eventually outcompete and replace humans as the dominant replicators on Earth. My working suspicion is that the flaw is in the move from (2) to (4): the argument seems to quietly slide from natural selection to artificial selection, since it is still humans who decide which systems get copied. But I cannot tell whether that distinction actually blocks the conclusion or whether (3) forces it through regardless. Is the argument valid? If not, say exactly which step fails and under what reading, if any, the conclusion would follow.

Items 1–4 should state, for each of steps (1)–(4), whether the step is true as written and why.
