I'm working through an introductory philosophy of mind unit on physicalism, and a short explanatory article I read summarizes Hempel's dilemma in a way I'm not sure I can trust. I'd like an accuracy audit of the article's summary—not a grade, but a sentence-by-sentence account of what to keep and what to push back on.

The article's summary runs as follows:

"Physicalism is the claim that everything is physical, but the real issue is what 'physical' means. If 'physical' means the kinds of things current physics talks about—particles, fields, forces, spacetime, and the like—then physicalism is almost certainly false, since physics has repeatedly revised and expanded its ontology and will probably keep doing so. If instead 'physical' means whatever some future, completed physics will end up positing, then physicalism is vacuous: on this reading it makes no definite claim about the world, and for all we know a completed physics could include irreducible conscious minds or other non-material ingredients, in which case physicalism would become compatible with what its critics wanted all along. Physicalism is therefore caught in a dilemma: it is either a risky empirical bet that today's physics is nearly finished, or an empty gesture toward a future science that might say anything. The only respectable way out is to define the physical negatively, as 'whatever is not fundamentally mental.' But that is a semantic maneuver, not a substantive discovery: it stipulates that minds cannot be fundamental instead of finding out what the world is made of, and it quietly gives up the content that physicalism was supposed to have."

I'm inclined to accept the first horn, but the move from the second horn to the charge of vacuity, and especially the article's treatment of the negative definition, feel too quick to me, though I can't articulate exactly why. (2) Is the thought that future physics might include irreducible minds a fair way to put the vacuity worry, or does it conflate vacuity with something else? (3) Does defining the physical negatively really amount to a semantic stipulation, or can it be a substantive empirical claim about the world? (4) Is the negative route really the only respectable response, or are there other live options—such as keeping the first horn while treating physicalism as a revisable empirical hypothesis, or defending a suitably constrained version of the second horn?
