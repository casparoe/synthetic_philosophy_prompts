Question: The pessimistic meta-induction says that because many successful scientific theories from the past turned out to be false, we should expect our current successful theories to be false too. Explain the argument, and then assess it: can a scientific realist accept the history it points to and still reject the conclusion? Give your own verdict.

Student answer:

The pessimistic meta-induction (PMI) starts from the history of science. Theories like the phlogiston theory of combustion, the caloric theory of heat, the aether theory of light, and Newtonian mechanics were all very successful in their day—they made good predictions and were trusted by scientists. Yet each was later overturned or replaced. So the argument runs: if you pick a successful theory from the past, it almost always turned out false; therefore, by induction, the successful theories we have now are likely false too.

Realists usually answer in two ways. First, they say "false" is too strong: those past theories were false only in their peripheral parts, while their central claims were approximately true and got carried over into the next theory. For example, the mathematics of Newtonian physics is still used today, so the core of Newtonian mechanics was true. Second, they say current theories are better than past ones because science has matured, so the old record isn't a fair sample.

I think the second reply is weak—every age thinks its own science has finally grown up—but the first reply is basically right. Since the parts that worked got preserved, the success of past theories isn't really a record of total failure. And even if the induction works, it only shows that our current theories will undergo some revision, not that they are completely false. So the PMI only establishes that science will keep changing, which a realist can happily accept. I conclude that the PMI gives little reason to doubt scientific realism.
