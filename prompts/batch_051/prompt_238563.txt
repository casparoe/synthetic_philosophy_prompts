I've been playing with a line about oaths — I've been calling it the guarantor view, for obvious reasons — and I want someone to poke holes in it before I teach it again. The claim is that an oath isn't just a promise plus solemnity; what makes it an oath is that the swearer invokes something outside their own will as a guarantor, something that would in principle enforce the oath even against the swearer. Swearing by the Styx is the pure case: even the gods need a power beyond themselves to swear by, and a false Styx-oath carries a penalty they can't laugh off. Modern courtroom oaths and inaugural oaths kept the raised hand but dropped the guarantor, which on my view makes them half-dead oaths, and that's supposedly why they feel so thin. Promises bind through my own constancy; oaths bind by offloading enforcement to something outside me — even if, nowadays, everyone knows that something isnt there. The obvious worry is that this makes an oath a kind of theatre, so if I know the guarantor won't act I shouldn't feel any more bound than by a promise; but people do feel more bound, don't they? Tell me whether this is already a named view, and give me the strongest counterexample you can think of.
