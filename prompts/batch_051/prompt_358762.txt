I have no background in linguistics or philosophy, but I've been thinking on my own about the parts of speech — noun, verb, adjective, preposition, and the rest — and about the claim people sometimes make that they are natural kinds: real joints in language that grammarians discover rather than invent. I've put together an argument against that claim, but I half-suspect one of the steps is doing more work than it should, and I can't locate the fault.

Here is the argument.

(1) A category is a real feature of language itself only if there is some property shared by all and only its members — a common nature in virtue of which they form a single kind.

(2) For the familiar parts of speech, no such property exists. Meaning won't supply it: nouns routinely name things that are not objects (destruction, arrival), and verbs can be used for states rather than actions (to know, to resemble). Word shape won't supply it: some languages mark nouns for number or case and others mark nothing of the sort, and the same is true of verbal marking, so the tests are facts about individual languages, not about the categories themselves. Word behavior won't supply it either: what nouns and verbs can do — take articles, stand as subjects, form predicates, combine with negation — differs enough from language to language that any behavioral test also ends up language-bound.

(3) If no property picks out a part of speech across languages, then the parts of speech are not real features of language itself.

(4) Therefore the parts of speech are not natural kinds; they are labels that the grammatical tradition imposes on languages that really differ.

I am particularly suspicious of (1) and (3) — the demand that a category be identifiable by the same test in every language feels too strong, and yet a real kind should surely show up in more than one place.
