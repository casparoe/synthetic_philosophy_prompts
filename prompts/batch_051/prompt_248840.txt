Take Nozick's experience machine, but change one thing. It is stipulated that the space of possible experiences is finite: there are exactly N elementary experiences, every experience anyone can have is a finite sequence of at most L of these elements, and any difference below the elementary level makes no experiential difference. You are shown the entire catalog in advance and have perfect memory across sessions, so in finitely many sessions you could have had every possible experience at least once; otherwise the machine works as in the original, with any experience in the catalog available and your memory of having plugged in erased. Work through each of Nozick's three standard reasons for refusing the machine — wanting to do things rather than merely have the experience of doing them, wanting to be a certain kind of person, wanting contact with a deeper reality — and say whether each gets stronger, weaker, or is unchanged once experience is known to be exhaustible. Does the known finitude of the catalog make plugging in more or less attractive than the original? End with a verdict on this mixed case: the machine lets you exhaust the entire catalog and then return to ordinary life with your memories of the catalog intact; should a Nozickian accept, and why? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
