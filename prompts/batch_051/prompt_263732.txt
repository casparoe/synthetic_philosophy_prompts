Last term, in a seminar on the ethics of cultural preservation, a classmate presented an argument he had devised to test a common intuition about language death. He has since come to distrust the argument, because it seems to force him into a conclusion he refuses to accept. He laid it out as follows.

Suppose the last competent speaker of a language dies, and no grammar, dictionary, writing, or recording of it exists. It is natural to think that something of value has gone out of the world at that moment — not merely that a person has died, and not merely in the way that a tool is discarded once its user no longer needs it. Starting from that intuition, he reasoned:

(1) The value lost in this case is not instrumental value for the speaker, who no longer exists, nor instrumental value for anyone else, since by stipulation no one else has any knowledge of, use for, or interest in the language.

(2) Therefore, the value lost must be intrinsic: the language itself is valuable independently of any valuer's needs, desires, or projects.

(3) If the language is intrinsically valuable, then whatever grounds that value cannot be its usefulness to its community (that would be instrumental) or its having been cherished (that would be relational). It must be something the language possesses in its own right — most plausibly, its being a complete and distinct way of structuring experience and carving up the world.

(4) But that property is had by every language. A small speech variety that diverged from its neighbor two generations ago and differs only in a few hundred words is no less a complete and distinct way of structuring experience. So every language — however recently formed, however few its speakers, however close its nearest relative — is intrinsically valuable.

(5) If every language is intrinsically valuable, then the impending disappearance of any language gives us at least a pro tanto reason to act to preserve it: through documentation, through transmission programs, through support for its remaining speakers.

(6) That consequence looks overstrong. By the argument's own lights, a three-speaker variety differing from a thriving neighbor only in minor lexical preferences would merit the same kind of preservation effort as the last surviving member of an entire language family, or a language whose oral tradition encodes centuries of unique ecological and cosmological knowledge. And the reasoning does not stop at languages. If being a distinct way of carrying on a human practice confers intrinsic value, the same would have to hold for folkways, cuisines, musical styles, and rituals — including practices that few of us think we have any reason to preserve at all.

(7) So the original intuition must be given up. If we refuse to hold that every fragile dialect and every inherited custom carries preservation value merely in virtue of being a distinct practice, we cannot claim that anything of value is lost when the last speaker dies; the apparent loss is only a sentiment we project onto the world.

He said he finds (7) untenable and is convinced the argument fails somewhere, but he cannot identify where without surrendering something he also wants to keep. For my part, I would most like to hold on to (1), and I am sympathetic to (2); beyond that I have no settled view. Could you examine the argument step by step and diagnose where it goes wrong, if it does? If it does not go wrong, then which premise or transition should be rejected, and what is the cost of rejecting it? In either case, what should someone who wants to keep (1) say in response?
