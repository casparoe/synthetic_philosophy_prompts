I am a novelist writing a book in which a government formally apologizes for harms inflicted generations before any of its living officials or citizens were born. In the public debates I have been reading, the entire argument runs through a single master analogy: an official apology is like one person apologizing to another. The defenders use it to set standards. An individual apology, they say, is hollow if it fails to name the wrong specifically, express remorse rather than formulaic regret, or carry concrete reparation; therefore a state's apology is hollow unless it does the same. The critics use the identical analogy to reach the opposite verdict. A personal apology is sincere only if the one who did the wrong utters it and feels what it expresses; a state has no memory of the dead, no remorse it experiences, and no wrongdoer who persists from then to now. On the analogy's own terms, they conclude, the state's apology is counterfeit — a fiction performing as a moral act.

I want an audit of how far this analogy actually holds, because I fear my novel has been quietly taking it for granted.

Please set out the correspondence in full: what is supposed to correspond to the wrongdoer, the injured party, the utterance of remorse, the inward feeling, the acceptance or forgiveness, the restitution, and the promise not to repeat. Then go through the points where the mapping breaks — the continuity of the agent across time, the absence of an inward mental state, the question of who is entitled to accept or forgive decades later, the fact that present citizens did not act but may nonetheless benefit. I want you to separate the disanalogies that genuinely bear on the two competing conclusions from those that are only superficial. Once they are separated, how much of each side's conclusion survives, or does the analogy carry both arguments further than it can support? If the analogy is beyond repair, say what a better one would be: what familiar act is a state actually performing when it apologizes — something done by a firm inheriting a predecessor's liability, by a family reckoning with its legacy, by a court memorializing a judgment, or something else?
