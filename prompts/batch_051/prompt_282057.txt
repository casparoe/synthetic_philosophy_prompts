I keep running into a decision-theoretic puzzle that I cannot find a stable name for, and I would welcome help identifying it and, more importantly, marking out its boundaries. The setup runs as follows. You must choose one of two places, say a city in the north or a city in the south. A highly reliable predictor, sometimes presented as infallible in the versions I have heard, has already predicted where you will go and has arranged to be waiting there, where something you badly want to avoid will occur. The two places are otherwise perfectly symmetrical, so neither has any independent advantage. The difficulty is that the deliberation seems to loop: if I resolve to go north, then the predictor is very likely north, which counts as a reason to go south; but if I resolve to go south, the predictor is very likely south, which counts as a reason to go north. There is no settled state of the world, analogous to the contents of a box, that my choice merely needs to match; the object of the prediction is the very act I am about to perform, and reflecting on that prediction makes each option look like the one I would regret.

I can see that this is near Newcomb's problem, but it also seems structurally different: there are no two options like one-boxing and two-boxing, and no option that dominates the other, because the locations differ only through the predictor's presence. I am not primarily after a bare label, though I would like to know it if one exists. What I want is a clear account of whether this scenario is best treated as a special case of Newcomb's problem, as a distinct problem because what is predicted is the act itself, or as something closer to fatalism with a decision-theoretic twist. Does the scenario have an established name, and how should one draw the boundary between it and the neighboring puzzles — for instance, how much depends on the predictor's being infallible rather than merely reliable, and what changes if one location becomes slightly more attractive?
