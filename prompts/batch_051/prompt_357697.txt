I am the parliamentarian of my high school's student government, a fifteen-member body that meets biweekly for about ninety minutes. We make decisions with modest but real stakes: we allocate a few thousand dollars from the activity fund, approve club event proposals, and send recommendations to the principal, who requires a one-paragraph written justification for anything we pass. My job is to keep our decisions procedurally defensible, and I keep noticing a failure our current rules do not handle.

Many of our motions have logical structure. At a recent meeting we considered a proposal to approve the spring carnival. It was presented alongside two supporting questions: (a) whether the carnival could be funded without touching the tutoring budget, and (b) whether any event that can be funded without touching the tutoring budget should be approved. Each supporting question passed on its own. Then the proposal itself failed. The individual members all had consistent positions; what was inconsistent was the council's aggregate judgment, because the majorities on the supporting questions logically required the conclusion that the majority then rejected. The chair ruled the motion failed by one rule, and I am fairly sure another equally defensible rule would have made it pass.

I have since read a little about what is called the discursive dilemma, and the related doctrinal paradox, and I understand the broad contrast between premise-based and conclusion-based majority voting. What I need is not another explanation of the phenomenon. I need a reusable, standing decision procedure, grounded in what judgment aggregation actually shows, that translates the theory into rules a room of non-specialist students can follow in a meeting.

The procedure must do the following:

1. Give the chair a plain-language script for deciding when a motion should be voted on as a single question and when it should be decomposed into premises, conditions, or sub-questions.
2. Provide a default rule for when premise-based aggregation should govern and when conclusion-based aggregation should govern, with criteria a future parliamentarian could defend rather than a mere preference.
3. Detect when the two procedures diverge — when the majorities on the premises logically require a conclusion the majority on the conclusion rejects — and specify what the council should then do: ratify the derived conclusion, reopen the premises, record a split decision, or something else.
4. Handle the cases that will actually arise: a premise that is itself compound, abstentions, amendments from the floor, and members who vote strategically against a premise they believe in order to block the overall proposal.
5. Mark, honestly, the places where judgment cannot be eliminated. In particular, it seems to me that whoever chooses how a motion is decomposed into premises has real influence over the outcome, and I want the procedure to state that rather than hide it.

Please apply the procedure to at least three worked examples, written as sample council motions with individual vote patterns shown. At least one example should be a case where the procedure yields a result I will not like — I generally want the council to approve popular, well-run events — because that is the test of whether the procedure has real teeth. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: which failure modes remain, and where the chair will still have to make a judgment call under time pressure. The deliverable should be something I can put into our standing rules and hand to next year's parliamentarian.
