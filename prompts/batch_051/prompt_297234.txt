I'm a historical linguistics PhD student, and I've been running the Ship of Theseus on a language, and I can't decide wether the variation makes the original puzzle easier or just breaks it. Take the original ship to be the Latin of Cicero's Rome, and the fully rebuilt ship to be the language of Dante's Florence. Instead of planks, swap the grammatical and phonological features one at a time: vowel length is lost, the case system erodes, word order shifts, the lexicon turns over. So far, the usual puzzle. But my version has a twist: the replacements are perfectly continuous, with an unbroken chain of speakers in which every generation understands its parents and its children, and no feature changes in any particular year—yet Cicero and Dante could not have understood each other at all. Now add a written standard identical to the original, copied and taught the whole time as a literary language once it has no native speakers. Which of the standard answers to the Ship of Theseus (continuity of matter, continuity of form, functional continuity, no determinate fact) survives this version? When, if ever, does the spoken thing stop being Latin and start being Italian? And is the preserved written standard the original ship in the warehouse, a second ship, or just a very good copy?
