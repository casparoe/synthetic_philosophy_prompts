Most legal systems hold that if I defraud you and conceal it well, I can be prosecuted only within a fixed number of years after the crime; once that window closes, the case is barred even if a document later surfaces that makes my guilt undeniable. The same systems usually impose no such limit on murder, and they carve out exceptions that restart or extend the clock when the wrong was actively concealed, when the victim was a child, or when new evidence such as DNA appears decades later; there is also a civil/criminal split, where a claim can be too old to sue over but still prosecutable, or the reverse. I am less interested in the doctrinal mechanics than in the implicit philosophy. Reconstruct the theory of the moral relevance of time that this practice presupposes: does it commit the law to the view that wrongdoing or desert decays, that a wrongdoer eventually acquires a right to finality, that the state's standing to condemn fades, that punishment and private remedy lose their point after enough delay, or only that evidence goes stale? Distinguish what the rules strictly presuppose from what they are merely compatible with, and say whether the whole pattern — conclusive evidence ignored, murder treated as timeless, hidden or childhood wrongs given extended clocks — hangs together under any single theory, or whether it is underdetermined and ad hoc. Then tell me what would have to change in limitation law if the underlying view were replaced by the rival claim that time never changes what is owed and that what changes is only our ability to know it. Please answer in continuous prose only: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, and write it the way you would write a letter or a long email.
