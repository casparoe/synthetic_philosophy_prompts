I'm not a lawyer or a philosophy person, just someone who overthinks things. A friend of mine got a speeding ticket last fall, and while he was complaining about it I started noticing how strange the law is about words. On the one hand, it's full of fuzzy terms: you can be penalized for going too fast for the conditions, for using excessive force, for selling goods that are unreasonably dangerous, for posting something obscene. There's no official number for exactly how fast is too fast on a rainy night — in fact you can get a ticket for going the posted limit if the conditions are bad. But the same legal system loves razor-sharp cutoffs that don't seem to exist in the world: the drinking age is exactly 21 rather than old enough to handle alcohol, the voting age is exactly 18, and a contract changes its legal status the moment you sign. Around the same time I came across a puzzle people call the sorites paradox: one grain of sand isn't a heap, adding one grain never turns a non-heap into a heap, yet heaps obviously exist — same thing with losing one hair and going bald. That's when it clicked for me that the judge's fuzzy words and the legislature's sharp numbers might be two different answers to the same problem.

So here's what I want to know: what view of boundaries and meaning does this mixed practice actually presuppose? Does the law secretly assume that vague words like "reasonable" or "obscene" have real, exact boundaries that a judge is just supposed to find? Or does it assume meanings are open-ended, with the sharp cutoffs being the artificial part tacked on top? Or is the system just muddling through with two incompatible pictures because courts have to decide cases and legislators need rules people can administer? Reconstruct the implicit theory, say whether it's actually coherent, and tell me what would have to change in how laws are written if the whole system had to commit to one of the standard philosophical positions about this puzzle. And please write your answer in American English.
