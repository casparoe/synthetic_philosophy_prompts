I teach a graduate seminar in social epistemology, and this term we have been working through the mechanics and normative significance of reputation systems. For our session on karma economies I want to give my students a precise counterfactual and ask them to reason their way through it, and I would like you to model the kind of answer I should hold up as a target. Please take on the role of an expert in mechanism design and the epistemology of online communities — someone who can combine game-theoretic reasoning about incentives with a sociologist's eye for how norms drift under a scoring regime. Think carefully and avoid the familiar list of complaints about social media; I want mechanisms, not adjectives.

Here is the scenario. Imagine a pseudonymous, text-only forum of roughly the size and broad culture of a large early-2010s subreddit. On January 1 the platform abolishes its one-dimensional karma — a free, unlimited up-or-down vote on every post and comment — and institutes a closed karma economy. Each new account is seeded with 10 units. An upvote costs the voter 0.2 units, which are transferred in full to the author; a downvote costs the voter 0.2 units and destroys 0.2 units of the author's balance. Every vote is logged and publicly attributed to the voter's handle. Karma held by an account decays at 2% per week. Voting no longer mints karma; the only injections are the 10 units that accompany each new account and the 5 units an account receives after a zero-balance suspension. An account whose balance reaches zero loses the ability to post, comment, and vote for thirty days, after which it is restored with 5 units.

Predict how this community looks after ten years. In particular, address (a) which kinds of content and conversational norms flourish and which die, (b) who accumulates karma and whether accumulated karma tracks epistemic reliability, argumentative skill, social affability, or something else entirely, (c) which novel strategic behaviors become stable — consider vote-trading rings, extortionary downvoting, sock-puppet farming of seed capital, hoarding, and vote-splitting — and (d) whether the site becomes a more or less trustworthy place to learn what is true. For each substantive claim, mark whether you take it to be robust or merely plausible, and state the factual assumption on which it most depends: what would have to be true for the prediction to fail. Begin with a short brainstorm of the four or five causal mechanisms you expect to do the most work, and then build the forecast out of those mechanisms rather than by analogy to any single existing platform. Conclude with a brief judgment as to whether you think the reform would serve or defeat whatever epistemic or civic goals a designer might plausibly have had.
