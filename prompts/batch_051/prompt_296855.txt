I've noticed something in people (and in myself) that I dont have a word for. A friend of mine will not open the letter from the bank when he suspects hes overdrawn, even though opening it would let him move money around before the fees stack up. A relative refuses a free screening for a condition that runs in her family, even though it's treatable if caught early and she says she'd get treated. And I keep walking past the kebab place I like because I'm half sure it got a bad hygiene rating last year; I could check on my phone in ten seconds, for free, but I'd honestly rather not know.

The reason this bugs me is the standard decision-theory result—the one that says a free piece of information can never lower a rational agent's expected utility, because the worst case is that you ignore it and pick the same action you would have picked anyway, and if there's any chance it changes what you choose, it only changes it for the better. Someone who actively avoids the information, even pays a little to avoid it, looks like a textbook violation of that theorem.

So my question isnt really just what this pattern is called, though I'd like the name if there is one. What I want is the carve-up: is this a genuine counterexample to the theorem, or does the theorem get out of it by saying the information isnt actually free once the dread of knowing is counted as a cost? If its the cost move, is there a version of the pattern where that move fails and the theorem really is in trouble, or is it protected only because you can always re-describe the utility function? And where is the line between a rational reason to not want to know and the kind of motivated ignorance that a good decision theory should call a mistake?
