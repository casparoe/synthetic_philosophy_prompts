I am a novelist at work on a book set in a near-future city where a universal basic income has just been enacted, and one of my central characters is a historian of political economy who must defend the policy at a contentious public meeting. I want her to make the strongest possible version of an older argument without sounding like a textbook, so I have been reading Thomas Paine's Agrarian Justice closely. I am particularly struck by his paired claims that, in his words, the earth in its natural state was common property, and that what justifies private landed property is not ownership of the soil itself but ownership of the improvement made by cultivation; every proprietor of cultivated land therefore owes the community a ground-rent for the land, even though the improvement is genuinely his own. I do not plan to quote the passage in the novel, but I want to understand its conceptual structure well enough to let my character argue from it in her own voice.

Please describe the most important implications of that distinction—between the earth as common inheritance and improvements as individual property—for how a contemporary defender of a universal basic income should frame the proposal. I am especially interested in what the distinction implies about whether the policy should be justified as compensation for a lost common right, as a dividend from collectively inherited land and accumulated techniques, or simply as a poverty-relief measure; what it suggests the size of the grant ought to be tied to, if anything; and how it shapes the response to the objection that a basic income pays people for doing nothing.
