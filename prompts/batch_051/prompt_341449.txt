Right, here's the brief. I want you to write as an ethicist who works on risk and responsibility, and to think carefully rather than reaching for the usual deontology-utilitarianism script. First brainstorm four or five separate candidate answers to the question below, pick the strongest two or three, and then write the essay around those.

The essay question: Suppose I set off a shonky firework in a public park, and doing so imposes a tiny risk on a stranger — say a one-in-ten-million chance of a serious injury — without asking her. The firework goes off harmlessly. Is she wronged? Some say no: no one got hurt and the expected harm is vanishingly small. Others say yes: I took an unauthorised gamble with her person, and that is the wrong, not the injury. I'd like the essay to go beyond both pieces of boilerplate. In particular, engage with the idea that what I actually do is spend part of someone's risk budget — a claim on a scarce, shared capacity to be exposed to life's dangers without suffering — and that whether a tiny risk wrongs her may depend on how much of that budget has allready been used up by everyone else, i.e. the background level of risk she could not avoid anyway. Defend a verdict rather than sitting on the fence; a qualified verdict is allowed (for instance: wrong only when the benefit to me is too trivial to justify the draw, or wrong only against a high background risk, or not a wrong but something I must compensate if the harm eventuates). Argue for whichever verdict you can make strongest.

Use exactly these section headings and lengths:
- 'The question and what is at stake' (about 180 words)
- 'The case that no wrong is done' (about 320 words)
- 'The case that a wrong is done even in the absence of harm' (about 320 words)
- 'The risk-budget idea and why it moves things' (about 300 words)
- 'My verdict' (about 280 words)

No citations or reading list please — engage with positions and arguments by description, and dont invent sources.
