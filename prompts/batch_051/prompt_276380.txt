I teach a 300-level epistemology course, 16 undergrads, one 75-minute session. We're doing disagreement next week, and I want an in-class game that makes Aumann's agreement theorem feel like a discovery rather than a theorem I state at the board. The thing I want them to actually run into is this: two people with the same prior and honest Bayesian updating can have different private credences about the same proposition, but when those credences become public in the right way — each knows the other's number, each knows the other knows, and so on — the gap can't survive as a stable resting place: either it closes or somebody has stopped treating the other's number as evidence.

I don't want to lecture it first. I'd like them to be the two agents, in pairs, with different private draws from some shared random setup, posting credences about a proposition like "the hidden jar is Jar A." I'm imagining a coin flips to choose between two jars with a 70/30 color mix (Jar A has 7 red and 3 blue, Jar B has 3 red and 7 blue, something like that), and each player draws one ball and sees only their own. Then they write down their credence that it's Jar A, reveal it, update on the other person's number, reveal again, and keep going until something happens. I want most pairs to start at 70 and 30 and then have to sit with the fact that "70/30" can't just coexist once its public.

Rules, materials (ideally easy to prepare — cards or paper bags are fine), a payoff scheme that keeps them honest without real money, and a timeline for 75 minutes. The debrief should map the moments in the game onto the theorem's ingredients: common prior, private information, common knowledge of the posteriors, the conclusion. It should also be honest about what the game can't reproduce — I'm worried the common prior will feel like a rigged assumption, and in real disagreement its exactly what's missing. And tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it: I can already see some pairs treating the other person's number as a social signal or a skill level rather than as evidence, or digging in at their original number to avoid looking wishy-washy. I want to know how to handle that in the debrief without making them feel dumb.
