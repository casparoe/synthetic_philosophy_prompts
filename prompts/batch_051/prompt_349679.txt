I am taking a course on the philosophy and decision theory of interstellar communication, and I find myself torn about the dark forest response to the Fermi paradox. The theory deserves its appeal. It begins from a single, almost minimal assumption: because intentions are unobservable across light-years, a civilization that detects us cannot be sure we are not an eventual threat, and it knows we cannot be sure of it either. From this it derives a cascade of suspicion in which broadcast is a gamble with potentially existential stakes, concealment is the uniquely safe policy, and a single hostile actor is enough to make the silence the rational equilibrium. I also have a firm intuition that this cannot be the right final word. A universe of rational civilizations all hiding and striking first looks like a stable pattern of paranoia rather than a conclusion forced by the evidence; silence is predicted equally well by cautious indifference or benign disinterest; and the policy buys safety at the price of renouncing the entire epistemic and cultural value of contact. I do not want a verdict on which side wins. I want to know what would justify each of the three resolutions: debunking the intuition as anthropocentric and dropping it, amending or abandoning the dark forest theory, or finding a reading of rationality on which both the strategic logic and the intuition survive.
