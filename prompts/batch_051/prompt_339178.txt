I am preparing a short talk for a philosophy of language reading group, and in my background reading I came across the following passage in a popular-science book about artificial intelligence. The book is written by a competent science journalist, not a philosopher and not a computational linguist, which makes it hard for me to judge where the scientific reporting ends and the philosophical interpretation begins. The passage reads:

"When a modern language model learns words, it effectively learns their meanings in an indirect but surprisingly complete way. The system scans billions of sentences and adjusts each word's representation so that words which appear in the same sorts of contexts end up close together in a mathematical space of several hundred dimensions. The resulting map is not a mere summary of usage; it is a genuine geometry of meaning. The best-known proof is the equation king − man + woman = queen: the system has isolated the direction corresponding to 'maleness' and the direction corresponding to 'royalty', and can move along them as though using a slide rule. Because machines trained in this way reliably solve analogy problems of this form, they display something like a primitive grasp of the concepts involved. The philosophical lesson is that meanings are relational rather than definitional. To know what a word means just is to know where it sits in this trained space, and a change in a word's meaning simply is movement of its point through that space. Traditional theories that locate meaning inside the minds of individual speakers have been quietly overturned by this technology, which shows that meaning is fixed by the public regularities of usage."

I am broadly sympathetic to the idea that context-based learning captures something important about word meaning, but the closing moves strike me as too quick.
