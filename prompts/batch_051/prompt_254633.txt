I teach an upper-level undergrad seminar on decision theory; students have covered expected utility theory, the Newcomb problem, and causal vs evidential decision theory, but not the newer evidential cooperation in large worlds stuff. I need a take-home final-exam question from you, with a model answer and marking scheme, on that topic. Build it around a self-contained voting example so nobody needs the literature. Here's the gist: a million voters, a proposition passes if at least 500,001 vote yes; every voter wants it to pass; voting costs ten dollars; the chance any individual vote is pivotal is vanishingly small. Assume the voters are near-copies of each other in the relevant respects, so conditional on you voting, the probability that similar others vote is much higher than conditional on you abstaining. The question should make the students (i) write down the expected-value comparison for both causal and evidential decision theory, (ii) explain how the evidentialist can get a reason to vote without claiming one vote influences other votes, and (iii) evaluate whether that reason is good or whether it's just a fancy way of being fooled by correlation. The model answer should be crisp, and the marking scheme should give the 30 marks and name specific mistakes that cost marks — like confusing the evidential effect with pivotedness, or saying the evidentialist is "just being irrational" without engaging the conditional probabilities. Keep the exam to 90 minutes, about 800 to 1000 words expected, total 30 marks. No citations or reading lists, everything should be answerable from the setup. And don't dumb it down: the whole point is to seperate the students who actually see the evidential/causal distinction in a new case from the ones who've memorized Newcomb but can't apply it.
