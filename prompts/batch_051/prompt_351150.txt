Ok so this is for a little film discussion group I'm pulling together on Kurosawa's Rashomon, and I want to do it through Jain anekāntavāda rather than the usual unreliable-narrator frame. I only half-remember the philosophy, so let me sketch what I have and you can correct me if I've mangled it. As I understand it, anekāntavāda is the doctrine that reality is many-sided: no single statement about a thing captures the whole truth, because each statement is true only from some standpoint, in some respect. That's cashed out in syādvāda, where you prefix claims with a syāt, a sort of in-some-respect marker, so you don't just say it is or it is not; you say in some respect it is, in some respect it is not, in some respect it's both, in some respect it's unsayable, plus the combinations, seven standard forms if I'm remembering it right. The point being that two apparently contradictory statements can both be true because they're about different aspects or standpoints, and the mistake is treating a partial truth as the whole.

Here's the reading I want you to develop and stress-test. The obvious move is that the four tellings of the death in the forest are each a partial, standpoint-bound truth: nobody gets the whole event, each person adds a side the others leave out, and the film is doing anekāntavāda in narrative form. But I've always felt the film is a lot less friendly to that than it looks. The accounts don't really complement each other; they contradict each other, and each is suspiciously flattering to the teller, the bandit becomes a dashing swordsman, the wife becomes a wronged innocent, the husband becomes a dignified victim. That looks more like motivated distortion than like seven sides of a truth. So: does the film actually support the Jain reading, complicate it, or just refuse it? I want a reading that takes both the film and the doctrine seriously, where the doctrine genuinely illuminates a scene or a choice, and where the film honestly resists the reading and you have to say so. What do you do with the woodcutter's version, which is supposed to be the objective one but is also undercut, and with the ending at the gate, the baby, the commoner's theft, the woodcutter's offer to take the child, the priest's little bit of recovered hope? Don't just turn the film into a diagram of the doctrine; im interested in what the film adds to the philosophical debate that the doctrine on its own doesn't say.

Oh and one more thing: at the end, tell me what would change your verdict, which scene or moment you're weighing most heavily, and what an alternative reading of that moment would have to show to flip you the other way.
