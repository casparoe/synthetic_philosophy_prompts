I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, and I have been working through a puzzle about how I understand fiction. I would like an honest assessment of a model I have been building. I expect it is probably a rediscovery, a mess, or both; I want to know which.

The puzzle is this. I can read a story in which gravity is negotiable, time runs backwards, or a parliament of bears governs France, and I enter those worlds without difficulty. I can also read stories in which characters commit and approve of terrible acts. But when a fiction asks me to accept, even provisionally, that some specific action is right in the story's world — torture as the highest form of hospitality, say — while leaving the action otherwise unchanged from the real-world version I find abhorrent, I hit a wall. It is not that I pause and morally object; it feels more like the sentence fails to compile. I understand the words, but the world refuses to assemble around the claim.

The model. I have been thinking of imagination as a sandboxed interpreter. Reading fiction means forking my ordinary world model and applying a set of patches supplied by the text. Most of my factual registers are writable, so the story can overwrite species, physics, history, and social roles. But some registers appear to be read-only. When a narrative attempts to write to one of them — to store the claim that torturing a child is good where something like the protection of children normally lives, or that betraying a friend is the most admirable thing one can do — the assignment does not take, or it throws a type mismatch, and the felt error is the resistance. On this account, imaginative resistance is not a considered moral refusal. It is the runtime catching an illegal write, the way a sandbox stops a process from writing to host memory.

The wrinkle. Read-only status does not look like a fixed list of moral propositions. If the story patches enough of the upstream evaluative context — if it installs a knowable God who commands the act, or rewrites the metaphysics of pleasure and flourishing so that the act really is the best thing available — I can often load the rest of the world. The block seems conditional on how much of the surrounding evaluative machinery the story is allowed to rewrite. Some assignments stay blocked no matter how many patches I apply, but the boundary is not fixed, and I cannot state in advance exactly where it sits.

What I want from you: (1) Is this model coherent enough to be wrong, or is it just a metaphor with compiler vocabulary bolted on? (2) Has the underlying idea already been worked out — have I merely re-derived a known position under an engineering name? I am not looking for references; I want to know whether the idea is considered dead, alive, or something that has to be qualified so heavily it stops being useful. (3) Give me the strongest objections you can, two or three cases that would crack the model, and say whether any of them is fatal rather than repairable. Where does the model wrongly predict that I will resist, and where does it wrongly predict that I will go along? Is there a repaired version that survives the worst case, or should the sandbox picture be abandoned altogether?
