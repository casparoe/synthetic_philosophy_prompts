After my daughter was identified as deaf, I began learning ASL and reading about how the definition of language gets settled, and I keep noticing that signed languages are treated as an awkward special case bolted onto theories built for speech. Before I read any further, I want the specification in front of me: what would a theory of language have to do to cover signed and spoken languages on equal terms, with neither modality as the default? Lay out the requirements — the phenomena it must handle (among them, grammar carried by the face and upper body rather than the hands, morphology produced simultaneously instead of in a sequence, verbs that agree through locations in signing space, classifier constructions, and the structured systems invented by deaf children with no linguistic input or by communities with many deaf signers), the contrasts it must respect, and the standing assumptions it must give up — and for each requirement give the phenomenon or consideration that forces it, mark it negotiable or non-negotiable, and note any apparent conflicts between requirements. I am more interested in the criteria themselves than in scoring existing views, but do say which single requirement does the most to rule out speech-first accounts.
