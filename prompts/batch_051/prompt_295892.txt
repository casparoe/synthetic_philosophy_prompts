I run a small discussion circle out of the back room of a public library — usually ten or twelve adults, mixed ages, none of them academics, and we get ninety minutes every other Tuesday. We have been stuck on cancel culture for weeks, and the argument always has the same shape: half the room says people are just facing consequences for what they said, and the other half says it's a mob handing out punishments with no rules. I've been trying to work out on my own why the two sides keep talking past each other, and here's what I keep landing on. Being deplatformed or dropped by your friends/colleagues works like punishment in all the ways that matter — you lose income, reputation, belonging, sometimes your housing — but at no point does anyone have to actually decide "this person will now be punished this much." Each individual just declines to platform them, or goes quiet, or rescinds an invitiation because it's not worth the hassle, and each of those moves feels small and defensible on its own. Only when you look at the whole pile do you see that a sentence has been handed down — often with no charge, no hearing, no way back in and no end point — and it was handed down by a process where everyone can honestly say they didn't do anything.

So for next week I want to run the session not as a debate but as an activity where the group actually has to make those little decisions one at a time, and only finds out at the end what they collectively did to someone. The setting: ten to twelve adults, ninety minutes, I have a whiteboard and index cards but no way to run things online. I'd like rules for a small social world — maybe a volunteer organization, a housing co-op, or a forum — where one member does something genuinely bad but not so bad that everyone agrees they're gone, and then the rest have to decide, round by round, who will keep working with them, who stays silent, and who withdraws. The decisions should be structured so no single person ever feels like they are passing sentence. Then a debrief afterwards that maps what happened onto the ingredients of punishment: did anyone decide the total cost? was it in any way proportional to the bad act? was there a procedure, a chance to make amends, or did it just trail off? I also want you to tell me what will predictably go wrong when I run it — I can already see someone saying "this is rigged to make one side look bad" — and be honest about which parts of the real thing the activity can't capture, like the scale and forever-ness of online pile-ons. I dont want to lecture first or even say the word punishment until the debrief; I want them to feel themselves becoming a court without meaning to.
