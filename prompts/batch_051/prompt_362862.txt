In the units of selection debate, I have been using a three-part checklist to decide whether an entity is a genuine unit of selection rather than a background condition or a mere summary of selection elsewhere. A candidate unit must (a) admit of heritable variation; (b) be such that its variants differ in expected replication or persistence; and (c) possess a fitness of its own, meaning its success is not fully explained by adding up or averaging the fitnesses of entities at levels above or below it, or of other entities at its own level.

I am no longer sure my checklist draws the line in the right place. Consider two cases.

One is a transposable element: a DNA segment that copies or excises itself into new positions within a host genome. Some variants transpose frequently and leave many copies, even though their insertions occasionally disrupt an essential gene and make the host unable to reproduce; other variants are transcribed infrequently and spread little.

The other is an obligate holobiont: a wasp that cannot develop past the larval stage without a particular gut bacterium, and the bacterium in nature is transmitted only through wasp eggs and cannot persist outside wasp larvae, so the two reproduce as a coupled pair.

On my checklist, does either of these count as a unit of selection? Does either case show that one of the three conditions is unnecessary, insufficient, or too coarse to separate genuine units of selection from bookkeeping artifacts?
