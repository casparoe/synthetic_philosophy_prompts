I’ve been turning over an argument I found in a comment thread under a rationalist post on Pascal’s wager, and I can’t tell whether it is a genuine refutation or a sleight of hand. The argument concludes that the expected-utility principle behind the wager proves too much, because it rationally mandates mutually incompatible actions. I’ll lay it out with numbered steps.

(1) If an act A has positive credence p > 0 of producing an infinite payoff, and every other possible consequence of A is merely finite (including finite costs), then the expected utility of A is infinite. Moreover, any act with infinite expected utility ought to be chosen over any act whose expected utility is merely finite.

(2) Pascal’s wager is an instance of (1): wagering for God has positive credence of yielding infinite salvation if God exists, and costs at most a finite amount if God does not, so it ought to be chosen over not wagering.

(3) For any specifiable act A, there is a possible deity D_A who rewards exactly the performance of A with infinite bliss and renders merely finite outcomes for every other act. Since D_A is internally consistent, a reasonable prior should assign it positive credence, however small.

(4) Therefore, by (1), every specifiable act has infinite expected utility and ought to be chosen over every alternative with merely finite expected utility.

(5) Now consider two mutually incompatible acts: devoting one’s life to D_A, and devoting one’s life to D_B, where D_A rewards only the first and D_B rewards only the second. Applying (1) to each act separately yields that each is rationally required.

(6) But no theory of rational choice can require two mutually incompatible acts; that would be a reductio of the theory, not a result.

(7) So (1) is false, and the original wager rests on a principle that proves too much.

I can find only two exits, and both feel expensive. If I reject (3) by denying that every specifiable deity deserves positive credence, I need a principle of prior construction that excludes D_A and D_B without also excluding the God of the original wager; mere mutual exclusivity does not force any particular one of a pair of hypotheses to zero, so I do not see what does the filtering. If I reject (1), I seem committed to saying that no positive-probability infinite payoff may be treated as decision-dominant, which sits badly with the usual reasons for caring about the far future and with treating Pascal’s-mugging-style cases as problems to be tamed rather than dismissed. I would rather keep (1) and find a principled filter on the deities, but I cannot articulate one.

Where does the argument go wrong, if it does? Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If a step needs to be repaired rather than rejected, give the minimal repair that preserves the original wager without mandating incompatible acts.
