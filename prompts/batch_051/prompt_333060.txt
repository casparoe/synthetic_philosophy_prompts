I'm a retired biochemist whose recently been reading around in philosophy of mathematics, and last weekend I sat through a dinner argument between two friends who both teach introductory real analysis. I've been chewing on it since, because neither of them seemed obviously wrong. Here's the exchange as faithfully as I can reconstruct it.

Peter said you should start a proof-based analysis course with a genuinely puzzling concrete problem—for instance, why the harmonic series diverges even though its terms tend to zero—and let students feel the need for a precise definition of convergence before you hand them one. Definitions announced at the start of a chapter, he said, get memorized, and you can't tell memorization from understanding on an exam.

Maria said that for a course that is trying to teach students to write proofs, this is exactly backwards. If you lead with vivid examples, students exit the course believing that "I can see it" is an argument. They will tell you that a limit of continuous functions is obviously continuous; then you show them a sequence of continuous triangular spikes whose pointwise limit is zero everywhere except for a jump to one at the origin, and they file it under "trick" instead of revising the concept. The definition has to come first, she said, precisely to break that habit.

Peter answered that by her logic we should start kindergarden with ZFC. Working mathematicians also reason from pictures, analogies, and false starts before they formalize, so holding novices to a standard of hygiene the professionals themselves don't observe at the point of discovery is just gatekeeping. Definitions first, he added, produce students who can verify a proof but can't generate one.

Maria said the ZFC joke was a caricature. She wasn't against examples; she was against examples before definitions. Her actual position was: state the definition, then run several candidate intuitions straight into it, including one that visibly fails, because that is how you teach the distinction between seeing and knowing.

Peter said that is still definition-first, and it leaves the definition unmotivated. The student learns to apply it but never learns why it has this particular shape rather than a simpler one, and a rule you can apply without a question you were trying to answer is inert. Maria replied that the shape is itself the lesson: the epsilons and deltas encode two centuries of failed intuition in compressed form, and reading them slowly is doing history of mathematics in miniature.

At that point I tried to lower the temperature by saying the whole thing sounded empirical—which sequence actually produces better transfer. Maria said sure, but you'd first have to settle what counts as better: passing a proof-writing exam, or noticing when your own intuition is unreliable. Peter said that depends on what the student is there for, and they are not all there for the same thing. No one could agree on that either.

So: could you adjudicate this move by move? For each main move, say whether it's clearly right, clearly wrong, or somewhere in between and why; name anything both sides are missing; and then say who is more right overall. Keep it under 500 words.
