In my school's philosophy club, a friend laid out the following argument, which he thinks shows that the way we usually add up small harms has an unacceptable consequence. Each step seemed plausible to me as he said it, but I cannot accept where the argument ends, and I want to know which step to reject and what I give up by rejecting it.

(1) Suppose I must choose between a single person being tortured for an hour and some number N of people each getting a dust speck in the eye.
(2) A dust speck in the eye is a real harm: it is small and momentary, but it is not nothing.
(3) When harms are added together, any harm that is not nothing—no matter how small—must, if repeated enough times, eventually add up to more badness than any single finite harm, no matter how severe.
(4) In a choice between two outcomes, the right thing to do is to bring about the outcome with the smaller total badness.
(5) Therefore there is some sufficiently large N for which the total badness of N dust specks exceeds the badness of one person's torture, so in that case I should choose the torture.
(6) But that is monstrous: no number of dust specks could ever justify deliberately torturing a person.
(7) Since (5) and (6) cannot both be true, one of the earlier steps must go. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it commit me to? I am most inclined to keep (3), because it looks like simple arithmetic, and I am also inclined to keep (6), because denying it sounds like endorsing torture. But I suspect I cannot keep both without giving up something else.
