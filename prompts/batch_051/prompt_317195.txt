I've ended up defending a claim in my philosophy of language course and I want someone to attack it properly, since I've started believing it a little too hard. The claim is that no complete natural language could lack indexicals—and I don't just mean pronouns like I and you, but the whole family: here and there, now and then, tense, maybe even spatial deixis. It's not just the empirical bet that every attested language happens to have some of these. It's the stronger claim that if you tried to remove them from a language—replace I with a name, now with a date, here with coordinates—you wouldn't just get a more cumbersome version of the same thing; you'd loose something essential, because indexicals let a thought or an utterance grip the particular situation of the speaker in a way a description can't duplicate. The example that convinced me is the difference between believing at noon that the meeting starts at noon and believing that the meeting starts right now: the first belief can just sit there inert if you happen not to realize what time it is, while the second one makes you get up and run. That gap is exactly what I think you'd lose if you eliminated indexicals.

I know there's a lot of variation in how real languages handle this. Some languages make do with fewer pronouns or have no dedicated third-person pronouns, and signed languages do some of this work with pointing and signing space, but I mean zero context-shifting expressions across the board while still having a full language. And honestly I've caught myself treating the no-indexicals view as a priori impossible now, which is probably a warning sign.

So play it straight for me: what would have to be true for this view to be false? Which real languages, invented languages, acquisition facts, or arguments about reference would count against it, and how much? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say which ones a committed defender would concede as genuine problems and which they'd insulate with qualifications. And tell me whether the view as I've stated it is so insulated that it stops being a substantive claim—and whether that's a problem.
