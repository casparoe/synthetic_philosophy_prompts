I serve on the board of a small housing cooperative that makes policy by consensus rather than by majority vote. We adopted this procedure deliberately, on the understanding that a decision is legitimate only if those affected by it could in principle accept it after a discussion in which no one is dominated and in which nothing but the quality of the reasons is allowed to carry the day. At our last meeting we considered a proposal to raise monthly fees and reduce the amount we set aside for long-term repairs. The discussion was long and largely civil, and the facilitator twice asked whether anyone wished to object. One of the newest members — also the member least able to afford the increase — said almost nothing and did not object. We recorded the decision as consensus.

After the meeting, I overheard two other board members arguing about whether that decision should stand.

Mara said the decision is not legitimate. The whole point of consensus procedure, she argued, is to ensure that agreement is not manufactured by hierarchy, by fear of social consequences, or by fatigue. A silent member is not a consenting member. The fact that she had the formal opportunity to object proves little, because she may have judged that objecting would mark her as difficult or damage relationships on which she depends. If we treat mere absence of objection as consensus, we have rebuilt majority rule under a friendlier name, with the chair's power to declare consensus doing the work a vote would otherwise do. We should privately ask the member whether she genuinely agreed, and if she did not, reopen the matter.

Daniel replied that this standard cannot be met, and that applying it as a test to any particular decision is a category mistake. The ideal of a coercion-free discussion is a regulative idea: it tells us how to design our procedures and what to aim at, not a property we can verify in any actual meeting. We can never know why someone stayed silent — genuine agreement, indifference, fear, or simple tiredness. If every decision can be reopened because someone was quiet, we create an incentive for endless challenge and give a de facto veto to whoever is least willing to speak. The member had the opportunity to object, no one threatened her, and the facilitator explicitly invited objections. At some point a procedure has to count as good enough.

Mara answered that "good enough" is precisely what consensus procedure is meant to displace. Majority rule is already a way of settling for good enough. The value of consensus is that it detects objections that a vote would override; if we adopt it and then shrug at the most predictable way it fails — the silencing of low-status members — we have kept the costs of the procedure while abandoning its point. Legitimacy need not be all-or-nothing, and we can do better at little cost: anonymous written objections, a private check-in with anyone who has not spoken, or a cooling-off period before a decision becomes final.

Daniel answered that those remedies carry their own costs. A private check-in turns the facilitator into an inquisitor and may pressure the quiet member as much as the meeting did, only in private. Anonymous objections break the connection between a reason and the person who stands behind it, which matters because the aim is agreement among persons, not the aggregation of preferences. And if a member will not object after being explicitly invited twice, no institutional device can consent on her behalf; at some point the burden falls on members to speak.

I now have to help decide whether the recorded decision stands, and I cannot tell who has the better of this. Please adjudicate the exchange point by point: for each move, say what is right and what is overstated, and then give an overall verdict on whether the decision should be reopened. In particular, I want to know whether Daniel's regulative-ideal reply is a genuine answer or a way of protecting a flawed procedure from criticism, and whether there is a principled line between a legitimate consensus and a merely unopposed decision.
