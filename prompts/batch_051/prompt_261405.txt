I'm giving a workshop paper on Marx and automation to an audience made up about equally of political philosophers, labor economists, and historians of economic thought, most of whom already find "technology either destroys jobs or it doesn't" debates unsatisfying. My thesis is that Marx's comments on machinery are best reconstructed as a distributional argument about who controls the productivity gains rather than as either a prediction that automation will abolish work or a Luddite lament: in the machinery chapter of Capital, automation under capitalism disciplines and cheapens labor, intensifies exploitation, and turns the savings in labor time into relative surplus value rather than into shorter hours; in the Grundrisse passage known as the Fragment on Machines, the same development is said to create a material possibility—only realizable once production is organized on a different basis—of making labor time increasingly marginal to wealth. I gloss the Fragment not as a forecast that labor will disappear but as an internal tension within a value theory that keeps measuring wealth by labor time at exactly the moment that the productive system stops needing much of it. The audience is predisposed to be skeptical in two opposing ways: the economists will press on the empirical record and the old compensation claim that displaced workers get reabsorbed elsewhere, and the political theorists and historians will suspect the argument smuggles in a functionalist or teleological story in which capitalism conveniently ripens into post-capitalism.
