I'm taking a seminar on bounded rationality and the limits of the principle that ought implies can, and a classmate presented an argument that has been eating at me. The argument is meant to show that the standard computational notion of feasibility is too encoding-sensitive to bear the weight epistemologists want to put on it, but the conclusion is so sweeping that I keep suspecting one of the steps is quietly doing more than it says. It goes like this.

(1) What rationality can require of a finite agent cannot outrun what that agent can feasibly compute; inferential obligations are bounded by feasibility. (2) The only crisp and widely accepted formalization of feasibility in computation is polynomial-time computability. (3) So a bounded-rationality epistemology will make an agent's inferential obligations depend on whether the relevant inference problem is polynomial-time solvable. (4) But polynomial-time solvability is a property of an encoding, not of the underlying mathematical question. The subset-sum problem, the problem of deciding whether some subset of a list of integers sums to a target value, is NP-hard (a polynomial-time algorithm for it would give one for every problem in NP) when its integers are written in the usual binary notation, but the same numerical task is polynomial-time solvable when those integers are written in unary, since the dynamic-programming solution is polynomial in the magnitude of the numbers and unary notation inflates the input length enough to absorb that. On the standard assumption that P differs from NP, the very same numerical problem flips from intractable to tractable with a mere change of notation. (5) Yet an agent's epistemic obligations cannot shift merely because the same numbers are presented in a different numeral system; the mathematics is unchanged, and a mind presented with binary notation is not thereby relieved of insight it would owe in unary. (6) Therefore polynomial-time tractability is the wrong way to spell out the bounds on rational inference.

The part I find genuinely alarming is what happens next. If you accept (6), it looks as though computationally bounded epistemology has just lost the only precise account of feasibility it had. And if you fall back to something vague like tractable in practice or reasonable time, my classmate says, the boundary between what a finite mind must and need not infer becomes interest-relative or anthropocentric. So the choices seem to be notation-sensitive obligations, an unformalizable conception of feasibility, or giving up on computational bounds in epistemology altogether.

I would most like to keep (1) and (5), and I suspect the trouble is in (2), (3), or (4), or in the move from (4) to (5), but every fix I try either makes feasibility circular or quietly sneaks in a canonical encoding. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If (2) is the weak point, please say what notion of feasibility could replace it without reintroducing the encoding problem; if something else is, tell me where the slide happens.
