In a reading group on technology policy, we found ourselves stuck on how one would actually implement the standard proposed in the Future of Life Institute's open letter "Pause Giant AI Experiments" (2023). The letter says: "Powerful AI systems should be developed only once we are confident that their effects will be positive and their risks will be manageable. This confidence must be well justified and increase with the magnitude of a system's potential effects." I would like you to treat that sentence as a design target rather than as a conclusion to defend.

Suppose a group of states creates a Frontier Licensing Office to make the sentence operational. Any developer who wants to train a model above a published compute threshold must first obtain a license. An application must include: (a) a public safety case arguing, with evidence, that the model's effects will be positive and its risks manageable; (b) a red-team audit purchased by the applicant from one of five accredited audit firms, with the full audit report published; (c) a bond proportional to training compute, held in escrow and refunded only if, after two years of post-deployment monitoring by an approved evaluator, the model is not attributed with any harm on a schedule that includes enabling mass-casualty events, large-scale cyberattacks, or systematic election interference; and (d) a 60-day window in which anyone may challenge the license, with an upheld challenge rewarding the challenger from the bond and a dismissed challenge requiring the challenger to pay the developer's review costs. Training above the threshold without a license is a crime, and a licensed model later connected to a listed harm triggers loss of the bond and a possible recall order.

Please analyze this as a mechanism rather than as a moral position. What does it incentivize among developers, accredited auditors, post-deployment evaluators, challengers, and the Office itself? Where is it gamed first, and by whom? Does it select for genuinely safer systems, for deep-pocketed incumbents, or for something else, and what happens to open-weight releases, compute disaggregation, offshoring, or safety-washing in the audit market? Say which failure modes can be fixed without gutting the scheme and which cannot. Throughout, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and avoid presenting a contested view as the consensus. At the very end, give a single machine-readable line: \answer{...} containing a short label for the component of the scheme you judge most likely to be gamed first.
