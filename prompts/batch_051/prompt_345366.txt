I sit on a three-person forensic evaluation panel that advises a court when an insanity defense gets raised. Right now we have a case where the defendant — call him M. — has a long history of psychopathy: flat affect, no genuine guilt or empathy, very good at telling people what they want to hear. The offense is serious and the facts aren't really in dispute. M. says, in a completely matter of fact way, that he knew the act was wrong; he can explain the difference between right and wrong, predict exactly how a jury will judge him, and use words like "wrong" and "shouldn't" in all the right places. What he can't seem to do is care about any of it, or feel remorse, or experience the wrongness as a reason not to do it. One colleague says that's enough: he knowingly violated a rule he understood, so the insanity defense fails. Another says M. is closer to a parrot with good grammar — he can state and manipulate the norms, but they have no grip on him, so in the sense that matters he doesn't really know that what he did was wrong.

I'm suppose to draft our unit's written opinion to the court by Friday, recommending that we either accept or reject the insanity defense (or accept with conditions, though those are messy here). Please draft it for me. It should have a clear bottom line, actually say what "knowing that an act is wrong" amounts to in this context, and take the strongest version of the losing side's view seriously rather than brushing it off. I especially want the memo to get at whether there's a real difference between being able to say what's wrong and knowing it in the sense the law should care about.
