I recently read Kazuo Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go, and I keep turning it over against a question I've been trying to work through on my own, without any formal background in the subject. The question is what, exactly, is supposed to be wrong with letting people buy and sell certain things, such as a kidney. A common answer is that putting a price on a body part turns a person into a commodity, and that some things are degraded the moment they enter a market. The novel looks at first like a natural companion to that thought. But what strikes me is that the system in the novel is not exactly a market. The students at Hailsham are not paid for what happens to them, and no one appears to buy an organ from a catalogue; they are raised to make donations as though giving were simply what they are for, with the whole arrangement wrapped in euphemism. So I would like a careful philosophical reading that works in both directions. On one side, what does the idea of commodification, or the corruption of a good by giving it a market price, genuinely illuminate about the school, the guardians' kindness, the students' art and their hopes for deferral, and the way Kathy, Ruth, and Tommy come to think of their futures? On the other side, where does the novel resist being read as a warning about markets, and suggest instead that the deeper wrong is something broader, a way of creating and using people as spare parts that would remain wrong even if no price were ever attached? And does the novel add anything to the philosophical argument that a standard objection to repugnant markets, stated in the abstract, would miss? I am not looking for a book report or for quotations; I want the conceptual tension worked through.
