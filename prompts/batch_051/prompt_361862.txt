I spent most of my career in physical chemistry, and one thing I still can't get straight after retiring into philosophy is a double standard about truth-seeking. In my old department, if someone spent fifteen years measuring the vibration frequencies of some short-lived molecule to an extra decimal place, nobody asked what the measurement was for; it was called pure science, knowledge for its own sake, and the person was seen as doing something almost noble. But my brother-in-law spent about the same amount of effort reconstructing, from old newspaper announcements and harbour records, the exact arrival and departure times of every passenger ship that left Rotterdam between 1880 and 1914. Same kind of doggedness, same indifference to practical payoff, same genuine accumulation of true facts. Yet everyone, me included, treats his project as a harmless obsession or a hobby, not as a contribution to human knowledge.

When I try to explain the difference to myself I reach for words like generality or explanatory power, but I'm not sure I actually beleive that story. Is there a principle that makes the first man's truth-seeking intrinsically valuable and the second man's merely a pastime, or is this just a caste system about which facts are allowed to count? I'd like the strongest defence of the asymmetry you can construct, and an honest verdict on whether it survives your own objections.
