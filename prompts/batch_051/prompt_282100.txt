OK so I'm in a democratic theory course and we've been on lottocracy for a couple weeks, and I'm also taking intro stats, and I can't shake the feeling that the whole case for sortition is secretly just sampling theory. The lottocrat says: pick the legislature by lot, and you get a body whose composition mirrors the population—same share of renters, nurses, non-voters, whatever, within sampling error. In stats that's exactly the claim that a simple random sample's proportion is an unbiased estimator of the population proportion. So descriptive representation is just unbiasedness, and a lottocratic assembly is an estimator of the demos. That mapping seems to exact to be a coincidence.

But when I try to actually cash it out it gets weird. In stats unbiasedness is a pretty weak property: a sample of size 1 can be unbiased, and nobody wants a legislature of one. What we care about is variance, mean squared error, nonresponse, measurement error, the sampling frame. Transfer those to lottocracy and you'd care about things like the probability that some group is totally absent from the assembly, not just its expected share. Also, survey researchers try really hard to stop respondents from talking to each other and changing their answers, whereas a lottocratic assembly is supposed to deliberate, which is basically peer effects as a feature not a bug. And the population's policy preferences right now may not be the relevant quantity at all, since the assembly is supposed to learn things after selection.

So is this parallel deep or a loose analogy with statistical vocabulary? Where does it actually hold, where does it break, and what would have to be true about political preferences and deliberation for the good properties of random sampling to actually transfer to a lottocratic legislature? Also, is there anything the parallel predicts that's false, which would show it's being stretched to far?
