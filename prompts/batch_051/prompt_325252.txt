I keep coming back to this passage in Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism": "Things had essences, for Aristotle, but only linguistic forms have meanings. Meaning is what essence becomes when it is divorced from the object of reference and wedded to the word." I'm a software engineer with no philosophy background, so maybe im just seeing my job everywhere, but that sounds like a worked example of static types: a type is what a value's actual shape becomes when you divorce it from the value and attach it to the identifier. And there's a bigger parallel I can't shake. I find myself splitting claims in a program into true-by-meaning statements (a `User` is this shape because the spec says so, independent of whatever data exists) and true-by-fact statements (this payload has that shape because we observed it). Quine spends the whole paper arguing the analytic/synthetic version of that line is a dogma, and production has tought me the type/runtime version is just as negotiable: casts, schema drift, language version bumps, and "actually that's the new contract" all move it around. Is this a real structural parallel or am I just pattern-matching on the word "meaning"? If it's real, what does it predict—ideally something false enough to test it? If it's superficial, where exactly does it break?
