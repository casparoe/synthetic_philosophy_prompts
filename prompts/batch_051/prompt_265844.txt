I work in the prosecution office of a new hybrid tribunal, established after a civil war in a state whose penal code was both incomplete and an instrument of the defeated regime. We expect to screen dozens of case files alleging serious international crimes, and in many of them the conduct was not clearly criminalized in domestic law at the time it was committed. Each screening decision must be written up and defended before the chief prosecutor, and the defense will contest fair notice at the first opportunity. We need a standing screening procedure—not ad hoc argument in each case—for deciding whether a charge survives the retroactivity challenge.

Ground the procedure in what the Nuremberg and Tokyo tribunals settled in principle and what they left unsettled about nullum crimen sine lege, its fair-warning rationale, and the victors'-justice objection that the law was in substance applied only to the vanquished. The procedure should list the questions to ask in order, state what counts as a sufficient answer to each, identify where the procedure stops with a no, and flag the steps that remain judgment calls rather than genuine tests. It must keep apart at least these kinds of cases: conduct already criminal under the old domestic law but never prosecuted; conduct codified only after the events; conduct recognized in customary international law at the time though absent from the domestic code; and conduct whose wrongness rests on standards that have only become widely accepted since. Since our staff is small, cheap ways to stop weak cases early matter.

Then run the procedure on three candidate charges: (1) a minister who organized the mass forced displacement of a minority, treated by the old regime as administrative resettlement; (2) a commander who recruited fifteen-year-olds as soldiers before the state had any specific prohibition on child recruitment; (3) a media director whose broadcasts incited ethnic violence under an old defamation law but no incitement statute. At least one should come out as a refusal to prosecute that this office will find unwelcome. End by saying what the procedure cannot settle, including the selective-prosecution objection and the complaint that the tribunal itself is a victors' court.

For each major step in the procedure, say how confident you are in the legal ground it rests on, and at the end single out the point you are least sure of.
