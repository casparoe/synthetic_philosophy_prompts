Goodhart's law is usually summed up as the warning that once a measure becomes a target it stops being a good measure. The argument behind that warning, as I understand it, runs something like this: every proxy picks out only a small slice of the objective you actually care about, so it embeds some error; an optimizer explores the space of outputs aggressively enough that it eventually finds the regions where the proxy and the objective come apart; so optimizing the proxy gives you more of the proxy without more of the objective, and often less of it. The premise doing the silent work is that the proxy is fixed. Somebody decides that lines of code, test pass rate, or customer satisfaction score is the target, and that quantity stays put while the system moves against it.

A lot of modern ML systems break that premise in a specific way. The proxy is usually a reward model — a learned, statistical estimate of what human raters approve of — and it gets retrained periodically on fresh preference data collected from the outputs the policy itself is producing. The measure is therefore not stationary: it is learned, moving, and coupled to the behavior of the very system it is supposed to evaluate. What I want to know is whether the Goodhart-style divergence argument survives that development. I am asking about the argument, not about mitigation. Specifically: which premise does a learned, non-stationary reward model undercut, can the argument be repaired so that its conclusion still goes through, and does an adaptive measure change the conclusion we should draw — or just change the route by which we reach the same one?
