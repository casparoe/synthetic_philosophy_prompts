I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've been asked to give the nine-minute opening remarks at a workshop on how institutions should handle low-probability, high-consequence events. The room is deliberately mixed: roughly half are political theorists who work on Machiavelli's treatment of fortune, and half are civil engineers, actuaries, and emergency planners who have never read him. I want the remarks built around a genuine tension in Machiavelli rather than a single slogan. On one side is the river image: fortune floods, and virtù means building dikes, channels, and standing arrangements in the calm season rather than improvising once the waters rise. On the other side is his more aggressive language about seizing and mastering fortune, which can sound like exactly the heroic improvisation the river image cautions against. Please write the speech so that this tension drives it toward a substantive conclusion — that resilience is best understood as pre-built capacity, not as flexible response after the fact — and so that the scholars feel the interpretive problem is being put to work while the practitioners leave with a sharper vocabulary than they arrived with, not having been lectured. Paraphrase the imagery rather than quoting from The Prince.
