I have a half-baked question about spectrum arguments for intransitive 'better than' — you know, each step from A to B looks like a tiny improvement, so A is better than B is better than C, all the way down to Z, but almost everyone also judges Z better than A. The standard transitivity-defender move is to say the adjacent judgments aren't really strict betterness: they're indifference or 'not noticeably worse,' and only strict betterness needs to be transitive. The intransitivity folks say no, careful subjects really do judge each step strictly better, and the cycle is a real feature of the concept, so much the worse for transitivity. That looks testable, but I rarely see anyone actually separate strict preference from indifference when collecting these intuitions.

So: design the best empirical test you can of the claim that in some well-chosen spectrum case, people's pairwise judgments contain a cycle of genuine strict betterness, not an artifact of vague thresholds, anchoring on the previous option, or forced-choice setups that turn indifference into 'better than.' Be concrete about the spectrum (and why it's a fair test rather than a stacked one), the elicitation method (forced choice? ratings? incentives? willingness to pay or to endure? post-hoc consistency probes?), and what pattern of results would convince you the intransitivity is real versus an artifact. Then say what a clean positive result still wouldn't settle — e.g., whether transitivity is a rational requirement the judgments merely violate, or something built into the concept that no empirical result can touch.
