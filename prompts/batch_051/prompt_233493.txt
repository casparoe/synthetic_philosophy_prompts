As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I am drafting a piece on whether there is such a thing as the scientific method. The scientists I interview tend to speak as though there is one; the historians and philosophers I read keep insisting that the phrase names a family of practices at best and a myth at worst. Before I take a position, I want to pressure-test the working checklist I have been using in my own reporting. For an investigation to count as an instance of the scientific method, I have been requiring that:

1. it begins from a hypothesis sharp enough to be refuted by the data;
2. its test isolates a cause, normally through a controlled experiment or randomized assignment, so that the result supports a claim about what would have happened otherwise;
3. its result is reproducible by an independent team following the same procedure;
4. its success criteria and analysis plan are fixed before the data arrive, so that the analysis cannot become curve fitting after the fact; and
5. its conclusion rests on more than one independent line of evidence.

The case that has made me doubt this checklist is extreme event attribution in climate science. After a record heat wave, drought, or flood, a team estimates how much more likely or more intense human-caused climate change made that particular event. In a standard attribution study, researchers run two large ensembles of climate model simulations, one for a world with observed greenhouse gas concentrations and one for a counterfactual world without those emissions; they define the event, for example a three-day heat wave above a specified threshold over a specified region; they estimate the probability of that event in each ensemble; and they report a risk ratio and a fraction of attributable risk. In the most careful versions, the event definition and analysis plan are fixed before the results are known, the work is peer reviewed, and the results are now invoked in litigation and in international loss-and-damage debates.

Yet the strains are obvious. Nobody manipulates the actual atmosphere; the counterfactual world exists only inside a model, and a model is not a controlled trial. The event is a single historical occurrence, so the finding cannot be reproduced in the field, only recomputed by another group using its own models and judgment. The central claim is usually an estimate of a quantity with an uncertainty range rather than a yes-or-no hypothesis that the data might refute. And considerable judgment enters through model selection, event definition, and the choice of a pre-industrial baseline, even when preregistration is meant to keep that judgment from becoming motivated reasoning.

I would like you to check extreme event attribution against my five criteria one by one, saying for each whether the practice satisfies it, misses it, or satisfies it only once the criterion is stretched. Then address two further questions. First, does the practice count as doing science in the sense my checklist was trying to capture, and which criterion should I abandon or rewrite because the case shows it is not a genuine requirement? Second, does the case support the Feyerabendian view that any attempt to codify scientific method will be false to scientific practice or empty, sometimes summarized as the claim that anything goes, or does it support the weaker conclusion that the method is a pluralistic family of practices with real but local constraints? I care less about the label than about learning which of my five requirements are actually doing work.
