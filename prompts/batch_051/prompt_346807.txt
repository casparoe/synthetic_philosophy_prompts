I cover the science of decision-making for a living, and for the last few months I've been keeping a log of my own choices. It hasn't been flattering. Here are my verdicts on five cases, and I want to know what principle – if any – captures them.

1. On Monday I pay £65 to book a cleaner for Saturday rather than spend an hour tidying the flat myself. On Saturday I cancel and tidy the flat myself, because I'd rather keep the £65 and the hour. No new infomation arrived during the week. Verdict: both choices were rational, and the pair of them looks daft.

2. Asked in January, I prefer £100 in twelve months to £105 in thirteen months. When the twelve months actually arrive, I prefer £105 in one month to £100 today. Both choices felt rational, but I'd pay £1 in January to tie my own hands, and then at the later date I'd pay £2 to untie them. I'm being pumped and I can see it.

3. I buy a treadmill for £700, genuinely expecting to run four times a week. Six months later I sell it for £250, having used it eleven times. Buying felt rational; selling felt rational; the whole episode felt like a slow leak of £450.

4. Contrast case: I order a spicy curry for delivery tomorrow, and tomorrow I swap it for a mild one because I've woken up with a cold. Both choices feel rational, but here I don't feel mugged. My body changed; the second preference came with new information. So whatever the principle is, it has to distinguish case 4 from cases 1–3 without simply saying "any reversal is a failure."

5. I sign up to a £10 monthly direct debit to a malaria charity after watching a documentary. Every month I think about cancelling, and every month I decide the £10 is worth more to them than it is to me. Persisting feels rational; it looks like the opposite of a money pump: same self, same information, same verdict each month.

My tentative principle is something like: a choice is rational if it maximises expected value from the perspective of the moment it's made, and a whole sequence is irrational only if it predictably leaves me worse off by a standard I endorsed at the start. But that seems to let the treadmill through, and I can't tell whether it's the principle or the verdict that has to give.

Can you give me the best principle, or small set of candidates, that captures all this? State it precisely; say which case motivates which clause; tell me what it says about a new case (I choose a no-refund annual railcard expecting to use it enough, then six months in I stop travelling by train and think it was a mistake, although nothing changed); and be honest about which of my verdicts the principle can't save and which I'd have to drop.

Keep the answer under 800 words.
