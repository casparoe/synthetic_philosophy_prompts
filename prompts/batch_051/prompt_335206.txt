I work in the accommodations office at a mid-sized university, and twice a year we have to rule on what technology a student is allowed to use in exams: calculators, spellcheck, text-to-speech, an AI note-taker, a phone with a reminders app, that kind of thing. The stakes are high, because we're either leveling the playing field or handing out an unfair advantage, and our decisions sometimes get appealed. Lately our office has been throwing around the extended mind thesis — Otto's notebook, the parity principle, the idea that an external tool can be part of someone's cognitive process if it does what a biological memory or skill would do. But nobody here has a philosophy background, and I've been asked to turn this into something two non-philosopher staffers can actually run, in about twenty minutes per case, so we stop deciding by vibes.

Write me a decision proceedure for individual cases: given a student and a proposed tool, the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it says "this is a values call; a human has to decide and argue it." The procedure has to thread a needle. On one side, if we treat every external device as part of the student's mind, then a student with a phone full of notes has the same memory as a student with a diagnosed memory disability, no accommodation means anything, or everyone gets everything. On the other side, if we treat no external thing as cognitive, we'd have to ban glasses and spellcheck, which we obviously don't want. So the procedure needs to say which features of a tool's relationship to a student make it more like Otto's notebook and less like a hired tutor, and it needs to be candid about which steps aren't settled by the philosophy and where we're really deciding on values.

Then run it on three cases: a student with dysgraphia who wants to use speech-to-text for an essay exam; a student with a math disability who wants a graphing calculator in a calculus exam; and a student with no diagnosis who wants to use an autocomplete AI assistant in an essay exam because "my phone is already my memory." For each, say what the procedure recommends, where it hesitates, and which step a defender of the student's request would attack. Include at least one case where the procedure probably tells us no, and say how you'd write that decision so it doesn't just look like a vibe.
