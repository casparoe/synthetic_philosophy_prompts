I've been turning over a parallel between two areas I usually keep separate, and I would like help deciding whether it is a genuine structural isomorphism, a loose analogy, or a confusion.

On one side is the philosophical account of joint action. Bratman's account of shared cooperative activity, for instance, involves mutual responsiveness among the participants, each participant intending to do their part because of the others' parts, a commitment to mutual support should one person's contribution break down, and common knowledge among participants that these conditions obtain. Gilbert goes further: a joint commitment is formed by the parties together, cannot be revoked unilaterally, and gives each participant standing to object if another walks away.

On the other side is the two-phase commit protocol in distributed systems. A coordinator asks each participant in a distributed transaction to prepare; a participant that votes yes is bound to go through with the commit if the final decision is commit, and must be able to recover to that state even after a crash; the transaction commits only if every participant votes yes and aborts if any votes no. A participant that voted yes and then loses contact can block the whole protocol, which is exactly the cost of preserving all-or-nothing atomicity.

The mapping I see is this: the prepare vote is the taking on of a joint commitment; atomicity is the joint-action property of 'either we all do our parts or none of us does'; the blocking state after a crash is the norm that one participant cannot simply dissolve the joint commitment; and the protocol's acknowledgment exchange is a crude implementation of the common-knowledge condition.

I am also tempted to run the analogy from distributed systems back into the philosophy. The FLP impossibility result says that no deterministic consensus protocol in an asynchronous system can guarantee both safety and liveness if even one process may crash. If shared intention really required common knowledge plus this kind of binding commitment, then human joint action should be subject to something like the same fragility. But people coordinate joint actions all the time under exactly the conditions FLP rules out—partial information, delays, people changing their minds, others dropping out. So one of three things seems true: the analogy is only superficial; the philosophical accounts overstate the knowledge or commitment conditions; or joint action has a resource that the distributed systems formalism omits.

I would like a careful assessment of how deep the parallel really is. What would have to be true of both sides for the impossibility results to transfer, and where does the mapping most clearly come apart? If it comes apart somewhere, I want to know whether that tells us something substantive about what philosophical theories of joint action have been idealizing away.
