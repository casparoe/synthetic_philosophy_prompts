I'm preparing a handout for an upper-level seminar called 'Categories in the Wild' that sits between philosophy of mind, typography, and design theory. The running example all term has been the deceptively simple question of what makes every 'a' an 'a'. We have deliberately set aside dictionary-style definitions such as 'the first letter of the Latin alphabet'; the puzzle is what a fluent reader knows that lets her treat endlessly many physically different marks as tokens of a single letter. The seminar has been working with a fixed set of benchmark cases: a single-storey handwritten-style 'a'; a double-storey Times-like 'a'; an uppercase 'A'; a child's backward 'a' that teachers correct but fluent readers still parse in context; a heavily stylized logo 'a' that is unrecognizable alone but legible inside a word; a Greek capital alpha visually identical to a Latin capital 'A'; and a Cyrillic lowercase letter visually indistinguishable from a Latin 'a' in many typefaces.

I would like you to write the centerpiece handout: a decision tree, or if a tree cannot do the job a taxonomy with an explicit sorting key, that places a reader into one of six or seven positions on what grounds 'a'-ness. The leaves must include at least the following five: Platonism about letterforms, on which the letter is an abstract shape or form that particular marks approximate; prototype theory, on which tokens are graded members clustering around a best example; a structural-role view of the kind associated with the Letter Spirit project, on which a letter is a bundle of roles inside a whole alphabet system and can survive complete stylistic change of its surface features; a historical/genealogical view, on which a mark counts as an 'a' because it descends from earlier 'a's through chains of teaching and copying; and conventionalism or response-dependence, on which there is nothing more to being an 'a' than being read as one by a community. Please build the tree from diagnostic questions first rather than from the labels: every branch node should be a concrete case judgment or comparison that a student could answer without already knowing which position it points toward. Flag any leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate and say why, and note any logically possible combinations of answers that remain empty or merely unnamed, with a brief diagnosis of whether each is incoherent, unattractive, or unexplored. Aim for seven to nine questions.
