Consider an asymmetry in the way we assign responsibility for wrongful acts. Suppose a man assaults a stranger, and we then learn that a tumor was pressing on regions of his brain that govern impulse control. Many people—and many legal systems, at least those with a serious insanity or diminished-capacity defense—will treat that fact as diminishing or even eliminating his responsibility for the act. Now suppose the same act, but the explanation is different: the man has a strongly heritable impulsive temperament and was raised in conditions of abuse and neglect that modeled and rewarded violence. Here the causal history was also fixed long before the act and was not of his choosing, yet ordinary judgment and the law by and large continue to hold him responsible. The tumor is treated as undermining his authorship of the act; the temperament and the upbringing are not. Call this the neurological-insult asymmetry.

Some philosophers regard this asymmetry as unstable. Gregg Caruso, in his debate with Daniel Dennett over whether anyone ever justly deserves punishment, argues that once we concede that a brain tumor can undermine desert because it operates outside the agent's control, consistency should push us to concede the same about genes, parenting, and social environment—and therefore about everyone, since every action has such a causal history. Dennett resists this, defending a line between gross disruptions of a person's capacities and the ordinary causal shaping of a competent agent.

I want to know whether the ordinary asymmetry can be justified, and not merely explained psychologically. Please give me the strongest principle you can find that does all of the following: it treats the brain-tumor case as responsibility-diminishing in a way the impulsive-temperament-plus-abusive-upbringing case is not; it does not collapse into excusing everyone; and it is not a restatement of the asymmetry itself. Then run that principle through each of the cases below and say whether it delivers the verdict you would be willing to defend, even where that verdict is unpopular.

(a) A man suffering from untreated schizophrenia kills a neighbor while acting on a delusion, though he has repeatedly refused to comply with treatment.

(b) A woman commits an impulsive assault, and her impulsivity is traced to a brain lesion caused by decades of heavy drinking that she knowingly chose to engage in.

(c) A man was not abused and shows no neurological abnormality, but from adolescence he deliberately cultivated a violent reputation, sought out groups that rewarded cruelty, and gradually made himself someone who attacks with little reflection.

(d) A teenager raised from early childhood inside a criminal organization commits a carefully planned act of violence. The violence is skilled and matter-of-fact, and he regards the organization's norms as simply how the world works.

(e) An executive embezzles money over several years through an elaborate scheme. Imaging later suggests a slow-growing tumor mildly disinhibited him, but the scheme itself was intricate, long-maintained, and concealed with care.

Finally, tell me explicitly whether your principle forces a revision of the initial asymmetry. If it makes the temperament case much closer to the tumor case than ordinary practice allows, or pushes you to excuse some of (a)–(e) that you would otherwise blame, say so. Should the asymmetry be kept with a patch, reversed, or given up?
