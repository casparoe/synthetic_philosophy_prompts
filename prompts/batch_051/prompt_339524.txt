I serve on a committee that oversees a county pretrial risk-assessment instrument. The tool assigns each defendant a score from 1 to 10 intended to predict the likelihood of failing to appear or being rearrested before trial. A recent audit surfaced a pattern I find hard to explain. At every score level, the proportion of defendants actually rearrested is nearly identical across the two largest demographic groups in our jurisdiction. Yet defendants who are not rearrested receive high scores at a substantially higher rate in one group, and defendants who are rearrested receive low scores at a substantially higher rate in the other. One committee member argues that the instrument is therefore unfair: it burdens one group with more detentions of people who would have appeared and the other with more releases of people who then offended. Another member argues that it is exactly what fairness demands, because a given score means the same empirical risk regardless of who receives it, and the unequal error rates follow automatically from the groups' different overall rearrest rates. Both positions strike me as reasonable, and I have come to suspect that this is not a flaw we can engineer away but a structural tension between two defensible fairness requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied. Is that suspicion mathematically sound, or is it a failure of statistical intuition? If it is sound, what exactly is the trade-off, and how should a committee like ours decide which fairness requirement to prioritize in a pretrial setting? Please separate what is mathematically settled from what is a genuine value or policy choice, and where the field is genuinely divided, say so rather than presenting one side as the consensus.
