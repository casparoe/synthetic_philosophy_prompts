Below is an argument I keep coming back to, and I cannot tell whether it is sound or whether one of the steps is quietly doing the trick. Please tell me whether the argument is valid, and if it fails, exactly where and how.

(1) If our best current physics is even approximately right, there is some nonzero probability that a functioning human brain, with precisely the experiences I am having at this moment, assembles spontaneously from randomly arranged matter, with no ordinary biological history behind it.

(2) In a sufficiently vast or eternal universe, such spontaneous fluctuations will produce a vastly larger number of these isolated brains — Boltzmann brains — than the number of ordinary embodied brains like mine produced by cosmology, evolution, and development.

(3) If I know that I am one among many observers sharing my present evidence, I should regard myself as a random sample from that whole class and should expect to be typical of it.

(4) Therefore I should now assign overwhelming probability to being one of those spontaneous brains rather than an ordinary person with a real past — even though, if I were such a brain, the memories and physical reasoning on which this argument rests would be unreliable side-effects of a fluctuation, which threatens to undercut the argument itself.

Is the argument valid? If it fails, identify the exact step at which it fails, restate that step in the most charitable way that keeps it true, and say whether the argument goes through on that restatement. If it is valid, say which premise you would resist and why.

Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion. Use the first four items to assess the four steps in order, and use the fifth item to give your overall verdict, how confident you are in it, and the single point you are least sure of.
