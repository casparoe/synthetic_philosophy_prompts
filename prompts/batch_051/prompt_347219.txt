Suppose a regional health authority adopts the following budget rule, intended as an operationalization of lexical threshold negative utilitarianism. Severe suffering is defined mechanically as pain above a fixed intensity cutoff that persists beyond a fixed duration within a given period — say, pain above 8/10 lasting more than 24 hours in a 30-day window. Each proposed intervention receives a score of +1 for every expected such episode it prevents in a year, and a score of 0 for every other outcome, regardless of how much aggregate sub-threshold pain it relieves or how many moderate discomforts it would otherwise address. Interventions that prevent at least one severe episode outrank all interventions that prevent none; among interventions that do prevent severe episodes, ranking proceeds by the number of episodes prevented, with all sub-threshold benefits demoted to a tiebreak. Analyze this rule as a mechanism rather than as a moral position. What does it incentivize in those who propose, measure, and administer interventions? Who predictably gets funded, and who disappears from the portfolio? Where does it get gamed first — in defining intensity scores, choosing durations, shaping caseloads, or elsewhere — and can those failure modes be repaired without abandoning the rule? Finally, say whether the rule's behavior in practice tells against the underlying lexicographic claim that no amount of sub-threshold benefit can outweigh a single case of severe suffering, or only against this crude implementation of it.
