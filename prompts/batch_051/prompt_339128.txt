When does Latin become Italian? The usual empirical route — find the earliest document that is already Italian rather than Latin — presupposes that we already know what makes a later spoken variety the same language as, or a different language from, an earlier one. I want to test that presupposition. Here are four criteria I propose for saying that the everyday speech of a community at time t is the same language as that community's everyday speech at an earlier time.

1. Channel continuity: each generation acquired its speech by ordinary childhood transmission from the preceding generation, with no generation having to learn the speech of its grandparents or great-grandparents as a foreign tongue.

2. Code continuity: the grammar changed piecemeal and by overlap — case endings, word order, auxiliaries, phonology, and lexicon shifted a few features at a time, and no single generation replaced the whole system at once.

3. Reflexive continuity: at each stage speakers had some metalinguistic stance toward their own speech — a name for it, a sense of whether it was the same as their elders' — and this chain of stances never broke sharply. For instance, whether they took themselves to be speaking Latin, or the volgare, matters.

4. Standard continuity: a public norm of correct written usage persisted, and its relation to the everyday vernacular changed in specifiable ways — classicizing written Latin held at a distance from speech, then a rival written standard slowly emerged.

Now the borderline case. Consider the everyday spoken vernacular of Rome around 800 CE: before any substantial written tradition that is unambiguously Italian has emerged, but after the noun case system has collapsed in speech, word order has shifted toward subject–verb–object, the everyday vocabulary has diverged widely from the written classical lexicon, and periphrastic forms have spread through the verb system. No generation along the way failed to understand its parents. Apply each of the four criteria to this variety and say whether it qualifies as Latin, as Italian, as both, or as neither.

Then apply the same criteria to a deliberately difficult artifact. A scribe in 810 has recorded a boundary dispute in the margin of a land register, writing it roughly as the parties actually spoke, except that he has "corrected" the endings he thought wrong. The line uses the everyday descendant of Latin caballus rather than equus for the horse, places a weak pronoun before the verb in the later Romance manner, and — because of the scribe's overcorrections — carries a Latin-looking desinence on the subject while the direct object has no case mark. Is this line Latin or Italian? If the four criteria pull in different directions, say exactly which criterion pulls where.

Finally, address whether "when did Latin become Italian?" has a single answer independent of which criterion we privilege, or whether it splits into several distinct questions: when the acquisitional channel broke (perhaps never), when the spoken code became recognizably Italian, when speakers stopped regarding themselves as speaking Latin, and when a standard distinct from Latin was instituted. Then say what would change your mind: which additional evidence or argument would lead you to a different verdict about the 800 CE vernacular, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
