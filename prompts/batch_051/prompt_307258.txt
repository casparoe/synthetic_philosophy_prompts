Hey, I'm putting together a little zine of decision-theory humor and I need a piece for it. Can you write me a deadpan FAQ page, maybe 600 words, from the joint Aleppo–Damascus tourism board in a world where the Death in Damascus thought experiment is literally true (so don't overthink the real cities — this is the stylized two-city version, and the only local hazard is Death's appointment book)? The premise: Death works strictly by appointment, has a perfect prediction record, and has already written down which of the two cities each visitor will end up in; whichever city you actually pick, that's the one he'll have predicted, and he keeps the appointment there. The FAQ should answer the questions a tourist would actually ask: do I get to pick where I die, or does the ledger pick for me? isn't it smarter to do the opposite of whatever he wrote? what about flipping a coin at the train station — has he already written down the coin? is there a no-show fee if Death misses me (he won't)? The satire should target the seductive mistake of using dominance reasoning against a predictor whose prediction isn't causally independent of your act: the traveler who says "whatever he predicted, I'd do better to go the other way" hasn't noticed that going the other way just becomes the prediction. The jokes should come from getting the decision theory right — act-state dependence, the evidential-versus-causal intuitions pulling apart, and the ratifiability-ish worry that once you've chosen, your choice is itself evidence about what the ledger said, so it might not still look good to you. Stay in character and deadpan; don't wink or state the moral outright. A refular reader who's only ever heard of Newcomb's problem should get it, and a decision theorist should laugh rather than wince.
