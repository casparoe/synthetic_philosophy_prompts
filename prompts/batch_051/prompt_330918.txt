A friend and I spent last night arguing about split-brain patients, and it left me unable to say exactly what my own view is. She said that once the corpus callosum is cut there are simply two minds, maybe two persons, living in one body. I kept objecting that this is too clean, but I couldn't state the alternative.

Here are my actual gut verdicts, as best I can articulate them. When a patient's left and right hands fight over the same object, I don't think there are two people; I think there is one person whose action systems are no longer fully integrated. When the speaking hemisphere says it wants to be a draftsman while the right hemisphere spells out that it wants to be a race-car driver, I do think there are two streams of thought, but I still wouldn't say there are two persons. When the left hemisphere confidently confabulates a reason for an action the right hemisphere was secretly instructed to perform, I see one subsystem rationalizing another, not a self lying to a separate self. But when each hemisphere is anesthetized in turn and each can converse separately, I'm strongly pulled toward saying there are two competent agents, and I can't see why this is just an illusion. And I want to resist saying that the adult patient's silent right hemisphere is a full person while the speaking left hemisphere is another full person; yet I cannot name the kind of "partial subject" or "degree of unity" that would let me say that.

What principle, or small set of candidate principles, captures the pattern in these verdicts? Please test it against the hardest pair — the conflicting-hands case and the separate-conversation case — and tell me at least one of my verdicts it forces me to drop. Then apply the resulting account to a case I haven't listed: a patient with only a partial severing of the corpus callosum, who shows rare, transient conflicts between what she says and what she does, but otherwise functions as one person. Please give your answer as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. One item should state the principle, one item should identify which of my verdicts cannot be accommodated, one item should apply the account to the partial-severing case, and the final item should state how confident you are in the account and single out the point you are least sure of.
