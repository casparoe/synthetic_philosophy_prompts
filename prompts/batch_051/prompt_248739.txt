I've been going around in circles about an argument a friend gave me on epistemic contextualism, and I'd like someone to actually check it step by step. As I understand the view, contextualism says the word knows works a bit like a gradable adjective: the standards for correctly applying it shift from one conversational context to the next. Thats supposed to handle the familiar bank case, where the same person with the same evidence can truly say I know the bank is open Saturday in a low-stakes context and truly say I dont know (or at least count as not knowing) in a high-stakes one. Pragmatic encroachment, by contrast, is usually put as a rival: knows doesn't shift its meaning at all, but whether you literally know depends in part on how much is practically at stake for you. My friend's claim was that the first view quietly collapses into the second once you look at first-person cases, where the speaker is also the subject. His argument:

(1) In the bank case, my sentence I know the bank is open Saturday is true as uttered in the low-stakes context and false as uttered in the high-stakes context, even though my evidence, my reliability, and the facts about the bank are identical.
(2) So the truth value of that sentence depends on the practical context of the person saying it.
(3) The sentence says the same thing in both contexts: it says that I know the bank is open Saturday. The only difference is the standards used to assess it.
(4) Therefore one and the same proposition, that I know the bank is open Saturday, is true in the low-stakes context and false in the high-stakes context, while my evidence and the world don't change.
(5) If whether that proposition is true can flip like that with only my practical situation varying, then whether I know the bank is open Saturday depends partly on my practical situation.
(6) And that is just pragmatic encroachment, or close enough to make no difference.

I have a feeling step (3) is sneaky, because contextualists usually say the two utterances don't express the same proposition at all — knows contributes something different in each context, like knowing-by-low-standards versus knowing-by-high-standards. But if that's the answer, I'm not sure what the view is then: if knows is doing this in self-ascriptions, the attributor-subject distinction can't save it, and I don't see how it avoids making knows massively ambiguous. So tell me: is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and what exactly should a contextualist say about knows in the two contexts? If it is valid, is the conclusion the real pragmatic encroachment view or just a harmless shadow of it?
