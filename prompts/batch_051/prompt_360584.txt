I keep running into the phrase the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics, and it always lands like it settles something. Most recently at our computational biology reading group, someone defended a model that fit the data well but had almost no mechanistic basis by saying, well, the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics, and the room nodded and moved on. I've also heard it used to end arguments about fine-tuning in physics and to wave off the question of why neural networks generalize. I gather it started as the title of an old Wigner essay, though nobody in the room has read it. I want to know what the sentence could actually be claiming. Seperate out the distinct precise theses it might be expressing: a claim about how often mathematics works, a claim about how surprising that is, a claim about what the universe must be like for that to happen, a claim about what a modeler is entitled to infer, or whatever else you find. For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or false, and say which reading the person who uses it to end an argument actually needs. Then give me a way to push back next time that is precise rather than just annoying.
