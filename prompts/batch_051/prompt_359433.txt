I've been rereading William James's *The Varieties of Religious Experience*, and I keep tripping over the move he makes near the end about mysticism. He says, roughly, that mystical states carry authority for the person who undergoes them, but they give no authority to anyone who hasn't had such an experience. So the mystic gets a kind of private evidence, and the rest of us are allowed—maybe even required—to shrug. That strikes me as a strange asymmetry. If my neighbor tells me she saw an intruder in her kitchen, her report is either good enough to get me to call the police or I treat her as unreliable; I can't comfortably say, "Well, that's real for her but not for me." What justifies granting religious experience this special first-person-only epistemic status? Is there a defensible principle here, maybe about the incommunicability of mystical states or about their transformative effects rather than their content, that survives a side-by-side with other subjective reports like hallucinations, delusions, or drug trips? Or is this just a polite way of saying mystics are deluded without having to say it? I'd like a committed answer, not a survey of positions. Take a definite stance on whether James's compromise is a real epistemic distinction or an ad hoc shield, and defend it against the obvious hard cases. At the very end, put your verdict on a single line in the form \answer{justified} or \answer{unjustified} or \answer{only-if...} with a short label.
