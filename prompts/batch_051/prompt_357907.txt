I retired a few years ago from a career in population ecology, and I've been using the free time to read decision theory and philosophy of probability. I've drifted into a position, and I want to know how exposed it actually is — not just the standard objections, but a real map of what would have to be true for me to be wrong.

The position is something like this: in any favorable gamble I'm going to be able to repeat many times — same bet, known odds, no consumption along the way — the rationally required policy is to size the stake to maximize expected log wealth, i.e. the Kelly criterion. But I'd go further: I definately don't think that's just expected utility theory with a log utility function plugged in. I think the log is doing the real normative work, and that "geometric rationality" is the primitive: options should be evaluated by their time-average growth rate, or geometric mean, whenever outcomes multiply rather than add, not by arithmetic expected value. In my old field there's a close analog — bet-hedging in fluctuating environments, where a genotype can have the highest arithmetic mean fitness but still go extinct because geometric mean fitness is what determines long-run growth. The arithmetic mean is just the wrong summary of a multiplicative process, and I suspect we've been making the same mistake in decision theory.

So here's what I want. Act as a professional decision theorist and philosopher of probability. Think carefully, brainstorm several candidate defeaters before settling on your ranking, and be honest about the parts where the view is weakest. Tell me: what would count against geometric rationality? I want the defeaters sorted from fatal to merely awkward. In particular, under what conditions does the whole doctrine collapse into ordinary expected utility with log utility, so the "geometric" framing is doing no work? Does the argument only work inside an infinite-horizon, stationary, known-odds setup, and what happens if the gambles are correlated with my labor income or with each other, or if the probabilities are ambiguous rather than crisp? What would a finite-horizon agent with intermediate consumption, or a one-shot bet, actually show against the broader claim? Does the view make any predictions that differ from plain expected utility theory, or is it observationally equivalent once all the idealizations are added? And be straight with me: has the view been qualified — "in the limit," "along a typical path," "for an idealized agent" — into unfalsifiability? If it has, is that a fatal problem, or is that just what you'd expect of a normative principle? I'd also like to know which of these defeaters a committed geometric rationalist would themselves concede counts against them, and if you had to pick one piece of evidence or one argument that would move you most, what would it be?
