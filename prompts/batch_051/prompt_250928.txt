I'll be honest, this one's a little personal: my gastroenterologist told me my gut microbiome never really recovered after a round of antibiotics, and she called me a holobiont under construction. I went down a rabbit hole reading about lichens and corals and honeybee colonies, and I want to channel all that into the final for my upper-level philosophy of biology seminar.

I want one essay question, answerable in 90 minutes, on whether holobionts, colonies, and superorganisms should count as organisms. The thing I'm trying to avoid is students reciting three criteria and then checking boxes. I want a question that makes them see how the criteria pull apart: genetic homogeneity makes a Portugese man o' war look like one big organism, but the fact that it's zooids are homologous to whole free-living polyps makes it look like a colony; functional integration and a germ-soma split make a honeybee colony look like a superorganism, but there's no single honeybee genome. And a cow with its rumen microbes is already a holobiont, so if they call the cow one organism, they have to explain why those obligate microbes with their own genomes don't force them to call the cow an ecosystem or a community.

Please write me (a) the actual exam question, (b) a model answer around 800 words, and (c) a marking scheme out of 20 with five must-hit points, the marks each is worth, and a one-line note on how candidates typically get that point wrong. The model answer should take a real stand on whether organism is a natural kind or just a useful shorthand, and it should distinguish the physiological sense of organism from the evolutionary/unit-of-selection sense without turning into a vocab list. Also, no bibliography or citations in the question — the students should argue from the cases. Difficulty should come from the crux, not from obscure examples. And please write the whole thing, question to marking scheme, in American English.
