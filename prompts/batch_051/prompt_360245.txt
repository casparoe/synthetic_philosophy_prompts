Me and a friend got into an argument about Descartes' line that animals are basically machines. His test was language: he thought the open-ended, situation-appropriate use of signs is the only sure sign that there's a thinking thing behind the behavior, and since animals don't do that — a parrot can squawk words but never answer back like a person — he concluded they're automata that can run around and cry out but have no mind. What messes with my head is that we've now built systems that do exactly the thing he took to be the mark of a mind: language models produce novel, context-appropriate language all day, and yet almost nobody wants to grant them Cartesian thought — people rush to call them next-token predictors or fancy autocomplete instead. Meanwhile, a lot of animal cognition research suggests animals do fairly impressive things like remembering particular past events, planning ahead, maybe deceiving each other, all without humanlike language.

So here's the question: does Descartes' animal-machine argument survive that state of affairs, or does the language test get hit from both directions at once — language without mind, and possibly mind without language? Walk me through which specific premise the language-model case actually attacks (the sufficiency of language as a sign of mind, its necessity, or something else), whether the argument can be repaired in some form that still draws a principled line between minded and unminded things, or whether it's just dead. I'd like your own view, not a survey. And please write it as continuous prose like an email or letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables.
