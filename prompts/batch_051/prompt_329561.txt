Consider the moral usually drawn from the behavior of the digger wasp Sphex. A female wasp stings a cricket into paralysis, drags it to the mouth of her burrow, leaves it there, and goes inside, apparently to check the burrow before dragging the cricket in. If an experimenter moves the cricket a few inches away while the wasp is inside, the wasp comes out, drags the cricket back to the threshold, and then — instead of carrying it straight in — goes inside to check again; this loop can be elicited over and over. The standard philosophical use of the story is that the wasp appears to be following an intelligent, goal-directed plan until a small perturbation reveals the behavior as a fixed routine: the wasp cannot notice that the routine has failed and cannot step back and revise its policy. The story is then turned into an argument about free agency: what matters for freedom is not an extra, indeterministic ingredient but flexibility — the capacity to notice a failed routine and change course. A free agent is, at minimum, a non-sphexish one.

Now consider a development the original discussion did not have in view. Contemporary tool-using language models are trained on a fixed objective and are, in an obvious sense, deterministic programs; nonetheless, at run time they can detect that a step has failed — a tool call returns an error, a draft contradicts itself, a plan is going nowhere — and they can backtrack, discard the failed approach, and adopt a different strategy. Under perturbation they do exactly what the sphexishness criterion seemed to ask of a flexible agent: they notice, replan, and revise, producing novel responses to novel disruptions. Yet almost no one takes this to show that they are free agents in the sense at stake in the original argument.
