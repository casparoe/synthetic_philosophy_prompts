I work in story development at a company that produces historical films and limited series, and I keep having the same fight with writers, directors, and legal. The fight is about biopics and historical dramatizations: when is it acceptable to compress chronology, merge real people into a composite, or put words in a real person's mouth? I recently had a long argument with a friend about this, and he kept returning to a passage from Plutarch's Life of Alexander, where Plutarch says:

"...a slight thing like a phrase or a jest often makes a greater revelation of character than battles where thousands fall, or the greatest armaments, or sieges of cities. Accordingly, just as painters get the likenesses in their portraits from the face and the expression of the eyes, wherein the character shows itself, but make very little account of the other parts of the body, so I must be permitted to devote myself rather to the signs of the soul in men, and by means of these to portray the life of each, leaving to others the description of their great contests."

My friend takes this as a standing license: the purpose is character, not chronicle, so invention that reveals the soul is justified. I think that is too easy. Please write a reusable decision procedure my development team could actually run on a proposed dramatization of a real, documented, dead person. The procedure should ask questions in order; say what counts as a sufficient answer to each; say where the procedure stops with a no; and mark the point where a human must decide because it is a question of taste or ethics rather than of the principle. Then run the procedure on three proposed liberties: a scene in which the subject gives a speech he never gave, a composite cousin invented to create domestic conflict, and a timeline that moves the subject's major public achievement five years earlier. Be explicit about what the procedure cannot settle. If my request rests on a confused reading of Plutarch—for example, if he was not offering a general license to dramatists at all, but describing a particular method for moral biography—tell me so plainly and correct the task rather than pretending the procedure resolves it.
