As a longtime reader of rationalist writing, I keep seeing discussions of authority and deference that settle a large question by pointing at a single case: someone cites Milgram to show that ordinary people obey too much, or someone cites an expert consensus to show that laypeople simply should defer. Those cases pull in opposite directions, and I have become convinced that what is missing is an explicit specification of what an adequate account of justified deference would have to get right. I want that specification before reading any further defenses or criticisms of particular views.

Write the desiderata for a theory of when a person is justified in deferring to authority, where authority is understood broadly enough to cover institutional command, expert guidance, and role-based rights to decide. The intended use is to guide a reflective adult deciding whether to go along with a directive or a consensus in an organization, a profession, a courtroom, a laboratory, or a state.

For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that forces it and state explicitly whether it is non-negotiable, a preference of a particular camp, or a contested choice that different serious theories could make differently. Cover at least the following, adding anything else that falls naturally out of it:

- The distinctions an account may not collapse: believing someone versus obeying someone; obeying an instruction versus accepting someone's standing to settle a question without fully explaining it; legitimacy versus the weight of the reasons one has to comply in a particular case; and the fact that compliance was achieved versus the claim that compliance was warranted.
- The ordinary cases: traffic rules and safety codes, a physician's or engineer's recommendation, a manager's decision binding a team, a jury or court settling a dispute, a military order under time pressure, and a regulator or review board deciding what may proceed.
- The hard cases: the Milgram volunteer who continues to administer shocks at the experimenter's prodding; the functionary who processes one small step in a bureaucracy aimed at atrocity, of the sort Arendt's report on the Eichmann trial tried to understand; the soldier ordered to do something lawful but arguably wrong; the whistleblower who breaks with the institution; the conscientious objector; and the layperson facing a credentialed consensus that later turns out to have been corrupted by capture, bias, or groupthink.
- The explanatory constraints: whether and how a directive can give a reason in virtue of its source rather than its content, why an authority's standing and its reliability can come apart, and how an autonomous agent can bind herself to a decision procedure without thereby losing responsibility for what the procedure does.
- The psychological constraints: whatever the theory says must be compatible with what the obedience experiments and the study of conformity, motivated reasoning, and the diffusion of responsibility show about actual agents. It should not presuppose that people deliberate from first principles at every step.
- The moral boundary constraints: the account must not treat clearly wrongful directives as merely competing considerations, must explain why criminal orders do not become permissible by transmission through a chain of command, and must explain in what sense a subordinate inside a hierarchy remains a moral agent for whose actions it still makes sense to ask who was responsible.

Then give the structural result. Identify any subset of these desiderata that appears jointly unsatisfiable, or satisfiable only by quietly abandoning one of them. I am most interested in whether the demand that laypeople defer to well-supported expert consensus and the demand that individuals remain answerable for what they do can be fully met by a single rule, and whether a Raz-style service conception or a consent view can earn content-independent authority without reducing the subordinate to an instrument of the authority's will.

Finally, provide a compressed assessment of the main existing families of views — voluntarist or command views, consent or contract views, service-based views of the sort associated with Raz, purely epistemic-deference accounts, and role-obligation or practice-based views — against the list. Keep this last part short and let the criteria carry the argument; if one requirement does the most to eliminate candidates, identify it.
