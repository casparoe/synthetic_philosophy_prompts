At a meeting of our parish council last month, someone arguing against a trial of a new low-emission waste incinerator closed their case with the words "better safe than sorry." It carried the room, but I have been turning the phrase over ever since, because the same four words seem to be available for quite different jobs. A neighbour uses them to mean we should wait until a technology has been proved completely harmless before allowing it anywhere near the village. A friend at work uses them to mean almost the opposite of doing nothing: we should take out insurance and run the cheap early tests even though we cannot yet show what the danger is. And another version, which I hear in news discussions about medicines and pesticides, seems to treat being cautious as proof of virtue and being willing to accept risk as proof of carelessness, almost regardless of what the risk actually is. I have since gathered that "the precautionary principle" is the polite name for this style of thinking, but its official statements seem to pull in different directions, and I cannot tell whether the principle is anything more than the proverb wearing a suit.

I would like you to prise the sentence apart for me. What are the distinct precise claims that "better safe than sorry" could be expressing? For each, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible principle, or something that is actually false or incoherent when stated carefully. I am especially interested in the readings that have real work to do in arguments about regulating new technologies: the idea that inaction is safer by default, the idea that the burden of proof should fall on the person proposing the new thing rather than on the person worried about it, the idea that only the worst possible outcome matters, and the idea that avoiding a harm always outweighs achieving a benefit. Please also say which of these readings would actually carry the argument my parish councillor was making, and which ones the phrase smuggles in without being argued for. Please write your answer in British English.
