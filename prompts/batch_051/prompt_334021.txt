One of my friends has a set of firm verdicts about when people owe it to others to be legible -- to make their feelings, intentions, or state of mind readable, rather than merely not lying. Her track record so far: a partner who says "I'm fine" while visibly fuming and then resents the other for not guessing is doing something wrong; she should say what is wrong or that she cannot talk about it yet. An acquaintance at a party who answers "fine, thanks" while privately falling apart has not wronged anyone; nobody is owed your grief at a party. An employee quietly looking for another job does not have to tell their manager first. A person who uses deadpan irony about a deadline with a literal-minded coworker, knowing the coworker will take them straight, is being misleading and not just unfunny. A parent who goes silent instead of explaining why they are angry wrongs their adult child. And a person with a naturally low-expressive face and voice does not owe it to strangers to perform more warmth than they have.

Now the new case: she has begun sharing an office with a colleague who reads her quiet, absorbed work style as lingering anger about a disagreement they had. The colleague is growing anxious and keeps apologizing. She knows this, and two sentences would clear it up without changing anything else about how she works. Predict how she will rule, and whether she would say the colleague is owed those two sentences.

Beyond the prediction: what principle best fits her track record -- a duty only against deceiving, a duty reserved for close relationships, a low-cost legibility rule, or something else -- and which alternative principles fit the earlier rulings just as well? Give the strongest competing reading, and specify the single further case that would cleanly decide between your top two.
