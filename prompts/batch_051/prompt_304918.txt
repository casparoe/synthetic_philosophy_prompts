You're a philosopher who works on decision theory and political philosophy—the Rawls/Harsanyi debate especially. I've been arguing with my partner about this and I'd like you to think it through carefully; maybe brainstorm a few candidate justifications before settling on the strongest one.

Here's the puzzle. Most people I ask think it's fine, or at least no one's business, for a person to sink their entire savings into a startup with a 90% chance of losing everything and a 10% chance of making them rich, as long as its expected value is positive and they understand the odds—your money, your gamble. But change only one thing. A small-town council is deciding what to do with the town's reserve fund. Same choice, same probabilities, same positive expected value: a safe investment with a modest return versus a risky venture that would probably wipe the fund out and force cuts to schools and pensions. Now almost everyone says the council would be reckless, maybe even negligent, to take the bet. The only thing that changes is that the decision is made by some people on behalf of others.

That can't just come down to the idea that you may gamble with your own welfare but not with other people's, because nobody demands that councils be maximally cautious about everything: towns routinely approve road designs that trade a small expected number of traffic deaths for convenience and cost savings, and that looks like expected-value reasoning about other people's lives. So we seem to allow expected-utility thinking about diffuse, modest harms to others, but switch to maximin-like caution when the downside is catastrophic and the decision is a one-shot. Is there a principle that actually justifies the difference between the individual gambler and the town council—and between the council's reserve fund and the road design? Do Rawls's arguments for maximin behind the veil and Harsanyi's Bayesian argument against it map onto these asymmetries at all, or are our judgments just an inconsistent mix of accountability, loss-aversion, and availability? Would minimax regret do better than maximin at capturing what makes the reserve-fund case feel different? Tell me the strongest principle you can find that saves the asymmetry, test it against nearby cases, and give an honest verdict on whether what's left is something a coherent decision theory should endorse.
