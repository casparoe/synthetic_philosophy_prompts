You are a philosopher of mind working on functionalism and the multiple realizability debate. I'm preparing a handout for a graduate seminar on the metaphysics of the special sciences, and I want you to write, in order of importance, the desiderata that an adequate theory of realization must meet. By 'theory of realization' I mean an account of what it is for a lower-level system to realize a functional or special-science property: which features of the realizing system matter, how realizers should be individuated, when two physically different systems count as realizing the same higher-level property, and what explanatory work the realization relation is supposed to do. For our purposes, such a theory is adequate if it explains how a higher-level kind can be genuinely multiply realized without collapsing into triviality and without losing its standing as a real explanatory kind.

The phenomenon your list must be responsive to is this. Psychological and other special-science predicates apply across systems whose underlying physical arrangements differ dramatically: a human, an octopus, a Martian with an alien biochemistry, and a suitably organized silicon system seem capable of sharing a mental state. That is the intuitive core of multiple realizability. But the same observation creates two standing threats. If realizers are individuated very finely, the supposedly shared higher-level kind fragments into a disjunction of idiosyncratic lower-level kinds with no scientific unity. If realizers are individuated very coarsely, the realization relation becomes cheap: nearly any system can be mapped onto nearly any computation, and functionalism falls into a trivial pancomputationalism. An adequate theory of realization has to walk that line.

Now write the list of requirements such a theory must satisfy, each with the case or consideration that motivates it. For each requirement, classify it as non-negotiable — a theory that fails it is not a theory of realization at all — or as a negotiable commitment of a particular camp (reductionists, functionalists, defenders of autonomous special sciences), which a rival may reasonably reject. Then answer the two questions I most care about: whether some subset of the non-negotiable requirements is jointly unsatisfiable, and which single requirement does the most work in excluding existing candidate positions. Please think through the desiderata carefully before you write; I want the weight to fall on the criteria themselves and the cases behind them, not on a scorecard of named positions.
