Suppose a jurisdiction that now applies a strict ignorance rule—a person can be convicted for violating a regulation even if they neither knew of it nor had any realistic way of learning about it—considers a reform. A new Office of Legal Intelligibility would assign every statute, regulation, and guidance document that carries civil or criminal penalties a discoverability score from 0 to 100 at the time of enactment. The score would be built from four components: (1) publicity, measuring whether a plain-language summary appears on a free government website and in at least two nationally visible general-interest outlets; (2) moral attunement, measuring whether the conduct is malum in se (an ordinary person would recognize it as wrong on its own, such as assault) or malum prohibitum (wrong only because a rule says so, such as a licensing or filing requirement), with malum prohibitum lowering the score; (3) complexity, measuring the length and number of cross-references in the provision; and (4) audience, measuring the size and expertise of the class to whom the provision is addressed, so that a rule aimed at licensed professionals counts as more discoverable for those professionals than for the general public.

In any prosecution under this regime, a defendant's ignorance of—or lack of realistic access to—the provision would be a complete defense if the score is below 40. If the score is between 40 and 70, good-faith ignorance would reduce the maximum available penalty by half but would not defeat liability. If the score is above 70, the traditional strict rule would continue to apply. The score would be displayed at the head of every published provision, would be presumptive but rebuttable by evidence about how the law would appear to someone in the defendant's circumstances, and would be revised every two years.
