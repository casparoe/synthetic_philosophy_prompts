I have been reading John Stuart Mill's On Liberty for a class on political philosophy, and I keep getting stuck on two moments in the same opening chapter that seem to pull against each other. Early in the chapter Mill sets out what is usually called the harm principle: the only legitimate ground for exercising power over someone against his will is to prevent harm to other people, and doing something because it would be good for the person himself is never enough. Yet later in that same chapter he introduces a large exception. He says the whole doctrine applies only to people whose faculties have reached maturity, and that for backward states of society, where he compares the population to children, despotism is a legitimate mode of government so long as the end is their improvement and the means actually achieve that end. That sounds exactly like the paternalism the harm principle was meant to forbid: ruling people for their own good rather than to prevent harm to others.

The tension feels sharper to me because Mill spent most of his working life as an official of the East India Company and generally defended British rule in India as a project of improvement. So the theorist who insists that each individual is the best judge of his own good seems willing to suspend that principle for entire colonized populations, on grounds that would never be accepted for individuals within a free society. I want to know whether these two passages can honestly be reconciled. Is Mill being consistent because he genuinely thinks the harm principle never applied to people he regards as not yet capable of self-direction, or is that just special pleading, defining the subjects of empire as exceptions before the argument even begins? Could he mean that despotism over such societies prevents harm to others rather than merely benefiting them? Or did he simply abandon his principle when it came to the colonies? I am asking for the strongest version of each reconciliation and a judgment about which one does the least damage to the text, and about whether any of them leaves Mill's liberalism intact.
