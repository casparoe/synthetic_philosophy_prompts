I retired a few years ago after a long career in physical chemistry—mostly spectroscopy, which has nothing to do with any of this—and I've been spending my retirement trying to read some philosophy properly. Lately I can't stop thinking about William James. From what I understand, James thought consciousness isn't a special substance or a private theater inside the head; it's something the stream of experience does, a way of taking account of itself, not a stuff it's made of. He also kept asking what practical difference a belief would make, and he had a lot to say about attention, selectivity, and the feeling of effort. I've been wondering: what would James have made of the claim that a large language model has experiences? He died in 1910, so the question never came up for him, but his way of thinking feels almost built for it.

The part I can't settle is this. On the one hand, James seems like he'd be more open to machine experience than a lot of philosophers, since he didn't think the underlying material mattered as much as what the system does. On the other hand, he cared a great deal about the continuity of thought and about attention as a felt, effortful selecting among possibilities. A transformer isn't a continuous stream at all: the network's state is recomputed fresh at each token, and between prompts there's nothing going on, no persisting inner life, no felt effort or selective interest in James's sense. I genuinely don't know whether he'd treat that as a fatal difference or as mere prejudice about implentation. What do you think he would have concluded? I'd like an actual prediction, not a he'd-see-both-sides hedge. Give me the Jamesian argument you think would carry the day for him, and tell me where he'd probably draw the line.
