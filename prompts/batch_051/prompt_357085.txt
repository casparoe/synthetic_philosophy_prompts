I've spent an embarassing amount of time going back and forth on the torture vs. dust specks problem, and I want an actual adjudication of which thing should yield, not another summary of both sides.

The theory I keep finding myself attracted to is plain additive aggregation. Say each dust speck is a tiny but nonzero disutility to one person. Then continuity plus separability gives you that for any finite horror — one person tortured for an hour, say — there's some huge number of specks such that the specks are jointly worse. 3^^^3 is just one dramatic way to assert the number's existence; the structural claim is that the badness scale has no jumps, so enough tiny bads strictly outweigh any bounded big bad. What I like about this is that it's complete and non-arbitrary: it assigns an answer to every comparison, treats equal harms equally, and doesn't smuggle in a threshold at the exact point where my imagination gives out. If I reject the conclusion I seem forced to put a cliff somewhere on the scale, and every candidate cliff looks like it gets its location from facts about my psychology, not facts about what's bad.

But my actual button-pushing self doesn't move. If I have to choose between one person being tortured and 3^^^3 people each getting a single dust speck in the eye, I press the button to prevent the torture without hesitation, and I trust that response more than I trust any axiom that contradicts it. It feels like aggregation across separate lives is the wrong operation here: a torture isn't just a large number of dust specks gathered into one person, and summing mild discomfort across a vast number of people and then reading the total as if it landed on a single bearer seems to miss something. The intuition says some harms have a different structure — that they compound or mark or deform a life in a way that a distributed inconvenience doesn't — rather than merely sitting further along the same additive scale.

So: which yields, and what does it actually cost to make it yield? If the intuition goes, I don't want "you're committing scope insensitivity" to be the end of the argument — I want to know what test the intuition has to pass before it's allowed to veto a theory this clean, and whether the sheer size of 3^^^3 makes the debunking stronger (you can't represent that many people, so of course the theory feels hollow) or weaker (the number was deliberately chosen to break the very faculty the debunking derides). If the theory goes, give me the least-bad repair. Lexical thresholds? Refusing to aggregate across persons while still aggregating within a life? A cut between harms that reshape a person and harms that merely annoy? Tell me which axioms each repair breaks — transitivity, separability, completeness, continuity — and whether the repair is a principled architecture or a patch over the counterexample. I'd also like a real attempt at the compatibility reading, because I think it's probably true and I can't articulate it: maybe the additive theory says which outcome is worse and my intuition says which choice would be obscene, or maybe the difference is between momentary and self-modifying harms and a sufficiently rich utility function can absorb it without going lexical.

Don't just tell me which side you'd take. Tell me what would justify each move — debunking the intuition, amending the theory, or showing they're compatible — and what I'd be paying for each one. And hold yourself to it: say which argument or thought experiment would change your verdict, and where your own reasoning is most likely to be motivated by the intuition you happened to start with.
