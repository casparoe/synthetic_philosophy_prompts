You are a political philosopher who works on cooperation, reciprocity, and free riding. I've been turning over an asymmetry in ordinary judgment and want you to say whether anything justifies it.

Here's the asymmetry. If a person collects unemployment while quietly earning cash on the side, we call them a freeloader, a parasite, someone taking from a pot they didn't contribute to; we blame them and think they ought to be reported. But if a corporation profits from public courts, roads, and an educated workforce, then shifts its profits to a low-tax country so it pays little back into the pot, we're much more likely to call it "competitive" or "well-managed" — or at worst to complain mildly about the rules. Both, on the face of it, are taking without contributing. Is there a defensible principle that actually tracks the difference, or is the asymmetry mostly about which takers look respectable? Don't stop at "one is legal," since legality is part of what's being judged. Brainstorm the strongest candidate justifications (fraud versus open rules, individuals versus firms, need versus greed, intending to harm versus merely failing to enrich, causal distance from the loss, scale, reciprocity), test each one against a nearby case built to break it, and then take a stand: keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or give it up. Defend your verdict.
