So my neighborhood assocation is trying to launch a local currency—paper notes accepted at the corner stores, the coffee shop, and the Sunday farmer's market, with a stamp that makes each note expire after three months so people spend it rather than stash it. It's turned into this whole group-chat fight. One friend, who studied econ, says it isn't real money because you can't pay taxes or rent with it and the city won't back it; another friend keeps saying all money is made up, that's the whole lesson of fiat currency, so if enough of us agree to treat the notes as payment, they're money and the expiring part is just a feature.

This week I was reading Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics for a class and hit this line in Book V: "But demand has come to be conventionally represented by money; this is why money is called nomisma (customary currency), because it does not exist by nature but by custom (nomos), and can be altered and rendered useless at will." That seems to settle it in favor of my second friend—money exists by custom, not by nature, and it can be made useless whenever we decide. But right before this Aristotle says demand is the real standard that holds exchange together, and money only represents demand conventionally. So now I can't tell what he'd say about our three-month notes. Would he think any convention a community agrees on counts as money, or does a local currency like ours fail at the specific job he thinks money exists to do? I'd like someone to walk through the logic of the passage and tell me which friend it actually supports, if either.
