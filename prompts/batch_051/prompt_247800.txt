I'm putting together a workshop on early Chinese ethics and I want the centerpiece to be an adversarial-collaboration document between a committed Mohist and a committed Confucian. The Mohist holds jian'ai, impartial care: you should care about everyone's well-being without automatically privileging your own family, because partiality is the root of war, theft, and most other social damage. The Confucian holds graded love: care naturally starts with parents and children and ripples outward, and a good life, a good family, and a good state are all built on those special bonds, so filial devotion is a virtue rather than a mere convenience. The two sides both think they can explain what the other side values, but they keep accusing each other of being cold (the Confucian on the Mohist) or corrupt (the Mohist on the Confucian).

Draft the joint statement such a collaboration would produce, in two parts. First, a numbered list of claims both can sign — not just ceasefire generalities like 'we both want people to flourish,' but the substantive things they genuinely agree on once they stop charicaturing each other. What exactly would they both say counts as a society failing, or a person failing, and where does each side admit the other is tracking something real? Second, a disagreements table: each row has the precise residual disagreement, a Mohist wording and a Confucian wording that each side would sign for its own position, and the crux sub-question such that settling it would move at least one of them. After the table, give two test cases both sides agree would bear on the disagreement — one that puts each side under the most pressure, if you can find one — with each side's prediction for the case written down in advance. Finally tell me which part of their dispute, if any, is merely verbal.

The thing I care most about is fair wording: neither side should be able to say 'that's not what we believe.' And don't leave the disagreement at the slogan level ('impartial versus partial'). Push it down into cases: is it ever wrong to spare your own son a punishment you would want imposed on a stranger? Is a good person who ignores his own mother to feed a stranger's mother making a moral mistake or just an unusual choice? Those are the kind of splits where the two sides actually come apart.
