Theories of valid consent usually advertise themselves by the cases they handle; I want to invert that. Lay out the desiderata that any adequate account of valid consent must satisfy, with the case or consideration behind each, before any particular account is scored. By valid consent I mean the conditions under which one person's agreement renders another's act permissible when it would otherwise be wrong—in sex, medicine, participation in research, contracts, and the ordinary permissions of daily life, such as who may touch, photograph, search, or question a person. The standard should be usable for both moral evaluation and the design of legal or institutional rules, but it should not be a summary of any actual jurisdiction's rules.

For each desideratum, state the requirement, give the case that forces it, and mark it negotiable or non-negotiable. Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; if you also grade the main families of view—ones that treat consent as a private mental act, as a public communicative act, as a hybrid, or as a fair transaction—do so only after the list is fixed, and briefly. Flag any subset of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and identify the single requirement that does the most work excluding candidate theories.

The list should address at least the following, along with anything else you think belongs. The three canonical invalidators—deception, coercion, and deficient competence—and whether they are failures of one underlying condition or three independent ones, including the border between coercion and a merely hard choice or constrained bargaining position. The distinction between lying to obtain a yes and merely withholding information the other person would have wanted, and whether the deceived person's own priorities determine what counts as material. Cases in which a person's private mental state and outward signals come apart: secret reservations, feigned willingness, or sincere assent induced by a third party's misdescription. The temporal shape of consent: withdrawal during an ongoing act, regret afterward, and advance consent to acts that cannot all be enumerated in advance. Consent to acts that deliberately set back the consenting party's interests, such as surgery, combat sports, or consensual practices involving pain or marks, and how a theory can explain the limits of such consent without collapsing into paternalism. Whether the criteria are uniform across contexts or legitimately domain-specific. And whether the resulting criteria can actually be applied in real time by an ordinarily attentive person, or only in retrospect by an adjudicator. Choose cases precise enough to separate one criterion from its neighbors.
