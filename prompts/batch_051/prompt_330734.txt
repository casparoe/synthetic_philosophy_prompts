A seminar of mine has reached an impasse on the concept of privacy, and I should like a definition that does better than the standard moves. Provide a working definition of privacy — or, if you find it cleaner, of what it is for a person's privacy to be violated — precise enough to yield a determinate verdict on each of the cases below. Then apply your definition to every case in turn, noting where it gives what you take to be the wrong answer, where it gives no answer, and where it forces you either to revise the definition or to defend a surprising verdict.

The cases are these:

1. A housemate finds a diary under someone's bed and reads it cover to cover.
2. A stranger on a bus notices the title of the book you are reading, remembers it, and later mentions it to a mutual acquaintance.
3. A supermarket records each item a customer buys through a loyalty card and sells the resulting profile to a data broker; the customer ticked the terms without reading them.
4. A colleague, seeing a party-political sticker on your laptop, revises their belief about whom you voted for.
5. A friend asks you, with no preamble, whether your marriage is in difficulty.
6. A police helicopter hovers over a two-metre fence to observe what a householder is doing in a back garden that is not visible from the street.
7. A researcher publishes a medical dataset after removing names and addresses, though a determined person could re-identify some patients from the remaining fields.
8. A government releases census data aggregated by local district.
9. A parent reads all the text messages on their fifteen-year-old child's phone while the child is asleep.
10. A doctor informs a patient's employer that the patient is having an affair.

I am confident that (1) and (10) are invasions of privacy and that (2) and (8) are not, though my reasons differ across the four; I genuinely do not know what to say about (5), (6), and (9). The definition should not simply equate privacy with secrecy or with control over information about oneself, since several of the cases have been chosen precisely because they break one or both of those equations. If you conclude that the ordinary term is doing more than one job, say what the jobs are and which cases move between them.
