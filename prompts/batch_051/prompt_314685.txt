Two members of my reading group, Mara and Dev, argued through most of our meeting last week over a passage from a short survey of classical Indian epistemology, and afterward both asked me to referee. The survey introduced the Nyāya doctrine of the pramāṇas, the accredited sources of knowledge, which Nyāya standardly lists as four: perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna), analogy (upamāna), and verbal testimony (śabda). It also sketched the long-running dispute between Nyāya and its critics over whether testimonial knowledge is a distinct pramāṇa in its own right or a derivative case of inference. Dev took the reductive side; Mara defended the Nyāya claim that testimony is its own source. I wrote the exchange down as faithfully as I could.

(1) Dev opened with the familiar reduction. When one believes a report, one is reasoning from two things — that the speaker said p, and that speakers of that kind are reliable about matters of that kind — to the conclusion p. The standing reliability premise carries the justificatory weight, so the knowledge is inferential, and no distinct source remains.

(2) Mara replied that this misdescribes how uptake works. A child learning word meanings from a parent, or a traveler accepting a stranger's directions, can arrive at knowledge without any prior record of reliability — indeed without any record at all. Understanding a competent assertion yields the knowledge straightaway; the inference from reliability is an optional, later reconstruction rather than the source of the warrant.

(3) Dev answered that the child case shows only that early testimonial warrant may be a default or entitled status, close to perception. It does not establish a separate pramāṇa. Once a hearer has accumulated correlations about how speakers perform, the justification for accepting new testimony is inferential, even when its steps are not consciously unfolded. The child's entitlement is a precondition for later inferential competence, not evidence of an independent channel.

(4) Mara responded that the mature case fares no better. A hearer can gain knowledge from an assertion at the moment of understanding it, before any reliability premise is supplied; and often no usable generalization is available. Testimony can communicate a unique, unrepeatable event from a witness one trusts on grounds that do not generalize to the event type; when no regularity between witness and event kind can stand ready, the inferential schema has no purchase, yet the knowledge is still transmitted.

(5) Dev came back by noting that justification need not be rehearsed and may be purely dispositional, so unconscious inference is no problem. And the appeal to trusting the witness on other grounds hands the reductivist a premise: those grounds are an inductive base about the witness. The uniqueness of the event is irrelevant because the operative generalization concerns the witness, not the type of event.

(6) Mara ended by arguing that this maneuver stretches the word inference until it covers any reliable disposition to move from evidence to belief, making the reduction merely verbal. A real account of testimonial knowledge must say what happens when a speaker's competence and act of asserting pass warrant to a hearer, not just relabel the phenomenon afterwards. If inference is defined that broadly, the distinctness claim is left standing and the subject has been changed.

I could not decide, and neither would concede. Please adjudicate this exchange move by move. For each numbered move, say which claims stand, which need qualification, and which fail, with brief reasoning. Then give an overall verdict: who is right about which specific points, and who has the better position on whether testimony is a distinct pramāṇa? Finally, identify a shared assumption behind the whole dispute that both sides ought to question.
