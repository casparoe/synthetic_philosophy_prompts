I'm a TA for an epistemology course and I'm grading a batch of short-answer papers. Here's the question I set and what one student wrote. Give the answer a numerical grade out of 20, and give me the specific, point-by-point comments you'd write to justify that grade—where the student earns marks and where the reasoning actually goes wrong, if it does. I don't need reading suggestions, just the grade and the comments.

Question (short answer, about three paragraphs): Some critics say that as we outsource cognitive work to AI—diagnosis, navigation, coding, legal research—we may get more accurate results while understanding the relevant domain less and less. Skeptics reply that this is no different in kind from calculators, textbooks, or relying on expert testimony, none of which we usually regard as objectionable erosions of understanding. Is the AI case importantly different? Take a definite position and defend it.

Student answer: The anti-AI worry is usually about deskilling, and that part is right. When you rely on a navigation app for every trip you loose your sense of the city; when an assistant writes your code you stop getting better at debugging. Understanding a domain is partly an ability, and abilities decay without exercise. The continuity reply is also right up to a point. Calculators made mental arithmetic rarer, but we do not think calculators are destructive; books and testimony already mean that none of us individually understands most of what we depend on. I don't understand how the chips in my phone work, but I am reliable about them because I trust the engineers.

Still, I think there is a real difference. A calculator or a textbook is transparent in the sense that you could, in principle, unpack and recover the reasoning that produced the output, and a good teacher or text transmits reasons along with conclusions. An AI typically gives you an answer with no reasons attached, and in many cases the process behind it is opaque even to its designers. So AI dependence can leave you permanently unable to reconstruct the basis of your own judgments in a way older tools did not.

But here is the part I want to insist on. Since the AI is reliable, beliefs I form by accepting its outputs are formed by a reliable process, so they amount to knowledge. And understanding just is having an organized body of knowledge. So the individual user does still understand the domain—only in a distributed or extended way, with part of the cognitive work happening outside their head. Therefore the real worry is not a loss of understanding but a practical one: if the system goes down, we cannot recover the skills and knowledge we used to have. That is a risk of catastrophic dependence, not an epistemic loss.
