I'm a novelist. I've been drafting a book where a character blind from birth gets her sight restored midway through, which sent me down the Molyneux's problem rabbit hole, and around the same time, for an AI subplot, I was reading about multimodal machine learning, and the two things have gotten tangled in my head. Molyneux asks, roughly: if someone can tell a cube from a sphere by touch, and then gains sight, would they recognize which is which the first time they look? The question seems to be whether shape knowledge is stored in a modality-specific code (a feel, an image) or in something amodal that either sense can read. Multimodal models look as though they're engineered around an answer to exactly this: you train them so that different sensory streams — images, text, increasingly touch-like signals — get mapped to a shared embedding, and then recognition transfers across modalities. So Molyneux's puzzle starts to look like a very old version of cross-modal representation learning.

I cant tell if this is a genuine identity of structure or just me pattern-matching on the phrase 'shared representation.' If it is real: what would have to be true about the blind person's tactile shape knowledge, and about how the model aligns its modalities, for the analogy to be deep rather than loose? And if you had to name one place where the parallel predicts something false — one concrete point where the human case and the model case come apart — what would it be? My own suspicion is the training signal: the model gets paired examples across modalities, while the character has only ever had touch, with no cross-modal supervision at all; but maybe that's less fatal than it looks.
