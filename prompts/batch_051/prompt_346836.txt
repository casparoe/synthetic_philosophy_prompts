I am a novelist, planning a book whose design depends on deliberately inducing and then controlling imaginative resistance: the way a reader will accept a story's factual stipulations — magic, faster-than-light travel, a talking dog — yet refuse to accept an evaluative stipulation, such as the narration's assertion that a character's cruelty was right, as true within the fiction. Before I read any more accounts of why this happens, I want the specification on its own: what would a theory of imaginative resistance have to get right to be useful to a novelist who wants to predict, steer, and occasionally circumvent the effect, rather than merely name it.

Write the desiderata the theory must meet. Each requirement should come with the case or consideration that forces it. At minimum, the list must cover: the basic asymmetry between factual and evaluative stipulations; the difference between a character's asserting a repugnant view and the fiction's presenting that view as true (narrator included); why readers comfortably imagine merely alien or distant value systems but balk at repugnant ones, so the phenomenon is a gradient, not a switch; whether resistance is triggered by moral evaluation only or by the whole class of thick, response-dependent judgments (beautiful, funny, delicious, honorable); how genre, irony, parody, and unreliable narration can dissolve resistance; the pressure to reinterpret a resistant passage rather than flatly reject it; and whether resistance is a failure of imagination, a refusal to import a commitment into the fiction's truth, or an imaginative success followed by refusal to engage. For each desideratum, mark it as non-negotiable or as a preference of a particular theoretical camp, with a sentence on why.

Then commit. Identify the single requirement on your list that does the most work in excluding the standard families of account, and say which family or families it excludes. Name the pair of desiderata, if any, that you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and say what that tension would mean in practice for someone writing against it. Finally, give your own view of the shape a theory must take to meet your non-negotiables, and tell me where in that view you are least confident. I want a definite, defendable position and its costs, not a menu.

For context: the novel is narrated in the second person, and early on the narrator asks the reader to accept, within the fiction, that the protagonist's betrayal of a lifelong friend was not merely necessary but the right and fine thing to have done. Later, what the novel can truthfully say happened depends on whether the reader granted that stipulation. That is why I need the real levers, not the labels.
