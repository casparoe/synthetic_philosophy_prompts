Someone in my decision-theory reading group has this argument and I can't find the fault in it, though the conclusion looks mad.

(1) A threat can give me a reason to comply only if complying makes the threatened outcome less likely.
(2) When the threatener cannot observe or be affected by what I actually do, and has already acted on a prediction of my choice, complying doesn't make the threatened outcome less likely—whether the harm is carried out is settled by the prediction, and nothing I do now can shift it.
(3) So such 'acausal' threats give me no reason to comply.
(4) For exactly the same reason, adopting a surrogate goal purely because someone in that position wants me to have it is never rational: my having the goal now can't make that person's favourable action more likely either.
(5) But by the same reasoning, in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma against a perfect duplicate of myself—identical in every relevant respect, so we reliably make the same choice, yet with no causal channel between our actions—I have no reason to cooperate, because my cooperating can't make my duplicate cooperate.
(6) That can't be right: if your exact duplicate would cooperate just when you do, defecting looks insane. A principle that makes cooperation with your own duplicate irrational has cost more than I'm willing to pay.

Which step should I give up, and what do I pay for it? I'd most like to keep (1), because its what I'd say to people who treat horoscopes as threats, and I'm certain I want to keep (6), but I don't see how to keep both.
