I spent my career as a natural scientist, mostly on life-history evolution and senescence, and I've only recently started reading philosophy seriously. I just worked through Cicero's little book on old age (De Senectute), where old Cato answers the usual complaints: old age takes you out of public affairs, weakens the body, drains away pleasure, and puts you near death. What struck me is how similiar Cato's central move is to the way evolutionary biology treats senescence.

Cato's line, roughly, is that the body's decay is not the real loss, because the body's strengths and appetites were never the goods that mattered; age mostly detaches you from drives that were overated anyway, and if you keep using your mind it stays with you. Aging, on this view, isn't a breakdown — it's a normal and in some ways useful final phase.

The biology seems to say the same thing in its own vocabulary: senescence is not just accumulated damage or a machine wearing out. It is at least partly the expected byproduct of selection optimizing the early part of the life history. Mutations with late-acting bad effects are only weakly selected against (mutation accumulation); genes that boost early reproduction at the price of later health get favored (antagonistic pleiotropy); and organisms divide limited resources between reproduction and maintenance of the body, so after the reproductive peak the soma is, so to speak, allowed to decay (disposable soma). On both sides the decline is not a defect relative to what the system is in the business of doing — it falls out of the system being organized around something else: the mind's proper activity for Cato, reproductive fitness for the biology.

I can't decide whether this is a real structural parallel or just a verbal one. Both amount to saying that the decay is natural rather than a malfunction, but Cicero is making a normative claim about which human goods are real, while the evolutionary theory is a descriptive claim about gene frequencies. What would have to be true on both sides for the parallel to be deep rather than loose? And here's a worry that feels like a test case: the biology predicts that whatever is expensive to maintain should decline late, including the brain, so cognition ought to senesce too — exactly where Cato says the old person need not decline. Does that show the analogy breaks, or just that Cato overstated an empirical point? Or, more sharply: is Cato treating the late-life body as sacrificed for the mind in anything like the way the soma is sacrificed for the germline, and is that sacrifice the same kind of explanation in the two cases?
