I'm a retired condensed matter physicist, and after years of renormalization-group work I've started reading metaphysics (Parmenides, Spinoza, the priority-monism debates — the terminology still somtimes gets away from me). Here's the parallel I can't get out of my head. In effective field theory you don't need the high-energy theory to do low-energy physics: heavy stuff decouples, irrelevant operators get suppressed, and each scale has it's own effective degrees of freedom — phonons, quasiparticles, order parameters, whatever — that are treated as perfectly real for the phenomena at that scale. Nobody actually rebuilds the phonon from the fundamental theory before using it, and nobody thinks that makes the phonon bogus. The scales look autonomous.

That smells like pluralism about what there is: no single privileged inventory of the world, just a stack of inventories at different scales, each with its own furniture, none of them more 'really real' than the others. But the standard physicist line is that effective theories are merely approximations and only the deepest theory is ontologically serious — which would make the whole stack compatible with a kind of monism. And the philosophers I'm reading divide the same way: monists say there's one basic thing (the One, the one substance, the whole cosmos) and everything else is derivative; pluralists say there are many basic things.

So is this a real structural identity between renormalization-group decoupling and pluralism, or am I just pattern-matching a fact about predictive success onto a claim about what exists? I'd like someone to tell me: what exactly would a pluralist have to add to the physics for the EFT picture to be genuinely pluralist, and where does the analogy break down — for instance because all the effective descriptions are consistent with a single underlying fundamental theory they approximate? What in the physics would have to be true for the scales to be autonomous in a metaphysical sense and not just a computational one?
