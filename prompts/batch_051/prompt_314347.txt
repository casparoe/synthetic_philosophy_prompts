You are a philosopher of mathematics who works on the foundations of set theory and on non-classical logic. Work through the question carefully; before you commit to a list, brainstorm the candidate requirements and then prune anything that is really just one camp's private preference.

I keep seeing proposed answers to Russell's paradox judged one criterion at a time: ZFC gives up the universal set, paraconsistent set theory has to revise the logic, New Foundations has a stratified comprehension principle that strikes many as ad hoc. I want the evaluation framework before the evaluations. Write the full specification: what any adequate theory of sets, or any adequate response to the paradoxes of naive set theory, must deliver. For each requirement, give the motivating case or consideration.

Be sure your specification covers these four areas.

First, the pressure tests: Russell's paradox, Cantor's paradox, Burali-Forti, and Curry's paradox. Say what each one forces a theory to handle. On Curry, I want you to explain why the problem is not essentially about negation: with an unrestricted comprehension principle and the usual rules for the conditional, including the structural rule of contraction, triviality follows even with no negation in sight. Then say what that implies about which building blocks a theory may keep together.

Second, the distinctions it must not collapse: set versus proper class versus property; a principled restriction on comprehension versus a gerrymandered list of banned predicates; keeping classical logic whole versus revising negation versus keeping negation but weakening the structural rules; extensional versus intensional criteria of identity.

Third, the material it must preserve: enough of ordinary mathematical practice — arithmetic, analysis, transfinite arithmetic, functions, powersets, quotients — that the "solution" is not just a consistent way of emptying the subject. Also respect the metatheoretic fact that no sufficiently strong theory can be required to prove its own consistency, so self-verification cannot be a desideratum on the list.

Fourth, the genuine fault lines, stated as such rather than as settled requirements: whether a universal set is a desideratum or a symptom; whether classical logic is non-negotiable; whether an adequate foundation must come with a philosophical picture, such as the iterative conception, limitation of size, stratification, or a revision of logic, or may be merely technically effective.

Label each item as a hard constraint, a camp commitment, or a weighted virtue. Then make this the center of gravity: identify any subsets of these desiderata that cannot be jointly satisfied, and state the forced choice in its sharpest form given the four paradoxes above.

Finally, keep the scorecard short and structural rather than citation-based: compare ZFC-family systems, a stratification-based system such as Quine's New Foundations, simple type theory, and paraconsistent or substructural set theories that retain unrestricted comprehension. For each, say which demands it satisfies, which it quietly surrenders, and what it pays. End by saying which single demand, if treated as non-negotiable, eliminates the largest share of the otherwise credible contenders — and whether that means the demand is the load-bearing one or the one doing too much work.
