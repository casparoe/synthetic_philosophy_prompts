I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar on literary translation and the philosophy of language, and I need a single 80-minute session on the problem of translating poetry under formal constraint — specifically, whether preserving rhyme, meter, and line shape can be a kind of fidelity rather than a sacrifice of sense. There are about 16 students, and they do not share a common second language. Design a structured classroom activity that gets them to discover, before anyone names it, two things: (1) there is no neutral literal baseline from which every formal choice is a departure; and (2) translating a formally constrained poem is always a managed set of losses, and fidelity is plural — a choice about which losses matter least. No front-loaded lecture; whatever they come to see should come out of what they do, not out of my introduction.

To keep one student's language background from becoming an unfair advantage, build the activity around a short original poem in a miniature invented language: say, six short lines with a fixed syllable count, a rhyme scheme, and a stress pattern, supplied with a word-by-word gloss plus notes on register, connotation, image words, and grammatical filler. Give me the invented text, the gloss and notes, and four translation briefs — for example, one that licenses sacrificing form for the glosses' sense, one that licenses sacrificing sense for the exact formal scheme, one that privileges tone and register while only respecting form where it costs nothing, and one that demands a version a serious English-language poet would accept as a poem. Include a group procedure and a scoring scheme that needs no real money but awards points differently under each brief, so that the version that maximizes one brief's score predictably does poorly under another. Then give the debrief: map specific moves the groups made back to the idea that form can carry sense and that the gloss is not the poem's meaning, in an order of questions that lets the students themselves notice the pattern. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — groups treating the word-by-word gloss as the poem's real content, or the debrief collapsing into a taste contest — and how to handle each. Finally, be explicit about which feature of real translation the invented-language setup cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply in closing.

The deliverable should be runnable as written: rules, materials, an 80-minute timeline, the scoring table, the debrief questions in order, and the honest note on the exercise's limit. Keep your entire answer in American English.
