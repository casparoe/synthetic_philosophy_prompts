ok so i'm putting together a list of objections to the knowledge norm of assertion — the idea that the proper epistemic rule for asserting is that you should assert p only if you know p — and i need one more that isn't already on my list. i'm writting a section of a paper on this, so i've seen all the obvious ones. here's what i already have, and please don't give me these or a close variant of them:

1. selfless assertion cases: a creationist teacher is required by her job to assert evolution to her class, even though she privately disbelieves it, so if knowledge requires belief she doesn't know it, yet many people think her assertion is proper rather than just excused.

2. misleading-evidence cases: you assert the time after looking at a stopped clock you have every reason to trust, and many people find the assertion perfectly proper even though you don't actually know the time. so knowledge looks too demanding.

3. stakes-shifting cases: entitlement to assert seems to rise and fall with what's practically at stake (can i flat-out tell someone the bank is open when their whole afternoon hangs on it), but on the usual invariantist picture knowledge doesn't shift with stakes in the right way, so the knowledge norm seems to mispredict when assertion is ok.

4. underdetermination by rival rules: the truth norm, the belief norm, and the justified-belief norm all have defenders and can accommodate a lot of the same data (lottery cases, retraction patterns, moore-paradox-ish infelicities), so it's not obvious the knowledge rule is uniquely motivated by the evidence.

5. the sufficiency problem: even if knowledge is necessary for proper assertion, it isn't sufficient — you can know something and still assert it improperly because it's irrelevant, rude, or wrong for the audience, which suggests the real constraints come from broader conversational norms rather than one special epistemic rule.

so i want a sixth objection that is genuinely new relative to that list — not a rewording, not a special case of one of them, not a combination — and then i want your honest verdict on whether it's actually any good or only medium. if you think the standard space is basically exhausted and the best remaining move is to sharpen one of the ones above, say so plainly. approach this like a philosopher of language who works on norms of assertion: brainstorm three or four candidate objections first, drop the ones that collapse into my list, and then present the strongest one you have left with a sentence on why it has real bite.
