Exam question: "Suppose you have a single dose of medicine and can use it to save either David or five strangers, where David needs the entire dose and each of the five needs only a fifth of it. Does the fact that five people would be saved rather than one give you a decisive reason to save the five? Explain Taurek's view and evaluate one objection to it."

Student answer:

Taurek's paper "Should the Numbers Count?" challenges the idea that the numbers give us a decisive reason. He imagines a case much like the one in the question: you can save David, who needs the whole drug, or five others, each of whom needs only part of it. Most people think you must save the five. But Taurek denies that five deaths are worse than one death, since there is no single person who suffers the loss of five lives. The losses are borne separately, one per person, and so they cannot be added up into one larger bad.

Taurek thinks each of the six people has an equal claim to be saved. Fairness therefore requires giving each person an equal chance, for example by flipping a coin between saving David and saving the five. The five's claims do not combine to outweigh David's claim.

This makes Taurek a Kantian in spirit: persons are ends in themselves, and the only procedure that respects each of the six equally is one that treats them as interchangeable claims. He would also allow you simply to save whichever person you happen to care about more, since the whole point is that numerical reasoning is forbidden; the coin flip is just a helpful way to avoid seeming biased, not a requirement.

One objection is that Taurek's view forces us to flip a coin even when the choice is between one and a thousand people, which strikes many people as absurd. Taurek could reply that the absurdity comes from our habit of aggregating separate losses, so the objection merely asserts what it is trying to prove.
