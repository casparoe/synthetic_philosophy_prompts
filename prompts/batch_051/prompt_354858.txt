I'm taking a seminar on law and probability, and there's a mismatch that keeps tripping me up. The civil standard of proof is preponderance of the evidence, which courts usually gloss as more likely than not. But when the only evidence is what the literature calls naked statistical evidence, courts refuse to let a jury find liability. Take the blue bus case: you're run off the road by a blue bus but can't identify the company; you show that the Blue Bus Company runs 80% of the blue buses on that route, so it's 80% likely the bus was theirs. Courts still won't find them liable, and most legal scholars treat that as obviously right. Or the gatecrasher: a rodeo has 1,000 spectators and only 499 of them paid, so a randomly selected spectator is more likely than not a gatecrasher. Under standard legal doctrine, the rodeo still couldn't sue a randomly picked spectator for the ticket price and win on those numbers alone. If preponderance really just meant probability over 50%, both cases should be easy wins. So the practice seems to smuggle in some additional criterion beyond bare probability.

I'm trying to figure out what this institutional practice actually presupposes about proof, knowledge, and responsibility. One candidate is that proof must be individualized: the evidence has to be about this defendant and this particular event, not about a reference class the defendant merely belongs to. Another is a knowledge norm, since a 51% or even 80% statistical chance isn't something you can properly claim to know about this person. Another is a sensitivity-style requirement: the evidence that would convict is the same evidence that would have been produced even if this defendant had done nothing wrong. I want an account of which of these (or what else) is strictly presupposed by the practice, which merely happens to fit it, and where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several different theories could each underwrite it. Finally, I'd like help with the apparent instability: courts happily accept DNA random-match probabilities, which are group-frequency evidence, and market-share liability in the DES cases lets plaintiffs recover on broadly statistical grounds. Do those cases show the legal system isn't actually committed to any consistent principle here, or is there a principled line that saves the practice?
