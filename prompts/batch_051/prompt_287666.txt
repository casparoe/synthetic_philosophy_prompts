I just spent an evening arguing with a friend about iterated amplification and distillation, and I still can't tell whether the whole approach secretly hangs on one premise we kept dancing around. The original argument, roughly, is that you can train an aligned system by having humans break a huge task into pieces small enough to check, train the system on those checked pieces, distill it into something faster, and iterate until it handles the original task both competently and aligned. The argument seems to need two things: decomposing a task into checkable subtasks is easier than doing the task, and a human can actually verify each subtask. But the proposals I've seen lately let the model generate its own decomposition, or have one model decompose work for another, because humans are too slow and the tasks are too big.

My friend is sure its fine: decomposition is just another capability you train, and if its bad you notice the failures downstream. I think the original argument may not survive once the decomposition step is delegated to a system the humans can't check directly, because checking whether a decomposition is good is often the original hard task wearing a hat. So write the thing I can send him. Does the alignment-via-decomposition argument still go through when models rather than humans do the decomposing? Which exact premise is under pressure, and is there a repaired version that salvages anything? I want a clear verdict — survives, dies, or changes form — plus the premise-level diagnosis, not a literature review.
