One student answered: "Legality is about whether the measure followed the legal rules — whether the right body passed it, whether the usual procedures were used, and whether courts upheld it. Legitimacy is about whether the people governed by the measure actually accept it as justified, so it's basically a question of public opinion. These can come apart. For example, if a government used an old public health statute to close a border during COVID, the closure could be completely legal, but if people saw it as unnecessary or discriminatory, they might not accept it, so it would be legal without being fully legitimate. The reverse is possible too: a technically illegal lockdown could still be legitimate if most people accepted it as the only way to stop the virus, because by definition if people accept a measure as right, then it's legitimate. I think legal authority alone is never enough to make a measure legitimate, since legitimacy has to come from somewhere outside the law, like necessity or public acceptance. At the same time, legality is not necessary for legitimacy, because the illegal-but-accepted lockdown shows legitimacy can exist without it. So legality is one possible source of legitimacy, but it is neither necessary nor sufficient."
