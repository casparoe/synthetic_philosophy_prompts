I'm a novelist, doing research for a novel set in Geneva in the 1750s, and one of the central characters is a Rousseau-ish crank. I keep running into summaries of Rousseau's views on the theater, and the way they handle it matters for whether I write him as a straw man or as the real position. A general-interest book on the Enlightenment I picked up summarizes the Letter to d'Alembert like this:

"Rousseau thought theater was inherently immoral and wanted it banned, full stop. His reasoning was that watching a play makes you identify with the characters, and that's exactly the problem: in ordinary life you keep your passions in check, but in the theater you let yourself be carried away by them, which weakens the very habits of self-control that morality depends on. He also thought acting was basically a kind of lying — an actor gets paid to pretend to feel emotions he doesn't feel, and a virtuous republic shouldn't pay its citizens to fake a self. His proof that theater can't improve morals is that the plays which try to improve their audiences always fail commercially, because people only go to the theater to be flattered, not corrected. So he proposed replacing theaters with public festivals, where everyone is at once actor and spectator."

I have a hunch the "banned everywhere" part is wrong, because I vaguely remember he allowed that Paris was already corrupt and maybe a theater there did less harm, but the rest I dont have the training to audit. Which parts of this summary would a Rousseau scholar accept, which are overstatements or conflations, and does his festival conclusion survive once the mistakes are corrected? I need the actual shape of the view so my character can defend it against someone who's read him, not just repeat a textbook bullet point.
