For a while now I've been in a small philosophy chat where the dust-specks-vs-torture thought experiment comes up a lot, and it always ends the same way: everyone says their piece, nobody moves. The setup is the usual one: you must choose between one person being tortured for fifty years or an astronomically large number of people each getting a single dust speck in the eye, understood as a tiny, momentary discomfort. Everyone agrees on the arithmetic, agrees that one speck is nearly nothing, and agrees that the torture is horrible. Still, some people say the specks clearly win once the number is big enough, because enough small harms add up; others say the specks can never win, because harms don't aggregate across separate people the way a single person's pain does, and no amount of barely-bad moments adds up to torture.

I have a hunch: maybe this isn't really one disagreement at all. Maybe one side is comparing sums of utility while the other is comparing worst-off lives or refusing to weigh someone's tiny harm against another's torture in the first place. Is that diagnosis too quick? Could it instead be a substantive disagreement about the badness of a single speck, a formal disagreement about comparing astronomically large quantities, or a bedrock disagreement about whether interpersonal aggregation is permissible at all? Give me the structural diagnosis, not a verdict, and say what woud actually count as progress given that diagnosis—a distinction, a decision-theoretic result, a psychology experiment, or maybe an argument that the question was badly posed and should be dropped.
