I have been collecting a musician friend's case-by-case judgments about when two performances or recordings count as the same work. She is a professional cellist, impatient with what she calls philosophical jargon, but her verdicts have been so consistent that I suspect she is running a hidden principle. I want to know what that principle is and what it predicts about three new cases.

Her judgments so far, as I wrote them down:

1. A performance of Beethoven's Fifth on period instruments and a performance of the same symphony on modern instruments are performances of the same work.
2. A live performance of the Fifth in which the first violins miss several notes and a note-perfect studio recording of the Fifth are performances of the same work.
3. A jazz quartet's piece that quotes the Fifth's opening motto and then improvises freely is not a performance of Beethoven's Fifth; she regards it as a distinct work after Beethoven.
4. Liszt's piano transcription of the Fifth, which preserves much of the melodic and harmonic material but recomposes connecting passages and adds virtuoso figuration, is not Beethoven's Fifth; she regards it as Liszt's transcription, a different work.
5. A performance of the Fifth whose first movement is taken at roughly half the marked tempo with heavy rubato, and a metronomically literal performance, are performances of the same work.
6. A performance of Bach's first cello suite on a modern cello with steel strings and a performance on a baroque cello with gut strings at lower pitch are performances of the same work.

I now need to know what she would say about three cases that push on a dimension her earlier examples never cleanly varied, namely whether the medium or sound source belongs to the identity of the work.

Case A: a MIDI realization of the Fifth, note-for-note faithful to the full orchestral score, but with every instrument replaced by a synthesized timbre shaped to approximate it.

Case B: a marimba transcription of Bach's first cello suite, preserving the pitches and rhythms of the score but transferring them to a keyboard percussion instrument.

Case C: a recording of the Fifth assembled in a studio by splicing together thousands of individually sampled orchestral notes, note-for-note faithful to the score, although no musician ever played the work through as a continuous performance.

What should I expect her to say about A, B, and C if she is to remain consistent with her record? State the principle or principles that would jointly make sense of her prior verdicts and these predictions. Where her record genuinely underdetermines the answer, say which candidate principles remain live, which prediction each supports, which one you think she is most likely running and why, and describe one further case that would most cleanly decide between the remaining candidates.
