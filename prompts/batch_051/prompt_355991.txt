I am a high school student writing a short paper for a political philosophy unit, and I have been relying on a widely assigned textbook on the history of modern political thought. The textbook summarizes John Stuart Mill's argument in The Subjection of Women this way:

"Mill's central argument in The Subjection of Women is that the appeal to women's 'nature' commits the naturalistic fallacy. The fact that women have been politically subordinate for almost all of recorded history does not, by itself, tell us how they ought to be treated; the customary is not the same as the just. Mill does not deny that men and women differ innately, but he maintains that the differences we actually observe—for instance, that women are more patient or less competitive—are produced by education and social custom rather than by nature. He concludes from this that, once discriminatory laws are removed, whatever differences remain between the sexes will prove to be politically irrelevant."

I had previously encountered a rather different account of Mill's argument, according to which his main point is that we cannot yet know what women's 'nature' is, because women have only ever been observed under conditions of legal and social subordination. The textbook instead makes the argument sound like a general is/ought point. Before I rely on this summary in my paper, I would like to know whether it is fair. Could you go through it sentence by sentence and separate what is accurate from what is a simplification or overstatement, identify what a defender of Mill would object to, and flag anything Mill explicitly denied or would have resisted saying? I am especially unsure whether the final sentence—that any differences remaining after legal equality will be politically irrelevant—actually follows from Mill's arguments, and how the textbook's reading relates to the unknowability reading I had encountered.
