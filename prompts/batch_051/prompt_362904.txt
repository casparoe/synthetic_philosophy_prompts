Student's answer: Epistemic akrasia is the state of believing p while also believing that one's total evidence does not support p. It is typically treated as the epistemic analogue of practical akrasia, where an agent acts against her better judgment. First-order evidence bears directly on whether p is true; a fingerprint at the scene is an example. Higher-order evidence bears on the quality or reliability of one's assessment of the first-order evidence; evidence that one's reasoning was impaired by exhaustion is an example. The main argument that epistemic akrasia is never rational relies on the enkratic requirement. As usually formulated, this requirement says that if S believes that S should not believe p, then S is rationally required not to believe p. Since an akratic subject believes p while believing that she should not believe p, she flouts the requirement and is therefore irrational. The requirement concerns what the subject believes about her own evidential situation rather than what her evidence is independently of her, so even a subject whose second-order belief is false ends up with an irrational combination of attitudes. Some philosophers offer purported counterexamples in which a subject has strong first-order evidence for p but strong misleading higher-order evidence that her assessment is unreliable. But those examples are not genuine cases of rational epistemic akrasia. Higher-order evidence is itself part of the subject's total evidence, so in such a case the total evidence, once the higher-order evidence is included, no longer supports p strongly enough for rational belief. The subject should revise her first-order belief downward and suspend judgment rather than retain p. The enkratic requirement therefore emerges intact, and epistemic akrasia is always irrational.
