I am a program officer at a private foundation that makes one-year grants of up to $250,000 intended to help public agencies and civil-society groups replace deeply entrenched technical or administrative standards — file formats, data-exchange protocols, unit systems, classification codes, and similar things. I screen about one proposal a week, usually in under an hour, and then write a short recommendation that our grants committee either accepts or rejects; the committee members are not economists or historians. Last night I got into a long argument with a friend about whether the United States will ever actually metricate, and whether it would matter if it did. My friend kept insisting that the customary system is locked in the way the QWERTY keyboard is, and that we are stuck with it regardless of merit. I kept objecting that the word locked in was doing all the work, and that persistence might just as well be ordinary inertia, ongoing interests, or an arrangement that is continuously re-chosen because nobody has sufficient reason to coordinate on a change. By the end we had no test that could have settled the question, let alone tell a grantmaker what to fund.

I would like you to turn what is known about path dependence, lock-in, increasing returns, switching costs, and counterfactual robustness into a reusable screening procedure I can actually run on a proposal. For clarity, by path dependence I mean the claim that the order and timing of earlier events can decide which of several possible arrangements persists; by lock-in I mean an arrangement that persists even though it would not now be chosen from scratch on its merits; and by counterfactual robustness I mean whether a slightly different starting point would predictably have produced a different outcome. The procedure should walk me through, in order: how to tell whether the target arrangement persists because of self-reinforcing mechanisms like network effects and sunk costs, or because it is currently defended by concentrated beneficiaries, or because it is simply not worth the trouble of changing; how to judge whether a one-year, $250,000 outside intervention could plausibly alter the lock-in rather than merely producing a pilot that fades; and which claims in the proposal count as demonstrated, which as asserted, and which are irreducibly judgment calls about magnitudes or about whose switching costs matter. It should stop with a no at the points where a proposal fails a test, and it should say explicitly what the procedure cannot do. Then run the procedure on two hypothetical proposals: (a) a consortium seeking funds to migrate a regional hospital system's clinical data off a proprietary exchange protocol that imposes licensing fees and periodic forced upgrades; and (b) a coalition seeking funds to push U.S. federal agencies toward exclusive use of the 24-hour clock in official schedules, signage, and databases. Do not bend the procedure to make both fundable; if one deserves a no, say so. Please write in American English, and do not include a bibliography — I need an operational tool, not a reading list.
