I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I've been trying to understand a dispute in literary theory about whether an author's intentions fix the meaning of a text. As far as I can tell, one side holds that the author's intention is the standard that settles what a text means; the other holds that once a text is written, its meaning is a matter of the language on the page and the conventions of reading, not of what the author had in mind.

This looks to me like a distinction I work with daily. A program has an intended behavior—written up in a specification, a ticket, a docstring, or a commit message—and an actual behavior, which is what the code does when it runs. I normally treat the actual behavior as authoritative: if the comment says one thing and the code does another, the code is the fact of the matter and the comment is wrong. But when the two diverge, I don't treat the divergence as an alternative valid meaning; I call it a bug and change the code until its behavior matches the intent. So intention seems to function as a background standard of correctness, even though I would also say the code is the final authority on what the program does.

Is this a real structural parallel, or am I pattern-matching on the word "meaning"? If the parallel is real, does anything transfer in either direction—for example, does the idea of a runtime or a compiler map onto an interpreter or reader in literary theory, and does the category of "bug" have an analogue for texts, or does that category break the analogy in a way that matters? And if the analogy fails, does its failure show anything about why the two cases are really different?
