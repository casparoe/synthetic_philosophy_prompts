I've been going round in circles about whether elegance in science is evidence, and I'd like a careful verdict on an argument I've been trying to make against my flatmate (a physicist). In our philosophy of science module we kept bumping into the point that lots of different theories can fit the same data, and I've been arguing that elegance is what breaks the tie — and not just as a matter of taste. I wrote the argument out as steps:

(1) The theories physics actually kept were nearly always more elegant than the ones they beat — Kepler's ellipses over circles on top of circles, Newton's gravity over the swirling-vortex picture, Einstein's relativity over the ether patch-ups.

(2) When two theories fit the data equally well, scientists regularly pick the more elegant one.

(3) If a rule for choosing theories has a track record of picking the ones that later turn out empirically right, then it must be tracking something real rather than just a subjective preference.

(4) The only real thing elegance could be tracking is truth, or something closely correlated with truth.

(5) Therefore a theory's being elegant is at least weak evidence that it is true.

It feels valid when I read it fast, but I half beleive my flatmate when he says it's all survivorship bias — except I don't see how a bias in the historical record can be located in one step of an argument. So: is the argument valid? For each premise, is it true as stated? If it fails, say exactly which step is the first to go wrong, and what the corrected version of that step would have to claim for the conclusion to follow.
