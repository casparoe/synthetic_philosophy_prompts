Context: I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I keep hitting a conceptual problem while building a feature. Our product lets a group submit full, strict rankings of a set of options, and a deterministic counting rule converts those rankings into a single winner; the current plan is an instant-runoff style method. The product team wants the UI to display this message: "Rank your options honestly. The algorithm is designed so you never have to vote strategically." Several of my colleagues believe this promise is just a matter of choosing a well-designed aggregation rule, and that our rule satisfies it.

Then I read a statement of the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem. As I understand it: with three or more options, any rule that accepts every possible profile of strict rankings, always returns a single winner, can return any of the options as winner under some profile, and is not a dictatorship, will be manipulable by some voter at some profile: that voter can get an outcome they strictly prefer by submitting a ranking other than their true one. If that is right, the UI promise cannot be jointly true with those other conditions for any such rule.

Please act as an expert on the formal theory and conceptual foundations of strategy-proofness in social choice. Think carefully, and before you settle on a verdict, lay out the different readings of the UI promise ("rank honestly, and you never have to think strategically") and state which reading the product actually commits us to.

Treat the product feature as the practice whose commitments I want you to unearth. What theory of strategy-proofness and honest preference expression does this design presuppose? In particular: (1) reconstruct the implicit theory that would make the UI promise true, distinguishing what the design strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with; (2) say whether the design is coherent given the theorem, or whether it quietly assumes away one of the theorem's conditions — and which one; (3) tell me what would have to change about the product if we replaced the implicit theory with a clear-eyed one, in terms of the copy, the algorithm, or the class of inputs we accept.

Assign your verdict a confidence level, and tell me what would change your mind about the diagnosis and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
