So here's something I've been chewing on. The legal system has no problem drawing exact lines through stuff that, outside the law, we treat as vague. You can vote the day you turn 18 but not the day before; in the US the legal limit for regular drivers is a blood alcohol content of .08, not .079; the speed limit is 65 and nobody pretends 66 is dangerous while 64 is safe. But the same system turns around and uses hopelessly blurry standards like "reasonable doubt," "reasonable person," and "maturity of judgment"—and in some places a 16-year-old charged with a serious crime can be moved to adult court even though they cant vote or legally drink. What view of vagueness does this practice of legislating sharp cutoffs presuppose? Does it presuppose that vague predicates are just semantic holes we can fill however we want, or does it commit the law to something stronger (or weaker)? And is there an actual inconsistency here with the law's comfort with vague standards elsewhere, or would the main theories of vagueness—epistemicism, supervaluationism, and so on—actually say the two practices can coexist? Where exactly is the practice philosophically underdetermined?
