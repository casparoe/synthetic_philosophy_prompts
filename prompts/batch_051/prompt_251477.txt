I've been trying to make sense of an idea called the unilateralist's curse. The way I understand it, when many people each have the ability to take an action that will affect a whole group, and they all have their own views about whether the action is worth taking, the person who ends up acting will usually be the one who is most overly optimistic about the benefits, since everyone more pessimistic will hesitate. The result is that the group's fate gets decided by its most extreme member. What I can't figure out is where this reasoning stops. If it is sound, it seems to condemn nearly all individual initiative. Starting a business, proposing marriage, reporting misconduct at work, even pulling a fire alarm when you think you smell smoke—in every one of these, the person who acts is probably more optimistic than the people around them. So does the unilateralist's curse show that we should almost always wait for broad agreement before doing anything that affects others? Or is there a principled reason it only applies to a narrow range of cases, and if so, what is that reason? Please write your answer in American English.
