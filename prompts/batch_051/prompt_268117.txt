I keep coming back to whether it's even coherent to strike bargains before AGI exists—like a group of labs agreeing now that whoever builds it will share the windfall in some specified way, or individuals leaving "if I'm the one who builds it, I'll..." instructions. What would make those bind after the fact? It seems to me any adequate theory of pre-AGI bargains has to do a bunch of jobs at once, and I'm not at all sure they're compatible. That's what I want checked before I read anything else.

Write the desiderata for a theory of pre-AGI commitment. What must an account of why and whether a deal struck before the transition can bind parties after it have to deliver? Cover at least: the continuity problem (is the post-AGI agent the same party who made the bargain, and in what sense?); the asymmetry of information and power between the parties at the two times; paternalism toward your own future self versus coercion of a distinct future agent; default rules when the bargain is underspecified and the post-AGI world has to fill in what was left open; whether the bindingness is supposed to be moral, rational, or merely strategic; and whether some things simply can't be bargained away even with consent. For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark it non-negotiable or just a camp preference. If some subset of the requirements can't be jointly satisfied—for instance I suspect you can't have both "the later agent is the same party" and "the later agent's radically changed preferences can void the deal" without some arbitrariness creeping in—say so, and then actually commit to which one you'd drop. No menu-of-options ending: I want a definite list and a definite call on the conflicts.

Then, briefly (this shouldn't be the bulk), say how the familiar paradigms—Ulysses contracts, constitutional precommitment, intergenerational social-contract arguments—do against your list, and where each falls short.
