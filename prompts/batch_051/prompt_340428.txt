In my high-school philosophy class we have been studying a principle that I find both compelling and practically alarming. According to the principle, what makes much risky conduct wrong is not only the harm that happens to materialize but the fact that one person unilaterally exposes another to a chance of serious harm, without giving that person the opportunity to accept or refuse the exposure. This principle has real explanatory power: it accounts for why a non-consensual medical experiment seems wrong even when none of the subjects is injured; why a company should not be allowed to put a low-dose probable carcinogen into a neighborhood’s water even if the expected number of illnesses is tiny; and why the fact that nothing bad ultimately happened does not always answer the moral complaint. It also provides an elegant test, namely: identify the risks you are imposing, then ask whether the person exposed consented.

Yet I also hold an intuition that I cannot sincerely surrender. Much of ordinary life seems clearly permissible even though it constantly imposes small, unconsented risks. When I drive to school, I impose a small risk of serious injury on pedestrians and other drivers; when a classmate brings homemade peanut cookies to a bake sale, she imposes a small risk of an allergic reaction on strangers; when someone cycles on a shared path, he imposes a small risk of collision. I do not actually believe these activities are the moral equivalent of an unconsented clinical trial, and I do not believe I am simply rationalizing a comfortable habit.

What I want is not a verdict that one side is correct. I want an account of what would have to be shown for the everyday intuition to be the thing that rightly gives way—for instance, that I am ignoring how thousands of individually small risks aggregate into something approaching certainty, or that I am confusing an act’s not being blameworthy with its not being wrong. I also want to know what would have to be shown for the principle to give way or be amended: whether it should accept a genuinely principled de minimis threshold below which risk does not require consent, or a distinction between wronging a person through imposed risk and wronging a person through caused harm, or an idea of reciprocal everyday social practice that counts as a form of tacit acceptance. Above all, I want to know whether there is a reading on which the principle and the intuition are strictly compatible, or whether each proposed reconciliation is just the same tension restated in finer language.
