Here are the criteria I was taught for knowledge in the Nyāya school: a cognition counts as knowledge only if it is a fresh, true awareness of an object, and only if it arises from one of four sources, namely perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna), comparison or analogy (upamāna), and testimony (śabda). Memory is deliberately excluded, on the grounds that its just a reproduction of something already cognised rather than a fresh awareness of the thing itself. Now consider a case. A skilled birdwatcher gets a two-second view of a small warbler in bad light and immediately says it is a willow warbler rather than a chiffchaff, and she turns out to be right. But when you press her on how she knows, she cannot point to any field mark she is using in the moment. She says she just recognised the whole bird, the way it moved, the overall impression birders call jizz. She has seen thousands of willow warblers over the years. Is her cognition knowledge under the criteria above? Work criterion by criterion: is the awareness fresh, is it really sourced from present perception rather than memory, and does treating it as perception smuggle memory back in through the back door? Argue whether the case qualifies, and say whether it shows the four pramāṇas need a fifth, or whether the criteria are fine and the cognition fails or passes on some other ground.
