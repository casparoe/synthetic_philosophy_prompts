Here's my working checklist for being an optimizer: (a) it maintains a population or distribution of candidate states; (b) it evaluates or at least compares candidates against some objective; (c) it biases persistence or copying toward candidates that score better; and (d) iterating this tends to move the population toward higher-scoring candidates. I stole most of this from how I think about SGD, evolutionary strategies, and hill-climbing.

Now the borderline case: natural selection on a real population of organisms. There is variation (mutation, recombination) and differential reproduction, so (a) and maybe (c) look fine. And (d) seems to fail sometimes: selection can fix a lower-fitness variant if a better one never appears, or take a locally good step that closes off better long-term options.
