I have been turning over an argument that seems to show that if a Malthusian is allowed to be right but early, then no long-run prediction can ever be falsified, and I cannot find the step to reject. Diagnose it.

Consider a stripped-down Malthusianism, not a historical thesis about the man himself: population tends to grow geometrically while the food supply can grow at best arithmetically, so under any fixed technology per capita resources must eventually fall and positive checks such as famine, disease, and conflict must operate. Two centuries later, food per capita has risen and the predicted collapse has not occurred. The Malthusian replies that the underlying tendency was never repealed: technology has been raising the ceiling, but the planet is finite, so eventually the ceiling stops rising and the old arithmetic catches up. The mechanism was right; only the date was wrong.

Here is the argument I cannot escape.

(1) A failed long-run prediction is not thereby refuted if the core mechanism that was supposed to produce the outcome can be kept intact and the failure can be blamed on a background factor that merely delayed it.
(2) The Malthusian case satisfies this: the core mechanism is the race between geometric population growth and arithmetic food supply; technology is the background factor that delayed the collapse; and finitude guarantees the delay is only temporary.
(3) Therefore the Malthusian prediction is not refuted. It is right but early.
(4) But any failed long-run prediction can be rescued in the same way: the astrologer says the transit's effect was delayed by a blocking influence, the doomsday prophet redates the end, and the failed economic forecaster blames an exogenous shock while insisting the underlying dynamics are still in place.
(5) If the rescue in (1) through (3) succeeds, then by parity the rescues in (4) succeed too.
(6) But then no long-run prediction can ever be falsified by its failure to come true, since every failure can be redescribed as delay with the deadline pushed indefinitely forward. That is too much to accept.

Each step looks right when I examine it alone, but the conclusion is unacceptable. My instinct is to keep (2) and reject (4) or (5) by finding a principled difference between the Malthusian case and the astrology case—mechanism, independent testability, quantitative precision, something—but every difference I try either lets a crank case through or rules Malthus out as well.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it commit us to?
