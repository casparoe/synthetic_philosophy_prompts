I'm in a historiography seminar where Ranke gets treated as the naïve pre-theoretical relic and Carr and Hayden White as the cure, and honestly I get the cure. The sceptical view has real pull: there is no pile of facts sitting around with their significance pre-attached, historians decide which facts get to count at all, and once you arrange a sequence into a narrative you could have arranged it another way — as tragedy or comedy or whatever — so a neutral account of the past as it actually was starts to look like a superstition. At the same time I cannot get myself to actually believe there is no plain fact of the matter about what happened. It seems just true, not constructed or told from a viewpoint, that the armistice was signed on 11 November 1918, that the library fire broke out before the brigade arrived, that one letter was written before another. Those are not stories I'm imposing; they're just what happened.

I don't want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would cost to give up the intuition, what it would cost to amend the scepticism, and whether there's a reading on which both survive. And I want to know wether my intuition is doing real philosophical work here or is just confusing the past itself with the books historians write about it — because if it's the later, why does it feel so much like evidence?
