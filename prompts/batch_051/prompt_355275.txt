I'm an undergrad in history and I've ended up as the student rep on my university's museum ethics committee, which is a bit odd because my actual focus is nineteenth-century West Africa, not museum policy — but I'm taking a module this term on cultural heritage and global justice, mostly about the Benin Bronzes and the Parthenon Marbles, so the chair has decided I'm the in-house restitution person. We're meeting next Thursday, and I've been asked to draft the short written opinion the committee will vote on.

The case is this. Our university museum holds four objects — three brass plaques and a carved wooden ancestor figure — that were taken in the 1890s from a town in what is now Nigeria during a British punitive raid. They came to the museum in 1962 as a bequest, with a condition in the donor's will that the collection be kept together and displayed in perpetuity. A local council and a descendant community association in Nigeria have formally asked for the return of all four objects, to be cared for and displayed in a community-run cultural centre that's currently under construction and due to open in about eighteen months. The strongest argument for refusing, apart from the legal condition in the bequest, is that our museum has climate-controlled storage and trained conservators (one plaque is quite fragile), and that the objects are part of the university's teaching collection and a public collection in the UK. We all basically agree the original taking was wrong, so I don't need the memo to establish that again.

What I need is a decision memo I can circulate: it should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame, and it should engage the conservation-and-access argument seriously rather than just saying "colonial looting was wrong so send everything back." Oh, and British English please, since it's a formal university document.
