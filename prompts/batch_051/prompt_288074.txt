At an interdisciplinary seminar I found myself listening to two colleagues, a machine-learning researcher and an evolutionary biologist, argue about whether natural selection is best thought of as a bad optimizer and what gradient descent can genuinely learn from it. I could see force in both positions and couldn't settle the argument myself, so I want you to adjudicate the exchange. Here is how I remember it.

(1) The machine-learning researcher argued that natural selection is a stupid optimizer. Selection has no gradient information: it never computes the local slope of a fitness landscape, so it cannot take directed steps toward higher fitness. It is limited to whatever blind mutations happen to be available and can only accept a variant if it is immediately better than the current design, even if that variant is heading toward a dead end. The predictable result, he said, is the kind of engineering kludge that evolutionary biology keeps turning up. The detour of the recurrent laryngeal nerve, for instance, looks like something no gradient-based search would produce if it could follow a coherent path through design space.

(2) The evolutionary biologist replied that this conflates the generation of variation with the action of selection. Mutation may be blind, but selection is not a random walk: it is a powerful sorting process, and because real populations carry standing genetic variation and recombine it, populations do not depend on one lucky mutation at a time. In fact, she said, under standard quantitative-genetic assumptions the expected direction and amount of change in a trait mean track the additive genetic covariance between the trait and relative fitness, which supplies precisely the kind of local directional information a gradient method would exploit. So selection looks far more like noisy gradient descent than like the blind trial-and-error her colleague described.

(3) The machine-learning researcher answered that this only makes the analogy look good by assuming the friendly parts of quantitative genetics: stationary environments, roughly additive fitness effects, and a landscape smooth enough that a covariance counts as a gradient. Real fitness landscapes are rugged and nonstationary, and selection gets stranded on local optima and plateaus for enormous spans of time. Modern gradient descent, he said, does not have those excuses: it computes gradients by backpropagation with respect to all parameters simultaneously and has momentum and adaptive step sizes. Calling selection a stupid optimizer was never meant to deny that it optimizes in some weak sense; the point was that its machinery is crude compared with the machinery gradient descent has available.

(4) The evolutionary biologist closed by saying the comparison is double-edged. The things evolution demonstrably possesses—open-ended novelty, evolvability, developmental robustness, generalization to environments quite unlike those of their ancestors—are largely the things gradient-based learning still finds hardest, with sparse rewards, catastrophic forgetting, and overfitting to a fixed objective as standard trouble spots. So perhaps the right conclusion is not that selection is a worse gradient descent, but that selection is not gradient descent at all. It is a feedback process that filters and recombines variants without first imposing a metric on the search space or a single fixed objective, and the word stupid smuggles in the very frame—a fixed objective being optimized—that is in question for living systems.

Please adjudicate this exchange in detail. Take moves (1) through (4) in order and place each on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a one- or two-sentence justification for each placement. Then say who is right about which specific point. Then say what, if anything, both parties are missing, and give the most defensible answer to the underlying question of what gradient descent can and cannot learn from natural selection. Please answer in American English.
