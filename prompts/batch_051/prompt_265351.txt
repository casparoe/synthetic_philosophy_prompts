I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and for years I have written about both foreign-policy debates and, more recently, machine learning. My command of the second field is that of a well-read generalist rather than a practitioner: I can talk about training distributions, generalisation, out-of-distribution behaviour, transfer learning, overfitting and spurious correlations, but I would not trust myself to implement any of it.

There is a long-running argument among historians and international-relations scholars — one that resurfaces whenever someone invokes Munich, or Vietnam, or the so-called Thucydides trap — about whether historical analogies genuinely teach lessons or systematically mislead. The sceptical side says such analogies are unreliable: they single out one earlier case, exaggerate its likeness to the present, suppress disconfirming differences, and are typically advanced by decision-makers who already know which policy they prefer. Different analogies point to opposite policies, and the "lesson" drawn is often just the policy preference wearing period costume. The other side replies that reasoning from earlier cases is unavoidable, since nobody confronts the present as a blank slate, and that the remedy is better discipline in selecting and testing analogies, not abandoning them altogether.

I would like you to restate this debate, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary of machine learning. Treat the sceptic's position as a claim about distribution shift, out-of-distribution generalisation, training data, overfitting and the absence of any held-out test set. Treat the defender's position as the corresponding claim about the necessity of prior experience and some inductive bias. Be explicit about which side of the historians' debate each ML concept gets assigned to, and do not silently import ML assumptions — a static environment, independently sampled data, a fixed loss function — and pass them off as the historians' claims.

Then give me what I actually want: the residue report. Which parts of the historians' debate translate cleanly, which translate only with strain, and which have no counterpart at all in the ML vocabulary? In particular, does anything important disappear because the objects of foreign-policy reasoning are themselves strategic, norm-laden, capable of learning from the very analogy being drawn, and partly constituted by the historian's own descriptions? And is the residue a limitation of my vocabulary, or a sign that the original claims were doing more rhetorical work than their proponents admit?

Please structure the response as:

1. The translation (about 300 words): the sceptical position restated in ML terms, then the rejoinder restated in ML terms.
2. Clean mappings (about 150 words): what carries over without distortion.
3. Strained mappings (about 200 words): what had to be bent to fit.
4. The residue (about 250 words): what will not go into the ML vocabulary at all, and what it consists in.
5. Verdict (about 150 words): whether the residue indicts the ML vocabulary, the historians' claims, or both.

Write in British English, please.
