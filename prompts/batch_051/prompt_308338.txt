In The Spirit of the Laws, Montesquieu advances a version of what has become a common argument: commerce does more than produce wealth; it softens manners, encourages peaceable intercourse among different peoples, and cures the more destructive prejudices. Contemporary defenders of markets often push the point further, treating the market as a school of character in which repeated dealing with strangers disciplines the passions and teaches honesty, reliability, and tolerance. Audit this analogy between market participation and moral education. Provide a table of correspondences, identifying what is supposed to play the role of pupil, teacher, curriculum, examination, and certificate. Then catalogue the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion, especially where the market rewards traits that are not the virtues the analogy credits it with teaching, or where the virtues it does teach fall short of justice. Conclude by stating whether the claim that markets civilize character survives the audit or was being carried by the analogy all along.
