A post-conflict state's reparations commission proposes a standing Historical Harms Registry. Candidate episodes — colonial land seizures, massacres, forced removals, famines, internments, and similar wrongs — may be nominated by any group that can show it descends from the affected population. A panel of historians, demographers, and legal scholars scores each nominated episode on five criteria: scale, measured as the logarithm of estimated deaths and displacements; duration of the wrong; deliberateness, with explicitly planned policy scoring highest, foreseeable neglect in the middle, and diffuse structural causation lowest; persistence of effects, meaning documented ongoing material disadvantage to the claimant group; and recency, with a discount for episodes that predate the founding of the modern state. These scores are weighted and combined into a single cardinal index, recomputed every seven years.

The index is then used to allocate three goods: state-funded memorialization, inclusion in the mandatory school history curriculum, and shares of a fixed reparations fund. Only the top-ranked third of registered episodes are eligible for any of the three. Any claimant group may challenge another episode's score by submitting new evidence, and any claimant group may invoke a non-comparability veto: a two-thirds vote of its recognized representatives removes its episode from the ranking entirely, after which that episode is ineligible for all three goods.

How would this mechanism actually behave? I want an analysis of its incentives and failure modes, not an abstract judgment about whether comparing atrocities is decent or indecent. In particular: who is encouraged to nominate, and how will groups frame their claims under these five criteria? What gaming strategies emerge — for example, evidence challenges aimed at rivals, logrolling, coalition formation, or pressure on the scoring panel — and which of them would corrupt the index rather than merely exploit it? What second-order effects follow for inter-group relations, for how perpetrator communities and the state respond, and for the standing of groups that veto? And can the main failure modes be fixed without gutting the scheme, or does the scheme's actual behavior reveal that the cardinal index is itself the problem? Be self-critical: say how confident you are in your main predictions, and identify the single point you are least sure of.
