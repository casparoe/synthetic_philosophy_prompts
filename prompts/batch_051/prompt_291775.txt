At a recent conference on scientific methodology, I listened to two researchers argue about Hempel's raven paradox. One defended the standard view. "Everyone accepts that a black raven confirms the hypothesis that all ravens are black," he began. "But that hypothesis is logically equivalent to the hypothesis that all non-black things are non-ravens. A red apple is a non-black, non-raven thing. So the red apple confirms the second hypothesis, and therefore confirms the first. If you accept the first confirmation, consistency forces you to accept the second." The other pushed back: "That is a sleight of hand. Confirmation is not merely instantiation. A black raven discriminates between the raven hypothesis and its plausible rivals, while a red apple discriminates between nothing relevant, because we already know it is an apple. What you call consistency is just a refusal to let background knowledge matter." The first replied: "But that is exactly the point at issue. You cannot simply declare background knowledge relevant without a principle that says when it may override logical equivalence. Otherwise anyone can dodge any unwanted confirmation by saying, 'I already knew that, so it does not count.'" The second answered: "And you cannot infer anything about ravens from shoelaces without a relevance principle, which formal equivalence does not supply. The burden is on you to show why an observation that cannot rule out any alternative about ravens should still count as evidence about them." They asked me to adjudicate. Please go through the exchange move by move: identify which claims are sound, which are mistaken or overstated, and say what both parties are missing. Conclude with an overall judgment about whose position is closer to correct.
