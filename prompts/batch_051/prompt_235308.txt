The debate between scientific realists and antirealists has now run for several decades, and one of its central fronts, the pessimistic meta-induction together with the family of realist replies to it, appears to have reached an entrenched stalemate. The broad shape is familiar. The realist begins from the success of mature science: mature theories make precise novel predictions, and the no-miracles argument contends that this success would be a miracle unless those theories were at least approximately true and their central theoretical terms at least approximately referring. The antirealist replies with the pessimistic meta-induction: the historical record is a graveyard of successful but abandoned theories, crystalline spheres, phlogiston, caloric, the luminiferous ether, whose central posits turned out to be false and whose central terms turned out not to refer; by a straightforward induction, our own successful theories should be expected to meet the same fate. Realists have responded less by surrendering the target than by repeatedly redrawing it. Selective realism says that only the parts of past theories that were genuinely doing predictive work were retained across revolutions, so the historical record has been misread as a graveyard when it is in fact a quarry. Structural realism says that what survives theory change is mathematical structure rather than ontology, and that realist commitment should be restricted to that. Realists also regularly object that the antirealist's historical cases are unrepresentative because the rejected theories were not mature in the way present-day physics and chemistry are. Against all this, the antirealist has sharpened the challenge into the problem of unconceived alternatives: the deeper worry is not just that past successful theories were false, but that the space of possible theories is much larger than our present concepts can survey, so the historical record understates rather than overstates our exposure to radical error.

The striking fact is that after all this, neither side has moved. The dialectical machinery on each side is flexible enough to absorb the other side's cases: the realist can always argue that the antirealist's favored episode did not involve a genuinely indispensable posit, and the antirealist can always argue that the realist's favored continuity is too thin or too handpicked to underwrite approximate truth. My hypothesis is that the debate has stalled because it is not one disagreement but several braided together: a disagreement about what realism is actually committed to, a disagreement about which historical episodes should count as data, a disagreement about how much weight an induction over theory change can bear, and perhaps a bedrock difference about whether explanatory considerations may legitimately support belief in unobservables at all. If that is right, then producing yet another historical case study or yet another continuity claim is systematically incapable of moving the debate.

I would like a diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement rather than a verdict for one side. Specifically: is the dispute substantive or partly verbal, and if partly verbal, where do terms such as approximate truth, maturity, indispensability, and success come apart between the two camps? Which components are empirical-historical, which are formal or probabilistic, and which are bare epistemic or methodological commitments? Would a shared classification of historical episodes resolve anything, or would the two sides still weight the resulting induction differently? And given your diagnosis, what would count as genuine progress, whether a particular kind of historical study, a formal result about the strength of the induction, a clarified formulation of realism that both sides could accept as the target, an agreed criterion for when a posit is indispensable, or something else? Alternatively, argue that the debate is structurally unresolvable and explain what, if anything, still makes it worth having.
