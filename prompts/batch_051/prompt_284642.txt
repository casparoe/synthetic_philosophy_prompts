I'm in year 12 and I keep having this argument with my maths teacher about whether a single probability number means anything on its own. The specific case: my cousin is applying to a competitive acting course with a 12% acceptance rate overall. But for applicants from our region it's 6%, for applicants who did a foundation year it's 22%, for applicants his age it's 9%, for applicants who went to the open day it's 17%, and if you stack enough of those classes on top of each other you can make it come out as some tiny number, or even 100%, depending on how small you make the class. I want to say the 12% is his real chance. My teacher says probability has to be relative to a class and the choice of class is arbitrary (he calls it the reference class problem) so there is no real single-case probability.

How would you test the claim that there is a right reference class for a one-off case like this, or at least that some reference classes are objectively better than others? I want a concrete design, maybe using calibration or scoring rules or betting if those help, but I don't know which is best. Say what result would count in favour of some classes being better, what result would count against it, and what even a good result would leave open (like whether we've found the right class or just a class that happens to be useful for our purposes). I'm not after a bibliography or anything, just a test I could describe in our school magazine.
