I've been reading Spinoza's Ethics after someone on a rationalist forum recommended Part III on the affects, and I keep thinking about the defintion of hope near the start of the Definitions of the Affects. I don't have the exact wording in front of me, but roughly: he defines hope as a kind of unstable pleasure that arises from the thought of a future or past event whose outcome we doubt, and he immediately contrasts it with confidence, where the doubt has been removed. The thing that strikes me is that hope isn't just a positive expectation; on his account uncertainty and fear are built into it. That matters for my job.

I'm the communications director for a climate policy nonprofit, and our team fights constantly over whether campaigns should lead with 'we can still win this' hope or with grim 'this is how bad it is' realism. We make these calls every week, under deadline, and the wrong frame can cost us donors or make volunteers burn out. I want a reusable decision procedure that a junior field organizer could run in about 20 minutes without reading Spinoza themselves: a checklist or ordered set of questions to decide whether a given email, press statement, or campaign should lead with hope, with confidence, or with something else like fear or sober realism. Ground it in what Spinoza actually says about hope as distinct from confidence, but don't make them read the Ethics. The executive director signs off on big paid campaigns, but most day-to-day choices are made by junior staff, so the procedure has to be simple enough to survive without me. Then run it on two worked examples: one where the procedure says to use hope even though my staff won't like it, and one where it says not to use hope even though I might really want to. Also mark the points where the procedure stops and a person has to make a values call rather than a factual call.
