I coordinate special education services at a large public high school, where my team makes accommodation decisions in weekly IEP and 504 meetings. A recurring fight is over external cognitive aids: a student with a math disability wants a calculator that displays each step, a student with severe ADHD wants his phone on the desk because a customized reminder app is how he remembers deadlines, a student with dyslexia writes everything through speech-to-text. Teachers call these crutches or cheating; parents say the aid is part of how their child thinks. Since a concussion last year left me depending on a planner and calendar alerts in a way that felt less like using a tool and more like losing part of my memory when my phone died, I no longer find the teachers' position obvious.

Please turn the extended mind thesis into a reusable decision procedure my team can actually run in 30 minutes without philosophical training. As I understand it, the thesis says that when an external resource plays a role we would count as cognitive if it were inside the skull — reliably available, automatically trusted, easily accessed, functionally integrated with the person's other capacities — the cognitive process, and on the stronger reading the mind itself, extends into that resource. I do not want a literature review or citations; I want the operational translation and will add the references myself later.

The procedure should be a checklist of questions in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question has become one of values or policy rather than theory. It must separate two questions: (a) Does the resource count as part of the student's extended cognitive system, on the best description the theory can supply? (b) What follows for whether the accommodation is granted? Mark the move from (a) to (b) as a normative step the theory does not by itself settle, and say which non-theoretical considerations — exam validity, teacher authority, fairness to other students, legal exposure — may legitimately override it. The decisions can be challenged in due-process hearings, so the procedure must be explicable to a parent and defensible to a hearing officer, and any denial should be signable by me and our 504 coordinator.

Then apply the procedure to three cases: (1) a dyscalculic student whose calculator performs steps the student cannot; (2) a student with severe ADHD whose phone runs a reminder system he has customized over two years; (3) a high-achieving student with no disability who requests the same phone-in-exam accommodation because "the extended mind applies to everyone." Include at least one case in which the procedure returns an answer my team would prefer not to enforce, and say explicitly what the procedure cannot settle.

Begin with the procedure itself, with no preface or commentary and without restating this request. Write in American English.
