I help lead an advanced reading group at my high school for students interested in strategic reasoning, and we have just finished a session on the dollar auction as a model of escalation of commitment. I have volunteered to write a short closed-book written exercise for the group, and I would like it to test something more precise than the usual request to define the auction and identify the sunk-cost fallacy. Please draft one essay question, suitable for about 45 minutes of writing, together with a model answer of roughly 600 words and a marking scheme out of 20. The question should be self-contained, so members can answer it from the concepts discussed in our session rather than from outside readings. It should force members to work out whether a bidder who genuinely treats past bids as irrelevant to future decisions could still have a forward-looking reason to match a rival's higher bid, and how that marginal decision differs from the decision to enter the auction in the first place. It should also make them consider the other bidder's incentives, since the standard escalation story is usually told as if only one side is trapped. In the marking scheme, please separate a top answer that can distinguish the rationality of continuing from the rationality of having entered, and that explains why treating sunk costs as irrelevant does not by itself forbid matching the rival's latest bid, from a weaker answer that only repeats the idea of throwing good money after bad. Please also name the specific mistakes that should cost marks, such as conflating 'it was a mistake to enter' with 'it is a mistake to continue,' or assuming that because the eventual outcome may be worse the next bid itself must have negative expected value.
