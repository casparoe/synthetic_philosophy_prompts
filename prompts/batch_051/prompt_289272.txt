Consider the standard money-pump argument for the transitivity of preferences. An agent has a strict preference cycle: A over B, B over C, and C over A. A trader starts him with A and offers swaps that move him around the cycle — C for A, then B for C, then A for B — charging a one-cent fee on each accepted swap, small enough not to reverse the underlying ranking. Since the agent strictly prefers each offered item to the one he holds, he accepts all three swaps, ends where he began, and is poorer. Now vary the case: the agent is a noisy chooser. Underlyingly he retains the same cycle, but whenever he is offered two options he takes the higher-ranked one with probability 0.9 and the lower one with probability 0.1, independently each time. Starting with A, a trader offers one swap per day — always the option the cycle ranks above whatever the agent currently holds — and charges a one-cent fee on each accepted swap. Work through how the money-pump argument fares in this version. Does the trader still profit over time, and does the agent's behavior exhibit the same kind of irrationality as the strict cycle? What does this version show about whether a person's utility is simply whatever their choices reveal? Answer as a numbered list of exactly five points, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion.
