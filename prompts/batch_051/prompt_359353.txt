I'm a PhD student in geophysics, and I've ended up stuck on a modeling choice that my whole group treats as a minor technical detail but which has turned into a real argument. I need to put a prior distribution over the permeability of a sandstone formation before I run a Bayesian inversion. The only constraints I actually have are that the permeability lies inside a known range, and that a flow measurement through the whole interval gives me a decent estimate of its arithmetic average.

A postdoc in the group insists I should use the most 'ignorant' distribution that satisfies just those two constraints and nothing more; he says any lumpier or narrower distribution would be smuggling in information I don't have. My advisor counters that 'most ignorant' is not well defined here: write the unknown as permeability, as log-permeability, or as hydraulic resistance, and you get three different 'flattest' distributions, all pretending to encode the same information. Then the postdoc says log-permeability is the natural parameter for this formation and that breaks the tie, which my advisor calls a convention, not an argument. I need to actually run the inversion, and I don't want to pretend my data are more informative than they are, but I also don't want to just privately pick whichever answer suits me. Who is right, and is there a principled way to choose a prior under exactly this kind of underdetermination, or is the whole exercise just subjectivity with extra steps?
