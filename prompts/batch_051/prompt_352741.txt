I'm putting together a handout for my PPE decision theory course and I'd like it to be a "sort yourself into a position" decision tree rather than a prose survey. The topic is rational choice when the probabilities are genuinely unknown — not merely uncertain in the ordinary risk sense. The deliverable is a tree whose branch nodes are diagnostic questions someone can answer without knowing the labels, and whose leaves are these positions, at minimum: maximin (the plain decision rule, before we get to Rawls), minimax regret, the principle of insufficient reason, subjective expected utility (full-blown Bayesianism), and the Harsanyi-style impartial-observer view. If the logical space also contains a "dominance only, otherwise no determinate answer" leaf, include it; if some interesting cell is empty, mark it and say whether it's empty because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. If the tree ends up placing the Harsanyi view as a conjunction of insufficient reason plus expected utility rather than a leaf of its own, say so rather than forcing an extra leaf. Each leaf should have a name (or "unnamed" if nobody defends it) and one sentence on the view's most awkward commitment.

Here's the angle. Build the tree from the questions, not from the names. The questions that matter to me are: (a) whether rational agents are permitted to have no probabilities at all, even equal ones; (b) whether you evaluate each option by its worst state alone or by something about the whole profile of what you could have chosen instead (regret); and (c) whether the rule has to be usable for an individual choosing a surgery or a career, not just for a constitutional choice behind a veil of ignorance. Use two running cases for the branch questions: a patient deciding whether to enroll in a first-in-human surgical trial where the complication rate hasn't been estimated yet, and a government deciding whether to impose a moratorium on a new technology whose worst case is catastrophic but whose probability can't be quantified. Phrase every branch as a concrete choice between two options in one of those cases, so a student can answer it without already knowing what minimax regret is.

Also flag any pair of leaves the tree can't really separate once the cases are fixed. I'm especially worried that Rawls's maximin and plain maximin either collapse or come apart depending on whether you impose his conditions, so make that explicit rather than silently treating them as the same or different. And don't present it as settled that Bayesianism is the correct default for private decisions while maximin is only for the original position; that's exactly the kind of contested claim I want marked as contested. Keep it to about six leaves, seven at most, and no more than five questions on the longest path.

Oh, and please keep the whole thing honest about what's settled and what isn't: settled parts include the fact that these rules genuinely recommend different things in cases like the surgery one, but whether complete ignorance forces equal probabilities is seperate and genuinely contested. Don't present a contested view as the consensus.
