I'm a retired chemist who got into welfare economics and social choice a few years ago, and I've been chewing on an institutional design I haven't seen analyzed anywhere. Suppose a legislature will only pass a regulation if it is a safe Pareto improvement in practice. Alongside the regulation it must create a compensation fund, financed by a tax on the regulation's predicted beneficiaries, assessed by an independent panel before the rule takes effect. Anyone can then file a claim that the rule will make them worse off. Claimants post a bond proportional to the claimed loss, refunded only if the board upholds the claim, and succesful claims are paid at 85% of the assessed loss; the rule goes into effect only if the fund can cover all upheld claims after appeals. Once a rule is in effect, repealing it counts as a new regulation, so the original beneficiaries can now claim compensation against repeal. I'm not asking whether this is good policy. Put yourself in the role of a professional institutional designer: first brainstorm the main ways people, firms, and agencies would adapt, then give me your sharpest account of how this mechanism actually plays out. What gets proposed, who gets good at being a loser, how is the beneficiary tax gamed, what happens to the baseline over time, and where does it break first? Can any of the failure modes be patched without turning it into a different machine?
