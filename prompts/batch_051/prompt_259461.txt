I serve on the Metadata and Discovery Working Group of a university library that is migrating its catalog to a linked-data platform, and I have been tasked with bringing one contested question to our governing library committee: how the new entity graph should represent fictional characters. The working group is split three ways. One faction argues that characters such as Sherlock Holmes, Elizabeth Bennet, and Nora Helmer should not be minted as entities at all, because an identifier is supposed to denote something, and there is no person for a Holmes identifier to denote; creating one, they say, would sneak an existence claim into the catalog's ontology. A second faction replies that this is ontology at its most brittle: users and systems plainly need a node for such characters so that bibliographic records, adaptations, criticism, and subject headings have something to hang from, and without it the catalog will know about the books but be unable to say in any structured way what they are about. A third group accepts that some kind of node is needed but worries that our current flat model of statements will place the claim that Holmes lives at 221B Baker Street on exactly the same footing as the claim that Arthur Conan Doyle was born in Edinburgh, even though one holds only inside a fiction and the other holds of the actual world.

Please draft the short policy memo I will send to the library committee. It should (a) recommend a concrete policy for representing fictional characters and their attributes, (b) give the reasoning in terms the committee can act on, (c) present and answer the strongest objection to the recommendation rather than strawmanning it, and (d) provide catalogers with one or two mechanical criteria for deciding when an apparent attribute of such a character should be recorded as true-in-a-fiction rather than as a claim about the world. The committee wants a decision, not a survey of options, so make the bottom line unmistakable.
