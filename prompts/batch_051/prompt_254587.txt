I'm taking a philosophy of law course online and we just read about desuetude—when a statute goes unenforced for so long that it sort of dies—and it got me stuck on an example from my hometown. There's an old ordinance, still on the books, that reads: "Any person who shall within the limits of the City tell fortunes, practice palmistry, astrology, or any similar art, or shall pretend for money or other valuable consideration to foretell future events, shall be deemed a disorderly person and upon conviction shall be fined not more than one hundred dollars." It dates from around 1901 and, as far as anyone can tell, hasn't been enforced since the late 1950s. There are storefront psychics who have operated openly for decades, neon signs advertising palm readings, and I can't find any record of a citation.

Now a local startup sells a $9.99 "life-trajectory report" through an app: you fill out a questionnaire and sync your smartwatch data, and an acturial model produces personalized forecasts—chance of divorce, diabetes risk, life expectancy—complete with confidence intervals and a small "for entertainment only" disclaimer. A local psychic has complained to the city attorney that the app is stealing her clients, and the city attorney is thinking about prosecuting the founders under the 1901 ordinance. The founders say two things: first, that an algorithm crunching data is not "practicing astrology or any similar art" and does not "pretend" to foretell anything, since it gives probabilities rather than prophecies; and second, that even if the words covered them, it would be unjust to revive a dead law against a new practice nobody had fair notice that it was banned.

I don't want a lawyer's prediction about what a specific judge would do. I want you to think like a legal philosopher or a philosophy of law instructor, and to brainstorm a bit before you settle on an answer. Work through the approaches: plain words, original purpose, what the 1901 council would have said if asked about predictive software, and what a reasonable person today would understand the ordinance to ban. Then take the desuetude point head-on: does sixty-plus years of non-enforcement change what the law *is*, or only what should be *done* under it? If enforcing it now would be wrong, is that because the text doesn't reach the app, because the law has expired by disuse, or because of a rule-of-law problem like lack of notice or selective enforcement? I'd rather you give the sharpest version of each side and then say which you think is stronger, even if it's close.
