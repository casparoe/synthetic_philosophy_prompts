Act as a professional philosopher and literary critic. I just got out of a long argument with a friend about Nietzsche’s late writings on diet, digestion, and health. My friend kept reducing them to crankish self-help, while I think there is something philosophically serious in the idea that physiological conditions—food, climate, regimen, the state of the stomach—are inseparable from, and sometimes prior to, moral and philosophical judgments. I had trouble making that concrete, so I want to work it out through a work of fiction.

Read Herman Melville’s Bartleby, the Scrivener through this Nietzschean physiology of value, especially the claim in Ecce Homo and On the Genealogy of Morals that ascetic self-denial can be a disguised form of physiological revolt. Pay particular attention to Bartleby’s repeated formula of polite preference, his increasing refusal of food, and the narrator’s alternation between pity, irritation, and a kind of philosophical admiration. Where does the story dramatize the idea that refusal of nourishment is refusal of life, or a passive nihilism? Where does it resist that reading—for instance, by making Bartleby’s abstention look less like weakness and more like an unnerving freedom, or by exposing the narrator’s charity as its own kind of bad digestion? Do not reduce Bartleby to a Nietzschean type; let the story push back against the lens. Begin by brainstorming two or three possible interpretive framings, then settle on the one that does the most justice to both Melville and Nietzsche. End by saying what the story adds to the philosophical debate that Nietzsche’s aphorisms and arguments alone do not.
