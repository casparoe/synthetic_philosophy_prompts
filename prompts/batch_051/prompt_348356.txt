I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about statutes of limitations, and I came away holding a view I'd now have to defend. She thinks limitations periods are a purely practical device — memories decay, witnesses die, evidence gets lost, people restructure their lives around the past being settled — and that time itself does no moral work whatsoever; it's just an imperfect proxy for those epistemic and pragmatic problems. I ended up thinking the passage of time is a genuine moral consideration in its own right. My position, roughly: for most wrongs, the moral claim to punish someone or to extract redress weakens, and eventually extinguishes, merely because a very long time has passed — even in a science-fictonal case where the evidence was perfectly preserved, everyone involved was still alive, and the wrongdoer hadn't changed a bit. She said that last clause gives the game away, that I'm smuggling in 'people change' and 'reliance' through the back door.

What would actually count against my view? I'm not interested in the standard practical objections about unreliable memory or evidence; I already grant those and still think time matters on top of them. I want the cases, hard intuitions, or structural arguments that would show the core claim — that time itself is a moral factor weakening old claims — is false or doing no independent work. Where does the position get in the most trouble: murder, torture, child abuse, wrongs concealed for decades so the victim couldn't have spoken earlier? Are those genuine defeaters, or just places where other values outweigh the temporal factor? I'm worried I've built a view that can't lose, because any counterexample can be filed under 'other considerations outweigh it here.' Be straight with me about whether the view is actually falsifiable, which of the possible defeaters would be decisive rather than merely awkward, and what the strongest version of the rival view — time is never a moral factor, only a proxy — would say about the cases that most tempt people toward my side.
