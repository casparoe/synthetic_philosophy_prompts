I'm a PhD student in philosophical theology, and I've been trying to make the doctrine of the Trinity come out as logically coherent without collapsing into modalism or tritheism. I've got a theory I keep trying to formalise, and I'd like an honest assesment before I take it to a seminar.

The move is this. I think the trouble is that people treat identity as a single relation that applies across the board, so from "the Father is God" and "the Son is God" they get "the Father is the Son" by transitivity, and then they either give up the distinction or give up monotheism. My proposal is that there are two sameness relations in play. First, consubstantiality, call it C: C is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive, and all three persons sit in one C-class because they are the same ousia. Second, hypostatic identity, call it P: P is also an equivalence relation, but each of Father, Son, and Spirit is P-related only to itself. Numerical claims, on my view, are always relative to a sameness relation: "one" means one C-class, and "three" means three P-classes. So there is exactly one God (one C-class) and three persons (three P-classes), and the classical inference is blocked because "the Father is God" does not express identity at all; it expresses consubstantiality. I call the whole apparatus "conceptual numerosity": you cannot count until you have said what you are counting by, and created things are just boring because all their sameness relations coincide.

To make it intuitive I use a unison example: three voices singing the same melodic line. Each voice has the whole melody, and the three are one melody but three voices. You wouldn't say the first voice is the second voice just because both sing the same tune. I'm aware the analogy might be messy, and maybe it makes the persons into mere performances of the essence, which is probably modalism, so I'm not wedded to it.

Where I worry it breaks: is this just Geach's relative identity with the serial numbers filed off? And even if the formal part is fine, have I actually dodged tritheism, or have I just defined "God" as one class of three things and called the class one? Also, if the persons are distinct under P, do I get three centres of consciousness and end up in social-trinitarian territory, with all the problems about whether God is a single agent? And what does "the one God" refer to on my scheme—is there a fourth thing, the class itself, over and above the persons? Be as brutal as you like, I'd rather find out now. Could you end with a one-sentence summary of your verdict as the last line, in the form \answer{...}?
