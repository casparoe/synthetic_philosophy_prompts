I am a high school student preparing an independent project for a cognitive science fair, and I have become interested in how people manage to learn a rule or concept from only a handful of examples. I have been working with Bongard problems — the kind of puzzle where you are shown six boxes on the left and six boxes on the right; all the left boxes follow some hidden rule, all the right boxes violate it, and the task is to infer the rule. What strikes me is that the same twelve boxes can almost always be summarized by many different rules, yet people tend to converge on one short, natural rule and to treat the others as absurd. I would like to present a designed example at the fair that makes this tension vivid.

Please construct a Bongard-style problem for me, described entirely in words using a small, fixed vocabulary of shapes, colors, sizes, and positions (for instance, one large red circle above two small blue triangles). It should have six left boxes, six right boxes, and one additional thirteenth test box. The problem should be engineered so that two genuinely different rules both place all twelve of the original boxes on the correct side, but the two rules disagree about which side the thirteenth box belongs on. One rule should be the short, natural rule that a human or a simplicity-based learner would immediately pick. The other should be a longer, gerrymandered, disjunctive rule — the kind of rule a skeptic about induction would defend on the grounds that it fits the twelve examples exactly as well as the short rule does.

Then please do the check. State both rules precisely; verify that each rule classifies all twelve original boxes correctly; say what each rule predicts for the thirteenth box; and explain why a simplicity-based or minimum-description-length learner prefers the short rule while a no-rule-is-objectively-better-than-any-other-consistent-rule skeptic denies that the thirteenth box has a determinate answer. Finally, tell me which feature of the case is doing the real work, what would happen to the example if that feature were removed, and how a defender of the gerrymandered rule would attack the example. The two rules must not be trivial restatements of each other, and everything must be checkable from the verbal description alone.
