I retired from chemistry a few years ago and finally got around to reading some philosophy I'd always meant to, mostly in the philosophy of mathematics. Lately I've been stuck on what I gather people call Benacerraf's dilemma, and I tried writing it out as a clean little argument so I could see exactly where it comes apart. Here's my attempt:

(1) If platonism about mathematics is true, then mathematical statements are true or false because of how things stand with abstract objects — numbers, sets, that sort of thing — which exist independently of us, outside space and time.
(2) If a statement is made true by how things stand with some objects, then, to know that statement, we'd need some kind of access to how things stand with those objects.
(3) The only kind of access a physical creature like us has to how things stand with objects is causal: our beliefs about them have to be produced, at least in part, by those objects acting on us or on our instruments.
(4) Abstract objects, being outside space and time, can't act on anything; they're causally inert.
(5) So if platonism is true, we can't have the access required by (2), and so we can't know any mathematical statement at all.
(6) But we plainly do know some mathematical statements — for example, that there is no largest prime.
(7) Therefore platonism is false.

I spent fourty years as a working scientist, and step (3) is more or less the operating system I ran on: no causal chain, no measurement, no knowledge. So I keep wanting to say the argument is valid and (3) is just true, and then something has gone wrong somewhere. I can't tell if there's a scope slip or an equivocation hiding in it — maybe 'access' is doing two different jobs in (2) and (3) — or whether the logic is fine and one premise just has to go. Can you tell me: is the argument valid as written? If not, which step exactly fails, and how? If it is valid, which premise would you reject, and does rejecting it actually dissolve the dilemma, or does it just move the bump under another rug?
