I have been reconstructing an objection to the epistemicist treatment of the sorites, and I cannot tell whether the argument goes through or where it comes apart. Its target is the combination of views that says there is a sharp cutoff for a vague predicate but that the cutoff is unknowable. I would like you to assess the numbered steps below, say whether the argument is valid, and identify exactly which step fails, if any does. If the argument is valid, tell me which premise the epistemicist should reject and how they would most plausibly resist it.

(1) According to epistemicism, for a vague predicate such as "heap," there is a sharp cutoff k: k grains are not a heap and k+1 grains are a heap, yet no one could ever be in a position to know that k is the cutoff. (2) If there is such a cutoff, it is fixed by the meaning of "heap," and the meaning of "heap" is fixed by the totality of facts about how competent speakers use the word — their applications, withholdings, hesitations, corrections, and their dispositions to apply or refuse it in new cases. (3) Those facts about use are ordinary empirical facts, and a sufficiently thorough investigator could in principle come to know all of them. (4) If an investigator knew all the facts about use and knew that those facts fix the meaning of "heap," then that investigator could in principle determine where the cutoff falls. (5) Therefore, if epistemicism is true, the cutoff is after all knowable in principle, which contradicts the unknowability asserted in (1). (6) So epistemicism is false.

My suspicion is that something goes wrong around step (4): I keep oscillating between thinking it is obviously true and thinking it conflates use fixing the meaning with use making the cutoff discoverable. Is the argument valid? If it is valid, is the conclusion forced only because step (4) is stronger than the epistemicist will allow? Give the strongest reading of step (4) the argument could plausibly claim, and say whether the argument still works on that reading.
