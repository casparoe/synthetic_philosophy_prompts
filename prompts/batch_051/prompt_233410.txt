A friend and I spent a long evening arguing about whether habitat destruction should be counted as an ecological crime, or whether—for the animals themselves—it can be a violent kind of mercy, and out of that argument came a concrete institutional proposal I would like you to dissect rather than endorse or dismiss. Suppose a well-funded foundation sets up a net-welfare land trust. Its charter allows it to acquire degraded or intact habitat, but every management decision on a parcel must be scored by one rule: choose the option that minimizes total expected animal suffering, summed over all sentient animals on the land, with no weighting for species charisma, biodiversity, rarity, or ecological function. Pain is counted in pain-days; a day of positive welfare counts as a small positive credit; and a death counts as the loss of all expected future net-positive days if that expectation is positive, and as the closing of a net-negative account if the expectation is negative. Predation, starvation, disease, and reproduction are all allowed to enter the ledger, and expected future offspring are included rather than ignored. Walk through how this trust would actually behave. Which habitats would it buy first, and which would it avoid, degrade, or deliberately convert? What would its managers do about large predators and about the nutrition-poor populations at the base of the food web? Where does its accounting break down or get gamed first, and which of those failure modes could be patched without abandoning the single-rule structure? In particular, say whether the charter systematically selects for barren ground, for dense populations of short and mostly miserable lives, for highly managed low-welfare-cost landscapes, or for some unstable cycle among them. Then state what this implies for the original argument about whether the loss or simplification of habitat is a net moral gain. End with a line of the form \answer{...} containing a short label for the general regime the land trust would most likely converge on.
