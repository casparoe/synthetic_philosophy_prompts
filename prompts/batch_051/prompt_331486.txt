I'm a journalist doing a piece on the EU's legitimacy problem, and I keep coming back to one tension that I'd love to have someone actually work through carefully. It's about subsidiarity and the democratic deficit, and whether the legal language the EU uses to soothe sovereignty worries is doing real work or just papering over the problem.

Here's the passage I'm chewing on. It's Article 5(3) of the consolidated Treaty on European Union:

"Under the principle of subsidiarity, in areas which do not fall within its exclusive competence, the Union shall act only if and in so far as the objectives of the proposed action cannot be sufficiently achieved by the Member States, either at central level or at regional and local level, but can rather, by reason of the scale or effects of the proposed action, be better achieved at Union level."

I geuss my question is something like this: on the one hand, this looks like a pretty strong structural limit on centralization. If member states can handle something themselves, Brussels is supposed to stay out. But on the other hand, the test isn't just about what's *possible* for member states—it's about what's *sufficient* and what's *better* achieved at the larger scale. And "better" is doing an awful lot of normative work there. A technocrat can almost always say a problem is better handled at scale because scale brings coordination, uniformity, spillover management, you name it. So does subsidiarity actually impose any meaningful constraint, or is it just a rhetorical place where the EU explains to national publics why their misgivings are parochial and sort of beside the point?

I want the piece to argue for a real conclusion, not just lay out both sides. So consider: is subsidiarity a genuine safeguard of democratic self-government, or is it a legal principle that mostly dignifies a technocratic expansion logic after the fact? And if it's somewhere in between, where exactly does the line fall? Maybe connect the philosophical question—what kind of justification does a collective decision need to override a smaller collective's authority—to the actual political dynamics in the EU, the yellow card procedure, the early warning system, all that. But don't hide behind institutional detail; I want the underlying democratic theory front and center.
