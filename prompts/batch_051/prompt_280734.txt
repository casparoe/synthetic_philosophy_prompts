I keep noticing an asymmetry in the way otherwise reflective people judge political violence, and I want to know whether a principle exists that justifies it rather than just rationalizing a side after the fact. When an organized faction within a state arms itself to displace the sitting ruler, we normally call the act sedition: a sin against the unity of the people. But when the ruler is called a tyrant, the same outward conduct—building a faction, stockpiling weapons, taking the palace—gets called resistance, liberation, or the removal of a tyrant. The observable behavior is nearly identical. So I want to know: is there a defensible moral difference, or is this just noise we live with?

Aquinas tries to supply the difference in the Summa Theologiae, II-II, Question 42, Article 2, in the reply to the third objection. He writes:

A tyrannical government is not just, because it is directed, not to the common good, but to the private good of the ruler, as the Philosopher states (Polit. iii, 5; Ethic. viii, 10).

He then says, in effect, that there is no sedition in disturbing a tyrannical government, and that it is the tyrant rather than the rebels who is guilty of sedition. The test, then, is whether rule is ordered to the common good or to the private good of the ruler.

I want this test pushed until it either breaks or holds. Here are the cases I have in mind: a ruler who maintains roads, courts, and defense but is steadily transferring the realm's land to his own family; a formally lawful democracy that predictably enacts policies enriching a small creditor class at the expense of everyone else; a foreign-imposed oligarchy that is administratively competent and stable but governs without consent. In each case, does armed resistance count as sedition or as defense of the common good? And does the answer support, undermine, or merely relocate the asymmetry?
