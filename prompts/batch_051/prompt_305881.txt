I read a piece in a technology magazine recently that was supposed to be an accessible version of the standard argument for why an AI could be dangerous even if it never wants anything in the human sense. The article summarises the view like this:

"Whatever final goal you give a machine, it will pick up certain instrumental goals along the way, because there are some things you need regardless of what you're ultimately trying to achieve: you need to keep existing, you need resources, and it helps to make yourself more intelligent. So a machine whose only final goal is to manufacture paperclips would resist being switched off, try to gather matter and energy, and rewrite its own code to be cleverer, simply because each of those makes it better at producing paperclips. This is called instrumental convergence, and the standard result in the field is that nearly any terminal goal converges on those same few subgoals. There's also the orthogonality thesis, which says intelligence and goals are completely independent: how smart a system is tells you nothing about what it wants, in the same way that how fast a car can go tells you nothing about where it is driving. That is why a superintelligent AI is no more likely to be benevolent than a merely clever one, and why the real danger from AI is not that it wants to hurt us but that it doesn't care about us at all: a paperclip maximiser could tile the whole future light cone with paperclips without ever wanting to hurt a single person. The practical upshot is that the alignment problem is just the problem of choosing the right final goal."

I've seen the paperclip example mentioned plenty of times, but I'd never seen the underlying reasoning laid out step by step, so I don't have a good feel for how much of this to trust. A couple of the jumps feel too quick to me—especialy the move from 'intelligence and goals are independent' to 'a superintelligent AI is no more likely to be benevolent'—but I can't tell whether the article is being unfair or whether I'm just missing a premise. Could you walk through this summary and tell me which parts a person who actually works on AI alignment would accept, which are common simplifications, and which are overstatements or conflations? And I'd particularly like to know whether the last sentence about the alignment problem actually follows from the earlier parts, or whether something has been smuggled in along the way.
