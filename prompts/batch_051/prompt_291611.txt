Having spent most of my working life in a laboratory, I am used to tracing a failure to something positive: a seal that cracked, a controller that sent the wrong voltage, a bearing that overheated. If someone had told me that the cause of an equipment breakdown was simply that a technician did nothing, I would have thought this was loose talk. Now that I have begun reading the philosophical literature on causation, however, I keep encountering cases in which the cited cause really is an absence—a nurse not checking a drug order, a switchman not throwing a signal, a gardener not watering the plant—and I find myself unsure how literally to take such claims.

Write an essay in which you defend a definite position on whether omissions can be genuine causes. In doing so, address at least these families of views: (a) counterfactual accounts, on which one asks whether the effect would have occurred had the omitted act been performed; (b) the view that causes must be occurrent events or physical processes, so absences are at best background conditions or explanatory shorthand; and (c) the idea that which omissions we cite is fixed by context, contrast, or what counts as the normal course of events. Use as a running test case the following pair of cases. In the first, two technicians each fail to perform a scheduled daily lubrication of the same bearing, and the bearing seizes; two lubrications over the relevant period were required for the bearing to survive, so if either technician alone had acted, the seizure would still have occurred. In the second, two technicians independently fail to press one of two separate shutoff buttons, each of which alone would have stopped the machine before the failure, so if either technician alone had pressed his button, the failure would not have occurred. Work through what the main accounts say about each technician in each case, and do not end on a noncommittal note: state what you believe the correct verdict is and defend it.
