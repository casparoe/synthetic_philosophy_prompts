Having recently retired from a research career in the natural sciences, I have been working through the philosophical literature on the ethics of belief, and I find myself returning to an old question: is doubt a duty? More precisely, is there a moral obligation — not merely an epistemic ideal — to withhold belief when the available evidence is insufficient? I am interested in arguments for the affirmative, the side of the debate usually associated with W. K. Clifford and his story of the shipowner.

I think I have collected the standard arguments for the duty of doubt. As far as I can tell, they are these four:

1. The shipowner argument. A person who lets himself believe what he wishes to be true, on evidence he knows to be weak, is answerable for the risks that belief imposes on others, even when the feared harm never materializes. Sincerity does not excuse him, because the careless formation of the belief is itself the wrong.

2. The contagion argument. Belief formation is not a private transaction. Beliefs spread through testimony, example, and education, so a person who believes on weak evidence degrades the shared epistemic environment and makes everyone's reliance on the word of others less secure.

3. The character argument. Every episode of evidence-insensitive belief entrenches habits of credulity and bad faith. Over time the agent becomes worse at distinguishing what he wants to be true from what the evidence supports, so doubt is required as a form of stewardship over one's own future capacity for honest judgment.

4. The universalizability argument. One cannot coherently will that everyone believe on insufficient evidence, because universal credulity would destroy the practice of taking one another's word for things, a practice on which even the credulous person depends.

I have the standard objections to the duty as well — above all the objection that belief is not under direct voluntary control, and the objection that a universal duty of doubt would be self-defeating or practically paralyzing — so please do not offer a criticism of the duty as if it were a fresh argument for it.

What I want from you is an argument for the moral duty of doubt that is not on my list and not a restatement, special case, or combination of items already on it. If you think I have omitted a well-known standard argument, say so briefly and name it, but do not count that as the new argument; I am trying to get beyond the stock moves. The new argument may draw on any plausible moral framework, but it must end up with a moral obligation, not merely an epistemic virtue or a pragmatic reason to be careful. Then be candid about whether the argument you have produced is any good: does it beg the question by assuming that evidence-insensitive belief is already blameworthy? Does it depend on a premise the Jamesian side would reject without much cost? Does it turn out, under pressure, to collapse into one of the four arguments above?

Please also mark explicitly which parts of your answer are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on this topic, and do not present a contested interpretation — for example, a view about which moral framework Clifford himself was actually relying on — as if it were consensus.

End with a final line in exactly this form: \answer{label; verdict} where label is a short name for your new argument (or none if you cannot produce one) and verdict is one of sound, unsound, or mixed.
