I'm a philosophy of biology PhD student and I keep going back and forth on the individuality debate. The standard evolutionary criterion says something is a biological individual only if its parts have a genuinely shared reproductive fate and internal conflict is low enough that selection acts mostly on the whole. The paradigm case that criterion was built on is a multicellular body, where the cells largely share a genome and a germline. But we now know basically every animal is a holobiont: its resident microbes have their own genomes, their own replication rates, and can be acquired horizontally, so they plainly don't share the host's reproductive fate. Does the evolutionary-individuality criterion survive that development? I'm not asking whether the host is still an organism in the everyday sense (it obviously is); I'm asking whether the standard criterion is still doing the real work, or whether it would now also commit us to saying the host isn't a biological individual, which would be a reductio of the criterion. If it fails, is there a natural repair that keeps something like shared fate but says it comes in degrees, or do we need a different criterion altogether? Also, as you go, separate what's actually settled in the literature from whats still contested, and flag any point where you're taking a contested reading as if it were the consensus.
