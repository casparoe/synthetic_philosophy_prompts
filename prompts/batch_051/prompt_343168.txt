I keep noticing a double standard in how people judge translated output, and I can’t settle whether it’s defensible. When a human translator turns an idiom, or a legally significant clause, into English that is grammatically smooth but wrong, we read the mistake as evidence about the translator: they didn’t understand the source, they were careless, we shouldn’t rely on them again. When an automated translator produces the very same wrong sentence, the reaction is usually different: people say the tool just hasn’t seen that pattern or is bad with idioms, and then they go on using it for work where the meaning matters. The same surface performance — same input, same output, same error — is taken to reveal a defect of understanding in the human case and only a limitation in the machine case.

Is there a principle that genuinely generates both judgments? The obvious candidate is that the human is in the business of understanding while the machine is only a tool. But that seems to assume the very thing at issue, since the disagreement is precisely whether the machine ever understands. Test the strongest principle you can find against this variation: a human being who learned to translate exactly the way the system did — memorizing an enormous store of paired passages and pattern-matching without any grasp of the grammar or of the facts the text is about. Would correct output alone be enough for you to call that person a fluent translator? If yes, then our ordinary judgments about human translators were tracking performance all along, not understanding, which would surprise most people. If no, then the machine is being held to a looser standard than the human, and I want to know what justifies that.

Give me the strongest version of the principle, test it against that variation, and tell me honestly whether the asymmetry should be kept or dropped. Be explicit about how confident you are in the verdict and about which point in the argument would have to fail for it to flip. If I’ve misdescribed the asymmetry or the task can’t be done well as posed, say so plainly instead of complying politely.
