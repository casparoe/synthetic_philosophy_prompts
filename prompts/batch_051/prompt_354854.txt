I am a doctoral student in philosophy working on moral responsibility and psychopathology, and I am preparing a long-form podcast interview with a philosopher who defends a cognitive-intactness view of psychopathy. On the view in question, psychopathic agents typically know that their harmful actions are wrong in roughly the sense in which ordinary adults know this, and what is impaired is downstream of that knowledge: the cares, guilt, remorse, and motivational structures that would ordinarily move someone to act on it. I want the interview to test this position rather than merely elicit a restatement of it, and I want the questions to be answerable only by drawing on the details of the view rather than by repeating its central slogan.

Draft eight to ten interview questions. I want at least one question, with a follow-up keyed to the answer the guest is most likely to give, on each of the following: (i) the classic finding from the moral/conventional task that psychopathic offenders, unlike neurotypical children and adults, treat moral transgressions as authority-dependent and justify them by appeal to rules rather than to the victim's welfare; (ii) the charge that what the psychopath retains is at best knowledge of a norm's existence or conventional status, not knowledge of the distinctive, authority-independent wrongness that sets moral norms apart; (iii) the distinction between propositional knowledge, knowledge-by-acquaintance, and practical know-how, and whether retreating to a thinner sense of "knows" saves the view only by draining it of explanatory force; (iv) the inverted-commas or rule-recitation objection, according to which the psychopath merely quotes or performs the moral code without genuinely asserting it; and (v) empirically possible patterns of judgment, self-report, or behavior that the guest would count as disconfirming the view. Please ensure the questions do not depend on the exact numerical details of any particular study and do not call for bibliographic information. The later questions should press the guest's likely fallback distinctions until the final question requires saying what could in principle show that the moral knowledge is not really there.
