I'm retired now — spent forty years in process chemistry — and I've been reading Kant's Groundwork with a small philosophy group. The bit that keeps snagging me is the universalizability test: act only on a maxim you could will as a universal law, the what-if-everyone-did-that test.

I've caught myself translating it into something from my old job: checking whether a process that runs fine in a single reactor is scalable — whether it still works if every plant on the planet runs it flat out. A maxim is one lab running its reaction; universalising it is the whole industry running it everywhere at once; and a maxim that fails the test is like a process that only works because everyone else is quietly absorbing your byproducts, or because you're the only one drawning from the shared feed. The contradiction in conception is just the physical contradiction — the process can't run at that scale — and the contradiction in will is more like the process that can run but no engineer would sign off on it, because it undermines the conditions that make the whole enterprise worth running.

The worry this leaves me with is: on that reading, the test only catches free-rider and tragedy-of-the-commons problems. It has nothing to say about a maxim like "when it's to my advantage and I can get away with it, I'll coerce anyone into a contract" — that universalises without any scale-up contradiction; the problem is something else (the person's standing, not the throughput). So I'm halfway to concluding that the universalizability test, by itself, is really just a coordination check and the actual moral work is being done by the Formula of Humanity smuggled in.

But I suspect the analogy is doing a lot of quiet work there, and as a chemist I know how an analogy can flatter a conclusion. Could you audit it for me? What corresponds to what, where the mapping actually breaks, which disanalogies matter for my conclusion and which are just surface, and whether the "it only catches free-riding" conclusion survives once you've pulled the analogy apart. British English, please.
