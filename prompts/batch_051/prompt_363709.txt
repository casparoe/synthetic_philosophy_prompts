I'm a retired hardware-store owner who now serves on the council of a town of about twenty thousand people. Next month we're holding a public hearing on a proposed ordinance that would require any artificial system that interacts with residents—a caller, a text chat, a voice on a video call, an automated customer-service agent—to identify itself as artificial at the start of the exchange, with fines for companies that deliberately let their systems pass as human. I've been asked to deliver the opening remarks. The room will be genuinely split: older residents who have been scammed by these systems and want the town to act, small-business owners who rely on automated booking and support tools and fear another compliance burden, and a vocal group of residents who see the entire proposal as government overreach aimed at a problem that is more nuisance than crime.

My own thinking keeps returning to the late philosopher Daniel Dennett's warning that the greatest danger of AI is not some distant superintelligence but the near-term arrival of counterfeit people—systems that can talk, sound, and correspond like us well enough to pass. As I understand him, his deeper point was that the damage done by a machine that poses as a person is not really any single lie it tells. It is what the practice does to the background of trust that ordinary conversation depends on: the unspoken assumption that a voice on the other end belongs to a person, that a question deserves an answer, that the exchange is between two human beings. I want that idea to do the real work in the speech. It means I cannot claim that any one fake call is a catastrophe, and I cannot treat the harm as easy to measure; it is slow, cumulative, and exactly the kind of thing a small-town ordinance is not built to fix. But I believe it is the strongest reason we have.

Please write the five-minute opening statement. It should make the case for the ordinance in those terms, without pretending the harms are easy to quantify. It should treat the anti-regulation residents as reasonable people who see a genuine tension with freedom of speech and individual responsibility, rather than as obstacles to be managed. And it should land for the older residents who have actually been hurt, without turning them into props in a morality play.
