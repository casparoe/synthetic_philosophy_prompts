I'm in a reading group on the philosophy of computation, and last week one member defended a startling conclusion: that the Church-Turing thesis, despite being treated as one of the great substantive claims of twentieth-century mathematics, cannot actually be confirmed or refuted by any evidence we could possibly produce, because the very activity of producing evidence for it guarantees that the evidence will come out on its side. Every step of her argument looked reasonable to me when I read it, but I do not want to accept the conclusion, and I have not been able to say where the argument overreaches. Maybe you can help.

Her argument was roughly this:

(1) The Church-Turing thesis says that the informal notion of an effectively computable function — a function computable by a finite routine that a human could in principle follow without insight or ingenuity — coincides exactly with the formal notion of a Turing-computable function.

(2) If the thesis is a substantive claim, then it must be vulnerable to counterexample: there should be some possible function that is effectively computable but not Turing-computable, or Turing-computable but not effectively computable, and evidence should be able to tell us which side a given function falls on.

(3) Consider any function we might produce as a positive instance. To produce it as effectively computable, we must exhibit a finite routine for computing it.

(4) Any finite routine we can exhibit and describe precisely enough to serve as evidence can be encoded as a Turing machine. So every positive instance we can generate is guaranteed in advance to fall on the Turing-computable side.

(5) Now consider a counterexample: a function alleged to be effectively computable but not Turing-computable. To make that allegation concrete, someone would have to produce the effective procedure for it. But by the same reasoning as in (4), that procedure would be codable as a Turing machine, so the alleged counterexample collapses back into a positive instance.

(6) The other kind of counterexample — a function that is Turing-computable but not effectively computable — never really gets off the ground, because a Turing machine's table of instructions is itself a finite routine of exactly the sort that "effectively computable" describes.

(7) So no possible example can ever tell against the thesis: any example we can formulate is either automatically absorbed into the positive evidence or ruled out by the very meaning of "effective procedure."

(8) A claim that is set up in advance so that every possible piece of evidence either confirms it or disqualifies itself is not a claim that can be rationally assessed against the world; at best it is a stipulation about how to use words, and at worst an article of faith.

(9) Therefore the Church-Turing thesis, as it is usually understood, is not a substantive claim at all.

I cannot decide whether the argument goes wrong at the step about positive evidence being circular, at the step about untestability, or somewhere else entirely, and I am not even sure whether the conclusion is false or just mislabeled. Which premise or step should be given up? Explain why that premise looked fine, and say what each of the standard ways of understanding the thesis's epistemic status — as a definition, as a quasi-empirical hypothesis, and as the endpoint of a conceptual analysis — has to give up in order to answer the argument.
