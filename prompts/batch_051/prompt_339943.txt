I've been going in circles about translation, and I want someone to make my gut calls add up. Say a linguist is learning a language cold, and a rabbit runs past and the guy says 'gavagai.' The linguist writes down 'gavagai means rabbit.' But another linguist could just as easily write 'gavagai means undetached rabbit-part' or 'gavagai means rabbit-stage' — same scene, same words, three different translations, and no observation seems to break the tie. Here are my verdicts. In that small case I do think rabbit is the right one; the other two feel like tricks, not real rivals. But when I zoom out to whole languages I flip: two complete translation manuals that fit every observation are both fine, and there's no hidden fact about which one is correct. So in the big case I think there's no fact of the matter, and in the little case I think there is, and that feels inconsistent. More cases. My niece calls the family dog 'wuff,' and I have zero temptation to translate it as dog-part or dog-stage; its just means the dog, because she uses it for the same dog over time, after it grows and after it gets shaved. That feels fixed in a way the linguist's rabbit case didn't, even though both are just animals plus words. And then the far edge: a tribe's creation chant, where one manual makes it about astronomy and another makes it about genealogy. I want to say at most one of those is right — if the chant is really about the sky, the genealogy one is just wrong — but my whole-language rule says I don't get to say that. So: what principle tells me when translation has a fact of the matter and when it doesn't? Test it against these cases, say which of my verdicts it can't save, and apply it to one I still can't settle — translating a noun for a kind of wind that only ever shows up inside a single proverb and nowhere else.
