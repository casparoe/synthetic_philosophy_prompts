I'm in a cultural evolution seminar and there's an asymmetry in ordinary talk that's been bugging me all term. When a phrase explodes online—say a stupid remix of a niche cooking clip—my classmates are completely comfortable talking as if the idea is a replicator: it spreads, mutates, gets selected for attention, and maybe uses people as hosts. But when my grandmother teaches me her exact method for patching wool socks, or when a carpenter insists on the one right way to sharpen a chisel because that's how his teacher learned it, no one calls those things memes. They call them techniques, craft knowledge, traditions. Yet sock-patching can be copied, it varies across families, it gets selected against when mending becomes more expensive than replacing; it just spreads slowly and through a vertical chain. So the asymmetry is roughly: we grant the full replicator-with-interests language to cultural variants that are fast, horizontal, catchy, and digitized, but not to cultural variants that are slow, vertical, embodied, boring, or stubborn. Is there a principle here or just a bias toward things that feel viral?

I'd like you to be the philosopher of biology auditing this. Start by spelling out the strongest principle you can find that would justify treating internet memes as genuine replicators (or as more literal cases of replication) while treating craft techniques as merely learned practices. Then stress-test that principle. Consider a familys private joke that behaves exactly like a meme but never escapes the household; a local baking technique that goes viral on YouTube for two weeks and then disappears; an architectural style that spreads across a continent over four centuries with almost no one naming it as a meme. For each, say whether the principle still draws the line where ordinary talk draws it, or whether the asymmetry starts to collapse. Be explicit about what "replicator" has to mean for the asymmetry to hold, and end with a verdict: should I accept the asymmetry, reject it, or replace it with something else? Don't just give me a taxonomy—give me the reasoning that would convince someone who disagreed.
