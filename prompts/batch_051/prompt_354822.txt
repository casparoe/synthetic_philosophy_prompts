A friend of mine went deep into Wang Yangming and now I'm stuck in an argument I can't get out of. It goes:

(1) For Wang, knowledge and action are originally one: there is no real knowing that doesn't already issue in doing, and someone who 'knows' what is right but doesn't do it doesn't actually know it in the relevant sense.

(2) So if a person truly knows that telling the truth is the right thing to do here, they tell the truth; if they lie anyway, their claimed knowledge was just words.

(3) But people lie, cheat, and procrastinate after thinking hard about what to do — they can say exactly why the act is wrong and then do it anyway.

(4) From (2), in every one of those cases the person did not know the act was wrong, no matter how sincere they sounded.

(5) Blame and guilt seem to be for exactly this: acting against your own better judgment.

(6) Therefore, on Wang's picture, genuine akrasia — knowing the better and doing the worse — never happens, and our whole practice of blaming deliberate wrongdoers rests on a false description of what was going on in them.

I don't want the conclusion, but each step looked fine to me. Which premise should I give up, and what does giving it up cost? If the right move is to keep all the premises and accept the conclusion, tell me what that actually commits Wang to, and whether it leaves ordinary blame intact or replaces it with something else.
