My friend and I have been going back and forth about whether its actually responsible to reproduce propaganda in order to critique it. The concrete version is a media-literacy class that screens a fascist recruitment film so students can pick it apart: is that inoculation, or does it launder the thing and hand it a second life? We ended up arguing about Paul Verhoeven's Starship Troopers, because the movie seems to do exactly this on purpose — it dresses up as the very militarist propaganda it's mocking, with the fake newsreels, the citizenship-through-service premise, and the clean-cut heroes, and then trusts you to notice that the whole thing is one notch too slick.

I want to read the film through that debate about harm, speech, and platforms. Does the film successfully critique what it reproduces, or does it partly become the thing it's satirizing? Where does the irony actually do critical work, and where does it risk tipping over into the thing it's parodying? One thing I keep coming back to: the critique only works if you catch the irony, and when a clip of one of those newsreel segments circulates on its own the irony can evaporate entirely. So maybe the film is a good test case for whether a 'critique frame' travels with the reproduction or gets stripped in circulation — and for whether a platform is right to leave up propaganda that arrives with a critique attached.
