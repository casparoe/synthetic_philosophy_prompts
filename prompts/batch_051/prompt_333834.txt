I keep coming back to an argument a student gave me last term, and I'd like a second read on it. It's meant to show that the constructivist's refusal to assert Goldbach or its negation is a practical limitation, not a logical one. Take it step by step and tell me whether it goes through constructively, and if not, exactly which step breaks.

(1) Pick any specific even number n > 2. There is a finite routine that settles weather n is the sum of two primes: just check the finitely many pairs of primes below n. So for each particular n, the intuitionist happily asserts 'either n is the sum of two primes or n is not the sum of two primes.'

(2) Goldbach's conjecture is exactly the claim that for every even number n > 2, n is the sum of two primes.

(3) So Goldbach is a universal claim built entirely out of decidable instances.

(4) If every instance of a universal claim is decidable, then the universal claim itself is decidable: either all the instances hold, or one of them is a counterexample.

(5) Therefore Goldbach's conjecture is either true or false even on constructivist standards, and a constructivist who refuses to assert 'Goldbach or not-Goldbach' isn't making a principled point about excluded middle; theyre just admitting that nobody has been able to check every even number.

I suspect (4) is the problem, but I want the precise diagnosis: which inference is it trying to make, and what would a constructivist replace it with? And is (1) really as innocent as the student says?
