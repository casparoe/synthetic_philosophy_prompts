I keep having the same argument with two friends and I'd like it settled rather than getting another round of 'yes but sovereignty.' One is a mildly libertarian engineer who thinks seasteading is obviously the answer to overbearing states; the other is a former EU lawyer who says you never really opt out, you just move to somewhere the state currently decides not to enforce. I proposed a rough test for when someone has genuinely opted out of being governed by a state, as opposed to just living somewhere the state doesn't bother them much. My four criteria: (1) the rules that actually govern your day-to-day conduct are made by a non-state body and can be changed without any state having a veto; (2) you pay no compulsory levies to any state, including taxes assessed on your income merely because you hold its citizenship; (3) if a state turned up with armed officials, it would have no legal route to coerce you that doesn't depend on your cooperation; and (4) you have a real fallback for the things states normally provide — security, dispute resolution, money, emergency services — that isn't just the state by another route.

Now consider a seastead I'll call Kelp: a floating platform in international waters, outside every territorial sea and exclusive economic zone, housing about nine hundred people. Nobody owns land; your apartment is a share in a company incorporated in a Carribean flag state. The operator runs a private security force, a fire service, a clinic, and a binding arbitration tribunal under a contract each resident signs. Residents pay monthly dues and can leave by selling their unit, no exit fee. There is no legislature, but the operator can amend the terms every five years after a residents' vote with a supermajority requirement. Most residents remain citizens of their home states, including one state that taxes its citizens' worldwide income wherever they live; a few have tried to renounce, but the process is pricey and not guarenteed. The nearest state's navy has so far avoided boarding the platform, saying it has no jurisdiction over a structure on the high seas, though a couple of states have fined residents for things they sold online to people in their old countries.

Apply my four criteria to Kelp one by one, and say where they give the wrong answer or just run out. Then tell me whether the test itself is aiming at the right thing: is 'opting out of the state' really a bundle of practical conditions like those, or is it categorical, like ceasing to be a citizen? If you were rewiring the test, which criterion would you drop or replace, and does that mean my lawyer friend or my engineer friend is right? Please write the answer in British English.
