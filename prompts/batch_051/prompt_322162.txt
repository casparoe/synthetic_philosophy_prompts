You are a professional philosopher specializing in early Chinese ethics. Work through the problem in stages: first brainstorm two or three candidate analyses of "partiality," then test and revise them against the cases below rather than settling immediately on a familiar formula.

Mozi, "Universal Love II" (兼愛中), in W. P. Mei's translation, gives this diagnosis of the world's disorders:

"At present feudal lords have learned only to love their own states and not those of others. Therefore they do not scruple about attacking other states. The heads of houses have learned only to love their own houses and not those of others. Therefore they do not scruple about usurping other houses. And individuals have learned only to love themselves and not others. Therefore they do not scruple about injuring others. ... Therefore all the calamities, strifes, complaints, and hatred in the world have arisen out of want of mutual love."

I want a working definition of the "partiality" Mozi is condemning here — not the whole theory of universal love, just the target concept. Your definition should classify these cases correctly:

1. A ruler launches a war of conquest to enrich his own state while knowing it will devastate another.
2. A father spends his time and money only on his own children's welfare and gives nothing to equally needy children abroad.
3. A woman feels far deeper grief at her mother's death than at a stranger's death, though she would help either if she could.
4. A public hiring committee member votes to appoint her less-qualified nephew over a more qualified stranger.
5. A person who can pull only one person from a freezing river chooses his brother over an equally or slightly more endangered stranger.
6. A municipal council funds a redundant local monument rather than paying for clean water in a distant region.
7. A person loves her dog more than she loves most humans and pays for the dog's expensive surgery while ignoring more cost-effective human suffering.

I am confident that (1) and (4) are cases of what Mozi is attacking, and I am inclined to say that (3) is not; the others genuinely puzzle me. Give the definition, work through the cases one by one, and flag any verdict you reach reluctantly or any point where the definition has to be revised. Then answer one further question: does the definition you settle on condemn the Confucian "graded love" — the special, stronger care for one's own family — as such, or only some of its manifestations?
