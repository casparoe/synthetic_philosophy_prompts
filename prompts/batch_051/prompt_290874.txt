I'm a comparative politics PhD student, and next month I'm presenting a theory-heavy paper at my department's comparative politics workshop. The paper defends a particular way of using the concept of democratic backsliding. My thesis is that the concept is neither a purely descriptive measurement category nor a merely partisan label for outcomes the speaker dislikes. It is applied correctly only when three conditions are met: (1) an incumbent or governing bloc uses legally available institutional levers to degrade the capacity of the political opposition and independent civil society to form, compete, contest, and win power; (2) the changes are cumulative and make future alternation materially harder rather than being isolated policy wins; and (3) the baseline against which sliding is measured is the country's own recent institutional practice, not some fixed endpoint of full liberal democracy. On this account, the Fidesz constitutional revisions in Hungary, the AKP's capture of the judiciary and media in Turkey, and the PiS remaking of Poland's constitutional court can all count as backsliding without smuggling in the assumption that the speaker's preferred party must win. But the concept does real evaluative work, and I want to stop pretending it is a neutral instrument.

The audience is mostly empirical comparativists. About a third work with the standard cross-national democracy indices and will immediately ask how the concept operationalizes. There are country specialists on Hungary, Poland, and Turkey, one person working on American political development and democratic norm erosion, and one normative political theorist who is openly allergic to vaguely disguised moralizing in empirical work. I need a Q&A prep sheet, not a restatement of the argument.

Please anticipate the seven or eight objections I will actually get from this room, not the ones I would get at a democratic-theory conference. Order them by how likely and how damaging they are, and phrase each the way the relevant audience member would phrase it. For each, give me a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is soft and where I should expect follow-up. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
