A friend and I have been arguing about Life of Pi since it came out, and its landed in my lap to lead a discussion on it for a philosophy-and-film group. I want to frame the discussion around the distribution of consciousness across animals: not just whether humans are special, but which animals are conscious at all, whether consciousness is one thing or a graded bundle of capacities, and how you could ever tell from the outside. The film seems to be in two minds about this. Pi's father tells him, after the tiger-and-goat episode, that animals are not friends and that what he sees in Richard Parker's eyes is just his own reflection, which is a pretty deflationary, almost Cartesian line. Then the lifeboat section is nothing but Pi attributing moods, intentions, dignity, and resentment to Richard Parker, and the narrative treats those attributions as practically useful and maybe true. And the ending, with the investigators and the human version of the story, pulls the rug out from under everything: if the tiger is a projection, then the whole animal-mind reading collapses.

What I want is a reading of the film as a sustained exploration of whether we can separate a real nonhuman mind from the stories we project onto it. Where does the film support a generous, continuity-between-humans-and-other-animals view, and where does it deliberately undercut it? I'd especially like help with the ending: is the business about which story is better a defense of the projection, an admission that the animal mind is unknowable, or something else? And do you think the distinction between affective consciousness and self-consciousness helps here, or does the film blur it on purpose?
