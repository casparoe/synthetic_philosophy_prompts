Just got back from a long argument with my friend about whether leaning on ChatGPT to do the actual reasoning in a philosophy paper is making us dumber. His whole line was that it's just a tool, like a calculator; mine was that a calculator doesn't tell you which move is worth making. By the end neither of us could actually state the line we were fighting over. So before I read one more take on this, I want the full spec: what does a theory of epistemic dependence on AI have to get right before it earns the right to tell me whether I'm eroding my understanding or just using a tool? Lay out the requirements — the phenomena it has to accommodate, the distinctions it can't quietly collapse, the empirical regularities it has to be consistent with — and for each one say why it's a requirement and which way it cuts. The cases I care about include at least: the person who uses a calculator after already learning arithmetic versus the kid who uses one while learning it, the GPS user who can't navigate their own neighborhood anymore, the doctor who defers to an ML readout against their own read of the scan, and the writer who lets the model draft the argument and then just edits the result. Then mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just one side of the argument dressing up its preferences as constraints. And then the part I actually want: which single requirement does the most to knock out the popular positions — the "it's just a tool" view, the "it inevitably deskills you" view, and the "understanding was never in the individual head anyway" view. Dont give me a full scorecard; I want to know where the line has to be drawn and why that one criterion does the most work. If two of the requirements can't be jointly satisfied, flag that too, because that's more interesting than any verdict.
