I've been trying to get my head around the games-as-art debate, and I've come to think a lot of the arguments are people using the word art to mean diffrent things, or quietly ranking different aspects of a game. So I put together a list of six things a game might do that could be where the art actually is. I made these up from my notes, so treat them as a palette to argue about instead of a canonical list.

Here are the six:

1. Expressive constraint. The rules work like formal constraints in a poem: they aren't there to be fun or fair, but to create a particular hardness, rhythm, or tone the player has to live inside.
2. Agency as material. The choices on offer, and the fact that their consequences are visible in the fiction, are the medium of the work the way paint is for a painting. What the player can meaningfully do is the aesthetic content.
3. Deliberate friction. Some part of the game is needlessly awkward or punishing, not as bad design but because the texture of doing it is part of what's being conveyed.
4. Negative space. What the game leaves out (a win state, a score, character interiority, clear feedback) does as much of the work as what it puts in, and the omission reads as intentional.
5. Spectator legibility. An audience can get a real part of the work from watching the player's choices and inputs, so the performance of play is the object rather than just a private blur.
6. Reflexivity. The game leans on or comments on the conventions of games, the way a sonnet can be about sonnets, so part of the art is noticing the medium itself.

For each one, give me a weight from 0 to 1 for how much of the aesthetic work of games-as-art it actually does, with the six weights summing to 1, and then rank them. For your top two, sketch one concrete design choice in an imaginary game that would embody each (don't lean on a real title). For the bottom one, say whether it's genuinely weak or just hard to combine with the others, and what a game would have to add for it to carry real aesthetic force.

One thing I specifically want: separate the parts of your answer where most people who seriously think about games and art would just nod along, from the parts where you're taking a genuinely contested line on aesthetics. Don't write as if your weighting is the consensus. If your ranking would flip under a different definition of art, tell me that, and which definition of art you're working with.
