I keep running into a pattern in the nudging/paternalism stuff I read, and I'd like to know if there's an established name for it, or if it's a genuine gap.

The pattern: a hospital cafeteria makes the vegetarian dish the default, and the defense is that nobody stops you ordering the burger, its just not the default. A pension provider auto-enrolls you at 6%, and the defense is that you can opt out with one phone call. A website pre-checks the box that shares your data, and the defense is that you can uncheck it. Each one, defended on its own, sounds fine; freedom is preserved, it's just a nudge. But I never see anyone defend the sum of them. Taken together, you'd have to be a full-time opt-outer just to end up with the life you'd have had if none of the defaults had been set. The individual interventions are choice-preserving, but the choice environment they jointly create isn't experienced as optional, and no single choice architect ever intended that environment.

What I'm after is the name, if there is one, for the gap between evaluating each nudge one-by-one and evaluating the resulting choice environment as a whole. The closest thing I know is the old tyranny of small decisions idea, but that's about lots of uncoordinated individual choices producing a collective outcome nobody wanted, whereas here the small decisions are made by different well-meaning designers, each of whom can truthfully say their own contribution preserves freedom. If there's no name, I'd want a term and a way of distinguishing it from plain too-many-nudges and from sludge/friction arguments, since I'm not saying each nudge is coercive—I'm saying the aggregate does something none of the parts do.
