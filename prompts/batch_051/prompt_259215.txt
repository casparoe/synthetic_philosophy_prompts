I am a novelist, and I have been drafting an essay about naming in fiction that I think presses on how philosophers of language distinguish given names from family names. I would like your comments on the essay idea and the specific claims below, chiefly on their philosophical accuracy and on where the argument is strongest and weakest.

The essay, in note form, runs as follows.

1. The standard picture, as I understand it: proper names are rigid designators that refer directly to an individual, not via a cluster of descriptions, and their reference is fixed by an initial baptism and propagated down a causal-historical chain. Fictional names are then classed either as empty names or as names that refer only within the pretense of the fiction.

2. That picture flattens a distinction novelists rely on constantly. Within a novel, a family name often behaves as a rigid designator of a lineage or a social position rather than of a person: it survives across generations, inheritance, recognition, and disinheritance, and readers continue to track it even when the individual bearing it fails every description associated with the family. A given name, by contrast, frequently behaves more like a description or a title: it can be ironic, aspirational, earned, or withheld.

3. The split is visible in ordinary narrative devices. A character may be known only by a surname for much of a novel and acquire a given name at the moment of recognition or change; a character may change surnames and remain plainly the same individual; two characters in different works may share a given name without being read as the same person, while a recurring surname across a cycle is read as asserting the continuity of a house rather than a person.

4. The proposal I want to develop: fiction separates two continuity conditions that theories of reference run together. One is the identity of the individual; the other is the continuity of a social role or line. The given/family name distinction is one of the main devices a novelist uses to control which condition is active at a given moment. If that is right, fictional reference is not just a matter of empty names or pretense; it is a managed system in which part of a name works quasi-rigidly and part works description-like.

What I most want from you is a critical judgment of whether this is sound and worth pursuing. Begin with your bottom-line assessment in the first sentence, then give the reasoning: where my handling of rigidity, empty names, and the causal-historical picture is fair or unfair; where the proposal earns its keep rather than collapsing into something familiar; and where it may overstate a merely stylistic convention. Give no preamble and do not restate the notes.
