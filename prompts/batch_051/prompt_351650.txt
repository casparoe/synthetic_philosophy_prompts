My father is eighty-two, and eighteen months ago he was diagnosed with early-stage Alzheimer's. His memory is genuinely uneven: he can describe his old work in precise detail but sometimes cannot remember whether he took his medication an hour earlier. Before he retired he was a bank examiner, and he still keeps the small paper ledgers he always used. In January he began telling me and my two sisters that his part-time caregiver was taking small amounts of cash from the drawer where he keeps his wallet and an envelope of banknotes. We listened gently but did not really credit the report. Privately we agreed that a man in his condition is an unreliable witness about recent events; one sister reminded us that people with dementia often make false accusations of theft, and said it would be irresponsible to act on his word and risk an unjust accusation against the caregiver. I decided, without telling anyone, to verify the claim myself. His ledger, the cash in the drawer, and the dates he had given matched exactly. We have since dismissed the caregiver.

Now my sisters disagree about what this shows. One says we acted correctly: we took the accusation seriously enough to investigate it, and before it was verified we owed no one belief when a caregiver's livelihood was at stake. The other says we wronged him, and that our skepticism had less to do with the actual patchiness of his memory than with a picture of people with dementia as globally untrustworthy; we made him prove on paper what a cognitively normal person's word would have settled much sooner. I can see something in both positions. I am not especially interested in assigning blame; I want to understand what happened and what principle should govern how we receive his testimony from here. Was our initial discounting a reasonable response to his real cognitive deficits, an unjust refusal to treat his word as something that could settle the question, or a mixture of both? And the next time he reports a harm or an irregularity, what should we do differently?
