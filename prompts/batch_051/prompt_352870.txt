I've been keeping track of how my health-policy professor rules on cases where you can only help one of two people, because I'm trying to figure out whether she actually believes you can compare the intensity of different peoples' pain, or whether she's secretly an objective-severity person who ignores self-reports. In seminars she's made these calls:

- Shattered femur, patient reports 9/10 pain, versus a mild ankle sprain, patient reports 3/10: treat the femur, no debate.
- Advanced metastatic cancer, patient rates their own suffering 8/10, versus well-managed diabetes with mild foot numbness, patient rates it 3/10: treat the cancer.
- Severe burn, objectively awful, self-report 10/10, versus a mild rash, self-report 2/10: treat the burn, obviously.

The problem is that objective severity and self-report always point the same way in the ones I've seen, so I can't tell what's doing the work.

Now we're on a small allocation committee together and a case landed on our desk. Two patients need the same slot for a knee replacement. They have the same stage of osteoarthritis, same functional scores, same imaging, same age, same general health. One patient insists her pain is an 8/10; the other says his is a 3/10. The committee has to decide Thursday.

What should she say, if she wants to be consistent with her earlier verdicts? Which earlier case is the closest analog? And what principle is she adopting either way: if she gives the slot to the 8/10 reporter, is she now committed to treating self-reports as comparable across people, and if she calls it a tie, is she committed to saying self-reported intensity doesn't count? Also tell me what further case would finally settle whether she's an objective-severity person or a self-report person.
