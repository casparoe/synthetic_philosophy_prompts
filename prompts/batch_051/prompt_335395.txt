I am an undergraduate in a philosophy of language seminar and need to write a short paper on conditionals. Please compare two accounts of the difference between indicative and counterfactual conditionals: the possible-worlds truth-conditional account associated with Stalnaker and Lewis, on which both kinds have truth values and differ mainly in which antecedent-worlds are selected, and the suppositional account associated with Adams and Edgington, on which an indicative conditional has no truth value and its degree of assertibility equals the conditional probability of its consequent given its antecedent, while counterfactuals require a different kind of supposition. To make the comparison concrete, use an example in which the same antecedent occurs first in indicative and then in counterfactual form, such as a murder trial where the killing indisputably occurred and the accused was convicted: one can accept that if the accused did not commit the murder, someone else did, while rejecting that if the accused had not committed the murder, someone else would have. Which view better explains that contrast? Does the way we assign probabilities to conditionals and embed them under probably support or undermine the truth-conditional view's assignment of truth values to indicatives? End with a definite judgment about which account is better overall and state its principal cost.
