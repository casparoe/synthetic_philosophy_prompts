I'm a product researcher at a mid-size health-tech company, and part of my job is designing the screens where patients agree to things: telehealth consent, sharing data with research partners, binding arbitration, billing. Legal currently treats a click on 'I agree' after a scrollable wall of text as enough, and my team is expected to sign off on the flows. But I keep hitting cases where I don't think any actual person agreed; they just clicked to make the task go away. I've been asked to write an internal decision procedure for deciding when a click-to-agree flow counts as getting a real yes from a patient, as opposed to legally defensible theatre. It has to be reusable across product updates, not a one-off, and it can't read like a legal memo: I want the ordinary notion of 'did this person agree,' plus the part where we should care about the difference between real agreement and mere assent.

Constraints: the screen has to stay short because patients won't read more then a few lines; we can add at most one plain-language comprehension question or a short forced delay, but not a quiz; and final legal risk sits with legal, not with us.

Please give me the step-by-step procedure a product researcher can actually run: what to ask about the term itself, the patient's situation, and the interface; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure stops with 'this can't go behind a standard click'; and where it hands the decision to a human because it's a values call rather than a design call. Flag the steps that are really judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run the procedure on these three:
(a) a default-opt-in for sharing de-identified patient data with outside researchers, with a 90-second opt-out;
(b) a binding arbitration clause added to existing terms, shown only as a 'we've updated our terms' banner months ago;
(c) a one-time consent for appointment reminder texts.
Use one of these to show where your procedure says no even though our legal team would be fine with it, and say explicitly what the procedure cannot decide.
