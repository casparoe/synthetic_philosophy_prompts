I've been mulling something I call 'eponymic drift', and I want an honest assessment rather than politeness. Stigler's law says discoveries end up named after someone other than the first discoverer, but that treats it as a kind of error. My idea is that eponyms are basically retrieval technology: the label that sticks to a result is the one that makes it easiest for a community to store, recall and cite the idea, so credit drifts toward names that are already salient, short, pronounceable or attached to a memorable story, almost regardless of priority. The original discoverer drops out because they make a bad label, not because the community is being unfair. Imagine two simultaneous co-discoverers, one with a familiar name and earlier famous work, the other with a longer unfamiliar name who actually did slightly more of the work; my bet is the eponym settles on the first. I'd also predict that when a field needs precision more than memorability, it quietly retires eponyms. Is this a real concept, or just a dressed-up metaphor? Has someone already worked this out under another name? And where does it break — what does it wrongly predict or excuse?
