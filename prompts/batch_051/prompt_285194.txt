I am part of a reading group on the neurobiology of moral status, and one member—a philosopher of cognitive science—has been building up a record of case-by-case verdicts rather than announcing a theory. Her rulings so far are these. A typical adult human, brain roughly 1,400 grams, is a full moral patient: her interests count equally with any other human's. An elephant, brain roughly 4,800 grams, with complex social bonds and mourning-like behavior, is also a full moral patient, but she has said explicitly that the larger brain does not outrank the human's. A chimpanzee, brain roughly 400 grams, with flexible tool use and rich social cognition but no full language, has strong moral status, just below a human's. A dog, brain roughly 80 grams, with clear sentience and social responsiveness, has moderate moral status. A mouse, brain under a gram and clearly sentient, has minimal but nonzero moral status. A silicon system that passes every behavioral test we can devise for human-level cognition but has no biological brain or neurons is not granted full moral status; she says behavior alone leaves its status unsettled.

The new case I want to bring her is this. A species—call them the vaal—has a brain of roughly 90 grams and, by stipulation, a total neuron count in the range of the dog case rather than the chimpanzee case. Nevertheless, through unusually efficient neurons and connectivity, they exhibit full human-level general intelligence, language, self-consciousness, and social emotion. Treat the behavioral and cognitive facts as stipulated, and do not get pulled into whether the biology is realistic. Before I present the case, I want to predict how she will rule and, more importantly, to make explicit the principles doing the work. Tell me: (1) what are the strongest candidate rules consistent with her six recorded verdicts; (2) what does each candidate rule predict about the vaal; (3) which of her earlier cases is the closest analog and why; (4) what is your best prediction of her verdict, with the reasons for preferring that reading of her record; and (5) specify one further case, tightly described, whose answer would most cleanly separate the leading candidate rules. If her record genuinely underdetermines the answer, say so and identify which principle she would be committing herself to under each possible ruling.

Throughout, separate what is settled by her recorded verdicts from what is genuinely contested among people who work on moral status, and do not present any one account of brain size and moral considerability as the consensus when it is not.
