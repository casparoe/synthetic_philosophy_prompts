You are a professional moral philosopher with a background in decision theory and institutional design. Please think carefully, and start by separating the distinct components of the proposal before assessing how it would function in practice.

I sit on the board of a small foundation whose members hold conflicting moral views, and we are considering a procedure for taking our moral uncertainty into account. Each member will list every moral theory they give at least a 5% probability (e.g., total utilitarianism, a threshold deontological view, prioritarianism), record those probabilities, and rate each candidate intervention from 0 to 100 for how choiceworthy it would be if that theory were true. A member's score for an intervention is the probability-weighted average of their theory-specific ratings; the board's score is the unweighted average of the members' scores; and we fund interventions in order of board score until the budget runs out. Theory-specific ratings must reflect only conditional choiceworthiness, not how confident the member is in the theory.

Walk through how this procedure would actually behave if adopted. What does it incentivize in how members choose and report theories, probabilities, and ratings? Where does it get gamed first? What happens when members use the 0–100 scale with systematically different standards, or when a theory denies interpersonal aggregation or judges two interventions incomparable? What is the effect of adding or dropping a theory no one currently takes seriously? Are its failure modes fixable without abandoning the core idea of probability-weighted choiceworthiness, or does a better rule suggest itself?
