Here's one I've been pushing in my philosophy of mind seminar, and I'm starting to wonder whether I've talked myself into a corner. My view: a whole-brain emulation of me — high-resolution scan, simulation of the neural dynamics on suitable hardware — would be literally the same mind as me, not a copy and not a mere psychological descendant, provided two conditions hold. First, the emulation is a replacement: it isn't run alongside the original, so there are never two simultaneous instances of the same causal profile both claiming to be me. Second, the emulation preserves the causal-functional organization of the brain at the level of cell types, connectivity patterns, and signal dynamics. I don't think we need molecular fidelity, and I don't think substrate matters: same causal structure, same mind, carbon or silicon.

Assume for the sake of argument that the scanning and simulation technology works; engineering-timeline worries aren't what I'm after unless they bear directly on the identity claim. What I want is a map of what would count against the view. Not the aquaintances in the department who say "but it's still just a copy" — I want genuine defeaters. Which empirical results from neuroscience or computation would force me to give it up or seriously revise it? Which conceptual objections should I actually worry about, the ones I'd have to concede are counting against the view rather than just assuming a rival metaphysics? Sort them from fatal to merely awkward, and mark the ones a committed defender of my position would still grant are trouble. Be honest about whether I've stuffed the position so full of conditions that it can't lose, and whether that's a special problem for this view or just what high-level theories of personal identity look like. And if something would only push me to say "well, the right level is finer-grained than I said," say so — and call that a retreat, not a confirmation.
