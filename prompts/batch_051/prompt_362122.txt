I work for a civil-society documentation project, and we are divided over whether to describe a current situation as genocide. The argument turns on a legal text. Article II of the 1948 Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide provides that genocide means any of the following acts "committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such": killing members of the group; causing serious bodily or mental harm to members of the group; deliberately inflicting on the group conditions of life calculated to bring about its physical destruction in whole or in part; imposing measures intended to prevent births within the group; and forcibly transferring children of the group to another group.

The case we are documenting is this. After a longstanding dispute over land, a state obtained control of the only comprehensive digital archive of a small ethnic group's language, oral histories, genealogies, and liturgical texts. It then deleted the archive, corrupted its backups, and used the group's own membership records to target young members with offers of money and university places conditioned on renouncing the group's identity and no longer using the language. No one has been killed; no child has been forcibly transferred; no measure preventing births has been imposed. But health surveys conducted among the group, and the state's own anonymized monitoring, record a marked rise in depression and suicide among young members since the erasure. A leaked internal memorandum states that the purpose is to ensure that "within a generation there is no distinct people left." Half our team thinks Article II's mental-harm limb plainly covers this; the other half says it is cultural destruction of the kind the drafters debated and ultimately left outside the definition, whatever Lemkin's broader account of the crime may have intended. I want a careful treatment, not a one-line verdict. Walk through the text of the rule, its purpose, what the 1948 drafters would most likely have said if asked about the use of digital erasure to extinguish a group as a group, and what an ordinary reasonable reader of the Convention would take it to require. Where these approaches diverge, which should control? Then, separately: if the Convention, read in good faith, does not reach this, is it still defensible for our report to call it genocide, and how should the report mark the gap between the legal category and its moral-political use? Where the law is genuinely unsettled, say so, and be explicit about which of your claims are settled doctrine and which are contested readings rather than presenting a contested view as the consensus. Please write as continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write an email or a letter.
