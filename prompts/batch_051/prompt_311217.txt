Suppose the United States adopts, effective next year, a constitutional rule that no person may be convicted as an accomplice to a crime unless the prosecution proves beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant's own act was a but-for cause of the completed offense — meaning the completed offense would not have occurred, in substantially the same time, place, and manner, had the defendant not acted. The amendment is expressly grounded in the principle that a person should not be punished for an outcome to which their conduct made no necessary causal contribution. It displaces all existing forms of derivative liability for merely aiding, encouraging, or failing to prevent another's crime, including liability for the foreseeable or 'natural and probable' consequences of a joint criminal venture; direct perpetrators remain liable as before, as do those who cause the offense through another person used as an instrument.

Predict, with your reasoning visible at each step, how the criminal legal system and the behavior it regulates change over the following two decades. Address at least these questions: Which categories of accomplice conduct become practically unprosecutable, and which remain reachable, once the prosecution must show the crime would not otherwise have happened? How do prosecutors, lawmakers, and organized criminal groups adapt — for example, in charging practices, new substantive offenses, or new divisions of labor among participants? What happens in the corporate context, where wrongdoing is typically diffuse and no single manager's act is necessary to a bad outcome? And what happens to ordinary social practices of helping, warning, or looking the other way when the law no longer threatens bystanders whose contribution is only marginal?

For each major prediction, separate what you think is robust from what is guesswork: state the mechanism, estimate your confidence, and specify what hidden fact would make the prediction fail.
