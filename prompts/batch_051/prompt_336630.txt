I work as a research assistant on a project that codes long-form interviews with Chinese and Vietnamese immigrant families, and part of my job is to decide whether a practice a participant describes should be coded as religion. I found my initial calls inconsistent across a run of borderline cases—ancestor veneration, a Buddhist home altar, feng shui, Confucian role ethics, and a soccer supporter club whose match-day rituals are as regular and rule-governed as any liturgical calendar—so I adopted a working rule to make the decisions uniform. I tag a practice as religion just in case it has at least three of five features: regular communal ritual at a fixed time or place; belief in non-physical agents of some kind; a moral code explicitly anchored in those agents; a canonical story of origins or transmission; and a felt contrast between sacred and mundane spheres. The rule gives me stable results that I can defend feature by feature: I count ancestor veneration as religion, but not feng shui, Confucian ethics, or the soccer club.

My worry is that the rule may be enforcing consistency by papering over the fact that the category itself is historically and culturally specific. My advisor keeps pressing me on the apparently Protestant, colonial origins of the modern idea of religion, and one interviewee explicitly resisted the label in her own case, describing what she does as filial piety rather than religion—even though her family's rite scores four of my five features. I have also caught the rule leaking beyond the codebook: I now find myself thinking of some participants as religious and others not, even when nothing in our research question requires it. The project has to draw lines somewhere, and I cannot hand-code hundreds of interviews with no category at all. Is my three-of-five shortcut defensible under those constraints, or is it a bias dressed up as a rule? If it is not defensible as it stands, what is the best revised version of the policy—say, one keyed more tightly to the project's actual research question—and would any scheme for coding these interviews be able to test whether religion is a coherent category at all, rather than settling that question in advance by stipulation?
