I'm the standards editor at a magazine that covers science and ideas, and I've been asked to turn our ad hoc practice on slurs into a written procedure that any copy editor can run. The decisions arise several times a week: a reported piece quotes a subject using a racial slur; a first-person essay uses a reclaimed slur; a review describes an artwork whose title is a slur; a news brief must characterize a slur shouted at a researcher without reproducing it. Right now editors decide differently each time, and the inconsistency has become the problem — the same word is printed in one piece and masked in the next, and every choice is relitigated at the proof stage. The stakes include reader harm, legal and reputational exposure, and trust with sources, and we do not want the magazine to become either a platform that launders slurs or one that edits away its own subject matter.

Philosophy of language is the discipline that is supposed to have sorted out some of this, and I know enough to see that it has not. On one side are accounts on which the derogatory force of a slur is part of its conventional semantic content, so that even quoting or mentioning the term does something; on the other are pragmatic and social-practice accounts on which the wrong is in the use, and a careful report or quotation can be insulated from it. There are further disputes about reclaimed uses and about whether the relevant unit is the word, the utterance, the speaker, or the target. I do not want a literature review, but I do want the procedure to reflect which of these distinctions actually matter under deadline rather than which merely name-drop nicely.

Please give me a reusable decision procedure — a checklist or ordered sequence of tests — that assigns a case to one of three outputs: print the slur in full; print it with partial masking; or do not print it and paraphrase. It must be usable by copy editors with no training in the philosophy of language, in about fifteen minutes, with any decision to print in full coming to me for sign-off. Address the use/mention and semantic/pragmatic distinctions head-on, and say which of them must survive in editorial practice and which can be dropped. Then apply the procedure to two or three worked examples, including at least one in which the procedure says to print the slur even though the staff will find it uncomfortable, and one in which it says to paraphrase even though the source used the word and the piece is explicitly about language. End by marking the cases the procedure cannot settle and the points where an editor's judgment, not the theory, has to decide.
