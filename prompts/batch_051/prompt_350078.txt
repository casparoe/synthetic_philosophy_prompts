At lunch the other week, two friends of mine got into an argument that never really got resolved, and I've been turning it over since. Ella, who thinks about economics, said it can be perfectly rational to care less about a future good just because it's later — a positive "pure" rate of time preference, not the discounting you'd do for uncertainty, inflation, or opportunity costs. Marcus, who thinks about moral philosophy, said pure time preference is just a bias towards the temporal near, a kind of favouritism towards one temporal location over another, and that no amount of tidy maths should make it respectable. I tried to write down the back-and-forth as accurately as I could from memory:

Ella: Suppose you can have the same good evening either next weekend or the corresponding weekend a year from now, with complete certainty that it will happen. I'd trade off a bit of its quality to have it sooner, and I don't think that makes me irrational. The wait itself matters.

Marcus: But why should the wait matter if the enjoyment itself is identical? The version of you a year from now enjoys every bit as much as the version of you next weekend. If you discount it, you're treating that future you as less important just because of the calendar date, which looks like an unearned predjudice against a person you happen not to be yet.

Ella: I don't see it as discounting him; I see it as expressing where I stand now. There isn't a neutral point outside my own life from which every moment carries equal weight. From here, a nearer good engages my plans, my attention and my anticipation in a way a distant one doesn't. Temporal distance from me is a real feature of the situation, not an illusion.

Marcus: But precisely the same argument works for distance in space. If you could help two strangers with identical needs, one in the next village and one in a village on the other side of the world, the fact that the nearer one engages your attention and involves your routines doesn't justify caring more about him. We'd call that arbitrary. So why is distance in time different?

Ella: Because I occupy a single path through time but not a single point in space. I can act on near and far places at once, but I can only reach next year by passing through all the intervening days. My relation to future goods is mediated by persistence, planning, and the risk that I or my commitments will change; the same is not true of someone in a distant country, whom I can help this afternoon. Time and space just aren't symmetrical for an agent.

Marcus: Careful: persistence, planning and risk are exactly the things we agreed to set aside when we talked about pure time preference. Those justify what you might call prudent or epistemic discounting, and I grant them. But the question was whether the bare fact of being later, with all those things held equal, gives a reason. You've given reasons that all fall on the permitted side of the line.

Ella: Fair challenge, but I suspect the distinction between pure and impure discounting is cleaner in textbooks than in a life. My attitude to temporal distance comes as a package, and the package does justify choosing the sooner good in the real world, even if I can't show that one strand of it does by itself. But if you force me to defend the bare-date strand, I can reach for one further thought: refusing to discount at all can leave long-run decision problems with no well-defined best plan, because the total value of the options tends to infinity. A small positive discount rate is part of what makes "which life should I lead?" a question with an answer.

Marcus: That's a formal point about comparing infinite sequences. There are other ways to compare them — for example, asking whether one plan is eventually ahead and stays ahead — so the maths doesn't force pure time preference. And in any case, the fact that a preference makes our decision problem tractable doesn't make the preference correct; it might just be a convenient simplification. You've shown we may need to discount heuristically, not that the future genuinely matters less.

Ella: I can live with that as a final word. But notice what you've conceded: actual temporal distance is tangled up with persistence, planning and tractability, so someone who says "I care less about the further future" is usually on safer ground than your original charge of bare calendar prejudice.

Then they both looked at me and asked what I thought, and I couldn't give anything better than "you both made points." Please adjudicate the whole exchange move by move: for each of Ella's and Marcus's moves, say where it's genuinely right, where it's weak or question-begging, and who ends up more in the right overall. I'm especially interested in whether Marcus's spatial analogy is fair, whether Ella's "package" answer is a retreat or a legitimate reframing, and whether the infinite-horizon point does any real argumentative work for her. Oh, and please write the answer in British English.
