I'm a novelist, and I need a short work of deadpan satire for a novel I'm drafting. In the novel's world there is a luxury mail-order house, The Honest Handicap, that sells expensive, inconvenient, and faintly ruinous objects and services to people who want their expenditure to be visible; the house keeps an evolutionary theorist on retainer, and its catalogue applies the logic of the handicap principle and costly signaling theory to the market for status goods without ever admitting that it is doing so.

Please write the house's autumn prospectus: roughly 700 words, in the courteous, exacting, quietly smug voice of a heritage-goods catalogue, organized as a brief house editorial followed by four or five numbered lots. The comedy should come from getting the theory right rather than from mere eccentricity. Each lot should exaggerate a genuine feature of costly-signaling reasoning: (a) an item whose cost falls differentially on low-quality buyers, so that the purchase itself is the honest advertisement; (b) an item that works as an index rather than a handicap—unfakeable because it is causally tied to the trait it advertises, not because it is merely costly; (c) an item that is pure conspicuous waste, useful only as a separating device; and (d) at least one item whose honesty is quietly destroyed by the house's own business model—say, a payment plan or a mass-market edition that removes the differential cost that made the signal informative—while the copy continues to praise its honesty exactly as before. The copy should never use words such as signaling, handicap, index, equilibrium, costly, or any theorist's name; it should perform the logic rather than explain it. It should remain legible and funny to a reader who knows none of the theory, while a reader who does should recognize that the theory is being applied fairly and precisely, not caricatured.
