my friend in the typography class has this arguement he says shows there's no real fact of the matter which letter a shape is, and nobody in our group has been able to knock it down. it runs:

(1) there's no single feature every 'a' has and no non-'a' has. single-storey and double-storey 'a's differ at least as much from each other as some 'a's differ from a 'd' or an 'o', and a sloppy cursive 'a' is just a loop with a tail.

(2) so whether a mark is an 'a' can only be a matter of overall resemblance to examples, and small changes—a nudge of a stroke, a bit more tilt, a slightly fatter bowl—never flip the classification on their own.

(3) that means you can start from a clear lowercase 'a' and move through a long chain of tiny, plausible letterforms, each of which any careful reader would class as the same letter as the one before, and end at a clear lowercase 'd'.

(4) but "same letter" has to be transitive: if x is the same letter as y, and y is the same letter as z, then x is the same letter as z. otherwise we couldn't sort shapes into letters at all.

(5) so by transitivity the 'd' at the end of the chain is an 'a'.

(6) and since you can run a similar chain from any letter to any other, every letter is every other letter.

(7) that's absurd, so the mistake must be in the original thought that a mark determinately is or isn't a given letter. letter identity is just a convention we pretend has sharp edges.

he wants to know which step to give up. i'd most like to keep (1) and the fact that we do reliably read novel fonts, but i'm not attached to the rest. so: where exactly does this go wrong, why did that step look fine, and what would a theory of letter categories have to pay to block the conclusion?
