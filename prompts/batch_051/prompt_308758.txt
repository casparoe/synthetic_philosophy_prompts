Topic: Carroll’s Tortoise and the justification of deduction

Assessment: Upper-level undergraduate philosophy of logic seminar; 90-minute essay; 20 marks.

Question

Carroll’s original Tortoise accepts P and accepts if P then Q, but refuses to accept Q. When Achilles supplies the further premise if P and (if P then Q), then Q, the Tortoise accepts that further premise and still refuses Q; the same thing happens with every additional conditional premise Achilles supplies.

Now consider a New Tortoise who has learned from the original dialogue and tries to be more cooperative. She accepts every conditional premise in the infinite sequence that Achilles could offer. Moreover, she volunteers a general statement: for any propositions A and B, if A and (if A then B), then B. She says she believes this general statement. Yet when she is presented with P and if P then Q, she still declines to enter Q in her notebook. Her explanation is: assenting to a truth is one thing; inferring is another.

Is this New Tortoise in the same predicament as Carroll’s original, or has she escaped it? Answer by:

(a) stating precisely what the original Tortoise shows about the difference between a premise and a rule of inference;

(b) diagnosing the New Tortoise’s error, if she has one, or defending the coherence of her position if you think she has none; and

(c) explaining what her position reveals about whether deduction could be justified by supplying further truths.

Model answer

(a) The original Tortoise treats the inferential connection between premises and conclusion as though it were one more premise. When that connection is written down as a conditional premise, the move from the enriched premise set to the conclusion itself depends on the same inferential connection, so a new conditional premise is needed, and the process repeats. The lesson is a category distinction: a rule of inference governs, licenses, or constitutes the transition from premises to conclusion; it is not another object-language proposition on a par with the premises. The Tortoise does not show that modus ponens is invalid, or that the premises fail to entail the conclusion; she shows that accepting the premises does not by itself compel acceptance of the conclusion unless one is also prepared to infer in accordance with the rule.

(b) The New Tortoise escapes the original regress only superficially. She has every conditional truth one might be tempted to add, including a general statement of the rule; but possessing or accepting a truth is not the same as being governed by the corresponding rule in one’s actual inferences. A subject can believe a universal generalization without being disposed to apply it to a particular case, just as one can believe that all promises ought to be kept while failing to recognize that this promise falls under the generalization. Her slogan therefore does not dissolve the puzzle; it relocates it. The original gap was between premises and conclusion; the New Tortoise reveals a further gap between accepting a rule-stating truth and applying that truth in an inference. A strong answer will say that whether this is an error or a coherent stance depends on what we mean by coherence. Descriptively, her position is coherent enough: logical consistency does not require a thinker to draw every conclusion entailed by her beliefs, since human reasoners are not logically omniscient. Normatively, however, she is rationally deficient: she fails to conform to a rule that is constitutive of, or at least central to, competent deductive practice.

(c) What the New Tortoise reveals is that the justificatory problem cannot be solved by enlarging the premise set, no matter how comprehensive the enlarged set becomes. If deduction needs a justification, it must be a justification of the rule or of the practice of inferring, not the discovery of another truth. The main options are: inference rules are constitutive of rational thought or of the relevant concepts, so that the demand for justification is misguided or answerable only from within; deduction is vindicated pragmatically or naturalistically, for example by its indispensability or reliability, even if that vindication is circular in a benign way; or no non-question-begging justification exists, and the demand cannot be met. A good answer will not simply assert that deduction is obviously fine.

Marking scheme (20 marks)

1. Reconstruction of the original regress — 4 marks. The answer must distinguish validity from acceptance and must avoid the error of saying that Carroll showed modus ponens to be invalid or that a missing premise is all that separates premises from conclusion. Typically candidates lose these marks by treating the Tortoise as a sceptic about entailment.

2. The premise/rule distinction — 4 marks. The answer must identify the difference between an object-language conditional or universal generalization and a meta-level rule of inference, and show why adding the former cannot replace the latter. Typical error: saying the conditional premise states the rule, so adding it is enough.

3. Diagnosis of the New Tortoise — 4 marks. The answer must explain why accepting every conditional truth, including the general statement, is not yet accepting or following modus ponens. Typical error: claiming either that the New Tortoise has exactly the original problem, with nothing new, or that she has wholly dissolved it.

4. Coherence assessment — 4 marks. The answer must separate logical consistency from rational or inferential competence, and may defend or criticize the New Tortoise, but only by invoking that distinction or one like it. Typical error: declaring her position logically inconsistent without addressing the failure to apply a belief, or declaring it fully rational and ignoring the normative deficiency.

5. Implications for justifying deduction — 4 marks. The answer must connect the diagnosis to a serious account of what a justification of deduction could be (constitutive, pragmatic/naturalistic, or skeptical), and must reject the idea that further truths complete the justification. Typical error: vague talk of circularity without committing to any position about the rule itself.
