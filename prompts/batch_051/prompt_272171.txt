I'm on the exhibitions committee of our city history museum, and we're stalled on the title and framing of a redone Rome gallery. The working title is "The Fall of Rome," but the younger curators want something like "After Rome: Continuity and Change, 300–700" and want to present the end of the western empire as a slow transformation—taxes, bishops, barbarian kings filling the space the emperor left—rather than a crash. The other camp says that's academic revisionism and that the public understands "fall." Our director has asked me to circulate a one-page decision memo by Friday: keep the fall title, adopt the transformation title, or approve some compromise, with a bottom line we can actually vote on.

At last week's meeting the fall camp read this aloud from Gibbon's General Observations on the Fall of the Roman Empire in the West (The Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire, chapter 38) and said, "there, that's our exhibit":

"The emperors, anxious for their personal safety and the public peace, were reduced to the base expedient of corrupting the discipline which rendered them alike formidable to their sovereign and to the enemy; the vigour of the military government was relaxed, and finally dissolved, by the partial institutions of Constantine; and the Roman world was overwhelmed by a deluge of Barbarians."

I honestly dont think the passage does the work they want it to. It reads like a story of long internal decay—Gibbon even blames Constantine—not a single 476-style catastrophe, and the chapter title says the fall happened "in the West," which seems to concede that the empire carried on in the East. Please act as an expert on the historiography of Rome's end and on clear institutional writing. Start by brainstorming two or three plausible readings of the passage and what framing each one supports, then draft the actual memo with a definite recommendation and an honest statement of the strongest point on the side you're ruling against.
