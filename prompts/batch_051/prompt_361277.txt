The exam question the student was set is:

Lucas, and in a different way Penrose, argued that Gödel's incompleteness theorems show that the human mind cannot be a mechanical or algorithmic system. Reconstruct the core of Lucas's argument, paying particular attention to the role of the Gödel sentence and to the conditions under which we are entitled to regard that sentence as true. Then assess what you take to be the strongest objection to the argument.

The student's answer is as follows:

Gödel's first incompleteness theorem says that for any consistent formal system F that contains enough arithmetic, there is a sentence G_F which is constructed so that it says, in effect, "G_F is not provable in F." If F proves G_F, then F is inconsistent; if F proves not-G_F, then F is ω-inconsistent. So if F is consistent, G_F is undecidable in F—neither proved nor refuted.

Lucas's argument begins with a proposed machine M that is supposed to simulate my mental arithmetic. Let F_M be the recursive formal system whose theorems are exactly the arithmetic sentences M can output. Since I am a competent mathematician, I can see that F_M is consistent, or at least I can see that the principles built into it are true. Hence I can see that G_M is true: it says it is unprovable in F_M, and by the first theorem F_M cannot prove it. But M cannot output G_M. Therefore I know an arithmetical truth that the machine cannot produce, so M does not capture my mind; since M was arbitrary, no machine captures my mind.

The standard objection is that the argument assumes I can survey the whole of F_M from outside. But I only need to do this for the particular machine proposed as a model of me, which is unproblematic: if a machine is put forward as a model of my mathematical competence, its programme and rules can in principle be inspected. The real difficulty is different. Gödel's second incompleteness theorem shows that no consistent system can prove its own consistency; it follows immediately that a machine cannot prove the consistency of arithmetic, while a human can prove it by ordinary mathematical induction. This is the point Penrose later added, and it is enough to complete the Lucas argument.
