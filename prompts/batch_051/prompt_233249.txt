I'm a journalist on the science and ideas beat, working on a piece about whether forecasting models trained on historical data can be trusted when the world shifts. In an editorial meeting, a senior editor dismissed the central worry by saying that every forecast assumes that "the future will resemble the past" — that this is just the basic presupposition of induction, not an extra risk being taken. I want to push back, but first I need to know exactly what that compressed sentence could be claiming, because the reading that matters is whichever one the editor needs for his dismissal to work.

Take the slogan "the future will resemble the past" and lay out the distinct precise claims it could be expressing, from the trivial to the radical. For each reading, specify what kind of claim it is, judge whether it is a platitude, substantive and defensible, false, or not well enough defined to have a truth value, and say whether it licenses the editor's move. I need this as a menu of readings, not a single preferred interpretation.

To keep this from being vague, use the "grue" example as a test instrument. Define "grue" as a predicate that applies to a thing if either the thing has been observed before some fixed future time t and is green, or it has not been observed before t and is blue. Every emerald observed so far has been observed before t and is green, so every observed emerald has been both green and grue; the two generalizations "all emeralds are green" and "all emeralds are grue" fit the observations equally well, yet they make opposite predictions for emeralds first observed after t. Use that to show that "resemble the past" is not a single instruction: the same data can be described as a green-looking past or a grue-looking past, and different descriptions of the past project to different futures. Say which readings survive contact with grue, and which ones the editor can actually lean on.

Format the answer as a JSON object with this schema. `readings`: an array; each element has `id` (string), `claim` (one-sentence statement of the precise thesis), `kind` (one of: semantic, metaphysical, epistemic, methodological, pragmatic, normative), `verdict` (one of: trivial, substantive_and_defensible, false, ill_defined, true_only_by_stipulation), `supports_editor_use` (one of: fully, partially, no, only_with_extra_premise), and `explanation` (a short paragraph justifying the verdict and the support judgment). After the array, include `best_reading_for_editor` (the id that makes the slogan most defensible while still doing the work the editor wants), `extra_premise_needed` (the minimal premise the editor must add, in one sentence, or an empty string), and `bottom_line` (two or three sentences).
