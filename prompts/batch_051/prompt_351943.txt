Alright, here's my situation. I run the support desk at a small software company, and I'm also the person who wrote our quality policy years ago, so this one is on me. Section 4.2 still says exactly:

"A request may be marked resolved when the responding engineer has sent the requester a written answer that directly addresses the reported problem. An engineer's quarterly performance score is the average helpfulness rating requesters assign to those answers."

I wrote that in 2011, before any of us had ever pasted a ticket into an LLM. Last month I caught my newest engineer doing exactly that: he pastes the customer's ticket into the model, glances at the first line, forwards the reply, and marks the ticket resolved. His helpfulness ratings are the best on the team. But I started tracking it, and a meaningfull chunk of his answers are subtly wrong — last week one told a customer to rotate an API key in a way that also broke the integration the key was meant to authorize — and a week later the customer opens a fresh ticket about the thing the first answer claimed to fix. The original ticket still counts as resolved, and his score keeps climbing.

So here's what I actually want to know. Does 4.2, read honestly, forbid what he's doing? The literal wording seems to let him through: the answer usually does directly address the reported problem, and the ratings are real. The purpose of the clause was plainly to reward actually helping the requester. The drafters (me, annoyingly) would have said, of course the engineer has to know what they sent, if asked at the time. A reasonable person reading the policy today might say sending an answer you haven't verified isn't providing an answer at all. Which of those readings should win, and is there any coherent reading on which he's meeting the rule rather than gaming it? Because if not, I'm about to learn every metric I wrote can be outrun by a teenager with a chatbot.
