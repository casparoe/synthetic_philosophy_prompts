Since retiring from a career in the natural sciences, I have been working through the philosophy of mathematics, and I keep returning to the two definitions that open Book VII of Euclid's Elements: a unit is "that by virtue of which each of the things that exist is called one," and a number is "a multitude composed of units." I would like to put forward a reading of these definitions that I have not seen worked out, and to request an honest assessment of it.

I call it the quotient view of counting. Its central claim is that a unit is not an object and not an intrinsic property of certain objects; it is a granularity policy imposed by whoever counts. To count is first to partition some portion of the world into pieces and then to resolve not to register differences below the level of a piece. A unit is a piece treated as atomic relative to that count, and a number is the score that results when the chosen pieces are treated as interchangeable for the purposes of the count. The "one" in "one chair" belongs to the act of counting rather than to the chair, since the same chair can also be several components, part of a pair, or a single item within a gross.

On this reading, Euclid's two definitions should be run together dynamically: the unit names the cut, and the number names the result of running the cut. Arithmetic, as I understand it, would then study what counts are stable across changes in the counter's purposes rather than what "numbers of things" really are. I find this attractive because it would explain why two competent counters can assign different numbers to the same material without either committing an arithmetical error: they have simply drawn the cut in different places.

What I need to know is whether this is a real position or merely a metaphor wearing technical language. It seems to me that it must have been anticipated, perhaps by Frege or by later writers in the philosophy of measurement, though I do not trust my sense of the literature. More importantly, where does the view break? Give me the strongest counterexample you know: a case in which the unit appears to be discovered rather than imposed, or a case in which the view would count something as a number that should not be one, or would refuse to count something that plainly should be. And tell me what, if anything, the view gets right.
