I'm giving a job talk next month to a search committee that's half philosophy and half public-health policy people, for a position split between the two departments. My talk defends a condition on risk imposition I call the participation condition. The one-sentence version: when you impose a small but genuine risk on someone without their consent, what makes it permissible is not that the individual expected harm is tiny; it's that the person bearing the risk is a participant in the practice that produces it — someone for whose benifit the practice is, on the whole, run, and who can access those benefits on roughly the same terms as everyone else. If they are a mere third party to the scheme, smallness does not do the moral work; the imposition is, in my view, a kind of taking, because their welfare is being used as a sink for someone else's activity.

The audience: two Scanlonian contractualists who think the real test is whether the risk would be ruled out by principles nobody could reasonably reject; one act-utilitarian risk analyst who thinks this all reduces to expected value against a background of background risk; and an epidemiologist who studies environmental injustice and will care less about principle and more about whether my condition, applied to real permitting decisions, lets polluters off the hook or actually nails them. They're collegial but they'll go for the throat in Q&A.

Sketch of the arguement I'm running, so the objections can target it: I start from two cases that pull expected-harm and participation apart. In the first, a manufacturer vents a carcinogen over a town, giving each resident an added lifetime cancer risk of one in a million, while the product is sold elsewhere; the expected-utility math per person is trivial, but nobody in the town is a participant. In the second, a live-attenuated vaccine is given to children, where parents consent, and the tiny risk of serious side effects is borne by the very population that gets the protection. I claim the first is wrong and the second is at least defeasibly fine, and that only the participation condition explains the difference, because an expected-harm threshold either permits both or forbids both. I then sharpen the participation notion as: benefits actually available, on non-humiliating terms, to the risk-bearers, not merely hypothetically available in some idealized market. And I close by claiming this fits our judgments better than Scanlonian reasonable rejection, because reasonable rejectability is too permitting when the rejectors are a dispersed, politically weak group.
