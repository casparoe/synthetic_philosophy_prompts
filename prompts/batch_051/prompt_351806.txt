Here's an asymmetry I keep noticing. If a scientist declared loyalty to a faction inside her field — she'll back whatever result helps the faction, smother whatever hurts it, and treat other labs as enemies to beat rather than hypotheses to test — we'd say she'd stopped doing science. If a judge ruled the same way, by party discipline, we'd call it corruption. But in democratic politics we treat that very structure as normal: party loyalty, message discipline, coalition management, and plenty of people think democracy literally can't run without it. Simone Weil had the harsher view — she thought parties are machines for producing collective power, built to grow and win rather than to track truth or justice, and that they should be abolished rather than tamed. Is there a principle that actually justifies treating politics as the one place where this is legitimate, or is it jsut an inconsistency we've come to live with? Give me the strongest defense of the asymmetry you can, test it against nearby cases (journalism, universities, courts, a citizens' assembly with no parties), and say whether we should keep, reverse, or give it up. Then tell me which consideration or argument would be most likely to change your verdict, and where your defense is most likely to crack.
