I am a PhD student in political philosophy, and I have been drafting a short essay on whether the concept of human dignity can do any normative work beyond what is already done by the principle of respect for autonomy. The essay takes as its point of departure Ruth Macklin's deflationary argument in her 2003 BMJ piece, "Dignity is a useless concept" (BMJ 2003;327:1419–1420). The sentence I keep returning to is the following: "A close inspection of leading examples shows that appeals to dignity are either vague restatements of other, more precise, notions or mere slogans that add nothing to an understanding of the topic." My essay does not try to refute this directly. Instead, I offer a reading of Macklin on which her diagnosis is exactly right for the cases she surveys—informed consent, confidentiality, non-discrimination, the use of newly dead bodies, and certain discussions around reproductive cloning—but then distinguish between two functions the concept of dignity might be asked to perform. The first is as a mid-level placeholder within applied ethics, where I largely concede Macklin's point. The second is as an explanatory ground for why the principle of respect for persons has the scope and weight it does in the first place: roughly, the idea that a human being's capacity for reason-giving marks them as a bearer of claims, rather than as a mere object of tradeoff. My concluding section tries to show that this second function is not simply a restatement of autonomy, because autonomy in the relevant practical sense is something we attribute to individual acts and choices, whereas dignity is a status we attribute to the person over time, including in conditions where autonomous choice is absent or impaired.

I would be grateful for comments on the essay, especially on three points. First, do I read Macklin fairly, or do I quietly change the subject from her deflationary thesis to a different and more abstract question? Second, is the distinction between dignity as a placeholder and dignity as an explanatory ground stable, or does it collapse once one asks what the explanatory ground adds to the content of the principle it is supposed to ground? Third, my final section applies the distinction to a concrete case—the sale of non-vital organs—where I argue that dignity explains why a fully informed and voluntary vendor's consent does not by itself settle the question. I worry that this application overreaches and reimports the very slogan-use Macklin warned against. I would particularly appreciate suggestions for tightening the argument, especially if there is a Kantian line of thought I am misusing or could use more carefully.
