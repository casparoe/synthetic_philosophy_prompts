I have been trying to get clearer about the idea of the child's right to an open future: the thought that children have a strong interest in reaching adulthood with a meaningful range of life-paths still genuinely open to them, and that parents can wrong a child not only by direct mistreatment but by making choices that close off too much of that range before the child can shape it herself. I am working with the following five criteria. A parental decision impermissibly closes a child's future when:

1. It forecloses a broad class of options that are ordinarily valuable in adulthood — across such domains as work, education, intimacy, mobility, and belief — rather than merely one specific path.
2. The foreclosure is irreversible, or reversible only at unreasonable cost, by the time the child is old enough to assess the matter for herself.
3. The decision is made chiefly for the parents' own satisfaction, identity, or convenience, rather than to secure the child's basic goods, such as health, safety, competence, and the capacity for an autonomous life.
4. The options the decision opens are not of roughly comparable breadth and value to those it closes.
5. The decision settles a determinate life-plan for the child before she has the competence to consent to it, or at least to evaluate it in a meaningful way.

Now consider this case. A couple of professional circus acrobats are raising their daughter inside the family troupe. From the age of four she trains daily; by twelve she is educated mainly by a tutor who accompanies the tour for a few hours each day, and she spends much of the rest of her time in rehearsal and conditioning. In the off-season she attends an ordinary school, though her friendships there remain shallow because the troupe is away for most of the year. She has real talent and says she enjoys performing, but she has never known another kind of life. Her parents intend for her to join the troupe at eighteen, where she could plainly earn a living, and they insist they would not stop her from leaving; they have also saved enough to support several years of further education or vocational training if she asks for it. They see the troupe as the best life they know and regard her training as a gift rather than a narrowing. The craft she is acquiring is a substantial achievement, and it plausibly opens adjacent paths such as coaching, choreography, or arts administration, but the ordinary adolescent range of broad schooling, varied friendships, and unpressured experimentation is becoming progressively harder for her to recover.

Work through the five criteria one by one, saying for each whether the case clearly satisfies it, clearly fails it, or leaves the criterion underdetermined, and explain why. Then say whether, on balance, the parents' decision impermissibly closes the child's open future, and whether any of the five criteria should be revised, dropped, or supplemented in light of the case.
