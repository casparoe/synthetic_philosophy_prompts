I am a retired physicist — most of my working life was spent on general relativity and its experimental tests, though I came late to academic philosophy and have been reading my way through the foundations of space and time ever since. I have been asked to give a single lecture to graduate students on what the hole argument does to our ordinary picture of spacetime, and I would like to open the lecture with a poem that makes them feel the argument before I state it.

Please write a pantoum about the hole argument. A pantoum is a poem in three or four quatrains: in the opening stanza all four lines are new; in every following stanza, the first and third lines repeat the second and fourth lines of the previous stanza verbatim; the second and fourth lines are new, except in the final stanza, where they are the third and first lines of the opening stanza, so the poem ends with its opening line. Use that recursive movement to enact the argument rather than decorate it: the same lines, like the same metric field dragged into new positions by a diffeomorphism, come back in altered surroundings, and the poem ends where it began, just as the hole argument keeps returning to its own dilemma.

The construction itself should be present in the poem, not merely alluded to. Somewhere in the repeated lines the reader should meet the actual move: a matter-free region; a smooth map that is the identity on and to the past of a Cauchy surface while it moves points about only inside that region, dragging the metric along; and the recognition that the two resulting descriptions agree everywhere outside the region and on that surface, yet assign different metric values point by point within it. The poem should then press the dilemma: by diffeomorphism invariance the two descriptions are one physical situation, but if the manifold's points are real individuals carrying the metric as a property, the full state on that Cauchy surface no longer fixes the metric throughout the hole. The closing return of the first line should land on what has to be surrendered — the primitive identity of the points, or the determinism the physicist took for granted — and the form should show that the argument will come round again either way.

Write it in my voice: a physicist who once took spacetime points for granted and is no longer sure what he is entitled to assume. You are a philosopher of physics with a working command of general relativity and a careful, published poet. Before you write, work through the argument step by step and decide which repeated lines will carry which stages of it; do not include that preparation in the response. Accuracy matters as much as craft: a specialist should recognize every move of the argument in the poem, and a newcomer should come away able to reconstruct its shape. Begin your response with the poem itself, with no preface, no note on the form, and no commentary before or after.
