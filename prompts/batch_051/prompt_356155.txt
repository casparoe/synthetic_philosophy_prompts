I recently read a chapter on modal metaphysics in an introductory textbook, and its account of the Barcan formulas struck me as admirably clear but possibly too quick. It summarizes them this way:

"The Barcan formula says that if everything is necessarily F, then necessarily everything is F: ∀x□Fx → □∀xFx. Its converse, the Converse Barcan Formula, is simply the implication run the other way: □∀xFx → ∀x□Fx. Both formulas are theorems of the simplest systems of quantified modal logic, and that is the formal way of saying that standard modal logic is committed to a single domain of objects shared by all possible worlds. In particular, the Barcan formula entails that no actually existing object could have failed to exist, and that there are no merely possible objects. The standard illustration is Socrates: since Socrates exists in the actual world, he must exist in every possible world; even in a world with no people, Socrates will be there. Defenders of the Barcan formulas therefore reject the common-sense idea that objects literally come into existence and pass out of existence."

I am inclined to trust the overall conclusion, because I have seen the claim that the Barcan formulas force a constant domain repeated in several places, but I cannot tell whether the formulas themselves do the forcing or whether an interpretive choice about domains is doing the work. Please audit this summary the way a philosopher of logic would. Go through it claim by claim and say what is right, what is subtly off, and where it conflates two things the literature keeps apart. In particular: (a) Are the two formulas correctly stated and correctly named? (b) Is it accurate that both are theorems of "the simplest systems" of quantified modal logic, and that "standard modal logic" is thereby committed to one domain for all possible worlds? (c) Does the Barcan formula by itself entail necessitism — that whatever actually exists exists necessarily — or does that require the converse formula, both formulas, or some further frame condition? (d) Is the Socrates illustration a fair consequence of what was already in the summary, and how would a contingentist or a proponent of free logic respond? (e) Taken as a whole, is the summary reliable enough for an introductory reader, or does it overstate what the formulas establish?
