Two of my grad students got into it at the department picnic last week, and I basically eavesdropped because I didn't know how to call it. We'd just done a couple weeks on the Aunt Hillary stuff from Hofstadter — ants, pheromones, whether a whole colony could be a mind — so obviously they were primed. Neither one is a pushover, and I honestly think they each landed some real blows. I've tried to reconstruct the exchang from memory:

Maya: An ant colony has a mind in the only sense that matters. It perceives, through pheromone gradients and encounter rates. It remembers — trails persist and decay. It decides — nest sites, foraging sites, even caste ratios. If you're a functionalist about minds, you're already committed to a colony having one, because it's got the same functional profile as a lot of run-of-the-mill cognition.

Jon: It doesn't have the same profile. Functional roles are roles inside an integrated system. A colony is a pile of separate agents acting on shared, public signals. There's no integration point. If the colony counts, your fridge light counts.

Maya: The fridge line is cheap. The colony integrates constantly: how fast one forager returns changes what dozens of others do, and that feeds back into trail strength. That's feedback, and its not less integration just because it's slower than a brain. You're defining "integrated" as "fast" and then declaring victory.

Jon: Speed isn't my point, and you know it. In a brain, the components don't have their own agenda. An ant does. An ant can quit, get lost, freeload, switch trails. When a neuron fires it isn't because the neuron decided the organism should turn left. So the signals in a colony are communication among agents, not computation inside one system. That's a difference in kind.

Maya: Components with local agendas don't rule out system-level states. Markets compute prices even though every trader has an agenda and partial information. If you refused to call a market a price-setting system because the traders are selfish, you'd be missing the pattern. The colony's pattern is where anything like a viewpoint would have to live — not in any ant.

Jon: A market doesn't use the price it computes; the traders do, and each trader is already a mind. Same in the colony: the signals get used by ants, each of which is already a mind. There's no extra subject that uses anything. A colony has no point of view; being a colony isn't like anything.

Maya: Now you're doing introspection, not functionalism. You said integration is what makes you a subject. Fine — give me the principle that counts fast neural feedback as integration but a colony's trail-reinforcement loop as not, without pointing back at "well, I'm a subject and the colony isn't." If your own case gets to count as evidence, that's special pleading.

Jon: Okay. In my case, integration isn't just causal coupling; it's coupling the system itself tracks. A brain monitors whether its own signals are coherent — prediction error, mismatch, whatever. A colony doesn't monitor its own signals for error; it just runs.

Maya: That's a real principle, but it sets the bar at self-monitoring, and then you lose a lot of animals you probably didn't mean to lose. Newborn rabbits, for instance. And anyway, a colony does have something like collective error correction: foragers stop laying trail when a site stops paying, and the colony "changes its mind," so to speak.

Jon: But that's just negative feedback. A thermostat shuts off when the room warms up; a bacterium alters its tumbling when attractant drops. If "stops reinforcing a dead-end signal" gets you a mind, you've let in every feedback loop in the building. Look, I'm not saying the line is obvious. I'm saying you haven't shown there's a line at all between thermostats and the colony.

Anyway, they eventually asked me to settle it, and I said I'd think about it. Which was a lie. So now I'm asking you. Walk me through it move by move: for each claim, say whether it's mostly right, mostly wrong, or somewhere in between, and briefly say why. Then tell me what both of them are missing. Don't just pick a winner — the interesting part is which specific moves survive and which ones don't.
