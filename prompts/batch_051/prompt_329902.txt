Let a world be represented by an infinite sequence of numbers \(A=(a_1,a_2,\ldots)\), where \(a_n\) is the lifetime well-being of person \(n\). Consider two principles for comparing such worlds.

**Pareto dominance:** If, for every person \(n\), \(a_n \ge b_n\), and for infinitely many \(n\), \(a_n > b_n\), then world \(A\) is better than world \(B\).

**Permutation invariance:** If world \(B\) differs from world \(A\) only by permuting which person has which well-being level—that is, if there is a permutation \(\pi\) of the indices such that \(b_n=a_{\pi(n)}\) for all \(n\)—then \(A\) and \(B\) are equally good.

Now construct two infinite sequences containing only \(0\)s and \(1\)s. Let \(A\) be the sequence with \(1\) at every even position and \(0\) at every odd position:

\[
A=(0,1,0,1,0,1,0,1,\ldots).
\]

Let \(B\) be the sequence with \(1\) at every position divisible by \(4\) and \(0\) everywhere else:

\[
B=(0,0,0,1,0,0,0,1,\ldots).
\]

Now reason as follows.

1. Both \(A\) and \(B\) contain a countably infinite number of \(1\)s and a countably infinite number of \(0\)s. Therefore there is a permutation of the positions carrying \(A\) to \(B\). So by permutation invariance, \(A\) and \(B\) are equally good.

2. At every odd position, \(A\) and \(B\) are equal, both being \(0\). At every position divisible by \(4\), they are also equal, both being \(1\). But at every even position not divisible by \(4\), \(A\) has \(1\) while \(B\) has \(0\). Thus \(a_n \ge b_n\) for every \(n\), and \(a_n > b_n\) for infinitely many \(n\).

3. Therefore, by Pareto dominance, \(A\) is better than \(B\).

4. But step 1 says \(A\) and \(B\) are equally good, while step 3 says \(A\) is better than \(B\). Contradiction.

So the two principles cannot both hold without restriction. Is the reasoning valid as it stands? If it is, state exactly where the inconsistency forces a choice, which principle should be rejected or restricted, and what is the strongest salvageable version of it. If you think the reasoning fails at one of the numbered steps, identify the step, explain the distinction it misses, and say what remains true after the distinction is drawn.
