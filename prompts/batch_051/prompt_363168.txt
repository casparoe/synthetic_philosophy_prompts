I'm a journalist who covers games and the strangely deep ideas that show up in them, and I've been going down a rabbit hole about Hex. The standard proof that the first player has a winning strategy in Hex is the strategy-stealing argument: you assume, for contradiction, that the second player can guarantee a win. The first player then makes an innocous opening move and subsequently copies the second player's supposed winning strategy, playing anywhere else whenever the copied strategy would ask for a stone that's already on the board, so the opening stone becomes a free extra move. The whole argument turns on the assumption that in Hex an extra stone can never hurt you — there's no such thing as zugzwang on a Hex board, because having more of your own pieces on the board can only help you connect.

What I can't figure out is how much of the argument is really being carried by that one feature, so here's the version I've been chewing on. Suppose you keep the board and the rules of Hex exactly, but flip the win condition: it becomes misère Hex, where the first player to complete a connecting chain between their two sides loses, so each player is trying to force the other to connect. Does the strategy-stealing argument still go through, and if it does, what exactly does it show — that the first player can force a win, that the second can, or only that somebody has a winning strategy without telling us who? If it doesn't go through, I'd like to know exactly which premise dies: is it only the extra-stone-never-hurts assumption, or does something else snap too, and is there a natural repair that gives a weaker but still interesting conclusion?

I'm more interested in the architecture of the argument than in who wins this variant. So also tell me what would change your mind: which consideration about misère Hex or about strategy stealing would make you flip the verdict — for instance, if it turned out the misère game still has no zugzwang in the relevant sense, or that draws are possible after all — and where do you think the reasoning is most likely to fail?
