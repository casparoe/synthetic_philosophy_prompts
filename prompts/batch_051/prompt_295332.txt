A short argument that the difference between indicative and counterfactual conditionals is only a difference in the speaker's attitude to the antecedent:

(1) A conditional is evaluated by considering the situations in which its antecedent holds and that are compatible with a contextually supplied body of background facts, and then checking whether the consequent holds in all those situations.
(2) For the indicative "If the fire didn't start in the kitchen, it started in the wiring," the relevant background is the fire investigator's present knowledge: the house burned, and the fire had to have started either in the kitchen or in the wiring.
(3) For the counterfactual "If the fire had not started in the kitchen, it would have started in the wiring," the relevant background is the same present knowledge; the subjunctive marking only signals that the speaker regards the kitchen origin as false.
(4) The antecedent and consequent describe the same events in each case.
(5) Therefore the two conditionals have the same truth conditions, and any felt difference between them must be a matter of pragmatics rather than content.
(6) But this seems wrong: the indicative is already secured by the investigator's knowledge, while the counterfactual requires further causal information about what the wiring would have done, which that knowledge does not supply.

Is the argument valid? Are the premises true? If a premise should be rejected, say exactly which one, give the correct version of it, and say whether the argument can be rebuilt to a different conclusion.
