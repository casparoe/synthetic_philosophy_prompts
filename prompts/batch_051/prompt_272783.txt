I'm a software engineer by training, with no formal background in philosophy or law, and I've been trying to get clear on a question that keeps surfacing when I read about anti-discrimination law. Courts and theorists seem to treat classifications on some grounds as suspect or specially protected—race, sex, religion, national origin, for instance—while treating other grounds as unremarkable, and the justifications they offer look to me like a list of features that push a ground toward the suspect side:

1. Immutability: the person didn't choose the trait and can't readily change it.
2. Visibility: the trait is easily detected and tends to dominate social perception.
3. A history of subordination: the trait has been the basis of systematic disadvantage, not just isolated incidents.
4. Political powerlessness: the group defined by the trait can't reliably protect itself through ordinary political processes.
5. Irrelevance to merit: the trait carries no information about a person's qualifications for the decision in question.

I keep seeing the same five features, and I suspect the list is incomplete. In machine-learning terms, it feels like I have five hand-engineered features that underfit the data.

Give me a sixth feature that is not on this list and is not a restatement, special case, or simple combination of the five. It can be a criterion you know from the literature or your own proposal. Then tell me honestly whether it pulls any independent weight: construct a case where your sixth feature and the five above would classify a trait differently, and say which classification is more defensible and why.
