I am a PhD candidate in philosophy of cognitive science, and I am drafting a chapter on how far moral status extends beyond its least controversial cases. I need a working definition of sentience — by which I mean the capacity for felt experience with a positive or negative character — to carry part of that argument, and I want it tested against hard cases rather than simply stipulated.

Two boundary verdicts are fixed for me: an awake, healthy adult human is sentient, and a thermostat is not. I am genuinely unsure about the cases in between. I also need the definition to respect the thought that sentience comes in degrees, so that it can support "more sentient than" judgments rather than only a binary cutoff. Finally, I want it to keep felt experience distinct from mere discriminative responding or negative feedback; if it cannot draw that distinction clearly, I would rather you say so than blur the line.

Please propose a definition, or a short battery of criteria, and run it through the following cases:
(i) a healthy adult dog;
(ii) an octopus;
(iii) a honeybee;
(iv) an oyster;
(v) the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans, on the assumption that its nervous system comprises 302 neurons;
(vi) a plant that emits volatile organic compounds when damaged;
(vii) a thermostat with a negative-feedback loop;
(viii) a healthy adult human under general anesthesia.

For each case, say what verdict the definition delivers, whether it concerns the individual as a sentient being or the individual's present state, and where the case falls on the graded scale. Where the definition yields a counterintuitive verdict or no determinate answer, do not silently patch it: say what gave way, give the revised definition, and then continue with the remaining cases. Finally, identify the verdict or ordering you regard as least secure, and say what empirical finding or philosophical argument would be most likely to change your mind about it.
