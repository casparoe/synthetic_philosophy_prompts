I've been arguing with a friend about whether the Uyghur camps in Xinjiang count as genocide, and every time I try to settle it I end up with a definition that sweeps in too much or to little. The background tension that keeps tripping me up is this. Lemkin coined the term in Axis Rule in Occupied Europe (1944) with something much wider than mass killing in mind: for him a genocide was a coordinated attack on the bases of a group's existence — political, social, economic, cultural, religious, biological, even moral — carried out with the aim of destroying the group as such, and it didn't require a single corpse if the group was being dismantled some other way. The 1948 Genocide Convention then narrowed the crime to a specified list of acts done with intent to destroy a national, ethnic, racial or religious group as such, and it left political groups and most cultural destruction out. So the guy who invented the word and the treaty that turned it into a crime weren't working with the same concept, which makes me suspect "genocide" is doing two jobs at once: describing a kind of thing that happened, and leveling a particular kind of accusation.

Here are the cases I want the definition to get right, with my own loose priors:
- the Holocaust — paradigm case, any definition that rules it out is wrong.
- the 1994 Rwandan genocide — should obviously come out yes.
- the Armenian genocide — I think yes, though Turkey's refusal of the word makes me unsure where exactly the resistance is located.
- the Holodomor (the early-1930s famine in Soviet Ukraine) — I can't decide: millions died, but was the point to destroy Ukrainians as such, or to crush a resistant peasantry?
- the forced removal of Indigenous children into residential schools in Canada and Australia — this is the kind of cultural destruction Lemkin cared about, but it only falls under the Convention if the intent requirement is met.
- the Khmer Rouge's killing of people it classified as class enemies and other political groups — the Convention excludes political groups, and I'm not convinced that exclusion is principled.
- the Xinjiang case itself: internment camps, sterilization allegations, reported erasure of mosques and language.
- a totally non-lethal campaign to extinguish a minority language and religion through forced assimilation, with no one killed.
- a conventional war that kills huge numbers of civilians as collateral damage, with no aim of destroying any particular group.

What I want is a working definition of genocide, not a survey of definitions in the literature. Commit to the definition you'd defend, run it through all nine cases, and give a definite verdict on each. The definition can be broader or narrower than the 1948 Convention, but if you depart from the Convention, say explicitly that you're amending or abandoning it, and why. Then tell me two things: where your definition forces a verdict you didn't expect going in, and which of the nine verdicts is the least secure under your own definition. I'd rather have a flawed definition defended honestly than a list of options I have to choose between.
