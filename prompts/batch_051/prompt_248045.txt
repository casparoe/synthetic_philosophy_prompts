I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a feature about Stigler's law of eponymy: the recurring pattern in which results named after people are very often named after someone other than the original discoverer. The piece won't lean on citations, so I don't need a bibliography; what I need is a rigorous stress test of one concrete reform.

The proposal is this. A large professional society in a field where personal eponyms are common—statistics, say, or physics—adopts a naming protocol. From acceptance onward, any theorem, effect, statistic, or algorithm that would traditionally have received an eponym is assigned a descriptive identifier by a central registry; the identifier cannot contain a personal name, an institution, a nationality, or a place. For fifty years, the society's journals require that identifier in formal citations and do not permit personal eponyms for the result. After fifty years, a historical naming committee may retrospectively confer an honorific name, under four restrictions: it may not name the result after a living person, an author of the originating papers, any of those authors' doctoral students, or a committee member. To honor someone, the committee must certify, on documentary evidence, that the person published or circulated the core result earlier than the currently credited discoverer and was overlooked.

I want to know how this would actually operate, not whether it is a good idea in the abstract. Where does it get gamed first, and by whom? The society cannot stop informal eponyms in the wider literature and the classroom—what does that do to the registry's authority? What perverse incentives are created by letting authors help choose the descriptive identifier? Does barring a result's authors and their students merely push misattribution one academic generation out, and can the documentary-evidence requirement be captured by partisans with good archives? What second-order effects should I expect for citation practice, for how outsiders learn the field, and for where prestige accumulates? Finally, which failure modes would be fixable by changing the rules, and which reflect deeper features of how scientific credit actually works?

Since this is for publication, please also separate what is empirically documented about eponymy and Stigler's law from what remains genuinely contested among people who study scientific credit, and do not present a contested position as the consensus.
