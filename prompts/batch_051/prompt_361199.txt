I am a PhD student working on theories of meaning in life, and I want to press on an asymmetry that seems to govern ordinary judgments about the subject. Consider a mid-career scientist who, at the end of a demanding week, judges that the week was meaningful: an experiment finally worked, a student grasped a difficult idea, and she caught a serious error in her own draft. Most of us accept judgments like this without requiring that any of it matter in a decade, let alone after the eventual heat death of the universe. But if the same person, in the same conversation, asks whether her life as a whole has been meaningful, the standards often sharpen. Now it seems to matter whether she has left something that counts from a point of view outside her own life, in the advance of her field, in history, or in some larger order. The episodes pass a modest, local test; the whole is held to a stricter and more cosmic one. I want to know whether anything justifies this asymmetry between what we demand of particular episodes and what we demand of the life that contains them. The absurdist tradition would treat the asymmetry as a symptom of asking the question of the meaning of life from a standpoint we cannot occupy; contemporary theorists often reply that the cosmic question was never the one being asked. Please provide the strongest candidate principle that could preserve both judgments, test it against nearby cases (a friendship, a novel, a cathedral, a forty-year career, each assessed episode by episode and then as a whole), and give an honest verdict on whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up. I also ask that you state what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would most threaten the verdict you reach, and where in your reasoning it is most likely to fail.
