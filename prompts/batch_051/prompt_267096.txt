I am taking an undergraduate module on algorithmic information theory and the philosophy of probability, and a seminar question has lodged itself in my mind: can a finite sequence be random? I would like a working definition of "random" as applied to a fixed finite binary string, and I have assembled a set of cases that keep pulling my intuitions in different directions.

(a) A 100-bit string of perfectly alternating digits, 010101... . I take this simply not to be random.
(b) A 100-bit string of all 1s, which was produced by a genuinely fair coin. Every specific 100-bit outcome has the same probability, and yet it feels wrong to call the string itself random even though the process that produced it was.
(c) A 100-bit string with no conspicuous pattern and roughly equal numbers of 0s and 1s, which a friend generated from a three-line computer programme. It looks statistically indistinguishable from an ordinary coin-toss string, but the whole thing has a very short description, so I lean towards calling it not random.
(d) A 100-bit string with no obvious pattern, roughly balanced, which I am told was produced by a fair coin. I have no hesitation calling this random.
(e) The exact same string as in (d), but I am now told it was actually produced by a deterministic pseudo-random generator from a short seed. Do I have to change my verdict, and should I?
(f) A 10-bit string, say 1001110100. It is too short to display any pattern and too short for any statistical test to get a grip, and I am unsure whether the question even makes sense at this length.
(g) A 100-bit string written out by a person trying to make it look random. It is roughly balanced but alternates more often than one would expect by chance, because people avoid long runs. I would call this not random, or at least less random than (d), even though there is no simple rule that compresses it.

I keep oscillating between two pictures. On the first, randomness is a property of the generating process: a string is random just in case it came from a genuinely chancy source, so the same digit string could be random in one world and not in another depending on its history. On the second, randomness has to be a property of the string itself, because (a) seems non-random no matter how it arose, and (c) seems non-random even though its digits look unremarkable. I would like a definition of "random finite string", or an argued case for why no single definition is available, that runs through (a) to (g), tells me which of my verdicts should be revised, and says what your account implies about a string that is genuinely produced by a quantum random number generator but happens to look like (a). Please write your answer in British English.
