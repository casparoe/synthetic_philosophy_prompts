I write about the self-help/ideas beat and one thing keeps nagging at me. Over the last ten or fifteen years Stoicism came back hard — the founders and podcasters, the pandemic-era rediscovery of Marcus Aurelius's Meditations, the daily-email meditations, the whole resilience industry now wearing a toga. Almost all of it is sold as a version of Epictetus's dichotomy of control: some things are in your power (your judgments, your choices, what you pursue and avoid) and some things aren't (your body, your property, your reputation, what other people do), and the trick is supposed to be caring only about the first column. I get the pitch. But the more of this stuff I consume, the more it looks like the columns get quietly swapped in practice: the things that are explicitly not up to you — the market, the subscriber count, whether a hiring committee says yes, whether a pandemic closes your gym — are exactly what the practitioner is trying to move. The advice is that you can control only the effort, but the effort is aimed entirely at the not-up-to-you column. It reads like a way to stay attached to outcomes while feeling principled about it.

I want you to analyze the modern Stoic revival as a case study in the split between what's up to us and what isn't, and run the analysis in both directions. Map the revival's actual practices — morning routines, journals, rejection-therapy challenges, memento mori repackaged as a productivity trick — into Epictetus's two columns, and say honestly how much of what gets labeled "in your control" is really just effort aimed at externals, and how much of the not-in-your-control column is still treated as the real scoreboard. Then say which features of the revival the dichotomy genuinely explains, and which it misdescribes, and what the revival and it's fixation on agency reveal about the concept itself. The sharp version of the question I want answered: does the revival's success show the dichotomy is unstable as practical guidance for functioning adults — because a response is always a response to something external, so the external never really drops out — or is the revival just doing Stoicism badly while the original line holds? Give me the strongest case for each verdict before you land, and don't let the theory off easy.
