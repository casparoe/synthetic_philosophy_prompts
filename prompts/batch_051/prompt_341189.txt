I'm a retired evolutionary biologist who's recently gotten into philosophy, and I keep running into people who say that natural selection is just gradient descent, or that evolution is a stupid optimizer, or that the fact evolution built minds shows gradient descent can build minds too. After a few of these conversations I made my self a rule. When someone wants to draw a conclusion about optimization from evolution, or about evolution from optimization, I make them answer two questions before I'll engage: what exactly is the objective function natural selection is supposed to be descending the gradient of, and what plays the role of the learning rate? I don't even need the right answers, just definite ones—if they say mean fitness and mutation rate, fine, at least then we can argue. If they can't give me either, I tune them out, basically to zero. Last week I used this on a guy at a reading group who said evolution proves a dumb local search process can produce general intelligence; he looked at me like I'd asked him to solve a PDE. My friend, a philosopher, says I'm being unfair: someone can have a real insight through a loose analogy even if they haven't worked out the formal mapping, and my questions are a way of refusing to engage instead of evaluating the argument. I see his point, but my feeling is that if you can't say what the gradient is, you aren't using gradient descent as a model—you're using it as a vibe, and a vibe isn't something I can argue with. Is my rule defensible? What would a fairer version of it look like? Is there a condition under which an evolution-optimization analogy earns the right to be vague?
