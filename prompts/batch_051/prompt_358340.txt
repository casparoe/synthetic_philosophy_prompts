You are a social theorist who studies accountability, ritual, and institutional design, and you are good at tracing the incentives, failure modes, and second-order effects of unusual social mechanisms. I want your analysis of a proposed institution, not a moral verdict in the abstract.

A recurring observation in anthropology and political theory is that communities under stress sometimes restore cohesion by concentrating blame on a single figure, a scapegoat, who is expelled, punished, or made to carry guilt that is really diffuse or lies elsewhere. Suppose a startup tries to turn this into a transparent commercial practice. It maintains a roster of Substitutes: consenting adults paid a standing retainer plus a per-event hazard fee. When a client organization suffers a scandal whose causal chain is genuinely spread out — a product defect that no single person knowingly caused, a harmful institutional culture, a large failed project with dozens of contributors — the client may invoke the service. The Substitute then executes a public sequence: resigns from a nominal position, delivers a full apology, and performs a calibrated penance, such as a substantial donation to a victim-compensation fund, a month of visible public service, or temporary self-exclusion from industry events. Crucially, the contract states that the Substitute is not admitting to having performed the harmful act, and the company keeps a public registry that openly describes the role as a formal receptacle for public anger rather than a factual wrongdoer. The client pays a transfer fee scaled to the harm, while the people causally responsible are not publicly named and stay in their posts.

Analyze how this institution would actually behave if it caught on. Start by brainstorming the incentives it creates for the client organizations, for the Substitutes, for victims and the wider public, and for journalists and regulators. Then trace the predictable failure modes and second-order effects: repeat offenders using the service as a cleanup budget; an arms race over how plausible or genuinely compromised a Substitute must seem for the ritual to work; the fate of apologies known to have been purchased; victim groups rejecting the penance as laundered atonement; insurers and investors learning to read a transfer as a signal of internal rot; and the emergence of norms, certification, or prohibition around the practice. Ask throughout whether the mechanism can work when everyone knows exactly what it is, or whether its reconciling power depends on concealment, sincerity, or some residue of genuine shame. Conclude by saying what the thought experiment reveals about actual scapegoat rituals and whether their social function can be had without the injustice. Think carefully, and be explicit about which parts of the analysis are genuine predictions and which are speculative.
