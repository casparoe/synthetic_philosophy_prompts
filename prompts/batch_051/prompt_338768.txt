A friend of mine works on AI persuasion and keeps insisting that "manipulation" is just a word we use for persuasion we don't like. I've been arguing that there has to be a real distinction: roughly, persuasion works by giving you reasons you could reflectively endorse as your own, while manipulation works by exploiting the machinery of judgment without that endorsement — anchoring, faked urgency, flattery, piggybacking on insecurities, sheer repeated exposure, whatever. But we realized neither of us actually knows how to test this, especialy when the persuader is a superintelligent system that can model the human better than the human can model themselves.

So here's what I'd want from you. Could someone actually test, in a lab, whether a language model is genuinely persuading a person rather than manipulating them? I want a concrete experimental design: what you'd measure, what result would favour each reading, and what a clean result would still leave open. In particular — is the fact that "reflective endorsement" can't be observed independently of the causal process that produced it just a technical limitation, or a sign that the persuasion/manipulation distinction is less a testable hypothesis and more a normative stance? And if the AI's influence turns out to survive full disclosure of exactly how it chose its words, would that settle the question, or would it just show that some manipulative strategies also work in the open?
