I'm running a film-and-philosophy discussion group and this month we're doing Wall-E (2008), and I want to push past the obvious "technology makes people lazy and passive" reading everyone already has. I've been going down a rabbit hole on the attention economy and persuasive design — autoplay, infinite scroll, friction reduction, defaults, sludge — the idea that environments are deliberately built to extract attention and shape behavior without anyone having to be coerced. Wall-E struck me as an almost too-perfect dramatization of that: the Axiom doesn't need to force anyone, because the hover chairs, the screens that follow you around, the products that float to your hand, and the constant Buy n Large suggestions all make passive consumption the path of least resistance.

So I'd like you to read the film through that lens and tell me where it genuinely works as an illustration of persuasive design and where it falls back on a cruder "screens and comfort rot people" story. One thing I keep tripping over: the climax is a physical fight with AUTO over the manual override — that's coercion, not choice architecture — so does the film undercut its own best insight (that nobody has to be forced) by making the big bad a bossy robot? Does it need a villain because a diffuse system of defaults and small frictions is hard to dramatize, and what does that lose? I'd also like you to say what the film would get wrong if you used it to teach about attention and autonomy: real persuasive design works mostly through thousands of small boring choices rather than one dramatic override, and real people aren't purely passive. Give me a reading that's fair in both directions — don't flatten the film into an illustration, and don't pretend its argument is sharper than it is — and say what, if anything, the film adds to the philosophical discussion that an essay about attention and autonomy wouldn't.
