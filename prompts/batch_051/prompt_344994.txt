Me and my mate just had a long argument about surnames and I'm still not sure either of us were right. He said a surname like 'Carpenter' works like a description — it means something like 'the person descended from a carpenter' — so if it turned out the original ancestor wasn't a carpenter, the name would have failed to refer, or would pick out a different line. I said that's daft, because we still use the surname to pick out the same actual people even if the etymology is wrong; the name just stuck as a label. Then he pointed out that in loads of cultures the family name comes first and marks a clan (Korean, Hungarian, Chinese), and in places like Iceland there isn't really a family surname at all — you get your father's first name plus -son or -dóttir, so your 'surname' changes every generation. That made me wonder whether family names work differently from given names when it comes to reference.

Could you explain the conceptual issue here, rather then tell us who's right? I want to understand what it means for a proper name to be a rigid designator (a label that picks out the same individual no matter how the facts turn out) as opposed to a description, how that distinction applies to ordinary given names, and whether hereditary surnames, patronymics, and the like fit the same story or need a different one. Keep it conceptual, with everyday examples rather than formal semantics.
