I'm in a seminar on emergency powers and constitutional gaps, and a passage from Locke has led me to an argument that now seems to prove far too much. The passage is from the Second Treatise of Government, Chapter XIV, "Of Prerogative":

"This power, whilst employed for the benefit of the community, and suitably to the trust and ends of the government, is undoubted prerogative, and never is questioned: for the people are very seldom or never scrupulous or nice in the point; they are far from examining prerogative, whilst it is in any tolerable degree employed for the use it was meant, that is, for the good of the people, and not manifestly against it: but if there comes to be a question between the executive power and the people, about a thing claimed as a prerogative; the tendency of the exercise of such prerogative to the good or hurt of the people, will easily decide that question."

Trying to give this precise form, I reasoned as follows. (1) The executive may, where standing law is silent or applies badly, act on its own discretion for the public good, and may even set the letter of the law aside (Locke's own example is pulling down an innocent man's house to stop a fire from spreading). (2) But at the moment of action there is no independent and authoritative judge of whether an exercise of prerogative really tends to the good or hurt of the people: the legislature is too slow or not in session, the courts are bound to apply the standing law, and the people are not consulted in advance. (3) So in any contested case it is the executive's own judgment of the public good that decides, at least until afterward. (4) A power whose limits are settled, when disputed, by the very person who holds it is in practice indistinguishable from arbitrary power. (5) Therefore Locke's bounded trust collapses into arbitrary power exactly where the distinction matters most — in the contested case he claims the tendency to good or hurt "will easily decide."

That conclusion strikes me as unacceptable, because Locke plainly takes himself to be describing a limited trust rather than an unbounded discretion. I would most like to hold onto (1), or something close to it: I think there really is a difference between pulling down a house to stop a spreading fire and, say, a ruler who suspends an entire tax code on a plea of public convenience. So if (1) is not where things go wrong, which step do I reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
