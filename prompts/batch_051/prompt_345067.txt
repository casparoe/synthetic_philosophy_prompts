You are a professional philosopher of perception, and I'd like you to think carefully about something that's recently taken over my head. A few weeks ago I had a huge row with my brother, and for the next day his neutral face genuinely looked sneering to me—not just that I expected sneering, but the face itself looked that way. My sister says I was just seeing a neutral face and immediately interpreting it as sneering, and now I'm not sure anymore. So I've been reading about cognitive penetration of perception, and I've collected my gut verdicts on a set of cases. I want you to help me work out whether there's one principle behind them.

Please start by brainstorming a few candidate principles and then test them against my cases before you commit. And please write your answer in British English.

Here are the cases and my verdicts:

1. Root on the trail. After a friend spent an evening telling me about copperheads, I was hiking at dusk and a curved root looked exactly like a coiled snake—I saw scales and a head, and only when I got closer did the scales 'flatten' back into wood. I'd say my fear genuinely changed the visual experience. That one's definately a clear yes to penetration.

2. Expensive-looking wine. I drank the same supermarket red from two glasses, one poured from a fancy-looking bottle. The 'expensive' one genuinely tasted rounder and more complex to me. My verdict: the expectation changed the taste itself, not just my report—though I'm less sure than I am about the snake.

3. Duck-rabbit. I know the drawing is both a duck and a rabbit, but I can't see both at once, and just telling myself 'see the rabbit now' does nothing; I have to deliberately shift my attention in a certain way for it to flip. My verdict: not penetration, just attention.

4. The arrowhead line illusion. You know the one: two equal lines look unequal because of the arrowheads, and they still look unequal to me even after I've measured them and know they're equal. My verdict: here my knowledge and belief completely fail to change how things look, so perception is not always penetrable.

5. The birder's field mark. After I finally learned the subtle wing-pattern that separates two near-identical warblers, the birds literally started to look different—the field mark pops out, and I now see one bird where I used to see a brownish blur. My verdict: my knowledge has changed my perception, but it feels different from the snake case, more like upgrading my system than biasing it.

6. My brother's face. The face that started all this: in the moment it looked sneering, but when I looked at a photo of the same expression an hour later it looked neutral. My honest verdict is I don't know. I'd have said it was in my perception at the time, but I can't rule out that I saw a neutral face and instantly interpreted it as sneering.

My working rule so far is something like: penetration is when a cognitive state changes the experience itself in the moment (snake, wine, maybe the face), whereas the birdwatcher case is long-term perceptual recalibration, and the duck-rabbit and arrow illusion are just attention or impenetrability. But I'm not at all sure that rule is principled rather then just words.

So: is there a single principle, or a small set of principles, that captures these verdicts? State it precisely, test it against all six cases, tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to give up and why, and tell me what it predicts about a few cases I haven't mentioned. If the right answer is that these are genuinely different phenomena, I still want a sharp boundary between them, not hand-waving.
