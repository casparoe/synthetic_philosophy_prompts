A pamphlet in my mother's neurologist's office defends advance directives with the following paragraph:

'An advance directive is simply a form of self-binding, like Ulysses having his crew tie him to the mast so that he could not steer toward the Sirens. The person signing the directive has the right to decide now what may be done to him later, and his later refusal does not cancel that right because the earlier and later selves are the same person directing the same life. We accept exactly this in contract law, where no one considers it paternalistic to hold a person to an agreement he now regrets. Overriding a valid advance directive is therefore not kindness to the patient but a refusal to respect the patient as the author of his own life. Once the directive is competently made and unambiguous about the situation at hand, the only serious question left for a doctor is whether to follow it.'

I find the argument appealing, but I suspect it is moving faster than the underlying philosophy allows, and I do not have the training to say exactly where. Please audit the paragraph sentence by sentence. For each of its five sentences, indicate what a defender of the view would accept as accurate, what a critic would flag as a subtle misrepresentation or conflation, and how much argumentative work the sentence actually does. Give exactly five numbered findings, each no more than two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion. Make the fifth finding double as your verdict on whether the paragraph's overall conclusion survives the corrections to the earlier sentences.
