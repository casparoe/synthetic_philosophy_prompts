I convene a graduate reading group on trust, testimony, and the ethics of caregiving, and this term we are examining the ordinary fictions adults maintain for young children: Santa, the tooth fairy, the dog sent to live on a farm, and similar benevolent deceptions. I would like you to write a sorting quiz, "Which view of benevolent deception toward children do you actually hold?", with nine concrete cases and a scoring key.

The quiz should sort respondents by their verdicts on cases, not by which label they prefer; therefore no question may simply ask whether lying to children is wrong, whether honesty is intrinsically important, or whether the respondent considers themselves a paternalist.

The leaves of the taxonomy must be six positions:

1. Trust-first: deliberate deception of a child is presumptively corrupting of the caregiver-child epistemic relationship, and benevolent intent does not dissolve the wrong.
2. Epistemic paternalism: benevolent deception is justified whenever the child lacks the capacity to handle a truth, provided the fiction genuinely serves the child's welfare or development.
3. Pretend-play view: fictions such as Santa are best analyzed as participation in a shared cultural pretense, so the norms of ordinary assertion and deception do not straightforwardly apply.
4. Developmental scaffolding view: such fictions are acceptable only if embedded in a trajectory toward the child's learning to detect frames, ask what a speaker means, and eventually interrogate testimony.
5. Child-autonomy view: children are owed truth as a matter of respect for them as persons, so benevolent deception is objectionable even when the child would not mind.
6. Case-by-case welfarism: there is no intrinsic prohibition on benevolent deception; each case is decided by expected harms and benefits, including the effects on trust.

For each of the nine cases, give two to four concrete answer options, each option a possible parental action or judgment. Six cases are fixed. They must be: a four-year-old who asks directly whether Santa is real; a child who has worked out for herself that the tooth fairy is her parents and asks for confirmation; a dog genuinely euthanized and described as having gone to live on a farm, with the question of whether any later disclosure is required; a ten-year-old who still believes in Santa and is being mocked at school; a child who asks a parent directly whether the parent has ever lied to her; and a child who discovers the truth and confronts the parent, accusing the parent of lying. Design the remaining three cases to separate positions that these six leave entangled. The diagnostic distinctions I most want the cases to expose are: whether the wrong, if any, lies in the deceptive act itself or in the child's later discovery of it; whether unprompted maintenance of a fiction differs from a direct lie in response to a direct question; whether the child's co-participation in the pretense changes the speech-act category; whether the parent's intention to disclose at some point matters; and whether some fictions are vindicated only if they teach the child something about testimony itself.

Then provide a scoring key. For each of the six leaves, state which answer to each question that position would select, allowing that some leaves will be distinguished only by patterns across several questions. After the key, discuss the empty regions of the answer space: combinations of answers that no leaf requires. For each empty region, diagnose briefly whether it is empty because the position would be incoherent, because it is too unattractive to have attracted defenders, or because it is merely unexplored territory worth flagging. Finally, say explicitly which pairs of leaves the nine cases cannot separate, and for each such pair explain whether additional cases would separate them or whether the two positions genuinely agree on all forced-choice verdicts and differ only in accompanying theory.

The audience is graduate students in philosophy; the cases may be ordinary, but the taxonomy labels should be defined with care. Avoid citations, references, and reading lists.
