I've been going back and forth with one of my PhD students about whether knowing is luminous — whether, whenever you know that p, you're in a position to know that you know it — and whether that can be squared with fallibilism. He got fed up with the usual back-and-forth and showed me a numbered argument that's supposed to show that the strict KK principle and fallibilism are simply incompatible. I can't tell if it's a nice result or a cheap trick. Here it is as he wrote it:

(1) Suppose S knows p fallibly; that is, the evidence on which S bases her belief that p does not entail p.
(2) If S's basis for p doesn't entail p, then S's total evidence leaves open at least one possibility in which p is false.
(3) In any possibility in which p is false, S does not know p (knowledge is factive).
(4) So S's total evidence leaves open a possibility in which S does not know p.
(5) To know that she knows p, S must be able to rule out every possibility in which she does not know p.
(6) But by hypothesis she cannot rule out the relevant possibility, since her total evidence leaves it open.
(7) Therefore S does not know that she knows p.
(8) And that contradicts the KK principle.
(9) So fallibilism and KK cannot both be true.

His punchline is that fallibilism is unstable: as soon as you insist on the second-order knowledge, you are forced back into eliminating exactly the error possibilities the first-order fallibilist story let stand. I suspect the fault is somewhere around step (2) or step (5), but I keep going in circles trying to say exactly what goes wrong. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, on what reading of it, and why exactly? If it is valid, then who should give way — the fallibilist or the defender of KK?
