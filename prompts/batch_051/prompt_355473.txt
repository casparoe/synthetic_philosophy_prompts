In the reply to the third objection in Summa Theologiae II-II, Question 42, Article 2, Thomas Aquinas writes:

"A tyrannical government is not just, because it is directed, not to the common good, but to the private good of the ruler, as the Philosopher states (Polit. iii, 5; Ethic. viii, 10). Consequently there is no sedition in disturbing a government of this kind, unless indeed the tyrant's rule be disturbed so inordinately, that his subjects suffer greater harm from the consequent disturbance than from the tyrant's government. Indeed it is the tyrant rather that is guilty of sedition, since he encourages discord and sedition among his subjects, that he may lord over them more securely; for this is tyranny, being conducive to the private good of the ruler, and to the injury of the multitude."

I want to reconstruct the underlying argument as a numbered proof and have it checked carefully. Here is my best attempt at extracting Aquinas's reasoning:

(1) An act is sedition only if it is directed against the unity of law and the common good of a people.

(2) A tyrannical government is directed not to the common good but to the private good of the ruler.

(3) Therefore, disturbing a tyrannical government is not, as such, directed against the common good.

(4) Therefore, disturbing a tyrannical government is not sedition, provided the disturbance does not cause the subjects greater harm than the tyrant's continued rule.

(5) The tyrant, by encouraging discord among his subjects to rule more securely, is himself guilty of sedition.

I find this argument intuitively appealing, but I suspect something goes wrong between (2) and (3), or between (3) and (4), perhaps an equivocation on "common good" or a slide from the definition of sedition to its application. A student pressed me on exactly where the inference fails, and I found I could not state the difficulty with precision. Please state exactly which step, if any, fails, and give the strongest reading of the argument on which it is valid, distinguishing that from a reading on which it is not. If the argument needs an additional premise to go through, say what that premise would be and whether Aquinas's text supplies it.
