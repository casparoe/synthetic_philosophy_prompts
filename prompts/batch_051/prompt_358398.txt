I cover science and ideas for a general-interest outlet, and I've been mulling over the research on tight and loose cultures — how many unwritten rules a community has and how harshly it punishes deviations from them. A planner I interviewed described a proposal that's apparently being discussed in one mid-sized city: instead of arguing about rules one at a time, each neighborhood would set a "norm density" score from 0 to 10 every four years, in a vote open only to property owners. At 0 the city enforces only criminal law and genuine health-and-safety nuisances; at 10 it enforces the entire municipal code plus a menu of minor quality-of-life rules covering noise, signage, weeds, street vending, parking, and even queueing in city offices. Enforcement would be proactive rather than complaint-driven, carried out by neighborhood-level code officers, and both the fines and the enforcement budget would stay inside the neighborhood — officers would be partly paid out of the fines they write. People can move freely between neighborhoods.

I want to analyze this as a mechanism rather than as a moral position: what it incentivizes among residents, landlords, businesses, and the code officers themselves; who sorts where and how fast; what happens at the boundaries between a 9 and a 2; what it does to tenants, since property owners set the score but tenants have to live under it; how it gets gamed; and which failure mode appears first. Can those failures be patched without turning the whole thing into ordinary zoning? I'm especially interested in whether letting people choose their local tightness would just sort the city by temperament rather than actually changing how much rule-following the city as a whole demands.
