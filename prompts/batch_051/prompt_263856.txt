After a year of arguing metaethics over dinner with my housemate (who is an ethicist and keeps saying noncognitivism has been dead since the nineties) I've somehow ended up on a conference program, and in three weeks I have to actually defend a version of expressivism about normative language in front of a room that is not going to be friendly. The thesis I'm defending is roughly this: normative sentences get their meaning from the desire-like, plan-ish mental states they conventionally express rather than from facts they describe, and the Frege–Geach embedding problem is solveable if you give a recursive semantics over those states — negation expresses a disposition to reject the state expressed, the conditional expresses endorsement of combining the relevant states, and validity is a rational-coherence constraint on attitudes rather than truth preservation. So a sentence like 'if lying is wrong, then getting someone else to lie is wrong' has genuine content because it constrains which combinations of attitudes you can coherently hold, and that is supposed to be enough for it to embed and move around in arguments. My talk's actual argument: (1) cognitivists haven't given a real semantics for 'wrong' either, so the burden-of-proof framing is a tie at best, (2) I sketch an attitude-based recursive semantics for negation, conjunction and conditionals, and (3) I deflate all the truth-talk about norms as a way of inferring or commending, not describing. The audience is a department colloquium: two formal semanticists who think anything that embeds needs a compositional interpretation into truth values or it's not meaning at all, a moral realist who will treat 'murder is wrong is not literally true' as a reductio ad absurdum, a logic grad student who works on nonmonotonic consequence and will want to know exactly what the consequence relation is on my picture, and one older Blackburn-style quasi-realist who now thinks quasi-realism only works by quietly smuggling truth back in. Nobody else in the room is an expressivist. Write me a Q&A prep sheet, not a general defense: the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this specific crowd, ordered by how likely and how damaging. For each, phrase the objection the way they would actually put it (in their vocabularly), say whether it has real force, what I should concede vs. hold, give me a two-minute reply, and note where the reply is soft. Also tell me which objection I should head off during the talk itself, and include the question I'm praying nobody asks.
