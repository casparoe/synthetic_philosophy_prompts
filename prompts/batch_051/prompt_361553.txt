In Nyāya epistemology, it is standard to treat śabda, or testimony, as an independent pramāṇa alongside perception, inference, and analogy, while also holding that not every assertion is a source of knowledge: the speaker must be an āpta, a trustworthy person competent in the matter and disposed to communicate it reliably. A psychologist I am working with disputes the usefulness of this taxonomy. She says that when we acquire knowledge from what someone says, we are in fact running an inference from observed cues about the speaker’s competence and sincerity, together with background regularities, and that calling testimony a distinct source of knowledge adds nothing testable.

I would like to examine the following claim: knowledge from testimony is not merely inference in disguise, because the epistemic route is distinct, and this is why we can gain knowledge from testimony even when the materials for a reliability inference are thin, conflicting, or absent. How would one test that claim rather than treat it as a matter of stipulation? Design the strongest concrete test you can, specifying the manipulation, the comparison condition, and the result that would favour each reading. Then say what a positive result would and would not settle. For example, would it establish that testimony is a separate cognitive mechanism, a separate normative kind, or only that it is a pragmatically useful way of carving up cases? I want the operationalization to be precise enough to be feasible in principle, but I also want an honest account of the residue that no such test could reach, especially where the Nyāya notion of an āpta imports normative conditions that are not straightforwardly observable.
