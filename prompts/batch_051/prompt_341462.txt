In my political theory reading group, two friends got into a long argument after we read a short piece on the Condorcet Jury Theorem. One of them (R) said the theorem gives a clean, mathematical reason to trust large democratic majorities: if each voter has even a slightly better-than-even chance of picking the correct of two options, and voters are independent, then as the number of voters grows, the probability that a majority picks the correct option shoots toward 1. The other (S) said this is basically useless as a justification of actual democracy, because real voters are not independent—they consume the same media, follow the same pundits, and copy one another, so their errors are correlated. When that happens, she said, a huge majority can just be a huge shared mistake; size alone doesn't fix anything.

R replied that she was overreading the independence assumption. Conditional on the correct answer, he said, independence doesn't mean voters can't share information, party affiliation, or a media environment; it means their votes each carry some signal that isn't wholly determined by everyone else's. Shared evidence doesn't automatically wreck the result, and there are generalizations of the theorem that allow some correlation among votes. In any case, he said, the theorem is a benchmark, not a description: it tells you which conditions—competence, independence, sincere voting—would have to hold for majority rule to track the truth. If you think those conditions fail, that's a diagnosis, not a refutation.

S answered that this was a retreat. On actual politicized questions, the shared signal is most of what moves people and the private signal is close to noise, so the conditions fail exactly where we'd want to use the theorem. A theorem that says "if these conditions held, majorities would be wise" cannot justify trusting majorities when we have no reason to think the conditions hold. R got the last word: the theorem cuts both ways, because if average competence is below one half, large electorates amplify error, not truth. So the real lesson is not "bigger is always better" but "bigger is better only once you improve competence and independence." That, he said, is still an epistemic argument for taking the quality of voters seriously, not a reason to discard the theorem.
