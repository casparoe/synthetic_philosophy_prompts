My aesthetics professor keeps using the fruit-tasting analogy to settle arguments about aesthetic testimony. He says nobody thinks you can come to know what a cherimoya tastes like from someone else's description of it, even a really good one; the only way is to taste it yourself. So likewise, nobody should think you can come to know that a painting is graceful, a building is elegant, or a piece of music is devastating just by being told—testimony is as useless in the aesthetic case as it is for tastes. He uses this to motivate something like the acquaintance principle, and to conclude that there's an important asymmetry between aesthetic judgments and ordinary factual beliefs, where testimony works fine. On his view, people who treat art criticism as a potential source of aesthetic knowledge are making a category error.

I dont trust the analogy, but I'm not sure where it fails. Audit it. Give me the explicit mapping: the fruit, its taste, the act of tasting, someone's verbal description, and my judgment of what it tastes like—what do they correspond to when we move to the painting or music case? Then identify where the mapping breaks down. For instance, a taste is notoriously private and hard to put into words, while critics seem able to transmit something that real listeners can go on to use. It's also worth flagging which features of the source domain get quietly imported into the target—like the assumption that aesthetic value is a simple sensory quality, rather than something partly constituted by understanding, categories, and context. Seperate the disanalogies that actually matter for the professor's conclusion from the ones that don't. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion—that aesthetic testimony can't give us knowledge or justified belief, or whatever the most careful version of his view amounts to—survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if there is a better analogy for the pessimist's point, offer one.
