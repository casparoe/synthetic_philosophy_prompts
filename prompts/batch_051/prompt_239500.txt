Suppose I use the following five-part checklist for what makes a paradigm shift in a scientific field. Before the shift, the field's normal science is organized around a shared exemplar: a concrete achievement that supplies its model problems, methods, and standards of solution. Anomalies then accumulate that the exemplar repeatedly fails to resolve, until the field enters a sense of crisis. A rival candidate emerges. The dispute between old and new is partly incommensurable: each side must judge the other by its own standards, and there is no neutral, theory-independent test that settles the choice. And after the new candidate wins, the field reorganizes itself — its textbooks, training, and sense of its own history are rewritten around the new exemplar.

Now take the rise of evidence-based medicine in clinical medicine over recent decades. The older working exemplar was the experienced clinician reasoning from pathophysiology and accumulated case experience. The newer one is the randomized controlled trial and, above it, the systematic review and meta-analysis, with unsystematized expert opinion demoted to the bottom of a hierarchy of evidence. I want a verdict on whether that change qualifies.

Go through the five criteria one by one, honestly marking where the case fits, where it fits only awkwardly, and where the criteria underdetermine the answer. Then say: is evidence-based medicine a Kuhnian paradigm shift in clinical medicine, a methodological reform within the same paradigm, or something else — for instance, a change in the field's standard of evidence that does not have the right shape to count as a paradigm shift at all? If it does not fit cleanly, tell me whether that exposes a weakness in the checklist, in the way the case has been characterized, or in the idea that Kuhn's framework should be exported to medicine here.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble and no restating of the question.
