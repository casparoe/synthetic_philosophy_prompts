I have recently taken up a post on the policy team of an international conflict-resolution organization, and one of my first responsibilities is to help decide whether we should publicly endorse proposed peace frameworks: ceasefires, transitional power-sharing arrangements, and treaties ending long-running armed conflicts. We review several of these a year; each decision is consequential because our endorsement affects donor funding and local legitimacy, and once given it is hard to retract. My team has about a week per proposal, produces a one-page assessment, and none of us are trained in philosophy, though our director expects more rigor than “both sides seem sincere.”

I have been told that Kant’s essay on perpetual peace gives criteria for distinguishing a genuine peace from a mere pause in hostilities, and that the distinctions it draws—between a treaty that secretly reserves causes of future war and one that eliminates them, between standing armies and defensive militias, between intervention in another state’s constitution and lawful assistance, between a world-state and a voluntary federation of free states, between hospitality and conquest—are exactly the distinctions my office keeps failing to draw. I want that turned into an evaluative procedure I can actually run at a desk.

Please write the procedure. It should be presentable as a sequence of tests or questions, in order, with instructions for what counts as a satisfactory answer, when the assessment stops with a recommendation not to endorse, and when it hands the question to a person because the issue is a judgment call rather than a matter of applying the criteria. Keep Kant’s philosophical architecture in mind—particularly the idea that durable peace requires a certain kind of domestic constitution and a lawful international order, not just goodwill—but do not assume my colleagues know the text or its vocabulary. The procedure must be honest about where it will misfire: where a Kantian test would reject a proposal my organization might have good moral or prudential reasons to support, and where the procedure cannot settle the matter.

Then run the procedure on two or three worked cases, including at least one in which the procedure gives an answer my organization will not like. Suitable cases would include: a ceasefire that gives a non-state armed group a share of power but leaves combatants under arms during a transition; a proposed regional union that pools sovereignty and creates a mutual-defense obligation while remaining a league of separate states; and an externally guaranteed settlement that gives outside powers an open-ended role in supervising a country’s internal arrangements. For each, show which step in the procedure does the decisive work and what a director would have to override to reach a different conclusion. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot evaluate—what it will systematically fail to see in a peace framework—so I do not present it to my director as more complete than it is.
