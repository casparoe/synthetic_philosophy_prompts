I've been going down a rabbit hole after rewatching The Prestige, the Christopher Nolan movie about the two rival stage magicians. Specifically the part where Angier gets the machine from Tesla that doesn't really teleport him but makes an exact duplicate a short distance away, and every night one of them falls through a trapdoor into a water tank and drowns while the other takes the applause. Angier's whole torment is that when he steps into the machine he doesn't know if he'll be the one who lands in the box or the one who walks out. At the same time I've been reading bits and pieces about Buddhist anatta, the no-self teaching: the idea that there's no permanent owner of experience, no little controller behind the eyes, just a stream of changing parts that we label a person. And the two things keep crashing into each other. The movie seems to take it as devaststing that there's no way to say which copy is really Angier, while anatta says that question was always confused because there wasn't a single continuous Angier there to begin with. But the movie also makes each drowning feel like a separate, real death, and I don't think the Buddhist line just erases that.

I'd like a proper reading of The Prestige through this no-self lens, not just using the movie as a prop for the idea. Does the film actually dramatize something anatta gets right, or does it push back on it? What do I do with the contrast between Angier, who multiplies himself and kills the extras, and Borden and Fallon, the two twins living one shared identity and one name? Where does the movie quietly presuppose a further-fact view of the self, and where does it make you feel the costs of that view in a way arguments don't? I'm writing notes for a film group and want to be faithful to both the movie and the idea, including the places they refuse to line up.
