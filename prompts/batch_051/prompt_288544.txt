I am a novelist. My current project is set in an intensive shrimp farm on the Gulf of Thailand, and one of its central scenes turns on a hatchery technician who is asked to perform eyestalk ablation on broodstock females, a routine procedure in which one eyestalk is cut or crushed to bring the animals into reproductive condition. I want the novel to get the moral texture of that work right, so I have been reading the animal welfare literature and have assembled a working checklist for whether a creature can suffer: (1) it has nociceptors, receptors specialized for tissue damage rather than general touch or chemical sensing; (2) those signals are processed in some central integrative structure, so the response is more than a local reflex arc; (3) it behaves as if the experience matters, learning to avoid the stimulus, guarding or rubbing the affected site, and giving up food or shelter to escape it; and (4) those behaviors are dampened by analgesics or anxiolytics, as pain behavior is in vertebrates.

The case that confuses me is the farmed whiteleg shrimp, the species behind most of the world's farmed shrimp. It has a ventral nerve cord and a modest concentration of nervous tissue in the head, but no cortex and no structure that maps neatly onto the vertebrate pain system, and it will tail-flick away from a sudden threat in a fast, stereotyped way that looks purely reflexive. Yet in laboratory work I have read about, shrimp daubed with an irritant on the antennae groom that site repeatedly, and the grooming declines if a local anesthetic is applied first; they also learn to avoid places associated with noxious stimuli. On the other side, the existence of true nociceptors in decapods is contested, and the animals continue to feed and spawn after eyestalk ablation, which is hard to interpret in either direction.

My question is not whether shrimp farming is cruel. It is whether the whiteleg shrimp qualifies as a creature that can suffer on my four criteria. Please go criterion by criterion and say where the evidence underdetermines the verdict. Then tell me whether the shrimp case reveals that my criteria themselves are vertebrate-shaped: are the anatomical demands in the first two criteria doing real work, or are they a historical accident of starting from mammals, and would satisfying the behavioral criteria alone be enough to license treating the animals as subjects rather than objects in the novel's moral world?
