I am a PhD student in cognitive science working on judgment and decision-making, and I keep running into a debate I do not know how to settle empirically. One side treats rationality as a trainable skill on the model of a martial art: the student drills isolated forms—calibration quizzes, base-rate problems, Bayesian updating exercises, reference-class forecasting—on the assumption that these katas will become available later in unstructured settings, the way rehearsed movements are supposed to appear during sparring. The other side grants that people improve on the trained exercises and perhaps on structurally similar ones, but doubts that any of it reaches the reasoning people actually care about: how someone updates after a surprising personal experience, whether they catch their own motivated reasoning in a dispute, or how they allocate time and money under uncertainty.

Rather than another iteration of that theoretical argument, I would like a design for the best feasible test of the transfer claim. How would you operationalize unstructured, real-world reasoning so that it is both ecologically credible and controlled enough that improvement cannot be explained away as practice effects or demand characteristics? What pattern of results would count as evidence of genuine transfer rather than mere near transfer to slightly altered versions of the trained tasks, and what result would count against the martial-art picture? Finally, what would even a strong positive result leave unsettled—for instance, whether the mechanism is automatic habit or deliberate application of principles, whether the effect survives outside the study context, and whether what improves is reasoning itself rather than something adjacent such as caution or confidence calibration?
