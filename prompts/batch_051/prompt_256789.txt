Had a long argument with a friend last night about what a grammatical rule even is, and he kept cornering me on one asymmetry. Take go/went. We call "went" an irregular past tense, like it's an exception to the add-ed rule. But it isn't "go" plus anything. It's a different word that got plugged into the past-tense slot (that's suppletion), and nobody treats that as a crisis; we just list it in the lexicon as a fact about that one verb. Fine. But then he said: when a sound change is supposed to apply across the board and some word didn't undergo it, linguists won't let that word just sit there as a brute exception. They reach for borrowing, analogy, a conditioning enviornment everybody had missed—anything. An unexplained exception to a sound change is treated like a scandal, while go/went is treated like a boring filing note. Both are cases of "the rule didn't reach this word." I couldn't give him a clean answer. So: is there a real difference between these two kinds of exception, or is the asymmetry just what we need to protect the slogan that sound change is exceptionless? If theres a difference, what's the sharpest criterion that puts went on one side and an unexplained sound-change exception on the other—and does the criterion survive a hard case?
