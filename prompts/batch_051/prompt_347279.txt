Here's an asymmetry I keep noticing in everyday moral argument. Ask 'what if everyone did that?' about fare-dodging, queue-jumping, or not voting, and people treat it as a knock-down objection. Ask the same question about a couple who decide not to have children, about everyone becoming a nurse, or everyone learning the trumpet at fourty, and people roll their eyes and say the question is silly. Same logical form, very different authority. Is there actually a principle that sorts these cases, or is the phrase just a rhetorical device we wheel out for acts we already dislike? I'd especialy like to know whether the best sorting principle looks Kantian (contradiction in conception vs contradiction in willing, or the idea that some maxims free-ride on a practice), or more Hare-style (a consistency test on my own prescriptions), or more Singer-style (weighing everyone's actual interests when the rule is universalised), or just outcome-aversion in disguise. And whatever you conclude: should we keep using the phrase, use it more carefully, or drop it? Please write your answer in British English.
