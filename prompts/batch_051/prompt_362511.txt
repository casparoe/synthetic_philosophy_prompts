Next month I'm standing up with a microphone at the wedding reception of two of my closest friends, who were also my PhD advisors. One is a philosopher of action whose career has centered on weakness of will, or akrasia—in particular, the view that the real puzzle is synchronic: an agent can judge, at the very moment of acting, that one option is best all things considered and still take another. The other is a behavioral economist whose career has centered on hyperbolic discounting and time-inconsistency—the view that many putatively weak-willed episodes are better understood as preference reversals across time, in which this evening's self discounts tomorrow's cost far more steeply than this morning's self did. They have spent fifteen years arguing about which of these two descriptions is more basic, and either one would be quietly annoyed by a toast that waved the dispute away as a purely verbal quibble or as an academic hobby that love somehow overrides.

The guest list includes a table or two of decision theorists and philosophers of action who will catch every technical turn, and a much larger group of relatives and friends who will simply be listening for warmth. The material has become personal for me as well: I'm badly behind on a dissertation chapter, and I now catch myself asking whether my 9 a.m. and 10 p.m. selves are one agent or two with different discount rates. Please write the toast. It should be genuinely funny without mocking either view, and it should not resolve, referee, or dissolve their disagreement. The philosophical problem should do the work in the speech. The most promising approach is to treat the wedding vow as the test case: what exactly is being done when two people, each holding a professional account of why the person standing here in twenty-five years cannot simply be assumed to endorse what is promised today, nonetheless promise to be there in twenty-five years?

Structure the response into the following titled sections, in this order and roughly at these lengths: "The Setup" (about 150 words, establishing one comic set-piece with no jargon), "The Tension" (about 200 words, letting their two rival diagnoses of that same episode rub against each other), "The Vow" (about 150 words, turning to what a promise across time could amount to once that tension is on the table), and "The Toast" (about 50 words, the closing lift, which should feel like affection rather than like an argument won).
