Having recently retired from a career in the natural sciences, I have been working through the philosophical literature on consciousness. I find there a familiar distinction between access consciousness — the property a mental content has when it is poised or globally available for report, reasoning, memory, and the control of action — and phenomenal consciousness — the property of there being something it is like for the subject. I have reconstructed the following argument, which appears to show that phenomenal consciousness, construed as something over and above access consciousness, can have no place in a serious scientific account of the mind. Since that conclusion is more radical than I find comfortable, I want to know whether the argument really goes through and, if not, exactly where it gives way.

(1) A concept earns a place in a scientific account of the mind only if there are public conditions under which it can be correctly applied to a subject; otherwise no observation, experiment, or measurement could ever count for or against its applying.

(2) The only public conditions available for applying any concept of conscious content are the capacities characteristic of global availability: the subject can report the content, use it in reasoning or memory, or let it guide action.

(3) In global workspace theory, a content is access-conscious precisely when it is selected and broadcast in this way, making it globally available to those consuming systems.

(4) Therefore, by (1)–(3), every scientifically respectable concept of conscious content applies to a content if and only if that content is access-conscious.

(5) But phenomenal consciousness, if it is genuinely something over and above access consciousness, is a concept that would apply to a content in virtue of a further intrinsic, subjective character, and not in virtue of these capacities.

(6) Hence phenomenal consciousness, construed as over and above access consciousness, cannot have a place in a scientific account of the mind; the only scientifically defensible concept of consciousness is access consciousness.

Is this argument valid? If it is valid, which premise should be rejected, and on what grounds? If it is invalid, identify the precise inference at which it fails and state the suppressed premise that would be needed to repair it. My own suspicion is that step (2), or perhaps a slide between the public conditions for applying a concept and what the concept is about, is carrying more weight than it can bear; but I have not been able to make the diagnosis precise, and I would appreciate a rigorous treatment. In doing so, please keep the empirical commitments of global workspace theory clearly separate from the philosophical conclusion.
