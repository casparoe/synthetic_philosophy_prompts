I'm a novelist working on a book whose central character is an eighteenth-century churchman who becomes weirdly fixated on the idea that a country can change its material circumstances just by changing the kind of money it uses, and I keep finding myself circling back to Berkeley's The Querist, that strange book that is just question after question about whether money is really a counter or ticket rather than wealth itself. The way people summarize it for me, the underlying picture is basically the cloakroom ticket analogy: you give them your coat, they give you a token, and the token has no value except that it lets you claim the coat back. Berkeley seems to want to say gold and silver are just those tokens, so Ireland would lose nothing by replacing them with paper counters issued by a public bank, provided everybody treats the counters as claims on the real stuff underneath, which is land, labor, produce, trust, that sort of thing. I can see an initial mapping: coat is real wealth, ticket is money, the cloakroom is the bank or the state, the ticket number is the denomination, losing the ticket is like losing the money. But I also have a subplot in the novel where a more modern character pushes a cryptocurrency-ish scheme by leaning on the same analogy, and I want to know where the coat-check picture quietly imports features that a monetary system does not have. Could you walk me through what maps onto what, where the analogy breaks, which assumptions from the ticket model Berkeley is smuggling into money, and whether his conclusion, that the material of the counter doesn't matter and only confidence in the system does, actually survives once the disanalogies are on the table? I'm less interested in whether Berkeley's economic plan would have worked for Ireland than in what the analogy can and cannot carry, because I need my narrator to push back intelligently.
