Here's something I've been turning over. The old argument that the arc of history bends toward justice — or at least that there's a long-run drift toward less violence, more rights, more cooperation — is usually run as an induction from the historical record, with some story about why the drift is baked in (our rationality, an expanding moral circle, whatever). But the existential-risk people have a reply I can't get out of my head: the fact that the past looks like a story of improvement is exactly what you'd expect even if history had no direction at all, because if a permanent catastrophe or a forever-tyranny had already happened, there'd be no one around to look back and notice the shape of the record. And the very same technology that made the trend possible is what makes the next century or two the only period with the power to end the sample. Does the classic argument survive this, or does it collapse into survivorship bias? I dont just want a side. I want to know which premise the selection effect actually attacks, whether the argument can be repaired — say, by scaling the claim down to a local and fragile tendency, or by making it a claim about what we should hope for rather than what we should predict — and what evidence, if any, would tell a real arc from a biased sample you only got to see because you survived it.
