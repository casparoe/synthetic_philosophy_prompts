The question I set was:

Explain the main motivation for structural realism in the philosophy of science, using a concrete example from the history of physics. Be sure to distinguish epistemic from ontic structural realism, and say which version (if either) the motivation actually supports. You may also briefly discuss one standard objection.

Here is the student's answer, verbatim:

Structural realism is the view that when a successful scientific theory is replaced, what carries over to the new theory is not the theoretical entities but the mathematical or relational structure. The standard example is the transition from Fresnel's elastic-solid ether theory of light to Maxwell's electromagnetism. Fresnel's equations for reflection and refraction survived almost unchanged, while the ether was abandoned. So we should be realists only about the relational content of our best theories, not about unobservable objects.

The view gets its motivation from the interplay of two arguments. The no-miracles argument says that the predictive success of mature science would be a miracle unless our theories are latching onto something real. Since Fresnel's theory was false in its picture of the world (there is no elastic ether), the no-miracles argument only makes sense if what we are latching onto is structure. The no-miracles argument is therefore best read as an argument specifically for ontic structural realism. The pessimistic meta-induction points out that many past successful theories turned out to be false, so we should expect our current theories to be false too. Structural realism absorbs this by saying what is systematically preserved across theory change is the structure, not the entities.

That is why the epistemic/ontic distinction matters less than people think. Since only structure survives theory change, the structuralist shouldn't stop at the merely epistemic claim that structure is all we can know; she should take the ontic reading, on which relations (and not the relata) are what really exist. Otherwise the survival of structure is left as a kind of unexplained coincidence.

One objection commonly raised is Newman's: if all we know is the abstract structure of the world, then any structure can be made to fit any large enough set of objects by defining suitable relations, so structural descriptions become trivially true. But structuralists avoid this by saying they are only committed to the specific structures actually posited by current physics, not to arbitrary re-definable ones, and that is enough to keep the view substantive.
