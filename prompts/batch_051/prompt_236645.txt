I'm taking an upper-level seminar on Leibniz's moral philosophy, and I've been asked to design a runnable 75-minute classroom activity for 14 students. I'd like the activity to make participants feel, rather than just hear, why Leibniz defines justice the way he does in this passage from his "A Dialogue" (after 1695), as translated in Lloyd Strickland's *The Shorter Leibniz Texts*:

"TO LOVE is to find pleasure in the happiness of others. Thus the habit of loving someone is nothing other than BENEVOLENCE by which we want the good of others, not for the profit that we gain from it, but because it is agreeable to us in itself. CHARITY is a general benevolence. And JUSTICE is charity in accordance with wisdom. … so that one does not do harm to someone without necessity, and that one does as much good as one can, but especially where it is best employed."

What I want the activity to get at: participants should discover for themselves that once you care about others' happiness in general, the immediate problem becomes how to distribute finite time, attention, and resources; and that "wisdom" in Leibniz's definition names a real constraint, not an optional add-on. I want them to feel the pull toward universal benevolence and then the discomfort of having to rank or refuse some beneficiaries. Please design the activity with roles or rules, a timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments back to the passage. End with an honest note on which part of Leibniz's view the exercise cannot reproduce and must be supplied by the facilitator.
