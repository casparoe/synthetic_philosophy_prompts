I run a small philosophy discussion group where most people have read a couple of popular essays on antinatalism but nothing technical, and we keep going in circles on the procreation asymmetry. I've noticed that when people say they "accept the asymmetry" or "reject the asymmetry" they often mean very different things: some mean we have no duty to create happy people but a strong duty not to create miserable ones, others mean the absence of pain is good even when there is nobody around to enjoy that absence, and others are really talking about how bad death is rather than how bad never being born is. Because the conversation never gets down to concrete cases, the disagreement stays invisible.

So I want you to make a "which procreation asymmetry do you actually hold?" quiz for our next meeting. Think of it as pop philosophy, but the sorting logic should be serious. You're a professional philosopher working in population ethics and questionnaire design, and I need you to think through the diagnostic distinctions before you write anything. Brainstorm the underlying dimensions and possible cases first; don't include that brainstorming in the final response, but let it shape the questions.

Here's the brief. The quiz should have 9 questions, each a concrete scenario with 2 or 3 response options, phrased so people answer about the case and not about which label they like. No question should just ask whether someone accepts the asymmetry.

At least four cases have to appear, because these are the ones our group is actually split on: (1) a couple deciding whether to conceive a child whose life will be overall worth living but will contain real suffering, (2) a farmer deciding whether to breed chickens who will live short, miserable lives, (3) a question about whether it would have been bad for you if you had never been born, and (4) a question that separates the badness of dying early and losing future good years from the badness of never existing. You can choose the other five, but include at least one about a person who already exists and suffers, so the quiz tests whether someone applies the asymmetry only to future people or also to the already-born.

The leaves should be built out of the logical space rather than off a fixed list, but these positions must appear as leaves if the answer patterns support them: Benatar-style strong antinatalism (the asymmetry plus the view that coming into existence is always a harm), a softer "asymmetry is true but procreation is still permissible" view, a person-affecting view on which the asymmetry is really about obligations to identifiable people rather than impersonal value, a negative consequentialist view that derives the asymmetry from giving pain avoidance much more weight than pleasure promotion, a classical total-utilitarian view with no asymmetry at all, and an Epicurean view on which non-existence is neither good nor bad and the whole asymmetry is a confusion. If some combination of answers has no occupant, that should be noted in the scoring key, with a guess about whether the view is incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored.

Also flag honestly any pair of leaves your questions genuinely cannot separate, rather than pretending the quiz is sharper than it is.

Please organize the response into these sections, by name and in this order: "What the quiz is testing" (about 100 words, for the reader), "The questions" (around 900 words, the nine cases with answer options), "Scoring key" (around 400 words, mapping answer patterns to positions and noting incoherent patterns), "The positions" (around 500 words, one paragraph per leaf, each ending with one sentence on that position's most awkward commitment), and "Where the quiz breaks" (around 250 words, on the pairs and patterns you can't distinguish and why). Dont worry about making the prose beautiful; I care that the sorting is sharp and seperates views people actually hold.
