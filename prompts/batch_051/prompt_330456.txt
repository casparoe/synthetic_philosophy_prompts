I am writing as an ordinary reader, not as someone trained in philosophy. Not long ago I noticed an odd asymmetry in my own responses to fiction. When a story asks me to accept a strange descriptive fact — for example, that in its world the moon is a bolt of pale cloth that the characters fold and put away each morning — I do it with almost no strain. I hold the impossibility in suspension, let the author arrange the furniture of that world as they like, and read on. But when the narration asks me to accept a strange evaluation in much the same spirit, the machinery refuses to turn. Suppose a novel, otherwise realistic, describes a merchant who cheats an orphan out of her inheritance, and the narrator speaks of this act as if it were plainly, gloriously just — not as an opinion held by this or that character, but as a stipulated feature of the story, the way "the moon is cloth" is stipulated. I do not quietly accept that in the story-world the cheating is just. I find myself instead silently rewriting the scene: the narrator must be unreliable, or the surrounding society must have a corrupt sense of justice, or the word "just" must be working in some ironic or private way. It feels as though the author can redecorate the physical world of the story by fiat but not its moral shape.

This has set me thinking about where the line runs and why. The puzzle is not simply that I find the deviant evaluation repugnant: in cold argument I can and do say "suppose, for the sake of discussion, that the merchant's cheating was just," and I can reason forward from that supposition without any of the same obstruction. Something different goes wrong when an imagined world is supposed to make it true inside the fiction. I have also noticed two complications that seem important. First, the resistance largely disappears when the story itself marks the deviant attitude as belonging to a villain, a fool, or a strange subculture whose outlook the narration flags as wrong; I can then occupy that attitude quite comfortably, because it is being held before me as furniture rather than being demanded of me. Second, I accept a radically different evaluation without any stumble when the story alters the underlying non-moral facts so that the evaluation follows from them: if the merchant's "cheating" is a ruse that shields the orphan from a poisoned inheritance, then calling it just causes no trouble at all. The resistance is sharpest when the facts of the scene stay recognizably the same and the evaluation alone is sent off course. I would like a careful explanation of what this phenomenon is and what drives it: whether the obstruction is better described as an unwillingness to imagine or an inability to do so, why descriptive impossibilities enter an imagined world so much more easily than deviant evaluations do, and what this asymmetry reveals about the way imagination is tied to our actual moral outlooks.
