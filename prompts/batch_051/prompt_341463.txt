I am preparing a chapter that uses counterfactual biography to clarify the conceptual commitments of early modern philosophers, and the section I need from you is a prediction rather than a summary. Give me your best considered estimate of what Montaigne would have made of the debate that erupted among anthropologists and philosophers after the Second World War over whether a universal declaration of human rights is defensible given the immense diversity of human customs, or whether such a declaration inevitably dresses one culture's mores in the language of nature and imposes them on the rest. I want the prediction to be built out of the actual argumentative machinery of the essay on cannibals, not out of a slogan like relativism or universalism: the reversal of perspective by which the supposedly civilized Europeans begin to look like the more barbarous party, the observation that people take their own customs as the standard and call whatever diverges from them by the name of barbarism, the comparison between ritual cannibalism and the cruelty of European torture and wars of religion, and the steady mistrust of custom as an authority. Where the modern case is continuous with his sixteenth-century examples, say so; where the analogy strains or breaks, say that too, and indicate what positions would be out of character for him just as much as which would be in character. Write the response in continuous prose, as a serious chapter section or a long letter to a colleague would be written: no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italic text, no tables, no citations, no bibliography, and no verbatim quotations; paraphrase any textual material instead. Aim for something in the neighborhood of fifteen hundred words, and use American spelling and punctuation.
