I've been teaching the brute luck / option luck distinction in my graduate seminar and every year I end up reconstructing the so-called harshness objection to luck egalitarianism on the board. I want to check whether my reconstruction is actually valid, because I half-suspect the move my students find most intuitive is the one doing all the sneaky work. Here's the argument, numbered the way I present it:

(1) Luck egalitarianism says inequalities that come from brute luck—unchosen circumstances, natural misfortunes, the family you're born into—are unjust and call for redress, while inequalities that come from option luck, meaning the outcomes of freely chosen gambles, are not unjust and create no claim of justice to compensation.

(2) Take someone who is fully informed, under no coercion, and with no special reason to think they're protected from loss, who puts their entire life savings into one volatile stock and loses everything.

(3) Their resulting poverty is about as pure a case of option luck as you can get: the outcome of a deliberate gamble they chose to take.

(4) So luck egalitarianism tells us their being worse off than others isn't unjust, and they have no justice-based claim on any share of the rest of us.

(5) But any moral theory that would leave such a person with no claim whatsoever—not to a rescue, not to a share—is morally unacceptable.

So luck egalitarianism is false, or at least badly incomplete. Is this argument valid, and if not, exactly which step fails? My own hunch is that step 4 quietly slides from "this inequality isn't unjust" to "this person has no claim to anything," which is a scope shift, but maybe the view really is committed to both. I also worry step 1 isnt a fair statement of the actual view: Dworkin's version builds in hypothetical insurance, so the stock gamble in step 2 might not be the right kind of option luck until we know what someone in that position could have insured against. Give it to me straight.

Answer in exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no introduction and no conclusion: (1) whether the argument as a whole is valid, (2) whether step 1 is a fair statement of luck egalitarianism and how to fix it, (3) whether step 2 sets up option luck the right way, (4) whether the move from step 3 to 4 goes through, and (5) whether step 5 is true and whether the conclusion follows once the earlier steps are repaired.
