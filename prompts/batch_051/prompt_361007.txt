I am currently taking a course on the philosophy of artificial intelligence, and we have been working through Turing's 1950 paper Computing Machinery and Intelligence. One section has been bothering me more than the rest. In his discussion of extra-sensory perception, Turing does not dismiss telepathy as a fringe worry. He treats the statistical evidence for it as too strong to ignore, and he suggests that the imitation game would need to be administered in a room shielded against telepathy so that a human contestant could not draw on a channel no machine could share. This departure from the paper's otherwise austere tone strikes me as strange.

I have also been reading about how mid-century parapsychology has aged. Under modern controls, preregistration, and meta-analysis, the effects reported in the period Turing was drawing on have mostly shrunk or failed to replicate, and that literature is now a standard case study in how publication bias and flexible analysis can produce apparently overwhelming statistical findings. I am not asking whether parapsychology has any residual credibility; my question is about what happens to Turing's argument.

I suspect many readers treat the ESP passage as a curiosity that can be dropped without cost, because an objection that is empirically false is simply removed. But I am not sure that is right. The passage seems to me to be doing at least two things: it shows how the imitation game is meant to be insulated against any channel other than the question-and-answer exchange itself, and it reveals a standard of evidence under which Turing was willing to revise ordinary scientific commitments in response to statistical anomalies. Does Turing's treatment of ESP rest on a premise that the later replication failures undercut, and if so, does the overarching argument of the paper survive? Which premise is under the most pressure, and is the damage local or structural? If the damage is local, say exactly what the test loses when the passage is deleted. If it spreads, say what a repaired version of Turing's argument would have to establish in order to regain what was lost.
