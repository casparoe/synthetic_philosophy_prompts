University research ethics committees routinely approve studies that deceive participants. A subject may be told that she is taking part in a study of word recall when the experiment is actually about social conformity; a fellow participant may be an actor; the stated purpose of the study may be a cover story. The committee treats the participant's agreement as valid informed consent even though key features of the interaction are misrepresented, provided the committee judges that the deception is necessary for the research, that the research involves no more than minimal risk, and that the participant is debriefed afterward. The same committees treat consent as plainly invalid when it is obtained by threats, when the person is intoxicated or cognitively impaired, and as suspect when participants are offered payments large enough to count as undue inducement. What theory of valid consent does this pattern of practice presuppose? In particular, reconstruct the implicit account of what deception does and does not do to the voluntariness or content of a person's agreement, and explain how the practice can coherently hold that being misled about the nature of the study is compatible with valid consent while being pressured by a threat or a large inducement is not. Say where the practice is actually committed to a particular theory, where it is merely compatible with several, and what would have to change in research oversight if the governing principle were that consent is valid only when the person has understood all the material features of what will in fact be done.
