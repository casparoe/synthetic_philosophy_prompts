You are a philosopher who works on social choice theory and the rationality of group decisions. I am trying to get clear about how a multi-member court or committee should aggregate its members' judgments when the questions it must answer are logically connected, and I would like a consistency audit of the position I find myself holding. Please work through the commitments carefully before you write, and then present the audit in continuous prose.

The background case is the standard three-judge discursive dilemma. A court must decide a breach-of-contract claim in which three things are in play: whether there was a binding contract, whether the defendant breached it, and whether the defendant is liable. The doctrine connecting them is that the defendant is liable if and only if there was both a binding contract and a breach. One judge accepts contract, breach, and liability. A second accepts contract but rejects breach and liability. A third rejects contract but accepts breach and rejects liability. Each judge is individually consistent; there is no irrational judge. Yet taken proposition by proposition, the majority accepts contract, accepts breach, and accepts the connecting doctrine, while the majority rejects liability.

Now here are the commitments I find myself holding about what the committee should do, not just in this case but in general.

(1) The committee's published findings should be logically consistent. It should never assert that there was a binding contract, that it was breached, and that the doctrine entails liability under those conditions, while also asserting that the defendant is not liable.

(2) Each contested question on the agenda should be settled by a simple majority of the members voting on that question. No supermajority requirement, no chair's tie-break, no weighting by expertise; majority on each question is what treats every member's judgment as counting equally.

(3) The agenda should not be gerrymandered to avoid trouble. If a decision genuinely turns on the contract question and the breach question, the committee should address both rather than arranging the agenda so that only the final verdict receives a vote and the awkward premises disappear from the record.

(4) When the majority's views on the subsidiary questions point one way and the majority's view on the final verdict points another, the committee should follow the majority on the final verdict and adjust the subsidiary findings to match. The verdict is what the parties are actually bound by, and no one should win or lose because subsidiary majorities happen to line up in a way that a majority of the committee would reject as the outcome.

(5) The committee is not a mind. Talk of what the committee believes or reasons is shorthand for individual votes plus the rules used to aggregate them, so my consistency requirement is a discipline on the official record, not a demand that some collective agent reason coherently.

(6) The outcome should not be path-dependent. If the members cast exactly the same judgments, it should not matter whether the chair puts the premises first, the verdict first, or the doctrine first, nor whether one aggregates from the bottom up or from the top down. A procedure whose result changes with the order or framing of the questions is arbitrary.

(7) The procedure should be robust across the full range of individually consistent votes the committee might actually cast. We cannot avoid the three-judge pattern by hoping it simply never arises.

My question is whether these commitments hang together. I do not want a survey of the field or a lecture on what particular authors think; I want an audit of my own package. For every pair of commitments that is in genuine tension, tell me how severe the tension is and what would have to give to resolve it. For any pair that only appears to conflict, say why the appearance is misleading. Then rank the genuine tensions by severity, and state which single revision would remove the most conflict at the least cost to what I want the committee's decisions to look like.

Please write the audit as continuous prose, as if you were emailing me. Do not use headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
