I'm a novelist, so I eavesdrop on arguments for material. Last week at my writing group, two of the other members, Rita and Marcus, got into it during the break, and I wrote it down as close to thier exact words as I could.

Rita started by saying she'd been reading about motivation and had come around to the view that we're all selfish all the way down, we just tell ourselves prettier stories. She used a scene from my manuscript as the example: my character Ana sits with a dying man she barely knows, and Rita said the scene rang false, because Ana is getting something out of it—a sense of being good, a story to tell, relief from the guilt she'd feel if she stayed home. She said if you trace any apparently unselfish act far enough, you find the actor collecting some benefit, even if it's only feeling like the kind of person who helps.

Marcus said she was stretching "selfish" until it broke. If selfish just means doing what you most want to do, then wanting a stranger not to drown is selfish in the same sense as wanting their wallet, and the word no longer does any work. He said the interesting thing is what the person wants, not that they want it.

Rita said fine, the word is loose, but the machinery is the same: an action only happens because the actor expects it to feel better—or hurt less—than the alternative. Another person's good can be part of what feels better, but it's still running on the actor's own preferences.

Marcus said that's exactly the slide he was objecting to. "Feels better" can be a consequence without being the goal. He brought up the warm glow: people who volunteer in order to get the warm glow usually stop feeling it, which suggests the glow depends on genuinely aiming at the other person's good. So the so-called selfish story keeps moving the target whenever it's pressed.

Rita said maybe the glow is just hard to fake on purpose; the point is that people help when the pull outweighs the cost, and the pull is still something in them. Then she added that, as a working rule for writers, she'd still give every character some self-interested reason in the background, because readers call a character unrealistic otherwise. Marcus said that's a claim about reader conventions, not about human minds, and that if everyone is selfish in the only sense she can defend, then calling someone selfish stops being a criticism, which would be a strange result.

Then Rita said: give me one action that isn't explained by the agent preferring to do it. Marcus said preferring isn't self-interest, it's just agency. If preferring the other person's good counts as selfish, you've defined altruism out of existence before the argument even starts.

At that point someone changed the subject. I went home and tried to outline a scene around their argument, but I realized I can't tell, step by step, who had the better of it. Could you go through the exchange and say who was right about which specific point, where each of them overreached, and what both of them were missing about the difference between claiming people always do what benefits them and claiming people should?
