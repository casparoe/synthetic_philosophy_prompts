Question: "Linguists often say their approach to grammar is descriptive, not prescriptive. Explain the distinction, and then say whether there is any sense in which a usage like 'I ain't got none' or using 'literally' as an intensifier is bad grammar."

Student answer: "Prescriptive grammar tells people how they ought to speak: don't split infinitives, don't end sentences with prepositions, use 'whom.' Descriptive grammar just describes the rules people actually follow, even the unwritten ones. Descriptivists point out that every native speaker has an internal grammar, so forms that look like errors, like 'ain't' or double negatives, are actually following the rules of the speaker's dialect. In many dialects 'I ain't got none' doesn't mean a positive; the negative concord is regular. So scientifically there is no such thing as bad grammar: a sentence is only grammatical or ungrammatical relative to a speaker's internal grammar, and any sentence a native speaker actually says is grammatical for them. Since millions of people use 'literally' as an intensifier, it's grammatical, full stop. The rules of Standard English are just conventions, not better or worse than the rules of any other dialect, so calling one usage worse than another would be descriptively wrong. Prescriptivists treat those conventions as natural law, but that's really just a social attitude, not a linguistic fact."
