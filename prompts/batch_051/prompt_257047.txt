Two people on a Discord server I lurk on got into a long argument about calibration drills — you know, the thing where you guess a percentage for a bunch of trivia questions and then check how good your guesses were. Ray said it's like katas in martial arts: the drill looks nothing like a real fight and nobody thinks you'll throw a perfect front kick in the street, but the point is to train the underlying habits — balance, focus, committing fully — so they're there when you need them. He said calibration drills train the underlying habit of noticing your own overconfidence, and that will leak into real life even though the questions themselves are just trivia.

Dana said that's exactly where the martial-arts analogy breaks. In martial arts, katas are famous for not transferring to actual sparring; what transfers is sparring itself. By that analogy, the only thing that improves real reasoning is reasoning under realistic, messy, high-stakes conditions — not guessing percentages about, I don't know, baseball attendance. She also said "noticing you don't know" is about as context-bound as a skill gets: it's easy to be humble on a trivia question with instant feedback and nothing at stake, and that tells you almost nothing about a breakup, a career decision, or an argument with your family.

Ray came back and said she was holding the practice to an impossible standard. Musicians do scales, he said, even though nobody confuses scales with a performance; the transfer is small but real, and "leak" doesn't have to mean always — a small shift in your base rate is already worth having. He also said rationality practice has its own version of sparring: good-faith argument, postmortems after bad decisions, forecasting tournaments. Those do look like the real thing.

Dana said forecasting tournaments are still low-stakes and scored by somebody else, which is exactly when it's easiest to switch off self-deception. The problem isn't that drills never transfer, she said; it's that they preferentially transfer to the easy cases where your already motivated to be accurate, and they leave the hard cases — where your identity is invested in being wrong — basically untouched. That's why people who've done a ton of this stuff still rationalize their politics and their personal lives like everyone else.

Ray said that last point is question-begging, because "a ton of this stuff" is doing the work: a martial-arts student who does katas but never spars also loses street fights. The fair question is whether drills are a useful part of a larger system, not whether they work on their own.
