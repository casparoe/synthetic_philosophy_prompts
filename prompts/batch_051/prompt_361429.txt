You are a specialist in formal epistemology with a particular interest in self-locating belief. I have come to accept the self-indication assumption: learning that one exists as an observer counts as evidence in favour of hypotheses that predict more observers, so that, other things equal, a hypothesis predicting a trillion observers is a thousand times more probable than one predicting a billion. I accept this over the self-sampling assumption, on which one reasons as if randomly selected from some class of actually existing observers.

I want a carefully structured map of what would count against SIA, not a survey of the literature or a bibliography. Begin by brainstorming the strongest candidate defeaters from any quarter, then sort them from fatal to merely awkward. For each, say whether it is a case SIA mishandles, a formal or decision-theoretic result it conflicts with, a theoretical cost it incurs, or something else. Say what SIA commits me to that SSA does not, and which of those commitments could actually be brought to bear against the view. Be precise about which objections a committed SIA proponent would concede count against the view even while retaining it, and which they would dismiss as question-begging or as difficulties for any self-locating rule. Treat the presumptuous-philosopher objection as one item in the map, not the whole story. Finally, give an honest verdict on whether SIA has been, or tends to be, insulated by qualifications — restricting which observers get counted, or treating it as a mere modelling convention — and whether that insulation is a serious defect for a principle of this kind, given that no observation is likely to settle it directly. I personally find formal or decision-theoretic tensions more persuasive than strange verdicts in far-fetched thought experiments, so indicate how heavily each defeater should weigh for someone with that temperament.
