After I retired from materials science I started sitting in on a philosophy of technology seminar at the local university, and one thing keeps bugging me. Whenever the seminar turns to technological determinism and whether a technology has politics — social media, nuclear power, highway overpasses — somebody eventually says "guns don't kill people, people do" or "technology is just a tool," and the conversation just stops, as if that settles it. I spent a career around instruments and lab equipment, so I'm suspicious of the plain instrumentalist reading, but I don't have the philosophical vocabulary to say exactly why.

I'd like you to take these two slogans one at a time and seperate out the distinct precise claims each could be making. For each reading, tell me whether it's a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or something genuinely false, and say which reading is actually doing the argumentative work when someone uses the slogan to end a debate about whether a technology has politics. For instance, is "guns don't kill people, people do" a claim about causal responsibility, moral responsibility, the neutrality of artifacts, or something else? Is "technology is just a tool" denying that tools constrain or nudge human behavior, or only denying that they fully determine it? My own hunch is that "just a tool" trades on sliding from "does not fully determine" to "does not matter," but I'd like to see the menu before I press that point. Lay the readings out like a menu I can bring back to the seminar, and please write the answer in American English.
