I am a third-year student in a philosophy of medicine course, and I would like help pinning down the philosophical commitments behind a practice I used to take for granted: informed consent. As it is usually stated, the doctrine requires that before a patient undergoes a significant intervention, a clinician must disclose the diagnosis, the nature and purpose of the proposed treatment, its material risks and benefits, and any reasonable alternatives, and must then obtain the patient's voluntary agreement. The usual justification is that this protects patient autonomy by letting patients make decisions that reflect their own values.

But our course recently covered the idea that some experiences are transformative. Living with an amputation, taking a medication that alters mood or cognition, adapting to a surgically changed body, or becoming responsible for a child can change what one cares about and how one weighs outcomes, in ways that cannot be fully simulated in advance. Many people who have undergone such changes say that no amount of prior description could have told them what the experience would be like from the inside. If that is right, the following puzzle seems unavoidable. Informed consent appears to presuppose a picture of the consenting person as someone who can survey alternative futures, assign reasonably stable values to them, compare them, and choose. But for transformative decisions, the values one would use to make the comparison are among the things the experience itself can alter. And yet we do not suspend informed consent for these decisions; we usually regulate them more heavily, insisting all the more strongly that the patient's choice be an informed one.

Please answer this question: on the most charitable reconstruction, what view of practical reasoning—and of the self as the bearer of preferences across time—does the doctrine of informed consent presuppose? Distinguish what the doctrine strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Then say whether that view can be made compatible with the possibility of transformative experiences, and if not, what would have to change in consent practice, such as the structure of the consent conversation, the timing of decisions, the role of advance directives, or the weight given to a person's preferences after the change, if the implicit view were replaced. Keep the answer to at most 1,000 words, and write it in British English.
