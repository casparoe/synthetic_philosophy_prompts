For the past several months I have chaired a historical commission preparing a report on the causes of a fatal bridge collapse in our region in 1912. I spent much of last evening in precisely the argument our commission keeps having, and I want to turn it into a document we can actually vote on.

The commission's researchers agree that three independent conditions converged: a foundation design now known to have been inadequate for the riverbed; a long period of deferred scour repair around the piers; and an unusually heavy spring flood shortly before the collapse. They further conclude that the failure was overdetermined: each of these three conditions was, in the actual circumstances, sufficient on its own to bring the bridge down, and none was strictly necessary, since the other two would almost certainly have produced the collapse even in its absence.

Half the commission holds the position my friend defended. If every candidate cause was sufficient and none was necessary, then any ranking among them is arbitrary, and a report that pronounces one of them the principal cause has stopped doing history and started doing politics. On this view, the report should present the collapse as jointly caused and decline to rank the causes. The other half replies that our enabling statute requires the report to identify the principal causes, and that our recommendations about design standards, inspection policy, and public memory will be empty if we refuse to say which causes mattered most. I am caught between the two.

Please act as an expert on causation and explanation in historical inquiry. Before drafting, take some space to work through the available positions and indicate which you considered and rejected. Then draft the short written opinion I will circulate ahead of our meeting. It should recommend a concrete, voteable policy for the report's causal chapter: how to handle sufficient versus necessary conditions, and how to select and define principal causes if you recommend doing so. It should engage honestly with the strongest consideration on the losing side, including my friend's worry that causal selection is nothing more than political interest in scholarly dress. End with a single recommended motion I can put to a vote.
