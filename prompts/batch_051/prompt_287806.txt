I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm preparing a feature on how a science of consciousness should handle what people say about their own experience. The reporting keeps returning to a single tension, and I'd like you to work through it with me rather than hand me a winner.

On one side is a theory I find genuinely attractive: heterophenomenology, the method associated with Daniel Dennett. As I understand it, the method says the study of consciousness should begin from subjects' verbal reports, but should treat those reports as data to be interpreted from a third-person point of view. The subject is described as the author of a kind of fiction about their own mind, and the investigator's job is to catalogue and explain that fiction, not to accept it as an authoritative window into a private inner theater. The scientific virtues are exactly the ones I want in a working research program: the method is publicly checkable; it does not ask one person to feel another person's feelings; it refuses to privilege anyone's introspection; and it gives a clean division of labor between what subjects claim and what the science is meant to explain. Without something like it, I do not see how the study of consciousness avoids collapsing back into untestable first-person storytelling.

On the other side is an intuition I also cannot shake. When I burn my hand and say that I am in pain, the report does not feel like a fiction I am authoring about myself, and it does not feel like data whose real significance is fixed only by a third-person interpreter. It feels like the best, perhaps the only, access anyone has to something that is actually happening: there is something it is like for me, and my saying so is part of how that fact shows up. A method that brackets that authority, or calls the report a fiction, can look as though it has set the target phenomenon aside before the investigation even begins. If consciousness is precisely the first-person fact such reports point toward, then a method that refuses to point with them may purchase rigor by quietly studying something else.

I am not asking you to declare which side should win. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes — for example, whether it can be debunked as a leftover of Cartesianism or as an introspection illusion — and what it would take for the method to be amended or abandoned — for example, by giving first-person reports an epistemic status stronger than fiction, or by adding a first-person methodology alongside the third-person one. I also want to know whether there is a division of labor under which both survive: the method's neutrality as a scientific policy, combined with the recognition that there is a real first-person fact that is simply not a datum in the way I keep imagining.

Throughout, please separate what is actually settled about the method's commitments from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and avoid presenting a contested reading as the consensus.
