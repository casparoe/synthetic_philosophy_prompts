Until recently I would have called this question merely academic, but I now sit on the board of a small company whose main product is an automated negotiation system, and our solicitor has raised the possibility of naming the system itself, rather than the company that operates it, as a party to certain licences. The standard argument for the move runs by analogy to corporate personhood: the law already treats corporations, which are neither biological nor conscious, as persons for many legal purposes; legal personhood is therefore better understood as a bundle of capacities than as a status earned by having a mind, and there appears to be no principled obstacle to extending that status to an AI system.

The difficulty is that the corporation which makes the analogy work is a thoroughly entity-like construct: it is registered, capitalised, has owners and officers, holds assets that can be attached, and persists through changes of membership. The systems now proposed for AI personhood are quite different. A large language model is a set of trained weights that exists in many copies at once, is updated in place, cannot itself hold property or be represented, and can be deleted, merged, or superseded without anything answering to corporate continuity. I would like to know whether the corporate-person argument survives this contrast. Specifically, which premise does the contrast actually threaten? Is there a repaired version of the argument that yields something weaker but still significant — for instance, that an AI could be a legal person only once it has been given an entity-like wrapper with registered boundaries, capital, and a designated representative — or does admitting that the scaffolding is necessary mean the analogy was doing no real work after all?
