I am teaching Le Guin's "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" in an undergraduate seminar this term, and I want to run the discussion through a particular debate rather than treat the story as a stock fable about utilitarianism: the debate over whether rights are trumps over the general welfare, side constraints that may not be infringed no matter how much good results, or instruments for protecting interests—and whether the concept of a right is even needed to say what is wrong with Omelas. The question has recently become personal for me. I have joined a county ethics board that must decide whether to approve a public-health policy that would impose a predictable and serious burden on a small identifiable group in order to secure a modest benefit for everyone else, and our disagreement has turned out to be exactly a disagreement about what rights are and whether rights-talk clarifies or obstructs the decision.

With that in mind, I would like a reading of the story through this lens. Does the story treat what is done to the child as the violation of a right that cannot be outweighed, as an impermissible sacrifice that the happy citizens rationalize into a permission, or as a kind of systematic wrong that the language of individual rights actually distorts? Which moments should I point my students to—the description of the city's happiness as dependent on the child, the moment of first learning, the reasoning of those who stay, the ones who walk away in silence? More specifically, does the story take a position on whether the child's treatment is wrong because it offends a trump or because it crosses a side constraint, or does it suggest that neither framing captures what the walkers understand and the stayers manage not to? Where does the story resist the rights reading, and where does it enrich the philosophical debate in a way that the conceptual distinctions alone do not? I am asking for criticism faithful in both directions: do not reduce the story to an illustration of the theory, and do not distort the theory to fit the story; end with what the story adds to philosophical argument about rights that argument alone tends to miss.
