For a class on moral and legal philosophy I have been collecting cases in which institutions treat a person’s “yes” as valid or invalid, and I have hit an inconsistency I would like laid out carefully. At my university’s teaching hospital, a patient can consent to surgery after a frightened, painful conversation in which they admit they only partly followed the explanation; a research participant can consent to a deception study whose actual purpose is deliberately withheld from them, provided a review board has approved the withholding; and a healthy volunteer can be paid an amount large enough that several students said they could not realistically decline, yet this payment is officially classified as compensation rather than undue influence. By contrast, under the same university’s sexual misconduct policy, an intoxicated student’s verbal agreement is not consent, and a yes obtained through a material deception — that one is unmarried or has a relevant infection, for example — strikes most people I have asked as not consent at all. Further afield, a software license nobody reads still binds the person who clicks “I agree.” Before I conclude that these practices are simply incoherent, I want to know whether a single defensible account of what makes consent valid — understood in terms of deception, coercion, and competence — could make them hang together.

Please reconstruct what each of these practices appears to presuppose: what must consent be for it to count as valid despite an unread contract, despite an approved deception, despite a large inducement, and yet not valid, or valid only faintly, in the case of the intoxicated student? Distinguish what the practices strictly presuppose from what they merely accommodate, and identify the points at which they are philosophically underdetermined, so that more than one theory would license them. Then choose one clear rival theory — for example, that valid consent requires actual understanding, or that any material deception invalidates consent, or that consent is valid whenever a competent person is not coerced — and say concretely which of the practices above would have to change if that theory were adopted.
