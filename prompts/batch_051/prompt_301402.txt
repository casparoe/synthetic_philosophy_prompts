In the Enchiridion chapter 3 Epictetus says: "If you kiss your child, or your wife, say that you only kiss things which are human, and thus you will not be disturbed if either of them dies." But in chapter 11 of the same book he tells us that when a child or spouse dies, or when your property is taken away, you should treat it as giving the thing back rather than as losing it. These look like different moves: the first has you revise how you see the person ahead of time so the loss won't disturb you; the second has you revise how you describe the event after it happens. Are they actually in tension, or is the second just a spelling out of the first? More importantly: if the first works, are you actually loving the person, or just loving the category "human"? And if the second is just a redescription of the event, isn't it compatible with all the grief it was supposed to remove? I want the candidate reconciliations, and a verdict on which one makes Stoic apatheia look like a livable ideal rather than a cheat. Which reading would Epictetus have rejected as special pleading, and why?
