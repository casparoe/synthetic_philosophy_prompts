I have long treated the Bayesian result known as Good's theorem as a rare fixed point in decision theory: for an expected-utility maximizer facing a fixed set of acts and states, costless information carries nonnegative expected value, because the decision maker can always ignore the signal and act exactly as she would have without it. I have also drifted into treating this as a general norm: refusing free information is at worst neutral, and the presumption that more evidence cannot hurt should be the default across decision contexts.

I want to know how exposed this whole position actually is, and I want the answer to separate three layers rather than blur them. First, the formal theorem itself: which conditions does it lean on, and what would the world, or the agent, have to look like for each condition to fail? Second, the informal extension from the theorem to the claim that a rational agent should generally welcome free information: where does the extension outrun the mathematics? Third, the practical translation of that extension into transparency defaults of the sort that say one should show everything and disregard what turns out to be unhelpful. For each layer, I want defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to separate escape routes that are genuinely live from ones that are merely logically possible.

I am especially interested in four candidate defeaters, which I can only sketch and would like assessed precisely. (a) Strategic settings: when my information changes what others believe I know, can extra information lower my payoff even if my own decision rule is exactly Bayesian? (b) Belief-dependent utility: if what I believe is itself an argument of my utility, as with anticipatory anxiety, hope, or self-image, has the theorem already been left behind, and does that count as a counterexample or a change of subject? (c) Modeling choice: Good's theorem fixes a state space in advance; if information expands what I am aware of and thereby expands my decision problem, is the theorem silent rather than wrong? (d) Non-expected-utility agents: are there coherent choice procedures, not mere irrationality, under which free information has negative value, and which of them, if any, should a Bayesian find threatening rather than dismiss as out of scope?

Finally, I want an honest assessment of whether the position, as I have stated it, has been insulated by its idealizations: whether the claim that one can always ignore the signal is doing all the work, and whether that is a legitimate guardrail or a refusal to risk anything. Tell me what would genuinely move me, what the position's own defenders should concede counts against it, and whether any of those concessions would force us to give up more than the slogan.
