I've spent the last few weeks trying to design a habit app with my roommate, and the whole concept was to rank everything you might do today by how much pleasure you expect it'll give you, then just do the highest-ranked stuff. Predictably, the test users who leaned into it got less happy, not more, and I ended up reading alot about the paradox of hedonism: the harder you aim at pleasure or happiness directly, the worse your chances of actually getting it, since it mostly shows up as a side effect of doing other things.

Reading around online, the standard moves about the paradox that I could find are: (1) the side-effect or byproduct move, where you accept that pleasure is a byproduct and aim at the activity instead; (2) the Sidgwick-ish move that a theory of what's good and a method for what to do are different, so you can believe pleasure is the metric while deliberately thinking about something else; (3) the move that says pleasure is always pleasure taken in something, so aiming at pleasure in the abstract isn't even a coherent target; (4) the hedonic treadmill move, where the real problem is that we adapt to whatever pleasure we get, so direct pursuit is chasing a moving target; and (5) the "so much the worse for hedonism" move, where the whole paradox is treated as a reductio against pleasure being the only thing of value.

But I couldnt shake the feeling there's a sixth move that's actually different, not just a rewording of one of those five, not a special case of one of them, and not a mashup of two of them. What is it? And would it change how I should design the app, or does it fall apart under scrutiny?

Please give your answer as exactly three numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion: the new move, how it differs from the five I listed, and whether it's any good.
