Exam question:

State epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness, and explain the "paradox of phenomenal judgment" as precisely as you can. Then consider the following reply: on the most plausible version of epiphenomenalism, phenomenal states stand in lawful, nomic correlations with the physical states that cause our reports, so our phenomenal judgments can be reliable even if the phenomenal states themselves never cause anything. Is this reply adequate? Defend your verdict.

Student answer:

Epiphenomenalism is the doctrine that physical states cause mental states, but mental states have no causal powers of their own; consciousness is a by-product of the brain, like a steam engine's whistle, which the engine produces but which does none of the work. A phenomenal judgment is a belief or report about one's own experience, such as my belief that I am in pain. The paradox of phenomenal judgment arises as follows. If epiphenomenalism is true, then my belief that I am in pain is itself a physical event, and its causes are purely neural; the pain is not among them. A neural duplicate of me that lacked the pain—if such a duplicate is possible—would form the very same belief. The belief therefore does not depend on the pain, and its occurrence gives me no reason to think that any pain exists. Epiphenomenalism thus undermines the first-person evidence for consciousness that motivates it, and it is self-defeating. The proposed reply, which introduces a lawful correlation between the pain and the physical state that causes the report, fails. A correlation, even a lawful one, is not causation. If the pain does not cause the report, the fact that they happen to travel together is a lucky coincidence, and a lucky coincidence cannot make a belief reliable or justified. The paradox therefore remains, and epiphenomenalism should be rejected.
