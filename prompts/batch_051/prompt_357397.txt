I'm in a grad seminar on democratic theory and we spent a couple weeks on the boundary problem: who gets to be part of the demos, the group whose members make binding collective decisions. I started from the all-affected principle because it seemed like the natural way to draw that line, and then I pushed on it until it seemed to prove that no bounded state can have a legitimate electorate, which is more than I wanted. Heres the argument as I wrote it out.

(1) A collective decision is legitimate only if everyone whose interests it affects has some genuine say in making it.
(2) To affect someone's interests is just to cause some change, however small, in how their life goes.
(3) The major decisions of any modern state — about tariffs, interest rates, border policy, carbon regulation — cause small changes in how life goes for nearly everyone outside it as well as inside it.
(4) So by (1)–(3), the demos for any major state decision is in effect global.
(5) The same logic extends past present humans: future people's interests and many animals' interests are affected by present decisions, so they'd also belong in the demos by (2).
(6) But a demos needs a boundary — some workable distinction between members and non-members — otherwise the machinery of collective decision (who votes, who is canvassed, whose assent counts) can't get off the ground.
(7) The only way to stop (4)–(5) is to say only sufficiently affected interests count, but any cutoff for sufficiently affected looks arbitrary, since affectedness comes in degrees without a natural joint.
(8) So the all-affected principle either collapses into boundaryless cosmopolitanism (plus future people and animals) or rests on an arbitrary cutoff. Either way it stops answering the boundary problem.

That conclusion feels like the argument proved too much, because (1) still seems right and the alternatives in (8) both look like nonstarters. Which step should I reject, and what do I pay for rejecting it? I'd most like to keep (1) in some form, but I don't trust that preference, so I'd rather hear what each candidate rejection actually costs before I commit.
