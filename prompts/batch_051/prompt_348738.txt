I teach at a college that has this in its catalog: "Students satisfy the language requirement by demonstrating proficiency in a spoken language other than English." Now a Deaf student who is a native ASL signer petitions to count ASL as satisfying the requirement. The registrar says no, because the rule says "spoken," and ASL is signed. The student's advisor says that's ridiculous: linguists have long agreed ASL is a full language with its own grammar, phonology (in the visual-manual sense), and native community, so treating it as not-a-language just because it isn't spoken confuses the language itself with the channel it uses. My question is: how should this rule be applied? Walk through what the plain text gives, what the purpose of the requirement gives (and say what you take the purpose to be, since its not in the catalog), what the people who wrote the rule would have said if someone had asked them about sign languages, and what a reasonable reader today would take "spoken language" to include. Then tell me which approach should win, and whether the word "spoken" in the rule is a harmless shorthand for "real language, not just gesture" or an actual mistake about what a language is. As you answer, separate what linguists treat as settled—ASL is a full language, for instance—from what's genuinely contested about how to read the rule, and don't make a contested take sound like the consensus. Keep the whole thing in American English.
