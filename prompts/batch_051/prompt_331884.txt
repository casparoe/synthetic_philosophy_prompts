I’m putting together a handout for an upper-level epistemology course on G. E. Moore’s “Proof of an External World,” and I want a decision tree that sorts students by what they actually think about Moore’s procedure rather than by the label they happen to like. The standard positions—Moorean dogmatism, neo-Moorean externalism, contextualism, skeptical invariantism, Wittgensteinian hinge epistemology—each involve several separable commitments, and students often hold an incoherent mix without noticing. I’m particularly interested in separating two questions that usually get run together: whether Moore’s argument succeeds as a proof of the existence of external things, and whether it is a satisfactory response to the philosophical skeptic. A person might answer yes to the first and no to the second, or reject both on different grounds.

Please write the instrument as a flowchart with about six or seven branch nodes and seven or eight leaves. Begin from Moore’s own procedure in the paper: he holds up one hand, then another, and says, roughly, that here is one hand and here is another, from which it follows that at least two external things exist and therefore that external objects exist; he then insists the proof is perfectly rigorous even though it does not persuade a skeptic who rejects the premise. The diagnostic questions should be concrete and answerable by someone who has never read Moore. I want the nodes to test whether a proof may use premises that the target skeptic would reject; whether the premise that this is a hand can be more certain than any philosophical argument against it; whether knowledge is closed under known entailment; whether the standards governing “know” shift between ordinary and skeptical contexts; and whether the hand-premise is a hinge commitment immune to doubt rather than an evidence-based claim. For each leaf, give the name of the position and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Flag any two positions your tree cannot separate, and diagnose any unoccupied combination of answers that has no natural defender—is it incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored?
