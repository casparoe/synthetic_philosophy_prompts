Here is a reconstructed argument for the language-of-thought hypothesis that I have seen floated in compressed form and would like pressure-tested step by step.

(1) A finite creature can entertain an unbounded range of distinct complex thoughts only by assembling those thoughts from a finite stock of simpler representational constituents.
(2) Assembling complex representations from constituents, in a way that respects the inferential relations among the resulting thoughts, requires rules that operate on the structural form of the representations.
(3) Rules that operate on the structural form of representations just are formal syntactic rules over the representational vehicles.
(4) So, any finite creature with an unbounded range of complex thoughts must run formal syntactic rules over representational vehicles.
(5) A system that runs formal syntactic rules over representational vehicles, where the permissible combinations determine the meanings of the complexes, is a language of thought.
(6) Humans are finite creatures with an unbounded range of complex thoughts.
(7) Therefore, humans think in a language of thought.

I half-suspect that the argument does not deductively establish what (5) advertises, and that somewhere a claim about the structure of thought contents is being swapped for a claim about the structure of the physical vehicles; but I cannot make the charge stick. There may also be a suppressed slide from 'the only workable architecture we presently know' to 'the architecture any such creature must have'.

Please tell me: is the argument valid? If not, exactly which step fails and why. If it is valid, which premise is the least defensible, and on what precise reading does it become plausible? In either case, state the strongest conclusion that the best reading of the argument could actually support, whether that conclusion still deserves the name 'language of thought', and where precisely a connectionist should enter the argument rather than merely rejecting its conclusion. Please write your answer in British English.
