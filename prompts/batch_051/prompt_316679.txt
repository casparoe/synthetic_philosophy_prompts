I teach a philosophy of science class for undergrads and the unit on confounding/causal inference always falls flat. I want an activity for the day we do Simpson's paradox — the thing where a treatment can beat another in every subgroup and then lose overall once you pool the groups (or the reverse). There are about 20 students and I have 75 minutes. Design a runnable classroom activity, ideally with just index cards (or a shared spreadsheet if cards get unwieldy), where each small group gets data from a made-up medical study comparing two treatments. The crucial constraint: students should first analyze the data seperately within two severity groups, call them high-risk and low-risk, and in both groups Treatment A should come out with the higher recovery rate. They should have to commit — write on a card or the board that A is better for high-risk and better for low-risk — so the pooled result later forces them to eat their own prediction. Only after that should they pool all their own data and recompute the overall recovery rate, where Treatment B should come out ahead. I want the reversal to be driven entirely by the way high-risk patients are unevenly distributed across the two treatments, not by one weird case or a rounding artifact, and I don't want the term Simpson's paradox said until the debrief. The students should notice the contradiction themselves and ideally start arguing about which conclusion is right before I step in.

Give me: step-by-step facilitation instructions with rough timing; a concrete dataset (or a reciepe for generating one) whose numbers actually produce the reversal, with the arithmetic checkable; materials; a debrief that maps the moment they noticed the flip to the difference between better overall and better within each subgroup and to the hidden variable doing the work; what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it — including the student who spots the uneven group sizes in the first five minutes and wants to announce it; and an honest note on what the activity can't teach by itself. My hunch is it can't teach that which number you should act on depends on the causal structure, not the arithmetic (for instance, whether severity is something you should condition on at all), but tell me if that's right and how to supply it in the debrief.
