I am a novelist by trade, and I think about minds through the vocabulary of narrative craft: point of view, free indirect discourse, interiority, character motivation, flat and round characters, the reader's attribution of mental states, and the author's singular authority over what a character believes, wants, or notices. I have only a passing acquaintance with academic philosophy of mind. I am told that the intentional stance, associated with Dennett, holds that we attribute beliefs and desires to a system when doing so is predictively useful, treating the system as a rational agent, and that this attribution carries no commitment to what, if anything, is actually going on inside it. Restate that framework, and the surrounding debate about whether an AI system can be said to actually believe anything, as faithfully as possible in my vocabulary, claim by claim: the predictive stance itself, the shift from a mechanical or design description to a psychological one, the idea that an ascription can be predictively indispensable while still being in some sense merely as-if, and the disagreement between those who take an AI's beliefs to be useful fictions and those who take them to be no less real than our own. Then give me the residue report. Which of those claims translate cleanly into how a novelist thinks about fictional minds? Which translate only with distortion? Which have no counterpart at all, and is the absence a defect of my vocabulary or a sign that the philosophical claim was never as clear as it appeared? In particular, I want to know whether the novelist's act of writing that a character believes something, thereby making it true by stipulation, is the same act as taking the intentional stance toward an AI, or whether one involves something the other lacks. Please do not rig the translation so that fiction comes out looking like proto-Dennett; I am interested precisely in what the translation cannot carry.
