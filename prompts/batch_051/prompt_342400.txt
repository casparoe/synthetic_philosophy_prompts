Robert Nozick, in Anarchy, State, and Utopia (1974), after introducing the experience machine, writes:

"We can continue to imagine a sequence of machines each designed to fill lacks suggested for the earlier machines. For example, since the experience machine doesn't meet our desire to be a certain way, imagine a transformation machine which transforms us into whatever sort of person we'd like to be (compatible with our staying us). Surely one would not use the transformation machine to become as one would wish, and thereupon plug into the experience machine!"

I keep getting stuck on a variant of this. Suppose the transformation machine doesn't just turn you into whatever sort of person you'd like to be, but also rewrites your values so that afterwards you'd genuinely want to plug into the experience machine and wouldn't feel any loss or regret. And suppose a future civilization lets people choose this at, say, 25, before their personalities have fully settled, maybe with the option to pre-commit not to rewrite themselves again. Does Nozick's threefold objection — doing vs. merely experiencing, being someone in particular, contact with a deeper reality — still apply once the "being" concern is handled by the machine? If not, what does that tell us about whether such a future is actually good to live in, as opposed to merely good-feeling from the inside? I don't want the usual "we wouldn't plug in" intuition; I want you to actually run the argument through the variant and say which of Nozick's reasons survive and why.
