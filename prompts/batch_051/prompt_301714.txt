I find myself increasingly committed to an outside-view approach to forecasting the fates of empires and great powers. By the outside view, I mean that one should first place a particular state in one or more reference classes of roughly comparable past states — multiethnic land empires, maritime empires, Chinese dynasties — then take the base rates for how long members of those classes lasted and how they typically ended, and let those base rates anchor the forecast. The inside view, the detailed narrative of palace politics, fiscal strain, and great-power rivalry, should be allowed to nudge the forecast only modestly. My reason for trusting this method is not that the narrative is false, but that a plausible narrative can be generated for nearly any outcome, whereas the base rates are at least an honest summary of how often states of the relevant kind have actually survived or collapsed.

I would like a map of what would count against this position. Which historical cases, methodological considerations, or formal results would genuinely undermine it, sorted from fatal to merely awkward? For each defeater, make clear whether it bites against the selection of reference classes — the reference class problem turned back on the method itself — against the use of base rates for entities that may be historically unique in important respects, or against the assumption that the past supplies an unbiased guide to the future. I would also like to know what this outside view predicts that a sophisticated inside view would not predict, and whether the position is, or is likely to become, so qualified that no historical record could count against it. If it is, tell me whether that insulation is a decisive objection or a common price of forecasting under deep uncertainty. Please answer in American English.
