Every time I teach Williams's integrity objection, I end up stuck on the same conflict, and a colleague and I have been going back and forth about it for years now. The theory side is genuinely compelling: impartial consequentialism says only the quality of the outcome matters, so there's one question to ask of any action, and everything else — including my own commitments — has to show up in the calculation on the same terms as everyone else's. And yet the husband who chooses to save his drowning wife and then explains that he did it because it was what an impartially benevolent agent would do seems, on Williams's telling, to have had one thought too many. I don't think that's a prejudice I'd happily give up; the wrongness feels like it's located in the theory, not just in my sentimentality. My intuition is that a love that runs through an impartial check at the moment it matters isn't love of the right kind, and a theory that asks for that has misunderstood what a person's projects are.

But I keep hearing the consequentialist reply, and it's strong: the theory is a criterion of rightness, not a decision procedure, and the best consequentialist may be exactly the person who doesn't think this at the moment, because cultivating dispositions like love and loyalty is what actually maximizes. I can't tell whether that's a reconciliation or a dodge — the theory still seems to demand that my wife count as one among others at the level where the justification bottoms out, and then to demand that I become blind to that fact. And from the other side, maybe the intuition is exactly the kind of self-flattering partiality that an impartial theory is meant to chasten, not defer to.

So I want you to negotiate this rather than pick a team. Give me exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, no introduction and no conclusion, however cramped that gets. I'd like the points to carry an actual argument: first, the exact point where the theory and the intuition genuinely conflict; second, the strongest reason to think the intuition should yield; third, the strongest reason to think the theory should yield; fourth, the best reconciliation on offer and wether it's a dodge; and fifth, your own verdict, with what would change it.
