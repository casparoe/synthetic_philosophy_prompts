Here is a disagreement I want pressed toward evidence. One philosopher says a law of nature is not a further ingredient in the world on top of events; it is just a regularity that earns its place in our best overall systematization of the history of particular matters of fact, and calling something a law adds nothing to the facts except a compact way of describing them. Another says that misses the heart of the matter: a law is something in the world that binds events, so that the events happen because of it; on this view, whether a generalization is a law is settled apart from how well it happens to summarize the rest of the actual history, and one could, in principle, excise and replace a stretch of the world without thereby changing which generalizations are laws. I want to know whether this dispute can be put in contact with data, even if only in a toy universe.

Design the strongest in-principle test you can of the claim that laws govern rather than merely summarize. The test may be a thought experiment in which we are shown only an initial segment of a toy universe's history and must settle which regularities are laws before seeing the rest, or it may exploit some concrete feature of scientific practice, such as what experimenters do when a long-standing regularity fails under a new intervention and whether they treat the failure as the discovery that the generalization was never a law or as the discovery that the law has a limited domain. Specify exactly what observation would favor the governing view and what observation would favor the summary view. Then say what the test would not settle even if it came out one way rather than the other: which part of the disagreement would remain a matter of framework, convention, or unconstrained choice.
