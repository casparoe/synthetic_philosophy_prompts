I'm the token philosopher in an interpretability lab, and the director has asked me to draft a norm for the whole group, which I'd like you to stress-test before I circulate it. Here's the proposal. Any time someone claims that a component of a model does something — "this attention head is doing indirect object identification," say — the claim has to come with an interpretability chain. The method or tool that produced the claim has to itself be interpreted by an independent method, which gets interpreted by another, and so on, until the chain bottoms out in a check one person can complete by hand in an afternoon with just the weights and a notebook. Each step after the first costs twice the compute of the one before. Walk me through what this does to lab behaviour: which claims actually get made, which projects quietly die, where people game the chain first, and weather the regress ever bottoms out for real or people just declare a human-checkable step that's secretly doing most of the interpretive work. Then tell me if there's a salvageable version of this norm, and what we'd have to give up to get it.
