Since last spring I have led the scenario unit at a mid-sized logistics cooperative, and each quarter we maintain a Strategic Assumptions Register of roughly fifty probabilistic entries on matters outside our control — fuel prices, port congestion, regulatory rulings, competitor moves. The register feeds the five-year plan and the board's capital-approval calendar, so these numbers now steer real money and consistency matters. Six months ago the chief executive asked us to add a new class of entries: probabilities assigned to decisions the cooperative itself will make over the next four quarters — whether to build a second intermodal terminal, whether to exercise an option to acquire a regional carrier, whether to exit a market in which we are losing money.

The request has split my team. One camp says this is incoherent: a choice under active deliberation is not an event to be predicted by the chooser, because assigning a probability to it requires stepping outside your own deliberation and treating yourself as an object, and because the number you publish feeds back into the negotiations and incentives that produce the decision. The other camp says this is overblown: we all carry degrees of confidence about what we will end up doing, writing them down only makes an implicit view explicit, and refusing to record a number is a way of dodging accountability. The disagreement recurs every quarter, and it has begun producing inconsistent entries and testy exchanges.

I want a reusable procedure, not a ruling on any single entry. The deliverable should be a worksheet or an ordered list of questions that a staff member with no exposure to these philosophical debates can run in under an hour, fitting on one page. It should specify: the entry screen that separates forecasts of facts from records of our own choices; for self-involving items, the questions to ask in order, what counts as an adequate answer to each, and where the procedure stops with "not a forecast — record as an intention or commitment instead"; what, if anything, can be substituted when a probability is demanded, such as conditional forecasts given each option or execution-risk estimates after a decision is genuinely made; and a clear statement of the point at which judgment cannot be mechanized and the CFO must decide. It should also say explicitly whether the objection to self-prediction is best understood as impossibility, instability, or self-undermining feedback, since the procedure should track the actual problem rather than a slogan.

Apply the procedure to three proposed register entries: (a) the probability that we decide to build the second terminal before year-end, where the same senior group assigning the probability is the group that will make the choice; (b) the probability that we complete the acquisition signed in a non-binding letter of intent by March, where we have publicly committed in principle but retained a walk-away right; and (c) the probability that our board approves the market exit after it receives the recommendation we are still preparing. Include at least one case where the procedure gives me an answer I will not like, and say plainly what the procedure cannot do about cases like that.
