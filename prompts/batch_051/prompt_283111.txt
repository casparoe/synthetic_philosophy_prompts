I have no formal training in mathematics or computer science, but for a few years I have been thinking on my own about a question I cannot settle: in what sense, if any, can a single finite sequence of numbers or symbols be called random? I understand that any specific finite string could have been produced by some enormously complicated but still finite deterministic rule, and yet most of us still have a strong intuition that one string is random and another is not. I later learned that specialists sometimes call this the problem of algorithmic randomness.

Here is the counterfactual I would like you to reason through carefully. Suppose that in 2035 a small company begins selling a cheap, reliable device that works as follows: the user enters any finite string, and the device prints a single number, namely the length of the shortest program that would cause a fixed, publicly specified reference computer to output exactly that string and then stop. The device does not reveal the program, only its length; in effect, it measures how compressible the string is. I have read that in our actual world no general machine of this exact kind could be built, but for the scenario simply assume that in that world it works and is cheap. By 2050 the device has become widely used. Courts allow its output as evidence in fraud cases about allegedly rigged lotteries and casino machines. A standards body recommends that a finite bit-string may be labeled random only if the device assigns it a length of at least 98 percent of the string's own length. Some insurance companies price policies for electronic gambling platforms partly on the device's scores of past outputs. Meanwhile, a hobbyist demonstrates that for some short strings the number changes noticeably when the reference computer is changed, and that the manufacturers cannot agree on a single standard.

Please predict, with visible reasoning, how that world develops over the following few decades. Which institutions and practices actually change, and which remain largely as they are? What new disputes, attempts at gaming the measure, or auditing methods are likely to emerge? How do thoughtful people in that world deal with the fact that a single past string is still weak evidence about the process that produced it? And ultimately, do you think this device settles the original question of whether a finite sequence can be random, or does it merely move the difficulty to a new place?

Please take the role of a professional philosopher of science who works on probability and computation. For each link in your prediction, indicate whether you regard it as robust or as a guess, and specify what assumption would most likely make it wrong.
