i'm on my university's academic conduct board and we've been handed a case that's splitting us down the middle. a senior registered early for an oversubscribed upper-level seminar — capped at 14, long waitlist — and then sold the spot to a junior for $150. they timed it so the senior dropped the course in the portal right when the junior was first on the waitlist; the system auto-enrolled him, so on paper it looks like an ordinay drop. university policy says course places are nontransferable and students can't buy or sell them, but the rule is buried in registrar fine print and nobody had been told about it. half the board says this is a harmless, consensual transfer: the senior got paid for something she'd secured by planning ahead, the junior got a course he wanted, the professor got a student, and waitlist order is arbitrary anyway, so punishing them would be making a rule out of nothing. the other half says this is exactly what the rule is trying to stop: it turns access to education into a commodity, lets scarce seats go to whoever will pay instead of whoever the waitlist says had the stronger claim, and treats enrollment as something you can hold and flip rather than a commitment to take a class. i've been asked to draft the decision memo. i need a bottom line — no sanction, light sanction, or full penalty for the two students — and a recommendation on the policy itself: keep the ban and start enforcing it, keep it but actually communicate it, or allow a regulated market in seats in overenrolled courses. the memo has to be fair enough to the losing side that a board member who disagrees with the outcome still feels their strongest argument was taken seriously, and it should give us criteria for deciding which scarce university resources are okay to sell and which aren't, so we're not just going on vibes next time.
