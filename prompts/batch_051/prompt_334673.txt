I'm a PhD student working in social epistemology, and there is a dispute in my field that has been running in place for years now — long enough that I've started to suspect the two sides are not quite arguing about the same thing. A now-standard distinction separates an epistemic bubble, in which a person simply lacks exposure to relevant outside sources, from an echo chamber, in which outside sources are not merely absent but actively discredited. The live question concerns what kind of epistemic failure an echo chamber is. One side argues that the chambered agent is best understood as an ordinary, even Bayesian, updater in a corrupted evidence environment. If a person's reliability priors have been shaped by a community that treats mainstream institutions as systematically dishonest, then discounting outside testimony and deferring to in-group sources is just what updating on one's evidence requires; a fully rational agent with those starting points would converge on the same false beliefs. On this view, the failure belongs to the environment that installed the priors, not to the inference. The other side replies that this is an exculpatory caricature of what real echo-chamber members do. They do not merely update on a skewed sample; they manage their own exposure, recruit defeating explanations the moment counter-evidence appears, treat distrust of outsiders as constitutive of good epistemic character, and reinterpret challenges so as to protect the group's core commitments. That, this side insists, is motivated cognition and closed-mindedness — a defect of the agent, not just of the network. Each side has support: formal models of social learning can be made to underwrite the first picture, while the social-psychology literature on motivated reasoning underwrites the second, and the same case — a person deep in an anti-vaccine community, say — can be re-described fluently in both vocabularies. So neither side lands a decisive blow, and the disagreement persists.

My standing hypothesis, which I keep going back and forth on, is that the two camps are working at different levels. The first fixes a trust network, however it was acquired, and asks whether belief revision inside it is rational; the second asks whether acquiring or maintaining that trust network is itself something the agent can be held responsible for. If that is right, both claims could be true at once: an agent can be locally rational relative to a globally corrupt epistemic position, and the real fight is simply pushed a level down, to whether the global defect is best charged to the individual or to the collective system that controls information flow. But I am not sure whether this diagnosis dissolves the debate or merely relocates it. I would like a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this is rather than another partisan defense of one camp. Is it, as I suspect, a levels-of-explanation mismatch that leaves a further substantive question about individual versus institutional responsibility? Is it partly verbal — a fight over the extension of the word "rational" — or partly a difference in standards about what a satisfactory explanation of irrational belief must look like? And, on your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a formal model that separates network-level and update-level rationality, an account of when agents are responsible for their source-trust practices, a change of question, or something else?
