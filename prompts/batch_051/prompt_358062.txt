Lately I've been struck by a parallel between two uses of 'overdetermination,' and I cant tell whether it's deep or just the same word doing different jobs. In philosophy of causation, overdetermination is the two-rocks-hit-the-window case, or the firing squad: each cause is sufficient on its own, all of them occur, and then we're stuck on which one was 'the' cause. But in social or political history people say an event was 'overdetermined' to mean something like: there were lots of factors in play, and if you deleted any one of them the event probably would still have happened. That looks almost like the mirror image of the philosopher's case — no individual cause is necessary because the remaining causes do the work, rather than each cause being sufficient. I'm tempted to formalize the historian's usage as: an outcome is robustly generated by a set of causes such that many subsets are sufficient, and no particular subset is the real one. But maybe I'm just pattern-matching on a shared word. I'd like the mapping checked carefully: where does it line up, where does it come apart, and does anything transfer? E.g., does the firing-squad problem have lessons for how historians talk about conjunctural causation (explaining an outcome by a specific combination of factors, none privileged), or does the historian's usage show that but-for tests ('would Y have happened if not for X?') break down in ways philosophers of causation should care about? Or does the analogy collapse because the two sides mean 'sufficient' differently? And if the mapping is real, say what it would predict that turns out false.
