I just got back from a long argument with a friend about Plato on drunkenness, and I'm honestly stuck. In Plato's Laws, the Athenian defends symposia with this weirdly practical-sounding claim: wine was given to make people more open and less guarded, and so a properly run drinking party becomes a kind of testing ground where you can see what somebody is like when their social armor comes off—and, more importantly, where they can practice pulling themselves back from a state that threatens to strip self-control away. There's a real systematic virtue to this. It explains why a city would allow wine culture at all, it ties drinking to the city's need to know its citizens' souls, and it turns drunkenness from a pointless vice into a controlled psychic stress-test. But my intuition absolutley refuses to go along: if you do the thing drunk, you didn't practice the virtue; you were chemically pushed past the very capacity virtue requires, and calling that moral training sounds like praising a fever for making you practice being sick. So which should yield? Is my intuition just a modern prejudice about intoxication I should debunk, or does Plato's test actually fail as a test of self-control? Or is there a reading where wine only reveals a person's underlying disposition—shamelessness, cowardice, irritability—rather than training self-control, and my intuition is aimed at the wrong target? I want a negotiated verdict, not just a pick-a-side answer: what would justify dropping the intuition, amending Plato's account, or ruling them compatible?
