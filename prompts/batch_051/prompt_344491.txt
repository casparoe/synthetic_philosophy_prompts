I am a novelist at work on a book in which the moral quality of a character's motives, not merely of her actions, is the central concern, and I find that I cannot write the final third of the manuscript until I settle a conceptual question that keeps recurring in the material. I need a working definition of moral worth: the quality an action or an agent has when the action reflects well on the agent, when we are entitled to admire the person for doing it. I suspect this is closely bound up with acting for the right reasons, but I do not know exactly what that locution should be taken to require.

In fiction, the same outward deed can be noble, merely useful, or faintly repulsive depending on why it is done, and I want a definition that will let me render that difference precisely, scene by scene. Here are the cases I need the definition to classify, all of which occur in some form in the book.

A. A woman donates a large sum to fund a hospital wing solely so that her name will be inscribed above the entrance. The wing is built and saves many lives.

B. A man finds an envelope of cash, identifies its owner, and returns it because he judges that it would be wrong to keep what is not his. The thought of being rewarded or well regarded never enters his mind.

C. Another woman also returns a lost envelope of cash. She returns it because she judges it her duty, and she also happens to know that the owner is known to reward finders handsomely. Either motive, on its own, would have been sufficient.

D. A teenager confesses to his parents that he broke a window, but only to escape the guilt he can feel accumulating whenever he lies to people he lives with.

E. A man tells his aging parents the truth about a serious mistake he made, moved entirely by love and by his inability to deceive people he cares for. If asked, he would say that truthfulness in such matters is overrated and probably irrational, and that he is not doing it because it is right.

F. A nurse follows every hygiene and quarantine procedure during an outbreak and saves dozens of patients, but her sole motive is a dread of being fined if an inspector finds a violation.

G. A man works tirelessly to elect a candidate he believes will humiliate a hated rival. The candidate wins and, unexpectedly to the man, the new administration prevents a war. The man would have been indifferent to the war had he known of it.

H. A driver stops at a red light, thereby avoiding a pedestrian who is crossing against the light. She stops purely from long-internalized habit and experiences no thought about the law or the pedestrian.

I. A soldier throws himself on a grenade to save the soldiers beside him, moved by an unreflective impulse that he could not, afterwards, articulate.

J. A judge decides a hard case by applying a principle she memorized in law school and has never reconsidered. The principle is the correct one, but she cannot now explain why it is correct and has never asked.

My present instincts are these: B, E, and I reflect well on their agents; A, D, F, and G do not; and C, H, and J leave me genuinely uncertain. I do not require the definition to ratify my instincts, but I do require it to say plainly where it disagrees with them and why the instinct was misguided.

Specifically, I want from you a definition of moral worth that gives a yes-or-no verdict for each case; an account of what must be true of an agent's motivating reasons for an action to have moral worth; and a clear distinction between moral worth, on one hand, and mere rightness or good consequences on the other. In particular, say whether the agent must be moved by the thought that the action is right, obligatory, or her duty, or whether it is enough to be moved by the very features that make the action right, even if she does not think of them in those terms, or even if she would sincerely disavow the moral description. Finally, tell me which single case the definition is least comfortable with, and what revision of the definition would be needed to accommodate it, or why the definition should refuse to accommodate it.

I am less interested in a label than in a principle that separates the noble deed from the lucky deed and from the merely useful deed. If the best definition makes one of my cases come out oddly, defend the odd verdict rather than silently adjusting the definition.
