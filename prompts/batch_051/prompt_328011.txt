I am a retired evolutionary biologist who has recently begun working through philosophy of mind, and a former colleague who now advises on AI safety has been pressing the following argument on me. He believes it settles, in the negative, the question whether an artificial system could ever be said to want anything.

(1) A system can be said to want X only if the state that directs its behaviour towards X is present because behaviour of that kind contributed to the survival, reproduction, or replication of the system or its ancestors. Call this the historical condition on wanting. (2) An artificial system fails this condition: its goal-states are installed by engineers; they are not present because pursuing those goals helped the system or its ancestors to survive, reproduce, or be copied. (3) Therefore no artificial system ever wants anything, however flexibly it pursues its objective, however much it deliberates about it, or however strongly it resists being shut down. (4) But the historical condition applies to every system, including evolved ones, and it excludes far more than it was meant to. My wanting to learn philosophy, to improve at chess, or to finish this reply was not itself selected for in my lineage; natural selection explains, at most, a general plasticity that made it advantageous for me to acquire and pursue such goals. (5) So, by the same reasoning, I do not genuinely want to learn philosophy or improve at chess; I merely have evolved drives that dispose me to acquire and pursue those aims. (6) That conclusion is false: wanting to learn philosophy is as plain a fact about me as wanting water when thirsty. (7) The argument therefore goes wrong somewhere, but I cannot say where.

My colleague insists the fault lies in (4), because the historical condition was never intended to apply to particular learned contents within a single lifetime; I suspect, without yet being able to defend it, that the fault lies earlier, in (1), because history cannot be what distinguishes wanting from mere goal-directedness. I would sooner abandon (1) than accept that no AI could ever want anything, but then I owe a non-historical account of wanting that still excludes thermostats and cruise control. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Please write your answer in British English.
