I am a retired physical chemist, and only lately have I begun reading the philosophy of science seriously. I find myself continually returning to the pessimistic meta-induction, and I would be grateful for help with a concrete case.

The question I am working with is this: when does the success of a scientific theory license the belief that its central unobservable entities and processes are at least approximately real? I have been using four rough criteria: (1) the theory generated quantitative predictions that were subsequently confirmed; (2) it unified phenomena that had previously looked unconnected; (3) its central theoretical terms did indispensable explanatory work; and (4) it guided further research productively.

Consider, as a test case, the caloric theory of heat as it stood in the early nineteenth century. Caloric was held to be a weightless, self-repellent fluid that flowed from hotter to colder bodies and was conserved in ordinary thermal processes. The theory gave a unified account of thermal expansion, heat capacity, latent heat, and conduction. What impresses me most is that Sadi Carnot used this caloric picture to analyze ideal heat engines, treating heat as a fluid falling between a hot reservoir and a cold one, and thereby arrived at quantitatively correct results about the maximum efficiency such engines can achieve. Yet by mid-century the mechanical equivalent of heat and the conservation of energy persuaded most physicists that heat is not a conserved material fluid at all; caloric, as a substance, does not exist.

Does the caloric theory count as successful under my four criteria? I ask that you go through the criteria one by one. If it does count, what does that imply about my criteria and about the pessimistic thought that today's best-confirmed theories may likewise have central terms that fail to refer? If it does not count, identify exactly which criterion excludes it, and tell me whether that criterion would also exclude parts of present-day physics that we would be most reluctant to surrender.
