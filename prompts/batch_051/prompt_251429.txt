In the churches I know best there is a marked asymmetry in how two claims from this passage are received. Here is the passage, from Paul's first letter to the Corinthians, 11:3–6 (King James Version):

But I would have you know, that the head of every man is Christ; and the head of the woman is the man; and the head of Christ is God.
Every man praying or prophesying, having his head covered, dishonoureth his head.
But every woman that prayeth or prophesieth with her head uncovered dishonoureth her head: for that is even all one as if she were shaven.
For if the woman be not covered, let her also be shorn: but if it be a shame for a woman to be shorn or shaven, let her be covered.

The same communities that treat "the head of the woman is the man" as a trans-cultural order of the sexes—taught, preached, and reflected in their practice—treat "every woman that prayeth or prophesieth with her head uncovered dishonoureth her head" as a first-century convention that no longer binds. Yet in the text the second is not a free-standing rule; it is introduced as a consequence of the first, and the two are woven into a single argument. I want to know whether anything justifies this sorting. Is there a principle that lets one keep the premise while setting aside its application, or is the distinction being drawn ad hoc? If you had to choose between treating the whole passage as culturally specific and treating the whole passage as binding today, which would be the more defensible choice, and why? In answering, test whatever criterion you propose against the other material in the same passage: the appeal to what "nature itself" teaches about long hair, the clause "because of the angels," and the qualification that "neither is the man without the woman."
