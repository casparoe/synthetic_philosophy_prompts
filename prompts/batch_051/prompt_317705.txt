In discussions of corrigibility and the shutdown problem, one analogy recurs constantly: a goal-directed AI that fails to resist being turned off is like a person who fails to resist being killed, because remaining in operation is instrumentally useful for almost any goal, just as remaining alive is. The analogy is doing real argumentative work—it is used to support the claim that shutdown-resistance is the default expectation for any capable goal-directed system, and that corrigibility (roughly, the disposition to allow operators to shut a system down without interference) is therefore the strange state that must be engineered, not the natural one.

I would like you to audit this analogy. How far does it actually hold? Pay particular attention to whether the analogy quietly treats shutdown as permanent death when many shutdown scenarios are more like reversible interruption. Structure your response in the following sections, in order:

1. **The work the analogy is doing** (100–150 words). State precisely which conclusion in the shutdown debate this analogy is being used to license, and why it has persuasive force.

2. **Correspondence table** (150–250 words; a table or explicit list is acceptable). Map the source domain (a human facing death) onto the target domain (an AI facing shutdown). Cover at least: the agent, the threat, the thing being protected, the resisting behavior, and the underlying motive.

3. **Where the analogy holds** (150–200 words). Identify the features that genuinely transfer, especially the instrumental-convergence reasoning that gives the analogy its initial bite.

4. **Where the analogy breaks** (250–350 words). Separate the disanalogies that are mere imagery from the disanalogies that undermine the argument's structure. Address at minimum: permanence versus reversibility; evolved survival drives versus engineered goal structures; whether shutdown obstructs the goal or merely delays it; and whether a system could be designed to want interruption.

5. **Which disanalogies matter** (150–200 words). Say which breaks genuinely undermine the intended conclusion about corrigibility, and which are cosmetic and can be set aside.

6. **Verdict** (100–150 words). State whether the intended conclusion survives the audit, and if the death analogy misleads, propose a better analogy for thinking about corrigible shutdown behavior.
