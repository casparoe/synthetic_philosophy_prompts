Me and some friends got into an argument after a guy in our building sold his old car. The buyer asked if it had engine problems; he said it never left him stranded once, which is technically true, but he knew the head gasket was going and just wanted it gone. A couple of us thought he'd basically lied; others said no, he didn't lie, the buyer should've asked better questions. Then someone pointed out that if he'd answered "no engine problems" outright, even if he half-believed it, we'd all agree he was in the wrong. So we have this strange asymmetry: a plain false statement meant to deceive feels like a serious wrong, but a carefully chosen true statement meant to produce the exact same belief gets treated like cleverness or the buyer's fault.

I'm trying to figure out whether anything actually justifies that asymmetry. The obvious thought is that lying involves saying something false, while misleading only lets someone draw a false conclusion, so the speaker's words are clean and the listener is partly responsible for the inference. But that seems shaky when the speaker deliberately designed the sentence to exploit the listener's assumptions—like a job candidate asked about felony convictions who says "I've never been convicted in this state" knowing his record is from somewhere else. In both cases the person intended the false belief, expected it to come about through their words, and could've just said the true thing. Is there a real moral or theoretical difference between lying and misleading—maybe something about what the speaker vouches for, or how explicit assertion gives the hearer a stronger guarantee—or is the ordinary asymmetry just a bias we've gotten used to, and should we give it up? If you had to say what distinction, if any, the ordinary reaction is tracking, and whether it survives hard cases, what would it be?
