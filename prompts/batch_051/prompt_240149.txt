Question

Does the capability approach succeed in identifying a currency of egalitarian justice that is superior to both welfarism and resourcism, or does it merely relocate the difficulties that afflict those alternatives?

Student's answer

The central question in the debate over the currency of egalitarian justice is what egalitarian institutions should equalise. Welfarists answer that they should equalise welfare, usually understood as preference-satisfaction or happiness. Sen and Nussbaum reject this for two reasons. First, welfarism is open to the adaptive-preferences objection: those in deprived conditions often adjust their expectations downwards and report high satisfaction with very little, so a welfarist metric may treat a deeply unjust distribution as just. Secondly, welfarism attaches normative weight only to how a person feels, and ignores what a person is actually able to do and be, which matters independently of satisfaction.

Resourcism, associated with Dworkin, avoids the adaptive-preferences objection by making impersonal resources, rather than inner states, the currency of equality. But Sen and Nussbaum argue that resources are only a means: people differ in their ability to convert a given bundle of resources into valued doings and beings. A disabled person and an able-bodied person controlling identical resource bundles are not equally placed, because the disabled person may need more resources, or different resources, to achieve the same functionings. Similarly, social norms and unequal divisions of labour can make it harder for women to convert the same income into effective agency.

The capability approach therefore proposes capabilities as the relevant currency, where a capability is a person's real freedom to achieve a functioning. Functionings are states of being and doing, such as being adequately nourished or participating in public life; capabilities are the genuine opportunities to achieve those states. The distinction matters because egalitarians should be concerned with the freedom people have, not with forcing people to make use of that freedom. This is why Sen proposes ten central capabilities as the metric of a minimally just society: a list running from life, bodily health and integrity, and the use of the senses, imagination and thought, through emotional health, practical reason, affiliation, concern for other species, and play, to control over one's environment. This enumeration supplies the approach with a determinate and internationally applicable standard.

However, the capability approach faces serious difficulties. The first is that Sen's list, although meant to give the theory determinate content, seems arbitrary: he offers no argument for why exactly these ten capabilities are selected, or for how conflicts among them are to be weighed. The second is that, once one attempts to measure capabilities, one inevitably falls back on achieved functionings as a proxy; the capability metric is therefore, at the point of application, simply a measure of the functionings people actually attain. But if the metric is in practice a functioning metric, then the approach fails to live up to its own ambition of evaluating real opportunities rather than outcomes.

In conclusion, the capability approach improves on welfarism by rejecting subjective welfare as the currency of equality and improves on resourcism by attending to the conversion of resources into doings and beings; nevertheless, its arbitrary list and its practical collapse into a functioning metric mean that it relocates, rather than resolves, the problems that beset its rivals.
