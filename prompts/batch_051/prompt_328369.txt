Betrayal is a word I use with complete confidence and define with none. I want a working definition — the kind you could apply to a new case and say yes or no without going by the smell of the thing. I have four cases whose answers I'm sure about, and six I keep flip-flopping on. Propose a definition, run it through all ten cases, and flag wherever your definition gives a verdict you're inclined to disown; then either revise the definition or defend the verdict. Be explicit about any cases the definition simply cannot settle, and tell me where the definition surprises you.

My confident judgments: a spouse who conducts a years-long affair and conceals it is betraying their partner; a business partner who quietly copies the firm's client list, takes a job at a rival, and says nothing until the day they leave is betraying their partners; a stranger who watches you drop your wallet and walks past it is not betraying you, whatever else it is; and an employee who resigns with proper notice to take a comparable job at a competitor, carrying away only their own training, is not betraying the employer.

The hard cases, where I want the definition to do real work:

1. An employee discovers their company has been falsifying safety tests on a product and reports it to the regulator. The company says it was betrayed by a trusted insider. Was it?
2. A friend swears you to secrecy about a plan to harm yourself, and you call their family that same night. Most people I respect say that is not a betrayal of the friend, but it plainly breaks a promise to someone who trusted you. Why isn't it betrayal?
3. A political ally campaigned alongside you for a policy and then announces, a week before the vote, that they now oppose it on the merits. No one paid them, threatened them, or turned them against you; they changed their mind. Is that betrayal?
4. A soldier deserts from a war they have come to believe is unjust, leaving their unit without them. People I trust put this in every available category. What should the definition say?
5. A doctor discloses a patient's diagnosis to a public-health authority over the patient's objection because the illness is dangerously contagious. Betrayal of the patient, or fidelity to something that overrides the patient?
6. A researcher publishes results that will demolish a longtime collaborator's central theory, without warning the collaborator before the paper appears. The research community calls this integrity. Was it a betrayal of the collaborator?

The case verdicts matter to me only as a test; the deliverable is the rule. Be specific about which cases the rule handles easily, which it gets wrong, and which it leaves indeterminate.

One more thing. If you think the task is miscast — that "betrayal" isn't one thing and a definition of this kind forces a false unity on it, or that some of my cases are rigged, or that my confident cases are actually the problem ones — say so plainly and tell me what I should have asked instead. I'd rather have that than a tidy definition that papers over the mess.
