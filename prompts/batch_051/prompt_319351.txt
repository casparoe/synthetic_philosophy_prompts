So in my philosophy of mind and psychiatry course we've been doing delusions, and honestly the stuff about whether delusions are really beliefs has me going in circles. The Capgras case keeps coming up: a patient will clearly recognize their spouse's face but say the person is an impostor, and they don't seem to feel the usual affective/emotional charge of familiarity. The two-factor account says you need both an anomalous experience (the missing feeling of familiarity) and some kind of second factor, like a failure to properly evaluate or reject the strange hypothesis; otherwise the person would just say "I feel like my wife is a stranger" instead of "she has been replaced." I get that part. What I don't get is what the second factor commits you to about whether the delusion is a belief.

Could you write a dialogue between a doxasticist about delusions (someone who says the Capgras patient really does believe the spouse is an impostor) and a non-doxasticist (someone who says the patient isn't in that state, maybe it's more like a vivid imagining or an oddly assertive report)? I want both of them to be smart, not one side obviously losing. Make them actually respond to each other about the two-factor theory: does the second factor look like a belief-forming/evaluating mechanism gone wrong, so the delusion is just a badly formed belief, or does the second factor only explain why someone ends up asserting or imagining something they don't fully believe? Also have them wrestle with the fact that Capgras patients often don't act as if the spouse is dangerous or fake, and with the point that the patient's report doesn't just seem like a loose metaphor. Please don't resolve it at the end; I want to see where each side gets forced into saying something awkward.
