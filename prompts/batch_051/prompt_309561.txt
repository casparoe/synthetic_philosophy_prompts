I keep going back and forth on a little argument about the grandfather paradox, and I want someone to check the logic rather than just tell me time travel is impossible for pysics reasons. Here's the argument, roughly:

1. If backward time travel is possible, then this situation is also possible: a traveler is in a room with their own grandfather before the relevant parent is conceived, holding a loaded gun, fully intending to shoot, with no physical or external obstacle to pulling the trigger.

2. In that exact situation, the traveler is able to kill the grandfather — the ordinary sense: means, opportunity, and intent are all there. Saying they can't kill him in that setup feels like changing the subject.

3. If the traveler were to kill the grandfather then, the grandfather would die before conceiving the traveler's parent, and so the traveler would never have existed.

4. If something would have prevented the very conditions of its own occurring, it cannot occur.

5. So the killing cannot occur, which contradicts 2. Therefore backward time travel must be impossible, since it entails a scenario where the traveler both can and cannot kill the grandfather.

I'm fairly sure the conclusion is too quick, but I can't pin down where. Is the argument valid? Is it sound? If not, which step — or which transition between steps — fails, and does it fail differently depending on whether you treat the past as fixed, branching, or self-consistent by law? And is there a coherent way to deny that the traveler can kill him despite the gun, the intent, and the opportunity?

Also, since people fight about this: please seperate what is actually settled from what is genuinely contested among philosophers working on time travel, and don't present one resolution as the consensus when it isn't.
