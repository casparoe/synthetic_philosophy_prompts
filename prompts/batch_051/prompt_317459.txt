I'm the head of underwriting at a small commercial insurer. We write property, marine, and some specialty lines, and every week my underwriters have to quote risks where the loss distribution is genuinely unknown in a weirder way than normal actuarial uncertainty: cyber coverage for a business type we've never touched, a one-off cargo route through a port with no clean loss history, a factory running a process that's only been around a few years. Our practice on these is all over the place. Some underwriters price them as if they're strictly worse than a comparable risk that has a well-modeled loss curve, even when the expected loss looks about the same. Others just wave them through because "everyone else is writing it." I'm fairly sure we're re-inventing the Ellsberg paradox in our book, except my team doesn't know the theory and they woudn't sit still for a lecture on it.

Write me a procedure an underwriter can actually run in under an hour before a pricing meeting. It should separate two questions that we keep blurring: (a) what does the evidence say about the loss distribution, and (b) how much do we not know about the loss distribution. I want a checklist or ordered questions, with what counts as a good enough answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a "no" or a "this needs someone with authority to sign off on a judgment call," and where it says "here the theory is silent and a human has to pick." The aim is a reusable tool, not a one-off ruling.

Then run it on three cases so I can see it working:

1. Flood coverage for a warehouse in a basin where we have fifty years of flood data and a decent model.
2. Cyber coverage for a cloud-based medical records startup with no loss history and no obvious reference class.
3. Spoilage coverage on a refrigerated shipping route we've written for a decade, but where we took an ugly loss last quarter and underwriters are now shying away.

I expect, and want, the procedure to give me an answer I won't like on at least one of those—probably the third. Also tell me what the procedure can't do: where applying the Ellsberg idea to a real underwriting book stops being about ambiguity and starts being about other things (reinsurance friction, monitoring costs, regret, trust in our own data). And say which step of the procedure is most likely to fail in practice, and what evidence—for example, finding that our "ambiguous" risks actually do have fatter tails for structural reasons, or that underwriter hesitation tracks their confidence in the broker rather than anything about missing probabilities—would lead you to revise the procedure or flip your verdict on one of the cases.
