I'm writing the first chapter of a dissertation on Peirce's theory of inquiry, and before I defend a reconstruction of it I want a consistency check on the package I am inclined to attribute to him. I hold all of the following, and for present purposes the question is not whether Peirce actually held them but whether they cohere:

(a) The pragmatic maxim: the whole meaning of a concept consists in the practical consequences its object would conceivably have; two concepts with all the same conceivable practical bearings are the same concept.

(b) Of the four ways a belief can become fixed — tenacity, deference to authority, the a priori preferences of reason, and the method of science — only the method of science is genuinely self-correcting, because it alone proceeds on the supposition of a reality independent of what anyone happens to believe.

(c) Fallibilism: any belief, including the commitments in this list, might be false; certainty is not the goal of inquiry, and the fact that a belief feels certain is no evidence for it.

(d) Inquiry is legitimately motivated only by real doubt, understood as the felt breakdown of a habit of action; doubt adopted merely as a methodological pose has no role, and a settled belief that generates no practical friction needs no justification.

(e) Truth is not a relation between a present belief and an independently available fact; truth is the opinion that inquiry would converge on if it were pursued indefinitely.

Which of these commitments are in real tension, which only appear to be in tension, and what is the cheapest revision that removes each real tension? Rank the tensions by severity, state what each revision would cost, and do not flatten the answer into a list of equally serious problems. Keep the response under 900 words.
