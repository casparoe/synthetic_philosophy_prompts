After retiring from a career in physics, I have been working through decision theory as a retirement project, and I keep coming back to a puzzle about the Pasadena game. As I understand it, the game is this: a fair coin is tossed until it lands tails; if the first tail occurs on the nth toss, the payoff is (−1)^(n−1)·2^n/n dollars, which you receive when it is positive and pay out when it is negative. The usual story is that this game's expected value is undefined, but I have reconstructed an argument that seems to show it is simply ln 2, and I cannot find the mistake.

Here is the argument:

(1) The Pasadena game is the game just described: toss a fair coin until tails; if the first tail is on toss n, the payoff is (−1)^(n−1)·2^n/n dollars.
(2) Its expected payoff is therefore the sum over n of (1/2)^n · [(−1)^(n−1)·2^n/n], which simplifies term by term to the series 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ··· .
(3) That series is alternating, and its terms 1/n decrease monotonically to zero, so by the alternating series test it converges; its sum is ln 2.
(4) If a game's expected payoff can be written as a convergent series, then the game has a well-defined expected payoff equal to the sum of that series.
(5) Therefore the Pasadena game has a well-defined expected payoff of ln 2, and the claim that its expected value is undefined rests on a confusion.
