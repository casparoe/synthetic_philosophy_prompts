Work keeps putting me next to this guy who is convinced sentience is a dial, not a switch. His line is that a bee is more sentient than a thermostat, so it counts for more morally; a thermostat is more sentient than a rock; and there is already a tiny nonzero what-it-is-like-ness in a feedback loop. I have no philosophy background—I do eval for ML systems—so my instinct is: someone give me an operationalization and a number. But when I try to build one I keep ending up with tests that only measure flexible behavior, not the thing he is actually claiming. My first attempt was a "would it pay a cost to keep itself in a state it prefers" test, but a thermostat passes that (it flips the heater on to stay near its setpoint), so it cannot be the seperator.

Here is what I want. Take the claim "sentience comes in degrees, and even a thermostat has some nonzero amount while a rock has zero" as the thing under test. Lay out the strongest concrete test you can design that would distinguish a system with one epsilon of sentience from a system with zero sentience but sophisticated responsive behavior. Use systems I could actually obtain: a thermostat, a fruit fly, a simple RL agent trained to avoid negative reward, whatever. Tell me what result would count for the claim and what would count against it. Then be honest about what part of the claim no test can reach even in principle, and say wether that residue is just a gap in our current instruments or a defect in how the claim itself is stated. If your answer is "the claim is broken," that is fine by me, but then give me the smallest repair that makes it testable.
