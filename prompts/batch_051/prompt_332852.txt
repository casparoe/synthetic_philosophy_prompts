You're an expert on inferential role semantics — the view people mostly associate with Brandom, though it's older — that the meaning of a declarative sentence is totally exhausted by its inferential role: which commitments it licenses, what entitles you to assert it, and what its incompatible with. I've been arguing about this with a friend who thinks the view is basically unfalsifiable, because any apparent counterexample can be handled by quietly adding one more inference to the story. I want a thought experiment that puts genuine pressure on that move — not to refute the view, but to expose where the line between semantic and merely collateral inferences is doing hidden work.

Here's the spec. Build me a pair of ordinary English declarative sentences, call them A and B, satisfying all of these conditions. (1) There is a small, explicitly stated inference game — a finite list of, say, six to eight rules that a community treats as meaning-fixing for some limited purpose — in which A and B are perfectly symmetric: every rule involving A still holds when you replace A with B, and every rule involving B still holds when you replace B with A. (2) Competent speakers nonetheless have a strong intuition that A and B differ in meaning. (3) The difference can't be mere pragmatics like politeness or emphasis, can't be logical equivalence in disguise (no p vs not-not-p style cases), and no indexicals or slurs. (4) The subject matter should be mundane — an office, a household, a clinic — nothing science-fictional, no perfect predictors. (5) Engineer the case so that a hardline inferentialist can only save the view by attributing to speakers an inference rule that those same speakers would sincerely disavow if you asked them whether it's part of what the sentence means.

Before you settle on your final case, brainstorm two or three candidate pairs and briefly say why each one fails or is less clean. Then present the final case and do the following: run at least one representative inference through the substitution both ways to show the symmetry is airtight; explain why ordinary speakers resist calling A and B synonymous; state precisely the extra inference the inferentialist is forced to posit and why adding it is costly rather than merely ad hoc; and identify the one feature of the case doing the work — if I removed it, would the case collapse into something boring for the inferentialist? Finish by saying how the hardest-line version of the view would attack your case, and whether you think that defense succeeds. Think carefully — the difficulty is making the symmetry airtight while keeping the intuitive difference undeniable.
