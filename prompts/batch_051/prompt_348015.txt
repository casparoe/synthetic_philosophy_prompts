My partner and I have been fighting about the draft aid-in-dying law our state is about to vote on, and neither of us can figure out who's right. The relevant clause reads:

"A physician may prescribe aid-in-dying medication to a qualified individual who has a terminal disease that will, within reasonable medical judgment, produce death within six months, is mentally capable, and is able to self-administer the medication."

My aunt has ALS. Her mind and speech are fine, she's made her wishes clear for years, but she can no longer swallow or use her hands, so physically she can't pick up a glass and drink. Her neurologist says respiratory failure is likely within a few months, though he won't pin it down exactly, because ALS is jumpy. She wants the option. The state medical association has said "self-administer" means the patient's own voluntary physical act of ingesting the medication, not through a feeding tube and not with someone else holding the cup. She would fail that test.

I'm less interested in the politics than the reading. What does the clause actually require in her case? Walk me through it from the plain text, from the purpose behind the self-administration requirement, and from what the people who wrote the bill would have said if someone had asked about ALS. If those three readings come apart, which one should control, and why? And if the honest conclusion is that the text excludes her, is there a small change to the clause that would include her without gutting whatever the self-administration rule was there to protect?
