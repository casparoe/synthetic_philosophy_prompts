A national research council is considering a new funding rule for social-science projects that invoke collective or group-level variables — inequality, institutional quality, social capital, culture, class position, and the like. Under the rule, such variables may appear in a proposal only as convenient shorthand for distributions of individual actions, beliefs, or interactions. Every proposal must specify a mechanism at the level of individuals through which the group-level variable is supposed to operate, and must describe the individual-level evidence that would confirm this mechanism rather than merely correlate with the outcome. Proposals that treat group-level properties as causally effective in their own right will be rejected as methodologically unsound. The council defends the rule as a guard against loose reification; its critics argue that it amounts to legislating methodological individualism and will quietly exclude legitimate holistic explanations.

Analyse the rule as an institutional mechanism rather than a philosophical position. What research gets funded under it, and what stops being proposed or gets redesigned to qualify? How are researchers likely to game the requirement — for instance, by attaching speculative micro-foundations to otherwise macro-level work, by relabelling group-level variables as aggregated individual states, or by selecting topics that individualise easily? Where is the rule most likely to distort the science, and where might it genuinely improve explanatory discipline? Pay particular attention to the point at which the individualist/holist distinction has to be drawn in practice, and say whether the rule's failure modes could be fixed without abandoning it.
