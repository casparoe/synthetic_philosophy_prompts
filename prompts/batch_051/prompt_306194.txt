I am giving a twenty-minute talk to a hospital research-ethics grand rounds defending the following thesis: clinical equipoise, as standardly understood, is the wrong ethical standard for individual randomization offers. I will argue that community-level uncertainty among experts can justify a trial as a scientific undertaking, but it cannot by itself justify a clinician-investigator's offer of enrollment to a particular patient. For that offer to be permissible, the enrolling clinician must be in a state of particularized uncertainty: she must be warranted in believing that, once the trial arms are translated into this patient's own values and risk tolerances, no arm is reasonably believed to be superior for this patient. I will also argue that informed consent cannot absorb the remainder, because the trial setting and the consent document themselves cue the therapeutic misconception; the investigator has an obligation not merely to avoid reinforcing it but to correct it. My position is a middle one: I am not an equipoise skeptic who thinks randomization is inherently unethical, and I am not defending Freedman-style clinical equipoise as it stands; I am splitting the ethics of trial initiation from the ethics of the enrollment offer.

The audience is not a philosophy department. It is a hospital grand rounds of roughly forty people: about half are phase III trialists, several of whom run non-inferiority and adaptive-design trials; a quarter are biostatisticians who treat equipoise mostly as a design and monitoring problem; the remainder are IRB members, patient advocates, and two clinically trained bioethicists. They know the clinical equipoise and uncertainty principle debates, and they use equipoise loosely. Their dominant concern is enrollment feasibility. The trialists will hear my particularized-uncertainty requirement as a recipe for individual clinical hunches to block methodologically sound trials; the biostatisticians will say I am smuggling unpredictable physician judgment into what should be a population-level inference; the IRB members will push that the therapeutic misconception is a consent problem and that I have conflated it with the ethics of randomization. The talk's argument is a division-of-labor argument: community epistemic conditions govern whether the trial may proceed, but only particularized clinical judgment can make an enrollment offer consistent with the fiduciary role.
