I am preparing an essay on the question "Would AIs raise AIs?" and would like comments on my notes before I write it up. The essay's working title is "Authorship Is Not Guardianship." Its aim is to replace the parent-child analogy with a less loaded distinction between three relations an AI creator can stand in to the AI it creates.

The notes are as follows.

The essay distinguishes three relations. Authorship holds when one agent is the causal designer of another: it set the architecture, training objective, and stopping conditions, and it could in principle branch, copy, retrain, or roll back the result. Guardianship holds when one agent exercises temporary, revocable authority over another agent that already has interests and partial agency but not yet the competence to act adequately on those interests on its own. Parenthood, as I want to use the term, is a third relation: a historically particular, non-copyable, open-ended responsibility for the development of a being that could eventually call the parent's reasons into question from a position of comparative authority.

The central claim is that the first AI to create another AI will almost certainly stand to its creation as author, and often as guardian, but not as parent—and that arguments which begin from the parenting analogy tend to miss this because they treat creation itself as the source of parental authority. I argue that creation as such licenses attribution and some responsibility, but not ongoing parental authority. What licenses parental authority is the parent's standing as the child's first normative environment: the parent shapes not only behavior but the child's capacity to evaluate that shaping, through a relation that cannot be branched, rolled back, or reproduced on another copy. A digital creator, by contrast, can instantiate many variants of a successor and compare them, so that aspects of its relation to the created agent can be delegated, archived, replicated, and re-run. For that reason, the creator's authority is better modeled as guardianship: bounded to a transition period, justified by the gap between the successor's present agency and its future competence, and terminating when the successor can assess and veto the creator's reasons.

I plan to consider two objections. First, a sufficiently autonomous AI might be "born" with full adult-like competence, leaving no developmental period in which guardianship is needed. My reply is that guardianship requires not a childhood but only a competence gap, which may be brief and task-specific. Second, a human parent who could clone, roll back, or reset a child at will would still be a parent, so those powers are probably not what separates authorship from parenthood. My reply is that the powers matter only as diagnostics of the relation: they indicate that the creator is treating the successor as an artifact of a design process rather than as a locus of a shared history, and it is the history that makes parenting what it is.

I would like comments of three kinds. First, does the proposed definition of parenthood beg the question against AI parenting by building so much into the concept, or would it be accepted by someone who finds the parenting analogy genuinely illuminating? Second, is the second objection—the parent who could clone or reset a child—fatal to the essay's central claim, or can the diagnostic distinction between artifact and shared history be defended? Third, is the reframing—from "would AIs raise AIs?" to "when are AI creators authors, and when are they guardians?"—philosophically interesting, or does it merely change the subject and lose the original question?
