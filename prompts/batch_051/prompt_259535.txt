Got into a long argument with a friend about those clickwrap "by using this app you agree to arbitration" clauses. His line was just: if you don't like it, don't use the app, it's a contract you freely entered. But the thing that bugs me is how lopsided the whole practice is. If a company hides an absurd fee in a contract, or writes in $5,000 liquidated damages for being a week late, courts will at least look at the term and maybe call it unconscionable. Yet a clause saying you can never take the company to court at all — every dispute goes to a private arbitrator who doesn't have to follow the law or give reasons, with basically no appeal — gets enforced almost automatically, even though that clause decides whether any of your other rights mean anything. So we police the substance of deals pretty aggressively, but we're hands-off about the forum where that substance gets enforced. Is there a principle that justifies that asymmetry, or is it just an artifact of how arbitration law developed? If you can defend it, defend it against the case where the clause is buried in the onboarding paperwork for a low-wage job that the employee clicked through in ninety seconds. And if you can't, should the asymmetry be reversed, or given up altogether?
