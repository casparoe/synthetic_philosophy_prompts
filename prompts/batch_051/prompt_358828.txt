Next month I am giving a colloquium talk to an analytic philosophy department whose members are mostly metaphysicians and philosophers of language, with two logicians; none work on Buddhist or Sanskrit philosophy, and most treat classical logic and bivalence as the default that one abandons only under pressure. My talk defends a therapeutic, no-thesis interpretation of Nāgārjuna's Madhyamaka. On this reading, the claim that all things are empty of intrinsic nature is not itself a metaphysical thesis about the ultimate character of reality. It is a device for exposing why any attempt to state how things ultimately are, including the statement that everything is empty, undermines itself by presupposing a standpoint outside the conceptual framework it is using. I will argue that the tetralemma, the scheme of four alternatives for a proposition (true, false, both true and false, neither true nor false), is best read as exhausting the positions available within a given framework, so that Nāgārjuna's rejection of all four is neither the assertion of the fourth limb nor a commitment to true contradictions, but an invitation to drop the framework itself. My argumentative sketch is this: any assertion about how things ultimately are must deploy concepts whose determinacy it takes for granted; those concepts are themselves among the things whose status is in question; the tetralemma exposes this because each of the four available assignments reintroduces the very characterization it was supposed to remove; so the appropriate response is not a better characterization but the relinquishing of the demand for one.

Prepare me for the question period. I need the objections I will actually get from this audience, not the ones I would get from Buddhist-studies scholars or from a workshop on paraconsistent logic. Order them roughly by expected likelihood combined with how damaging they are. Phrase each objection the way a member of this department would actually put it: for example, a metaphysician who insists that rejecting all four limbs of the tetralemma just is accepting the fourth; a philosopher of language who thinks the predicate "empty" must express a property if it is meaningful at all; a logician who wants to know whether the argument formally requires giving up classical negation and, if so, which non-classical logic is licensed. For each objection, give me what I should concede up front, what I should hold, a two-minute reply, and a note on where that reply is weakest. Include as the final item the question I am hoping nobody asks, and say why I should prepare for it anyway.

Throughout, separate what is settled in Madhyamaka scholarship from what is genuinely contested, and do not present the therapeutic reading as the consensus; flag where my argument goes beyond the texts or leans on a controversial interpretation.
