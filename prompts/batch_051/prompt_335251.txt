I sit on the rules committee of a competitive debate association; next month we vote on whether to sanction a one-year pilot of a new event its proponents call "adversarial inquiry." The proposed format is a timed, one-on-one match on a preassigned proposition, scored like a sport. Matches would not be decided by which side persuades the judges of a conclusion; instead, each competitor earns points on three scorecards: (i) inference — points for valid, explicitly flagged arguments and for fidelity to a shared fact sheet, with deductions for invalid moves or unsupported claims; (ii) steelmanning — before answering, a competitor must restate the opponent's strongest argument to the opponent's or the judges' satisfaction, with heavier deductions for strawmanning; (iii) concession — points awarded when a competitor retracts a claim after it has been shown defective, scaled by how promptly and cleanly. Proponents are explicit that a competitor could lose the substantive exchange and still win the round on points, and they regard this as a feature rather than a bug. The pilot would run one academic year, after which the committee would vote to adopt, modify, or kill the format.

Please draft the short written opinion I'll circulate before the vote. It should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame, and it should treat the strongest case on the losing side as seriously as the winning one — not just restate that competition corrupts reasoning or that our current formats already reward gamesmanship under the table. Address the underlying question head-on: could making argumentation a scored sport improve reasoning, or does it replace inquiry with a performance? Be explicit about confidence: distinguish what follows from the incentive structure of the rules from what depends on empirical bets about how competitors and judges will actually behave, and flag where you are speculating. If the task as I've posed it rests on a confusion — for example, if you think the committee is framing this as a rules-design problem when the real error is treating reasoning as a contest at all — say so plainly rather than writing a memo that politely works around it.
