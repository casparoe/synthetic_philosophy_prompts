Me and a friend have been going back and forth about what the gender-equality paradox would mean if it held up, and I keep finding myself stuck because every allegedly new explanation turns out to be one of the usual suspects in different clothes. I'm writing up a short explainer for a discussion group, so I want to stress-test the standard menu before I add anything to it. Let's grant, for the sake of argument, that the pattern is real: by some measures, countries that score higher on gender-equality indexes show larger sex differences in field-of-study choices, with women relatively more concentrated in health and education and less in engineering and computer science.

Here are the standard explanations I already have and don't need repeated:

1. Choice amplification: richer, more egalitarian countries give people the material security to choose fields according to their own preferences, so average underlying sex differences in interests, risk tolerance, and competitiveness show up more strongly there.
2. Economic necessity: in poorer and less egalitarian countries, women go into male-typical fields because they need the income, not because they prefer them, which suppresses the sex difference there.
3. The measurement objection: the equality indexes and field categories are too crude or uneven across countries, so the paradox may be partly an artifact of how the variables are defined.
4. Residual socialization and anticipated discrimination: even high-parity countries channel girls away from math-heavy tracks early, and male-dominated technical fields can be unwelcoming enough to steer women elsewhere.
5. Comparative advantage: even where girls match boys in math, they often outscore them in verbal skills by a bigger margin, and people tend to choose fields that play to their strongest subject, so girls drift toward health, education, and law rather than engineering.
6. A biological-essentialist line: the relevant average interest differences are partly built in, especially along the people-versus-things dimension, so the paradox is what you'd predict once constraints are removed.
7. The welfare-state channel: egalitarian countries tend to expand public sectors that create a lot of stable, sex-typical jobs—nursing, teaching, social care—which give women a safe route into paid work and shift the aggregate numbers without any deep preference story.

I want one more candidate explanation for the pattern that is not on this list and not a variant, special case, restatement, or combination of anything on it. It can be cultural, economic, psychological, demographic, institutional, whatever. Then tell me honestly whether it's any good—does it have a real mechanism, would it survive the obvious objections, and does it actually add something the seven above don't?
