I am a PhD student working on the philosophy of games. In a recent seminar, two of my fellow students argued about a case, and afterward they asked me to referee; I could not, and I would like help adjudicating it. The case was this: in a single-player survival game, a player has an AI companion with an elaborate history, apparent preferences, and responsive behavior. The player can sacrifice the companion for a small resource gain, or take a costlier nonlethal route to the same in-game result. Lena argued that sacrificing the companion is genuinely morally criticizable: the companion is a responsive participant in a long-running relationship, and destroying a relationship-partner for a minor resource saving is a vice regardless of whether the partner has consciousness. Marcus replied that objective wrongness needs a patient—someone who can be harmed—and an unconscious bundle of scripts cannot be harmed, so the act is at most bad because of its effects on the player's character or on other people. Lena answered that Marcus is assuming too strong a condition on having interests: the companion has in-game interests—it seeks food, avoids danger, expresses preferences—that persist and develop, and a virtuous person would not set those back for a trivial gain; calling the act merely bad for the agent misdescribes why it seems wrong. Marcus replied that if apparent interests plus a history of interaction were enough, then a reader who relishes a character's suffering in a novel, or a child who mistreats a doll, would be directly wronging the character or the doll, which over-generates; the intuitive difference is that in those cases no actual other exists whose interests can be affected. Lena answered that the game case is unlike passive fiction: the player is not merely imagining an outcome but bringing it about within an ongoing interactive practice to which the companion contributes, so the companion is not just an object of make-believe. Please assess the dispute step by step: what does each move get right and wrong, what relevant distinctions are either side missing, and who is more right overall?
