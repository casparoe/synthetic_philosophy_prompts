Next week I present at a joint statistics–philosophy seminar, and I need a prep sheet for the question period rather than a restatement of my argument. My thesis is that the standard trichotomy of interpretations of probability—frequentist, subjectivist, and propensity—is not a partition of coherent positions. I will defend two claims. First, there are two distinct probability concepts: an epistemic concept of graded rational belief relative to an information base, and an ontic concept of a single-case disposition of a physical setup to produce an outcome. Second, frequentism is best reconstructed as a failed attempt to define the ontic concept purely extensionally, while subjectivism errs by treating the epistemic concept as the whole of probability and dismissing the ontic concept as a reification. My argument sketch is as follows. In one-off prediction cases—a particular patient's response to an untested drug, a particular bridge's failure under a specified load—scientists assign probabilities that resist paraphrase both as actual relative frequencies and as mere subjective feelings. The frequentist paraphrase changes the subject from this event to an imagined reference class; the subjectivist paraphrase treats the assignment's object-directedness as an illusion while quietly relying on it when the assignment guides action. I conclude that the two concepts are not rivals because they have different satisfaction conditions, and that the long-running debate persists because each side wants a single formal semantics for all probability talk.

The audience matters a great deal here. Roughly two-thirds are working statisticians with strong frequentist commitments: they care about long-run error control, regard subjective priors as unscientific, and will hear any appeal to unobserved dispositions as metaphysics. Two Bayesians in the room hold that probabilities are always degrees of belief and that objective chance is at best a convenient fiction for exchangeable sequences. The remaining third are philosophers of science who will dislike gratuitous ontology and will also notice any slide between semantics and methodology. The format is twenty minutes of talk followed by a ten-minute question period.

Give me the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased as those specific people would phrase them, with how likely and how damaging each is. For each objection, tell me what I should concede, what I should hold, and exactly where my reply is weakest. Include the objection I should be praying nobody asks. I am most worried about being pressed on what fixes the physical setup to which a propensity attaches without covertly defining that setup by the very frequencies the propensity was meant to explain, and about making the epistemic concept sharp enough to earn its keep beyond the usual talk of degrees of belief.
