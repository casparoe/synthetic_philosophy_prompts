The people running a mid-sized discussion site have had it with human moderation, so they propose this. They take a fixed, off-the-shelf sentence-embedding model and run every post through it. They keep two canonical collections: 500 posts the moderators have already judged clearly banned, and 500 clearly allowed. For each new post they compute its mean cosine similarity to the banned collection and to the allowed collection, then subtract the allowed score from the banned score; if that margin is above a threshold, the post is removed automatically. Appeals then alter the collections: if an appeal succeeds, the post is added to the allowed set, and if a removal is upheld, it is added to the banned set. Treat this as a machine, not a moral position, and tell me how it actually behaves. What sorts of writing does it incentivise, and what text-level evasions does the geometry make easy? How do the two collections drift as appealed posts get added, and what does that drift do to the decision boundary over time? Which failures come straight from the structure of semantic embedding spaces themselves (negation, irony, antonyms sitting nearer to each other than to neutral text, non-compositional phrases, code words), and can any of those be fixed by tweaking the score, the threshold, or the update rule without opening up a new way to game it? And after a year of this, what does the site actually look like?
