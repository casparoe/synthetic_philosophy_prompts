I recently had a long argument with a friend about historical materialism, and one exchange left me with a question I cannot answer to my own satisfaction. My friend defended the classical materialist argument: a society's economic base—the forces and relations of production through which it reproduces itself—explains and constrains its superstructure, the legal, political, religious, and artistic forms that arise upon it. I kept objecting that this division looks unstable once the forces and relations of production themselves include algorithms, datasets, intellectual property, recommendation systems, and media platforms—things that are intangible, symbolic, and partly ideological, and that the classical picture would most naturally have placed in the superstructure. Our argument stalled there. I want to press the question further. Suppose, for the sake of argument, that the most dynamic sectors of an economy produce value primarily by producing, organizing, and circulating meaning and information, so that items of the sort just listed are not decorative additions to material production but central means by which production as a whole is coordinated and valorized. Does the base-superstructure argument survive this development? If it does, explain what the base now consists in and why it remains genuinely more fundamental than what is left of the superstructure. If it does not, give the strongest repaired version of historical materialism that still preserves a direction of explanation, and state precisely which premise of the classical argument had to be revised and why.
