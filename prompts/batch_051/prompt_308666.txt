I've been teaching a grad seminar on practical rationality, and I keep coming back to the idea of rational irrationality — the claim that it can be rational, all things considered, to deliberately make yourself irrational, or at least to cultivate dispositions that would be irrational if you held them one-off. The standard case is the hitchhiker in Parfit: the only way he can make a credible promise to pay the driver is to actually intend to pay, and since he can't just decide to intend, he might rationally take some device or pill that makes him the kind of person who pays — even though, when the time comes, paying is irrational because the ride is already sunk and the driver can't extract the money. I'm not asking wether that specific story works. The general position I find myself defending in seminar is that we can have reason to bring about and maintain attitudes like guilt, anger, spite, and certain forms of self-deception precisely because they are sometimes unresponsive to evidence or to local cost-benefit calculation, and that this unresponsiveness is a feature rather than a bug.

What I want is an honest map of what would count against this view. What would have to be true for rational irrationality, so understood, to be false or empty? Distinguish evidence that would be decisive from evidence that would merely be awkward. Are there cases the view cannot describe, consequences it's committed to that are unacceptable, or results showing it collapses into either ordinary means-end rationality or into a kind of double-counting? One thing I especially want probed: defenders of the view often say the self-binding act is rational from the earlier strategic standpoint while the resulting state is irrational only from within — is that a real distinction, or is it an equivocation that insulates the view from testing? Tell me honestly whether the position risks anything, and whether it being well-insulated (if it is) is a problem. And write your answer in American English.
