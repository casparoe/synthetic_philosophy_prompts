Two of my housemates got into it last night about that old dust-specks-vs-torture thought experiment—you know, the one where you choose between torturing one person for fifty years and giving some astronomically huge number of people each a single dust speck in the eye, which stings for a second and is gone. I mostly just listened, because it got heated and neither would budge, and I'm still not sure who was right where.

Here's roughly what they said, in order.

(1) Jordan: "The only consistent answer is the torture. Every dust speck is bad by some tiny amount, so with enough specks the summed misery is obviously larger than fifty years of torture. If you pick the specks, you're not doing the arithmetic; you're just refusing to multiply."

(2) Lila: "But nobody experiences more than a speck. For each person it's a slight annoyance, forgotten in a minute. A slight annoyance isn't a fragment of a torture, and you can't pour everyone's irritation into one big bucket and call it a worse harm. The tortured person has a complaint that dwarfs every single other person's complaint."

(3) Jordan: "But you do accept a rate of exchange, you just don't want to look at it. Suppose avoiding one speck is worth one extra second of torture for the one guy. Then avoiding it for enough people is worth fifty years. If instead you say no number of specks is ever worth a second of torture, then you've already gone lexical, and I think that's at least as weird as adding utilities. Pick your poison."

(4) Lila: "I don't have to fix a ratio, because torture and specks aren't the same currency. That's the whole point. The harms are different in kind: one destroys a life, the other is an annoyance. So the tradeoff can be lexical—no number of specks outweighs a torture—and that's not irrational, it's just refusing to treat people as containers of utility."

(5) Jordan: "But lexical rules sound deep until you leen on them. Would you really turn down a deal that gives everyone on earth a mosquito-bite-level nuisance every day for a year, just to avoid one minute of torture for one person? At some point the number of separate minor harms has to matter, otherwise your 'respect for persons' is just your gut pretending to be a principle."

(6) Lila: "I didn't say the number never matters. I said it matters differently. A billion specks that each person wouldn't bother filing a complaint about don't add up to one person's torture. But if the case were changed so the tiny harms added up to something serious within each person's life—or stood for an ongoing practice that humiliates whole groups—then aggregation kicks in, for a reason a contractualist can accept. That's a feature of my view, not an inconsistency."

Please adjudicate this move by move: for each of the six moves, say whether it's basically right, basically wrong, or partly right, and briefly justify each rating. Then say which of the two is closer overall, and finish by naming the strongest point that the other side still has on the table.
