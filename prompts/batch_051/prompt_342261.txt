I chair an interdisciplinary faculty seminar on the foundations of rational choice, and for the past year our discussions have repeatedly stalled on a single fault line. One group—call it the eductive camp—holds that the classical solution concepts of game theory, above all backward induction in finite perfect-information games, can be justified by reasoning alone: if the players are rational, know the game, and know that the others are rational, know that they know, and so on, then the backward-induction path is what rationality itself selects. The other group—call it the evolutive camp—replies that common knowledge of rationality cannot do this work, because it is either inconsistent with or unusable at exactly the nodes that would not be reached if the backward-induction prediction were correct; on their view, equilibrium concepts are best understood not as deductions from common knowledge but as rest points of learning or selection processes that need make no such counterfactual demands.

The two groups have agreed to attempt an adversarial collaboration rather than another exchange of rebuttals, and I would like you to draft the document they would jointly circulate. It should contain: (1) a numbered list of claims both camps can sign—substantive rather than vacuous—about the formalism, about what the extensive form of a finite game does and does not assume, about what, if anything, standard impossibility and no-go results actually rule out, and about what would count as a successful foundation for an equilibrium concept; (2) a table of the residual disagreements, with each camp's position stated in wording that a knowledgeable member of that camp would accept; and (3) a list of the genuine cruxes—the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side—together with any models, experiments, or constraints both camps agree would bear on each crux, and each side's prediction recorded in advance. Anchor the document in a finite Centipede game and in the question of how a rational player who finds herself at a node that contradicts the backward-induction prediction should revise her beliefs about the other player's rationality. Finally, if you believe part of the dispute is verbal because the two camps mean different things by knowledge, rationality, or justification, say which part and propose a reformulation that dissolves the verbal layer while leaving any residual disagreement visible.
