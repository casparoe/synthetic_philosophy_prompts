I've been re-reading Section VI ("Of Probability") of Hume's Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, and this bit keeps nagging at me:

"Mr. Locke divides all arguments into demonstrative and probable. In this view, we must say, that it is only probable all men must die, or that the sun will rise to-morrow."

It pushed me to own up to a policy I've been following for years without really inspecting it. I flat-out believe any claim I'd assign above 0.99 probability, disbelieve anything below 0.01, and suspend judgment in between. The passage seems to support this: if even all-men-must-die is only probable, belief can't require certainty, so a high threshold is my honest substitute.

But the policy misbehaves in two ways. First, in a fair 1,000-ticket lottery, I believe of each ticket that it will lose (0.999), and if I conjoin those beliefs I believe no ticket wins — while also knowing exactly one wins. Second, I just finished a long report at work with around 200 seperate factual claims; I'd assert each one individually, since I checked each, but I'd also happily sign a preface saying at least one of them is false, since my track record says any 200 claims contain an error. I don't feel inconsistent in either case, but I might be fooling myself.

So: is this standing habit — high-probability threshold plus honest preface — defensible? If not, where exactly does it break down, and what is the smallest repair that keeps what's useful? Give me a definite position with reasons, not a survey of options, and keep the whole thing under 500 words.
