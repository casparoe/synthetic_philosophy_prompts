As a novelist, I keep returning to a remark in Hume's essay "Of the Standard of Taste." Paraphrasing: Hume observes that a poet may ask us to enter into vicious ways of feeling, but if the poem presents those vicious manners without marking them with the appropriate blame and disapprobation, this disfigures the poem and constitutes a real deformity. He treats the reader's inability to go along with the requested perspective as a defect in the work itself.

Now suppose that, starting tomorrow, imaginative resistance disappears entirely. Readers can enter into any perspective a novelist asks for, no matter how morally alien, without psychological friction, and they feel no impulse to demand that the narrative mark that perspective as blameworthy. Nothing else changes. Predict, with reasoning, what happens to serious fiction, criticism, and ordinary readers' moral sensibilities over the next thirty years.

Please organize your response into the following sections, in order:

1. The mechanism (5–7 sentences): What imaginative resistance does, and whether Hume was right to locate the defect in the work rather than in the reader.
2. First-order effects on literary practice (6–8 sentences): Which kinds of books get written, which genres flourish or wither, and what happens to moralized reading.
3. Institutional and critical adaptations (5–7 sentences): How critics, publishers, and readers compensate, and whether new norms of blame arise outside the text.
4. Robust versus fragile predictions (4–6 sentences): Which of your predictions hold across different assumptions about moral psychology, and which depend on narrower assumptions.
5. Reverse test of Hume's claim (4–6 sentences): What this scenario reveals about whether unmarked viciousness really is an artistic deformity, as Hume thought.
