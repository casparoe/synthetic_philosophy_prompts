I am a PhD student in the philosophy of music, working on musical expressiveness, and I have been defending a perceptual-resemblance account of what it is for a piece of music to be sad. My working view, as it now stands, is this: for a sequence of sounds presented in a recognized musical setting, the music is sad just in case (i) a competent listener attending to the sounds alone, without relying on titles, biography, or program notes, is disposed to hear them as a presentation of sadness, and (ii) that disposition is best explained by the audible resemblance between the sounds and the characteristic contours of human sadness-expression — falling pitch contours, a constricted pitch range, slow tempo, and a heavy or sobbing attack. On my account, the sadness is a property of the perceived musical surface, and it need not correspond to any emotion actually felt or intended by the composer or performer.

The case that has been troubling me is this. A composer colleague has submitted an eleven-minute piece for amplified solo voice to our workshop; the printed program identifies it only as Study No. 4. In performance, the vocalist stands with her back to the audience, and what fills the hall is the sound of a woman weeping. The work consists of the sounds of real grief: convulsive sobs, sharp intakes of breath, long descending moans, and uneven silences. They are not stylized into melodic or rhythmic patterns. The vocalist, however, is not acting; she is genuinely weeping over something that had happened to her earlier that day, and most of the audience does not know this. Several listeners afterward describe the piece as the saddest music they have ever heard, and some add that they could not tell whether they were witnessing a performance or a private breakdown, which made the experience more disturbing rather than less.

My question is whether Study No. 4 qualifies as sad music on my account, and whether the case shows that my criteria need revision. Does criterion (i) require that the sadness be heard as an appearance or a represented emotion rather than as a real episode of grief, and would the ordinary hearing of genuine weeping satisfy it? Does the "resemblance" in criterion (ii) include the limiting case in which the sounds are not merely like human sadness-expression but are instances of it, so that the piece comes out as sad music; or does my account imply that the piece is less sad than a stylized imitation of weeping? I suspect I need to distinguish being expressive of sadness from being a real manifestation of sadness, but I am not certain that distinction does the work. Please work through the case criterion by criterion and say whether I should revise the account, and if so, how.
