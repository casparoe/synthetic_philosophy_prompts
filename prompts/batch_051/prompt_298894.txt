I would like to ask you to audit a set of commitments I hold about consequentialism and the problem of cluelessness. I do not know where the real conflicts are, so I will state the theses as flatly as I can and ask you to test their joint tenability.

1. I accept a maximizing form of act-consequentialism, understood in its expected-value form: an act is right just in case its expected consequences, given the agent's evidence, are at least as good as those of any available alternative.
2. I accept the cluelessness thesis: for nearly every significant choice, the long-run consequences depend on downstream causal chains so complex that we cannot responsibly assign probabilities to the outcome's being good on net, bad on net, or roughly neutral, and the sign of the long-run expected value is effectively unknowable.
3. I think cluelessness should not produce paralysis. The proper response is to continue using expected-value reasoning over the limited horizon whose effects we can predict with reasonable confidence, while treating the long tail of unresolved consequences as a standing uncertainty rather than as a reason either to do nothing or to fall back unreflectively on ordinary intuition.
4. I cannot, however, identify any principled, non-arbitrary point at which one should stop tracing an action's consequences. Any horizon I adopt strikes me as a pragmatic convention rather than a theoretically grounded boundary.
5. I also accept a view about uncertainty across moral theories: agents should maximize expected choiceworthiness, weighting each theory by their credence in it, assessing options by each theory's own standards, and choosing the option with the greatest expected choiceworthiness.
6. I want to preserve a set of ordinary ethical judgements as well: I think a parent can be called a good parent for showing up, and an employer fair for paying on time, solely on the strength of foreseeable local effects, without our needing to trace the indefinite future; and I think the decision to have a child, though it is a paradigm of cluelessness in its long-run effects, is not thereby reduced to a coin toss, and does not require a consequentialist to suspend judgement about it altogether.

I would be grateful for a careful assessment of whether these six commitments can be held together. Which pairs are genuinely in tension, and how severe is each tension? Which pairs merely appear to conflict, and why does the appearance dissolve? Please rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally serious, and for each say what exactly would have to give and what the least costly revision would be. Please write your answer as continuous prose, as one would write a considered letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, and no tables.
