I'm preparing a unit on the epistemology of markets and want a worked rating rather than a survey. The anchor is the common-prior no-trade benchmark. Set it up this way: one risky asset pays off according to an unknown state of the world; traders are risk-averse expected-utility maximizers; they share a common prior over states; all of this is common knowledge; and the only possible reason to trade is private information, because endowments and risk preferences are already arranged so that the initial allocation is Pareto optimal. In that setup no common-knowledge speculative trade should occur: an offer to trade would itself reveal that the proposer has information the counterparty lacks, and the counterparty would infer adverse selection and refuse. Real markets, by contrast, produce heavy volume and prices that seem to gather dispersed information.

I want you to rate four candidate departures from the benchmark according to how much each one, taken on its own, lets real-world prices aggregate dispersed private information rather than merely generate volume.

1. Heterogeneous priors. Traders begin with different probability models of the world, so they can disagree without holding different observations.

2. Bounded rationality and overconfidence. Traders fail to draw the full winner's-curse inference from a counterparty's willingness to trade, or they overweight their own signals.

3. Noise and liquidity trading. Exogenous demand from hedging, index rebalancing, endowment shocks, and naive speculation gives informed traders someone to trade against; the flow moves prices as a side effect.

4. Differences in endowments and risk preferences. Even with a common prior and common knowledge, there are genuine gains from risk-sharing and consumption smoothing, so trade would occur with no informational motive at all, and price changes may reflect demand and risk-bearing capacity rather than facts.

Give each departure a score from 0 to 10, where 10 means the departure on its own could account for most of the informativeness of real-world prices and 0 means it contributes nothing to price informativeness. Then allocate exactly 100 points across the four departures to reflect the share of the total explanatory work you would give each one in the best combined account; the weights must sum to 100 and need not be proportional to the 0–10 scores.

Before the numbers, state for each departure which feature of the benchmark it removes and the minimal mechanism by which removing that feature permits trade without triggering the full winner's-curse inference. After the numbers, defend your largest weight against the runner-up, and finish by identifying which two departures are hardest to separate in actual market data and whether that identification problem should make us more confident or less confident that prices aggregate information. Base the numbers on the structure of the departures, not on empirical literatures or specific market episodes, and do not include citations.
