It has become a commonplace in the philosophy of history to distinguish collective memory from historical inquiry: collective memory is identity-constituting, selective, present-oriented, and normatively charged, whereas history aspires to be critical, evidentiary, distanced, and open to revision. Consider the 1995 controversy over the Smithsonian's planned Enola Gay exhibition at the National Air and Space Museum. The museum intended to mark the fiftieth anniversary of the atomic bombing of Hiroshima with a display that would have placed the bombing in a broader historical context, including discussion of the war's origins, Japanese militarism, and the effects of the bomb on civilians. Veterans' organizations and many in Congress condemned the planned exhibition as unpatriotic and historically distorted; the Smithsonian ultimately scaled the exhibit back to a display of the aircraft's fuselage with comparatively little interpretive material, and the museum's director resigned amid the dispute.
