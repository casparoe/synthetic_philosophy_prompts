I'm giving a joint colloquium talk next month to the history department and the philosophy of history group at a research university, and I'm defending a version of the hinge-of-history thesis. My claim, briefly, is that the present century should be treated as a period of unusually high historical leverage—not because we are the culmination of history or because the long run is guaranteed to be good, but because humanity has only recently acquired the capacity to permanently foreclose its own long-term future, through nuclear war, engineered pandemics, or the development of misaligned AI, and this capacity has arrived before the political institutions that could govern it. The argument runs in three steps. First, if you grant even a modest degree of impartiality across time, the far future dominates in expected value. Second, the levers that determine whether that far future is realized are unusually concentrated in a short window while dangerous capabilities outrun institutions. Third, this window is transient: once the relevant capabilities are either governed or have caused a catastrophe, the concentration of leverage passes, so the same stakes will not simply recur for every future generation. This is a structural claim about the relation between technology and institutions, not a claim that our values are correct or that survival guarantees a good future.

The audience is going to be the hard part. The historians are mostly early-modern and modern Europeanists, and several of them work on millenarian movements, apocalyptic politics, and the way every generation since at least the Black Death has found reasons to think it stood at the end or the turning point of something. The philosophers of history split two ways: a few are sympathetic to big-history and deep-time perspectives, but most are hostile to speculative philosophy of history and will regard a claim about a uniquely pivotal present as unfalsifiable, self-aggrandizing, and a secularized version of the very millenarianism their historian colleagues study. This is not a friendly room.

Give me the eight to ten objections I am actually going to get from this particular audience, ordered by how likely they are and how much damage they can do, and phrased the way a historian of apocalypticism or a skeptical philosopher of history would actually phrase them—not the objections I would get from a longtermist or AI-safety crowd. For each objection, give me a reply that can be delivered in about two minutes, say what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and note where the reply is soft. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
