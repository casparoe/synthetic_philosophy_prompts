I am finishing a long essay on what neural machine-translation systems and literary translators each do when they are asked to carry a poem across a language barrier, and I want to close the piece with an original poem that makes an argument as much as it sounds like one. The centerpiece of the essay is a short poem by the sixteenth-century French poet Clément Marot, written for a sick young woman, built on a tight rhyme scheme and a skipping meter; the essay follows the fact, made prominent by Douglas Hofstadter in Le Ton beau de Marot, that the little poem has been rendered into English dozens of times with no two translators agreeing about what should survive. Translators who preserve the sense tend to flatten the music; translators who preserve the rhyme and meter are forced to revise the sense; attempts to split the difference often end by losing both. My working assumption is that this is not a solvable optimization problem but a genuine dilemma about whether, and how, form itself carries meaning.

To embody that claim, please write a sestina, the full thirty-nine lines, from the point of view of a translator working under exactly this constraint. Use these six end-words in this opening order: sense, sound, form, turn, change, keep; run them through the standard sestina rotation (ABCDEF, FAEBDC, CFDABE, ECBFAD, DEACFB, BDFECA), and let the envoi gather all six words with three at the line-endings in the conventional way. The repeated words should not be ornamental: their forced return under new syntactic pressure ought to mirror what a translator experiences when the fixed points of a form cannot be dropped, yet each stanza compels them to mean something slightly different. Let the first two stanzas build the naive picture of translation as extracting a content and rebuilding an equivalent container around it; let the middle stanzas turn on the discovery that rhyme and meter are not packaging but part of what the poem says; and let the envoi refuse the comfortable resolution that a good translator can have both sense and sound intact. The result should be accurate enough that a working translator or a reader of Hofstadter would recognize the argument, and good enough as verse that a reader acquainted with neither would understand why the poem keeps returning to the same six words.
