I'm a second-year PhD student in political philosophy trying to settle on a dissertation area in the ethics of sweatshops and exploitation. My advisor keeps saying the field is "actually a mess underneath the consensus," and the more I read the more I think she's right. The familiar debate is easy to state: some people think buying from or operating sweatshops is wrong even when the jobs make workers better off than their alternatives, because the employers take advantage of desperate people or benefit from background injustice; others think a mutually beneficial job offer can't be wrong in a way that makes it worse than making no offer at all, and that anti-sweatshop campaigns often hurt the very workers they're meant to help. But I don't want to re-litigate that debate — I want to figure out where the interesting unfinished work is.

Here are some of the questions I keep circling: whether consent given under severe poverty can legitimate an exploitative arrangement, or whether poverty undermines the conditions for valid consent; whether the so-called non-worseness idea is right (roughly, that a mutually beneficial but unfair transaction isn't morally worse than no transaction at all) and what would follow if it were; what an individual consumer in a rich country owes when any single purchase is causally inert but the aggregate practice matters; whether the real wrong lies in background institutions like trade rules, migration restrictions, and land tenure rather than in the employment relation itself, so that sweatshop-focused moralizing is a distraction; how to distinguish taking advantage of vulnerability from creating or maintaining it; and what "structural exploitation" actually adds beyond individual-level exploitation. There are probably better questions I'm not seeing, and part of what I want from you is a sense of which of my candidates are dead ends.

Please act as a senior philosopher who works on exploitation and labor ethics and has strong views about what's actually worth doing. Brainstorm for a bit about the open questions in this area, then rank the ones you'd most want a PhD student with about three years and a philosophy background (but no real economics training) to work on, and defend the ordering. I care less about the list than the reasoning: for each question, say how upstream it is of other work, how tractable and how crowded it is, and what would make you move it up or down the ranking. Don't give me a reading list — I want a defended agenda, not a survey.
