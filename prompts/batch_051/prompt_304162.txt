Here are the criteria I use for a binding promise. (1) The promisor voluntarily performs an act — paradigmatically an explicit speech act such as "I promise" — whose conventional meaning commits them to some future course of action. (2) The act is addressed to a promisee, a person capable of understanding that the commitment is being made to them. (3) The promisor thereby intends to give the promisee standing: the standing to demand performance, to remonstrate after non-performance, and to release the promisor. (4) The promisee still has that standing. A promise ceases to bind once the promisee releases the promisor, and an utterance that never actually conferred standing on anyone was never a promise.

Now consider this case. On her deathbed, a friend asks you to finish and publish her unfinished book, and to edit it so that it preserves her vision rather than making it commercially successful. You promise. She dies a few hours later. You are the only person who knew about the book or the promise; no publisher, estate, family member, or prospective reader expects anything. The friend can no longer demand performance, remonstrate, or release you, and there is no obvious way in which she can still be wronged.

Is the promise still binding? Work through the four criteria one by one, saying plainly where the case satisfies them and where it does not. If the case fails some criterion and yet the promise seems binding, say what is doing the binding work instead, and whether the fourth criterion is a real condition on promissory obligation or merely a consequence of having started from promises between two living people. If you think the promise is not binding, do not let the verdict rest on any one criterion alone; say what more general principle about promising explains it. In your conclusion, state whether the case shows that the criteria themselves need revision, and if so, which criterion you would change.
