I've been reading Moritz Schlick's essay on the meaning of life on and off for a few weeks now, and I keep finding myself talked into the core of it, so I want to see what it would take to talk me out of it. As I understand him, Schlick's move is to say that the question what is the meaning of life? only looks unanswerable because we smuggle in the demand for a purpose outside life, some final goal or cosmic task, and there just isn't one. But that isn't bleak for him, because there is another kind of meaningfulness that doesn't need a goal at all. The distinction he leans on is between work and play: work is done for the sake of some result beyond the activity, while play is done for its own sake and carries its value in itself. A meaningful life, on his account, is one in which more and more of what you do becomes play-like, not relaxed leisure but the absorbed, self-justifying activity of making art, of being in love, of chasing a question for its own sake, the way a child is carried along by a game without asking what the game is for.

I don't want the standard objection that this is just an aestheticized or privileged picture of the good life, though if you think that's the most damaging point I'll hear it out. What I actually want is a map of what would count against this view. Which observations, cases, or lines of argument would have to go through for the view to be false, or at least badly incomplete? Rank them from decisive to merely awkward. Be specific about which counterexamples defenders of the view would themselves concede are genuine costs, say lives built around long sacrifice, obligation, or grinding toward a distant end that feel intensely meaningful without being at all play-like, and which ones they would dismiss as already assuming that meaning has to be a durable achievement rather than a quality of activity. Also tell me straight whether the view is at risk of being insulated into unfalsifiability: if meaningful activity gets quietly redefined as whatever someone is absorbed in, does the position stop making a claim that could be wrong, and is that a problem for a view of this kind? This matters to me partly because Schlick himself was a verificationist of sorts, so I'm not even sure his view is meant to be true or just a way of living, and I'd like to know how that should change the status of any counterexamples. For me personally, the thing that would move me most is a convincing description of a life whose meaning is tied precisely to a future goal where the present is mostly drudgery; I honestly can't tell whether that's a counterexample or just a different sense of meaning the view never promised to explain.
