Before I read yet another paper claiming to have reverse-engineered what some system is 'really' optimising from what it does, I want the full spec of what an adequate inference is even supposed to get right. Write me the desiderata for a method or theory of inferring an optimiser's objective and its search space from its behaviour. I don't want a bibliography or a scorecard against named authors; I want the requirements list, with the case or consideration behind each.

At minimum, I'd expect it to distinguish the objective function from the search space and from the search process, and say why it matters if you attribute a weird behaviour to one rather than the others. It should also respect the basic underdetermination point that a finite batch of behaviour is compatible with loads of objectives, so the interesting question is which further requirements narrow it down and at what price. It has to handle computationally bounded optimisers, where the system can't be treated as finding the global optimum, and it has to not collapse an objective into a hard constraint or a side effect. Include the cases that actually separate methods: two different objectives that produce identical behaviour on the training distribution but diverge off-distribution, the system gaming a proxy rather than the intended target (Goodhart-style cases), and behaviour that is instrumentally useful for many objectives at once. Tell me which of these requirements are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's preferences, flag any subset that can't be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement excludes the most candidate methods.

Then, because I don't want to treat the list as settled: tell me which of your own requirements you are least confident in, and what argument or counterexample would make you drop or demote it. basically, where would you bet the list breaks first?
