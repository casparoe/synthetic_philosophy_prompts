I run an undergrad philosophy club — about 15 students, mixed majors, and we meet for a hour once a week. I want to do a session on the weird fact that the law constantly treats things that obviously aren't people as if they were people: a corporation can own property and be sued, the estate of a dead person can be a party in court, in some systems a ship can itself be the defendant, and so on. I don't want to give a lecture first, and I don't want to say the phrase legal fiction until the very end. What I want is an activity, maybe a role-play or a structured deliberation with some small real stake, where the students get cornered into inventing or depending on one of these made-up legal entities themselves and only afterwards notice what they did. What I want them to come away seeing is that these fictions aren't just errors — they do real work — and that the question wether a corporation or an estate is any less real than a contract or a debt is stranger than it first looks. Design the whole thing for me: the setup and materials, the roles or rules, exactly what they have to decide, and then a debrief that maps what happened in the room onto that philosophical problem. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong when I actually run it and how I should handle each thing, and be honest about which part of the idea the activity cannot get them to feel, so I'll have to supply that part myself. And please write your answer as continuous prose, like an email or a letter — no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
