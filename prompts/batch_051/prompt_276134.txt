I work as a journalist covering science and ideas, and I am currently preparing a long piece on reproductive choice and the genetic technologies that are quietly moving from specialist clinics into the consumer market. For background, a colleague passed me a briefing document that summarises the philosophical debate over what it calls procreative beneficence and liberal eugenics. The summary runs roughly as follows.

The principle of procreative beneficence holds that prospective parents are morally required to have the happiest, healthiest and most talented child they possibly can. Liberal eugenics is described as the practical extension of that principle: the state should fund and encourage the relevant technologies — preimplantation genetic testing, gamete selection, perhaps eventually gene editing — so that parents can meet this requirement, while formally leaving the final choice to families rather than coercing them. Critics, the document says, reply that the whole programme remains eugenic in all but name, because it treats children as products to be optimised against a single social scoreboard and because it expresses the judgement that disabled and merely ordinary lives are inferior. That expressive harm, according to the document, is enough to refute the position even in the absence of state force.

I have enough background to suspect that this conflates several distinct theses and slides without argument from the thought that there is a reason to select one embryo over another to the claim that parents are required to maximise every trait within their control, and that it may be smuggling a contested conclusion into the word eugenics. But I am not a specialist, and the briefing was written by someone who plainly knows the literature better than I do. Could you offer an accuracy audit of this summary: which parts are faithful to the underlying positions, which are common simplifications, and which are genuine distortions; how a careful defender of procreative beneficence would answer each of the critical charges; and whether the document's overall conclusion — that the position can be dismissed on expressive grounds even without coercion — survives once the accurate claims have been separated out? Please write your answer in British English.
