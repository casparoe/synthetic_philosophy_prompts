I run an after-school math circle for about a dozen 16- and 17-year-olds, and next session is on randomness—specifically, the thing I actually want them chewing on is whether it makes sense to say that this particular finite string of heads and tails sitting on the board is random. I've seen the usual demo where you show the class two sequences and ask which one came from a real coin, and it's fun for ten minutes but then I end up lecturing the moral at them anyway. I want something where the distinction between "looks random" and "can't be exploited by a strategy" emerges inside the game itself.

So please design a 75-minute classroom game with real but tiny stakes (I can buy a bag of candy or a roll of quarters) in which students are cast either as fakers, who have to invent a sequence of, say, 30 coin flips that will pass as real, or as bettors, who have to judge sequences and can put actual small bets on them. I'd like it in two phases. In the first phase, let the bettors have some handle: they can see many sequences, maybe they can watch the fakers at work, so they pick up the fingerprints people leave (avoiding long runs, over-alternating, making the first and second half too balanced). In the second phase, take all of that away and make them bet on a single sequence with no provinence, and then arrange things so they notice that the tricks that worked before are just facts about human fakers, not a test of the string itself. Please don't explain any of this up front; let them get good at phase one and only then hit the wall in phase two.

Then write the debrief as a sequence of moves rather than a lecture. Map specific moments in the game onto the ideas that every sequence is exactly as likely as any other, that a finite string is better thought of as more or less compressible (or more or less exploitable by a betting strategy) than as random or not random full stop, and that the interesting notion of randomness is about whether some strategy could have beaten the sequence, not about whether it looks irregular. Tell me what will predictably go wrong—the smart kid who says the faker could just have typed HHHH... by luck, or the one who insists the real coin's sequence is obviously random because it happened—and how to handle those moments without deflating the game. And be honest about what the activity can't get across: it probably can't get them to randomness for infinite sequences or to a real definition of what a successful betting strategy is, and it shouldn't pretend to.
