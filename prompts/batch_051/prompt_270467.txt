I've got a rule for how much I trust different fields, and I want to know if it's defensible. The rule is: I look at how fast and how plainly the field gets corrected. Civil engineering and surgery get corrected quickly and loudly—bridges fall, patients crash—so I treat their consensus as close to fact. Nutritional epidemiology and most macro forecasts make claims that only get 'tested' after decades, with definitions that can be stretched, and when a prediction fails the field usually says it never really made that prediction, so I discount them near zero. I use this mostly for practical decisions: what to eat, whether a stimulus bill will work, whether a new teaching method is worth adopting. When two fields conflict, I go with the faster-feedback one automatically. My friend says I'm commiting a genetic fallacy and that slow feedback doesn't make a field wrong. But I can't inspect the instruments myself, and feedback speed is cheap to observe, so it seems like the best proxy I have, even though I've never tested the rule itself. Is the rule defensible? Is there a sharper version that keeps the benefits without writing off whole disciplines, and how should I tell when a slow-feedback field actually deserves more trust than my rule gives it?
