In *On Liberty* Mill says:

"He devolves upon his own world the responsibility of being in the right against the dissentient worlds of other people; and it never troubles him that mere accident has decided which of these numerous worlds is the object of his reliance, and that the same causes which make him a Churchman in London, would have made him a Buddhist or a Confucian in Pekin."

In *The Subjection of Women* he keeps arguing that a lot of what people call "nature" in women is actually custom. I'm trying to figure out whether these are actually the same move or just adjacent. The *On Liberty* passage says we pick up our confidence from whatever social world we happen to land in; the *Subjection* says we look at the arrangements around us and mistake their regularity for natural fact. Are these two versions of the same error? If so, does the cure Mill prescribes in the *On Liberty* passage—collision with error—transfer over to the subjection-to-custom case? Or does the appeal to nature have a different structure because it's doing normative work, not just epistemic? I can't tell whether Mill is treating the second as a special case of the first, or whether there's a real difference he'd want to preserve.
