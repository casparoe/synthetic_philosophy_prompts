I work on ancient political thought and democratic theory, and I keep running into the same interpretive move: the Mytilenean episode is treated as an early textual witness for the epistemic value of democratic deliberation, because a large popular assembly first votes in anger and then, upon reconsideration, reverses itself. But Thucydides’ own description of the interval is thinner than the theoretical use made of it. In Richard Crawley’s translation of Thucydides, History of the Peloponnesian War 3.36, he writes: “The morrow brought repentance with it and reflection on the horrid cruelty of a decree, which condemned a whole city to the fate merited only by the guilty.”

I want to know whether the claim that this episode is a case of deliberation in the normative sense—collective judgment responding to reasons about the city’s interest and justice—rather than a case of emotional contagion or rhetorical manipulation that happens to produce a less bloody outcome, can be tested rigorously. We cannot rerun the assembly, but suppose I wanted to bring evidence to bear on the deliberative reading using the best available indicators, whether textual, comparative, or experimental. What would a well-designed test look like? Be concrete about what would count as evidence for the deliberative reading and what would count against it. Then say what no test of this sort could settle even in principle, and whether that residual unattainability is a real problem for the theory or only for our desire for certainty.
