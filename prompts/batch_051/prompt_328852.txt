I spent most of last evening arguing with a friend about whether the meaning of a literary text is fixed by what its author intended. I was defending the anti-intentionalist side, roughly the position associated with mid-century New Criticism, and by the end I was no longer certain which claims that position actually commits me to. My friend offered the following summary of what he took me to be defending: "Once a text is made public, its meaning is not a private event inside the author's head. It is fixed by the words themselves, in their public senses, together with the internal relations among the parts of the text. The author's stated intentions are not the standard against which a reading is judged; a note in a sketchbook or a remark in a letter tells us about the author's biography rather than what the poem means. The author is therefore just another reader, and her later interpretation carries no more authority than anyone else's. And if meaning is entirely a matter of what the words publicly say, then any interpretation that respects the words is as good as any other: a reading can be wrong only by getting the words wrong, and between two readings that both respect the plain sense there is no fact of the matter about which is better." I recognized the first part of this as close to what I meant, but the final sentences seemed to turn a claim about the sources of meaning into a claim about the equal standing of all competent readers, and I suspect he has slid from anti-intentionalism into reader-response relativism. I would like an accuracy audit of his summary. Please separate what is accurate, what is a simplification or overstatement, and what a careful anti-intentionalist would reject or qualify. In particular, tell me whether the move from "the meaning of a text is public and not fixed by authorial intention" to "any reading that respects the words is as good as any other" actually follows; if it does not, pinpoint where the slide occurs and say what the accurate version of the position licenses one to conclude instead.
