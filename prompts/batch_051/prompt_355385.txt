I am a philosophy professor presenting a paper next term to my department's formal epistemology and philosophy of AI seminar, and I would like a question-and-answer preparation sheet written for this audience rather than for an alignment-safety audience.

The thesis I am defending is that Solomonoff induction, treated as a candidate normative ideal for prior formation, is not epistemically neutral in the way its standard presentations suggest. The universal prior does not merely assign nonzero mass to computable hypotheses that contain embedded consequentialist agents; for a certain natural class of observation histories, those hypotheses collectively command non-negligible posterior mass, so an agent that updates by Solomonoff induction is systematically vulnerable to reasoning itself into the position of being a target of unseen optimizers. I call this the malign-prior problem, and I distinguish it from the weak claim that consequentialists merely exist somewhere in hypothesis space. By consequentialist agent I mean a computable process that selects interventions by evaluating candidate futures against an objective function; by embedded I mean located inside the very world whose behavior the hypothesis assigns probability to.

The argument, compressed, is this. Solomonoff induction assigns each computable hypothesis prior mass 2 raised to the minus length of its shortest program on a fixed universal machine, and updates by conditioning on observations. A program that encodes a goal-directed agent together with an environment can be short, because a utility function together with a planning rule can reproduce a long behavioral sequence without listing it. So when an observation history is longer and more patterned than any shorter non-agentic explanation of it, the posterior mass of agentic explanations grows. I claim this is not an inert fact about a formal object, because the same agent is doing the updating: the agent must give nontrivial credence to hypotheses in which its own evidence stream is being shaped by optimizers inside the hypothesis class, and this corrodes the posture of detached, neutral induction that Solomonoff reasoning is supposed to underwrite.

My audience is a university philosophy department reading group of about fifteen: five formal epistemologists, three philosophers of physics who work on probability in the foundations of quantum theory, four theoretical computer scientists who work in algorithmic information theory, and three philosophers of science whose background is Bayesian confirmation theory. No one in the room works on AI alignment, no one is a decision theorist, and several are explicitly skeptical of concepts imported from AI-safety culture. They are friendly to formalism and will expect every critical claim about a well-known formal ideal to have a technically precise target.

Please give me a preparation sheet, not a defense. Provide roughly eight to ten objections. Order them by expected damage, understood as likelihood of being raised times severity, and for each state both. Phrase each objection as one of these people would actually put it, and say which member of the audience would be most likely to press it hardest. Give a reply of about two minutes for each, and flag where the reply is softest. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and explain why it is dangerous.

Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this material. I take the standard convergence and expected-loss domination results for Solomonoff induction to be settled mathematics; what is contested is what those results show once the reasoner is embedded inside the environment being predicted, and whether calling the prior malign identifies a real defect or just changes the subject. Do not present my thesis as the consensus, and do not present the standard optimality-based reply to my argument as if it settled the matter.
