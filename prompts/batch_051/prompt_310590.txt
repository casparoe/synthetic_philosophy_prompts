I've ended up convinced that the original/derived intentionality distinction is doing basically no philosophical work — it's a boundary we draw for rhetorical reasons, not a difference in kind. My road in was roughly Dennett's two-bitser: a vending machine that reliably takes real quarters and rejects slugs has an internal state that is, in some thin sense, about quarters, but no one wants to call that original intentionality — it's inherited from the designers' purposes. What bothers me is that when you zoom in on a human brain, the aboutness of its states looks just as inherited, from evolution and training and language; and when you zoom out on the machine, the intentional stance runs fine. So the 'original' side of Searle's divide (the side the Chinese Room is supposed to lack) looks like a placeholder for 'whatever we don't yet want to call mechanical.' Lately I've noticed the same move everywhere: people say a language model's apparent concern is not real because it's derived, while human concern is original, and I think they're just refusing to say where the line could possible go.

What would count against this deflationary view? I want an honest map of its exposure, not a defense. Which arguments, cases, or empirical findings should actually move me away from it, ranked from fatal to merely awkward? What consequences is the view committed to that its defenders should concede are costs — e.g., does it collapse into 'nothing is really about anything' or 'everything is sort of about everything,' and is that a reductio or just the view? Is there a version of the original/derived distinction that survives the two-bitser, and what's the strongest case for it? And honestly: is my view even falsifiable, or have I just adopted a stance that's insulated from evidence — and if so, is that a problem for a view of this kind, or just what it is to have a stance?
