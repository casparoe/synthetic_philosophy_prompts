I am a retired evolutionary biologist; most of my career was spent modeling natural selection, genetic drift, and local adaptation in wild plant populations, and I am fluent in that vocabulary in a way I am not yet fluent in the terminology of academic philosophy. Philosophy of language is a late interest of mine, and I keep meeting a debate I would like to think through with the instruments I actually know how to use: whether there is any such thing as bad grammar. On one side, roughly, is the prescriptivist, who holds that the rules of a language supply standards of correctness and that a sentence violating them is simply incorrect, not merely unfashionable or socially disfavored. On the other side, roughly, is the descriptivist, who holds that a language, considered as a natural object, is nothing over and above the systematic behavior of its speakers, and that calling a usage 'bad grammar' is a social verdict rather than a property of the utterance itself.

I want you to restate both positions as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary of population biology and evolutionary theory: variants and their frequencies, selection pressures and selection regimes, drift, local adaptation, maladaptation, norms of reaction, phenotypic plasticity, stabilizing and purifying selection, spandrels, and the like. Please use a single running example so the translation grips a real instance rather than floating in abstraction; the double negative in English seems to me a good forcing case.

Then give me the residue report. Which commitments of each position carry over cleanly, which survive only with distortion, and which have no expression in the biological vocabulary at all? In particular, I want to know what happens to the prescriptivist's normativity: the claim that a usage can be wrong, not merely disfavored or lower in expected uptake. Does that map onto anything in my framework, or does the translation show it to be a category mistake, an empirically hollow claim, or simply something biology is silent about? I would also like your judgment on a conjecture of mine: that 'bad grammar' is best rendered as a locally maladaptive variant under an artificial selective regime, penalized in formal and written settings while neutral or even positively functional in the speech community where it is native, and that descriptivism amounts to the principle that variation is not, by itself, a defect. Is that a fair translation, or does it import biological assumptions that obscure more than they clarify? Treat the residue as the interesting part, and do not quietly settle the original debate in the course of translating it.
