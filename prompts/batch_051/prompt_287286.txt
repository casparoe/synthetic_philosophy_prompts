I keep coming back to the Ellsberg paradox in my reporting on how people make decisions under uncertainty, and it's left me torn between a clean theory and a gut feeling I can't quite disown. The clean theory is subjective expected utility: if your preferences obey a handful of axioms, including the sure-thing principle, then you act as if you're maximizing expected utility relative to some single probability assignment and a utility function. It gives a unique, tidy decision procedure, and from the inside its hard to see where you'd break it without inviting a dutch book or some other inconsistency. But in the Ellsberg setup there are two urns with a hundred balls each: Urn A has exactly 50 red and 50 black, and Urn B has red and black in unknown proportion. With equal payoffs, I'd rather bet on red from Urn A than red from Urn B, and I'd also rather bet on black from Urn A than black from Urn B. That feels sane to me — the known urn is just less, I don't know, slippery. Yet the standard argument says no single probability p for drawing red from Urn B can fit both preferences: the first preference pushes p below one-half and the second pushes p above one-half. So my gut sense that ambiguity is a distinct kind of uncertainty, and that rational people can be averse to it, seems to contradict the theory. Walk me through what each side actually costs. If I stick with expected utility, do I have to call my Ellsberg preference a bias and train myself out of it, and is that debunking move convincing or is it just refusing to listen to the intuition? If I keep the intuition and amend the theory — multiple priors, Choquet, maxmin, or whatever the sensible version of that is — what do I really lose: uniqueness, immunity to dutch books, dynamic consistency? And is there a reading on which choosing the known urn is justified by something else entirely, like the unknown urn just giving me worse information, so I was never violating the theory in the first place?
