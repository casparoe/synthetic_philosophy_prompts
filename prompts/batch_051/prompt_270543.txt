I'm a journalist writing a long-form piece about whether a society can be morally improved by changes that help nobody — what philosophers call the leveling-down objection to egalitarianism. The lay of the land is this. Prioritarianism says inequality itself has no value; benefits matter more the worse off their recipients are, so making the well-off worse off without making anyone better off is never an improvement in any respect. Egalitarianism, at least in its outcome-focused form, says inequality in the distribution of well-being is in itself bad; but then, its critics object, it must say that a change that narrows the gap by reducing the well-being of the better off and raising no one's well-being is in at least one respect an improvement — a conclusion many find absurd. I don't want a survey of the debate; I want your own probability, with the reasoning forced into the open. Please estimate your credence in the following proposition: when a change reduces the well-being of the better off, raises no one's well-being, and leaves the distribution more equal, the resulting state of affairs is in at least one genuine, non-instrumental respect better than the state it replaced. Decompose this into the sub-questions that do the work for you — at minimum: the probability that some form of egalitarianism (rather than prioritarianism, sufficientarianism, or a purely instrumental view of equality) is the correct account of distributive value; the probability, conditional on egalitarianism being correct, that the correct form is outcome-focused rather than concerned only with how inequalities are produced; the probability, conditional on that, that the expression 'better in one respect' picks out a real evaluative fact rather than a loose way of speaking that dissolves into an all-things-considered judgment; and the probability that this respect, if genuine, is a moral one rather than merely aesthetic or formal. Give each a rough number and a one-line justification, show how they combine into your overall credence, and then identify which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to, and what argument, distinction, or case would move it most.
