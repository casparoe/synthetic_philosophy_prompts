A country with a habit of turning military defeats into national stories — think the Confederacy, Masada, Dunkirk, the Battle of Kosovo as it's retold — passes a Commemorative Balance Act. Under it, any monument, museum exhibition, school lesson or state ceremony that commemerates a defeat has to give at least a third of its display space, pages or time to a counter-account of the same event, commissioned from the other side's national historical society. If the other side no longer exists as a state, an international historical association appoints the writers. Commemorations that don't comply lose public funding and access to public land.

Treat this as a mechanism, not a moral position — I don't want a verdict on whether commemorating defeats is good or bad. Walk me through what actually happens. Where does it get gamed first: tame "opposition" historians, defeats relabelled as tragedies or victories, counter-accounts written to be weak on purpose, funding routed through private donors? And what happens in the hard cases — a civil-war defeat where the "other side" is your own state, a defeat where the other side is now your ally, one where the other side is long gone? Which failure mode breaks the thing first, and can any of them be patched without making the Act a dead letter?
