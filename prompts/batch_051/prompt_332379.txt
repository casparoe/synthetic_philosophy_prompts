Two friends of mine went back and forth over dinner about whether metaphors carry meaning over and above the literal meaning of their words. I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy training, so I could follow the individual moves but not tell where the argument actually turns. I've reconstructed the exchange below.

(1) A said that a sentence like "my lawyer is a shark" has no special metaphorical meaning. The words keep their ordinary meanings, the sentence is literally false, and the metaphor's job is to cause listeners to notice certain features of the lawyer, such as ruthlessness and aggressiveness. B replied that this can't be the whole story, because the flat paraphrase "my lawyer is ruthless and aggressive" points at the same features but is plainly not equivalent to the metaphor. If both were mere causal devices for the same noticing, the difference between them would go unexplained.

(2) A said the difference is real but it is a difference in effect, and an effect is not a meaning. Two literal sentences can differ in phrasing, or two jokes can differ in the reaction they produce, while saying the same thing. Moreover, if metaphors really carried extra content, that content should be stateable in other words; the fact that every paraphrase loses something is evidence that there is nothing there to paraphrase.

(3) B said paraphrase is the wrong test. Lots of content-bearing things have no single lossless paraphrase: a proof or a program can be rewritten in different ways while still saying how to get a result. And metaphors are not isolated word-level effects. "Spend time," "save hours," "invest energy," and "budget attention" form a system, and that systematic structure is better explained by an underlying way of thinking about time as a resource than by a catalogue of unconnected effects.

(4) A said the systematicity can be explained by conventionalized language: "spend time" is a dead metaphor whose figurative history no longer matters to its current use. The interesting case, the live metaphor, is exactly the one where the effect is new rather than stored in the language. B replied that even conventional framings keep shaping reasoning, since describing an argument as "well-founded" rather than "headed in the right direction" tilts people toward evaluating it one way rather than another, so the line between dead convention and live effect cannot do the work A needs it to do.

Please adjudicate move by move: say for each of the four moves how strong it is and why, what it establishes if it succeeds, and what it overlooks. Then give an overall verdict on which side is more right, and mention anything you think both of them are missing.
