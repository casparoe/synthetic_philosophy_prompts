I am a novelist. My most recent book reconstructs a failed urban insurrection—a composite built from the surviving records of several such episodes, with the victors' consolidated account kept deliberately offstage—telling the story almost exclusively through the insurgents' own paper trail: council minutes, informant reports, intercepted letters, and the notes they kept on their own errors. I have been invited to present a paper at a university history department's methods workshop, where I will defend the following thesis. The adage that history is written by the victors should be read as a claim about the production and preservation of records, not as a claim that the victors' account has greater explanatory authority; and a disciplined reconstruction from a defeated group's own records can be more accurate, and not merely more sympathetic, because defeated communities face stronger incentives to preserve exactly what victorious narratives smooth over—contingency, failed gambits, the role of accident, and the precise points at which the outcome remained open.

Here, in compressed form, is the argument I intend to make. Victorious powers write histories that justify the outcome, which selects for inevitability and foresight. Defeated communities, denied control of the official record, invest in a different epistemic economy: error logs, internal recriminations, and accounts that preserve the counterfactual dependence of events on particular decisions. The loser's vantage therefore enjoys a real but bounded epistemic privilege for questions of causation and contingency—a privilege that must still be weighed, source by source, against every other kind of evidence.

The audience will be mostly archival social and political historians, committed to source criticism and hostile to what they call a novelist's right to invent interiority. Among them will be two narrativist philosophers of history, who will press from the opposite flank: that my appeal to greater accuracy reinstates a naive realism about what historical narratives can do. There will also be a historian of memory whose work concerns how the voices of the defeated enter the archive only through institutions the powerful control.

Prepare me a Q&A prep sheet, not a fuller defense. Give me the six or seven objections I will actually get from this audience, ordered by how likely and how damaging each is, phrased the way each of these three groups would actually phrase them, each with a two-minute reply and an honest note on where that reply is weakest. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, tell me what I should concede up front, and tell me which commitments I should hold firm on even if they cost me the room.
