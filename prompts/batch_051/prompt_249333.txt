Two people in my reading group got into it over a novel by a Black author that some reviewers are calling politically damaging because it shows characters doing ugly things and doesn’t exactly model respectability. We had just read Du Bois’s essay where he argues art is never politically neutral—that every artwork is a kind of propaganda whether the artist admits it or not. Jamal said the critics were basically right: if you accept Du Bois, then a novel that gives white readers a picture of Black pathology isn’t bad just aesthetically, it’s bad as propaganda. Priya objected that Du Bois in the same essay insists Black artists should not have to bow to what the white public or Black middle-class leaders want; he says the artist needs freedom to pursue truth and beauty, not just positive images. Jamal came back and said that’s fine as a long-term ideal, but in a racist society every story about Black people gets treated as representative, so the artist can’t pretend the political effect is someone else’s problem. Priya said that line of thinking is exactly what turns art into the uplift posters everyone says they hate, and a novel that’s psychologically true is more radical than one that tries to be good propaganda, because it refuses to treat Black characters as symbols. Jamal said refusing to treat them as symbols is itself a political move, which just proves Du Bois’s point that it’s all propaganda, so you still have to judge it by its likely effects. Priya said judging it by its likely effects is different from saying the author should have written a better message, and Du Bois wants the freedom to make art that’s true even when the effect is unpredictable. They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn’t. Could you go through their exchange and say who’s right about what—and whether either of them has Du Bois right?
