The tautology objection to natural selection runs like this: if fitness is measured by actual reproductive success, then the statement that the fitter leave more offspring is true by definition, and natural selection explains nothing. The propensity interpretation answers that fitness is not actual success but a probabilistic disposition — roughly, the expected number of surviving offspring an organism or type would produce in a specified environment, fixed independently of what any particular individual happens to leave. I want to know whether this answer makes a real empirical difference or merely moves the bookkeeping one step back. How would one test the claim that fitness, so understood, is a genuine explanatory property of organisms rather than a convenient redescription of which organisms happened to reproduce? Design the strongest test or battery of tests you can. Be concrete: say what you would manipulate or measure, what pattern in the results would favor the propensity reading, what pattern would favor the bookkeeping reading, and how decisive each pattern would be. You might, for instance, exploit the gap between expectation and realization in a uniform population, or measure components of fitness such as viability, fecundity, and mating success in one environment and use them to predict success in another, or ask when two types with the same expected success but different variances should be attributed different fitnesses. Then say exactly what the tests cannot settle — for example, whether the propensity does causal work or is only a summary statistic, whether expected values transfer across environments, and whether talk of single-case chances means anything over and above observed frequencies. I am less interested in any particular species or data set than in the logic of what would have to be true for the propensity interpretation to be more than a philosophical dodge.
