I teach moral psychology, and a few years ago I fell down the WEIRD rabbit hole—the stuff about western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic samples being the outliers rather than the human baseline. Since then I've been running a personal epistemic rule, and I honestly can't tell anymore whether it's defensible or just a bias with a nicer name.

The rule: when I'm sizing up a moral claim as an objective matter (say, that you ought not to favor your own kids over strangers when allocating some scarce public good), I start with my own reaction and then discount my credence in the claim's objectivity by an amount that tracks how much I'd bet the judgment varies cross-culturally. If the WEIRD literature makes me think my reaction is culturally local, I shave off a lot; if I'd expect it to show up everywhere, I shave off nothing. I deliberately don't check whether the variation would survive if people shared the same non-moral beliefs, because I don't trust myself to do that check honestly. And I don't let the discount touch my first-order behavior: I'd still condemn someone who harmed a kid, and I teach these claims as flat truths. My colleague says this is having it both ways—a disagreement I wouldn't count against the wrongness of torturing a child for fun, I do count against something like a duty of impartiality. Meanwhile I also use the same WEIRD findings to insist we can't generalize from our students' intuitions to human moral psychology at all.

So is the discounting rule defensible? Under what conditions does cross-cultural variation in folk morality actually bear on whether a moral claim is objectively true, and when is it just a fact about which intuitions travel well? Is there a restated version that keeps the good parts without the part where my own uncertainty is doing the work?
