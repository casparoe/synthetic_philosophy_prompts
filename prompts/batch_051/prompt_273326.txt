I've spent the last few months reading around the archaeology of belief, and I've more or less settled into the confident position: you can reconstruct a good deal about what past people believed and how their institutions — especially religious ones — actually worked from objects, architecture, and where people chose to put things, without any texts or oral tradition. I can point to the usual wins: graves with grave goods implying some kind of afterlife belief, temple layouts implying graded access to a sacred zone, deposits of figurines or specially butchered animal bone that aren't plausibly ordinary trash. What I want is the best case against my confidence. What would have to be true, or what should I go looking for in the record, for this position to be badly wrong or much weaker than I now think? I don't just want the line that absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence. Map it for me. I already know a few of the standard worries: equifinality (the same material pattern can be produced by quite different behaviors), the lazy use of ethnographic analogy (treating modern or historically attested peoples as templates for deep prehistory), and the cynical line that when archaeologists say ritual they sometimes just mean they don't know what the thing was for. Which objections are fatal to the whole project of reconstructing belief from objects, which would only shrink the range of beliefs we can claim to recover, and which are merely awkward for everyday practice? And be straight with me: is my position well insulated, and if it is, is that a problem for taking it seriously?
