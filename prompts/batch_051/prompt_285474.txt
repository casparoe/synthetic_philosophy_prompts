I'm a novelist, and for the past year I've been reading on how founding stories turn into history—mostly the Trojan War, Arthur, and the Exodus. I've landed on a working view that I'm using as the spine of my next book, and I want to know how exposed it actually is. The view is this: a story becomes history not when it gets corroborated, but when a community starts using it the way it uses history—to anchor a chronology, date festivals, legitimate a dynasty or a border, explain why a war was fought or why a treaty still obligates. The Trojan War, whatever actually happened in the Late Bronze Age, was doing that kind of work for the Greeks by the time they hung genealogies and colonial foundations on it. Arthur does it for the Welsh and then, differently, for the English. The Exodus does it for Israel even on the assumption that the numbers and the plagues don't survive archaeology. So history, on my view, is a role rather than an evidentiary status: a legend can be history while still being legend, and a true event can fail to be history if no one ever puts it to that kind of work. Thats the position.

What I want is a map of what would count against it. Not the standard objections dropped from a great height, but the cases, arguments, and observations that should actually move someone who holds the view, sorted by how decisive they'd be—from nearly fatal to merely awkward. Which historical or comparative cases would be hardest: communities that clearly have history in my sense without doing the chronology-and-legitimation work, or stories doing exactly that work that nobody wants to call history, or the three cases pulling in different directions? What would an honest defender concede counts against it, as opposed to what a critic says? And I'd like a real judgement at the end about wether the view has been quietly insulated into unfalsifiability—and if it has, whether that's a problem for a claim of this kind or just the cost of doing business for a novelist's working theory.
