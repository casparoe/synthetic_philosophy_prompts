My research is in the philosophy of international criminal law, and I defend a broadly Lemkinian view of genocide: the concept earns its place by naming the destruction of a human group as a social entity — its institutions, its cultural continuity, its capacity to persist as a collective — rather than by measuring how many people are killed. On this view, some campaigns that kill enormous numbers do not merit the term, while some that kill relatively few do. I want to know what could unseat that view. Please assemble the strongest defeaters: cases in which a group's social fabric is destroyed while its members survive, including aggressive assimilation or displacement; campaigns of mass killing undertaken for purposes unrelated to destroying the group as a collective; the thought that the law ought to track individual harm rather than the fate of a collective; and the worry, from the politics of naming, that the view hands too much discretion to those who may withhold or deploy the term for political ends. For each defeater, indicate whether a committed Lemkinian could absorb it as a mere qualification or would have to concede that it genuinely counts against the position, and note where the view makes predictions its rivals do not. Then rank the defeaters from decisive to merely awkward, and close by saying whether the view is insulated by its own formulation and whether that insulation is a problem.
