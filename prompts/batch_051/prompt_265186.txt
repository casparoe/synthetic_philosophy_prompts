I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I've been going down a rabbit hole on the old dispute about whether it's ever okay to believe something on insufficient evidence — the one usually put as Clifford saying we have a duty not to believe anything on poor evidence, and James replying that sometimes a belief can be self-fulfilling or practically forced, so evidence can't always be the whole story. I'm planning a piece that tries to drag this out of the seminar room and into the newsroom, because a lot of editorial judgement seems like applied epistemology: when do you run a single-source claim, how sure do you have to be before calling something debunked, whether covering a preprint as if it were settled is defensible, whether signal-boosting a claim counts as endorsing it, that sort of thing.

What I want from you is a defended set of priorities, not a survey. If I write the piece, which implications of the Clifford/James debate should I actually build it around, and which are overrated or plain don't follow? Rank them by how much they'd change how a newsroom actually operates if people took them seriously, and tell me your ranking criteria — I'm less interested in the list than in the reasoning behind it. I'm especially curious whether the biggest practical payoff comes from something abstract, like the idea that journalism isnt really in the believing business but in the publishing and amplifying business, so the whole debate might be aimed at the wrong mental state — or whether that's a seperate issue and the real payoff is institutional: what a publication may collectively assert is different from what any reporter individually believes. Please weigh those, plus anything else you think deserves attention, and tell me what a piece for a general reader should foreground and what it should leave alone.
