I am a philosophy professor working in private law theory, and next month I am presenting a paper at a workshop that will be dominated by contracts and civil procedure scholars with strong freedom-of-contract sympathies, plus two political philosophers who work on the public–private distinction. My paper defends this thesis: the familiar moral objections to mandatory pre-dispute arbitration—that it strips plaintiffs of their day in court, that it is imposed through adhesion contracts, and that repeat players enjoy structural advantages—can mostly be answered or absorbed by a thoughtful defender of private ordering, but one objection cannot: mandatory arbitration withdraws an entire class of disputes from the public process by which statutory and common-law norms are articulated, tested, and revised, so its central wrong is not to the individual claimant but to the public character of law itself. My argument proceeds by taking each standard objection in its strongest form and granting what the private-ordering defender can say, then arguing that confidential, non-precedential, party-controlled adjudication amounts to a form of private legislation whose costs are democratic rather than distributive, and finally insisting that these costs survive even if arbitration were faster, cheaper, and fairer than litigation. Draft my Q&A prep sheet: the objections this specific audience will actually raise, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased in their own vocabulary rather than that of a plaintiffs' attorney, each with a two-minute reply and a note on where the reply is soft. Include the objection the freedom-of-contract contingent should press more than it usually does, and the single question I am hoping the two political theorists do not ask.
