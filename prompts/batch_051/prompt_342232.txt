I am a novelist, not a logician, and I have been reading around in formal systems and self-reference because the book I am drafting turns on a character who notices, a little too late, that a rule-governed practice has a limit which cannot be seen from inside it. The puzzle I keep returning to is the MIU-puzzle, and I want to test a large philosophical claim I have put into this character's mouth. The system works as follows. You start with the string MI and may apply four transformation rules: append U to any string ending in I; double the part after M; replace III with U; and delete UU. The question is whether MU is reachable. The standard resolution is that MI contains one I, one is not a multiple of three, and every rule preserves the property of having a number of Is not divisible by three; MU contains zero Is, so MU cannot be derived. So much for the puzzle itself.

The character's argument, as I have drafted it, runs in five steps. (1) A derivation inside the MIU-system is simply a finite chain of strings, each obtained from the preceding string by one of the four rules; the system itself produces strings and never asserts anything about what it cannot produce. (2) The proof that MU cannot be derived stands outside this process and surveys all four rules at once, noting an invariant — an I-count not divisible by three — that holds for MI and is preserved by every rule but fails for MU. (3) The MIU-system cannot formulate or establish this invariant about itself; it manipulates M, I, and U, but it contains no means of saying that every reachable string has an I-count congruent to 1 or 2 modulo 3. (4) Thus the underivability of MU is a true fact about the MIU-system that the MIU-system cannot prove — a toy instance of what Gödel demonstrated for certain systems of arithmetic. (5) Therefore jumping out of the system, or JOOTSing, is not a local trick for solving this one puzzle but a general condition: any formal system will contain truths about its own limits that can be established only from outside it.

I half-suspect that the comparison to Gödel in step (4) is doing more work than it can bear, but I cannot put the error exactly, and a mathematician of my acquaintance merely said the argument was cute and declined to explain himself. Please tell me whether the argument goes through and, if not, which numbered step fails or is misleading, with a corrected version of that step. Lay out your verdict as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
