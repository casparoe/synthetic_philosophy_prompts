A friend and I just spent a long evening arguing about whether constitutional monarchy can be genuinely democratic, and by 2am I'd said a bunch of things that each felt right in the moment but that I've never had to line up next to each other. My friend kept pushing the dignified-versus-efficient line—that the monarch is pure theater and the real constitutional work happens elsewhere—and I kept answering that the theater is doing real work. Here's roughly the full set of commitments I ended up defending:

(1) A hereditary head of state is democratically indefensible, full stop; nobody should hold a public role by birth, even a mostly symbolic one.
(2) But the UK, Scandanavia, and Japan seem in practice to be among the healthiest democracies going, and the monarchy looks mildly useful rather than merely tolerated.
(3) I think the usefulness is mostly the monarch as a constitutional fire extinguisher: a non-partisan reserve actor who could refuse assent, dismiss a prime minister, or speak above party politics if the system genuinely went off the rails.
(4) Yet I also think this only works because by convention the monarch basically never uses those powers except in a real emergency; the power is real precisely because it sits their unused.
(5) And I think the vagueness and unwrittenness of the conventions (especially in the UK) is a feature, not a bug—the flexibility is what lets the whole thing adapt.
(6) But if I were writing a constitution from scratch tomorrow, I would not include a hereditary monarchy, even a purely ceremonial one.

I want an audit rather than a lecture. Which of these are actually in tension with each other, which only look like they are, and which single revision would clean up the most? Rank the tensions by severity instead of treating them all as equally bad, and say specifically what would have to give in each case.
