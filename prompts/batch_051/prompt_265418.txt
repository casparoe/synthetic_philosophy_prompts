You are a political philosopher and historian of medieval thought, writing a careful case analysis for a reader who already knows the basics of Aquinas and of the Second World War. Work slowly and explicitly: first reconstruct the relevant principles, then map them onto the case, then assess fit. Brainstorm alternative classifications before committing, and flag every place where the theory is silent or pulls in two directions.

Analyze the attempt of 20 July 1944 — the bomb plot against Hitler at the Wolf's Lair and the planned coup known as Operation Valkyrie, led by Colonel Claus von Stauffenberg and a clandestine circle of officers and civilians — through the lens of Aquinas's account of tyranny and tyrannicide.

Lay out the parts of Aquinas's view you are using. In the De regno and in the treatment of sedition in the Summa theologiae, Aquinas holds that a government ordered to the ruler's private advantage rather than the common good is tyrannical, and that resisting a tyrant is not itself sedition; in an important sense the tyrant is the seditious one. But he complicates this. He distinguishes a tyrant by usurpation, who seizes power without any genuine claim, from a legitimate ruler who degenerates into tyranny in the exercise of an office rightly held. He treats resistance as primarily a public remedy — something belonging to the community that made the ruler, or to its public authority — and is markedly wary of private persons taking it on themselves to kill the ruler on their own initiative. And he adds a prudential condition: resistance is to be forborne when it would likely produce a worse disorder than the tyranny it removes, or merely replace one tyrant with another.

Now work through the episode with these tools.

Salient facts to incorporate: Hitler had acquired office through legal forms under the Weimar constitution before dismantling that constitution, so the case looks like lawful ruler turned tyrant rather than bare usurpation. The plotters were a self-selected minority, not the community or a constituted public authority; many had sworn personal oaths of allegiance to Hitler; their plan assumed that killing Hitler would trigger a seizure of the state by the Replacement Army; and they themselves feared that failure, or even success, might bring civil war, the SS in power, or chaos on the Eastern front.

1. Classification. Under Aquinas's categories, what kind of tyrant was Hitler, and what exactly were the conspirators — private persons, the community acting through an organ, or some intermediate thing? Assess the expected aftermath at the moment of the bomb: what did success plausibly produce, what did failure produce, and which projection should govern under the prudential condition?

2. Verdict. What does the theory say the conspirators ought to have done, considered distinction by distinction? Where do Aquinas's principles conflict for this case?

3. Fit. What does the theory explain well about July 1944, and what does it miss or misdescribe? Weigh in particular the personal oath to Hitler, the absence of any public authority to appeal to inside a totalitarian state, and the plotters' claim to be acting not against the state but for the restoration of a lawful constitutional order.

4. Reverse direction. Is this episode a confirmation of Aquinas's caution about private action against tyrants, an anomaly that exposes how much his theory presupposes a functioning community and public authority, or both? It is acceptable to conclude that the case is partly indeterminate; say precisely where and why.
