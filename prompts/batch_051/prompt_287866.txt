Act as a professional philosopher working on the ethics of loyalty, whistleblowing, and secrecy, and think carefully before you commit to an answer. I’m a student in a course on loyalty and professional ethics, and my professor has been handing down strong verdicts on bleeding-heart whistleblower cases all term, but she refuses to state the general rule she’s actually applying. I’m trying to reverse-engineer it before the exam.

Her track record so far:

1. A quality-control engineer at an auto parts company discovers the firm is shipping brake pads that fail in freezing temperatures. She files internal safety reports for eight months, gets ignored, then sends the test data to the federal safety regulator. Prof: she did the right thing.

2. A hospital orderly tips off a local newspaper about spoiled food and rodents in the kitchen. He’s never raised it with management or the health department, and parts of his tip turn out to be exaggerated, causing a panic. Prof: he acted wrongly; he should have gone to the health inspector or up the chain first.

3. An intelligence analyst discovers the agency is running warrantless surveillance on domestic advocacy groups. After two years of working the official complaint channels with no response, he leaks only the existence of the program, nothing operational. Prof: justified.

4. A junior employee, passed over for a promotion, gives a reporter a confidential internal memo showing the person who got the job once overbilled a client by a small amount and later quietly refunded it. Prof: wrong—that’s a grudge using private documents, not whistleblowing.

5. A postdoc learns her close friend and coauthor fabricated a figure in a published paper. She confronts him first, gives him a chance to correct it with the journal, and when he refuses reports him to the university integrity office. Prof: right; the friendship makes it agonizing but doesn’t make it wrong.

New case: a chemical-plant safety engineer spends a year reporting internally that the plant is dumping a carcinogen into a river that supplies the town’s drinking water, and management repeatedly buries her findings. She files the internal test data with the state environmental agency, and only after the agency delays for six weeks does she give the same documents to a local reporter. The plant manager who helped bury the findings is the same boss who passed her over for a promotiton, and by her own account her motives are about half stopping the poisoning and half ending his career.

What would my professor most likely say about this new case, if she’s consistent with her earlier verdicts? What are the two or three most plausible rules that fit her track record, what does each predict here, and which is your best guess about the rule she’s actually running? Also, what one additional case would best settle which rule she holds? Start by brainstorming the different principles that fit before you commit.
