Okay, so I'm entering a type design competition and the brief has a clause that's caused a proper row at the studio. The exact wording is:

"Each lowercase character must preserve the essential structure of that character as it appears in the source model. Weight, contrast and finish may be reinterpreted, but no character may be simplified to the point that its identity depends on context alone."

My entry is based on a French Renaissance source, so the a has a double-storey structure with a final tail. I've drawn mine as a single-storey or schoolbook a (just a bowl and a tail), because I want the family to work for children's reading books as well. The judges say this falls foul of the clause because the essential structure of the source's a is double-storey. I think that's too quick: it's still an a, just an a from a different structural model.

So my actual question isn't just "am I disqualified?" but what "essential structure of the character" could even mean here. Read by the plain words, does the clause rule out my a? Read by its purpose, does it? Is the single-storey/double-storey difference a difference in the essence of the letterform or just a difference in style? And wether it matters that, in context, nobody would read the glyph as anything other than an a.
