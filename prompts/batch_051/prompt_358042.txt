In the first chapter of *An Essay on the Principle of Population* (1798), Malthus asks his reader to compare two rates of increase: population, when unchecked, grows in a geometrical ratio, while the means of subsistence can at best be made to grow in an arithmetical one. In a recent discussion group I attended, someone compressed this into the financial analogy that population is like capital growing at compound interest, whereas food production is like the same capital growing at simple interest; the second can never keep pace with the first, so the eventual collision with scarcity is mathematically built into the system. The analogy was then used to defend the view that Malthus was right but early: the basic exponential-versus-linear structure remains sound, and the fact that fossil energy, industrial nitrogen, and the demographic transition postponed the crunch only means his timing was off, not his central mechanism.

As a retired microbiologist, I keep seeing the same shape of argument when people treat a bacterial batch culture as a miniature Malthusian trap: exponential proliferation until the limiting substrate is exhausted, then stationary phase or collapse. I would like an audit of this analogy rather than a history lesson. What corresponds to what if I draw up a proper correspondence table—what plays the role of the population, the subsistence, the carrying capacity, the lag phase, and the eventual check? Where does the analogy break, and which disanalogies matter for the intended conclusion? In particular, I suspect the batch-culture and compound-interest pictures both quietly import the idea that the limiting factor is fixed or exogenous, whereas in human economies the limiting factors themselves expand and have historically expanded. Does the right-but-early conclusion survive once the disanalogies are made explicit, or is it being carried largely by the analogy?
