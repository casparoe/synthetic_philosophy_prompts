I've been reading about Abelard's view that sin lies wholly in consenting to what one knows one ought not to do, and not in the outward act itself. I think I follow the basic idea, but I keep getting stuck on a consequence that seems to follow from it.

If the guilt is complete at the moment of consent, then actually carrying out the deed adds no guilt of its own. That seems to imply that, as far as moral culpability goes, there is no difference between someone who consents to a wrong and then does it and someone who consents to the same wrong but is prevented at the last moment. But our legal systems almost everywhere treat the completed act as worse than the bare attempt: the person who succeeds is punished more severely than the person who tries and fails. If Abelard is right, does that mean this entire legal practice rests on a moral mistake? Or does he have some way of saying that the outward act matters even though it adds nothing to the sin?

I cannot see a third option: either the act matters, or it does not. If it does not matter, why should the law care whether the deed was completed? If it does matter, how is that consistent with saying the sin is only in the consent? Please show me what is wrong with the way I have set this up, and where Abelard's view actually leaves the difference between attempt and completion.
