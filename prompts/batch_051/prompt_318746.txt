I want to know whether an argument can survive a change in its background assumptions. The argument is the familiar one in animal ethics that runs from sentience to equal consideration: if a being can suffer, then it has interests; those interests deserve equal consideration with our own; and to discount them merely because the being is not human, while giving full weight to the interests of non-rational or severely cognitively disabled humans, is speciesism. This argument has almost always been run with an implicit binary picture of sentience: a being either is sentient or is not, and once it has crossed that threshold, its interests get the same consideration as ours. Now suppose we replace that picture with a graded one, on which sentience is a matter of degree with no clean threshold. At one end there are clear cases such as a healthy adult human; somewhere in the middle are insects with only the most rudimentary capacity for nociception; and at the far end are systems that merely register and respond to stimuli, such as a thermostat adjusting to a temperature change or a simple robot that backs away from a ledge, which we might naturally describe as sensitive to their environment without feeling anything. On this revised picture, the relevant capacity shades off by degrees rather than switching on. My question is whether the argument from sentience to equal consideration survives this revision. Which premise does the graded picture actually put under pressure: the claim that sentience is what matters, the claim that all interests get equal weight, or the hidden premise that sentience is binary? Is there a repaired version of the argument that keeps the species-neutral core while conceding gradations, or does it force the conclusion that a being's interests count in proportion to its degree of sentience, thereby abandoning the equal-consideration result? If the argument is dead in its original form, say what should take its place; if it needs repair, give the repaired version and state what it no longer entitles us to claim. Do not use headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables; answer in continuous prose only, the way you would write an email or a letter.
