Below are the notes for a short essay in formal epistemology. I would like detailed comments on the essay as planned. Please address each of the following: (a) whether the central argument is valid once the thought experiment is made precise; (b) whether the essay characterizes the self-sampling assumption and the self-indication assumption fairly; (c) whether the proposed conclusion is too strong, too weak, or about right; and (d) what the strongest objection to the essay would be, and how the essay could be revised to answer it.

The essay concerns the disagreement between the self-sampling assumption (SSA) and the self-indication assumption (SIA) about how to update on one's own existence. As the essay defines them, SSA says to reason as if one were randomly selected from the observers in an appropriate reference class, while SIA says that, other things equal, the fact that one exists favors hypotheses on which there are more observers. The two rules come apart in the familiar presumptuous-philosopher scenario, in which two theories are stipulated to make the same predictions about everything observable, except that one theory predicts vastly more observers than the other. SIA then appears to make the more populous theory overwhelmingly likely, while SSA does not, and critics of SIA treat this as a reductio.

The essay's working claim is that this scenario gets its argumentative force from leaving the observer's evidence thinned out to the bare fact of existence. Real observers always have richer indexical evidence: they are a particular person, at a particular time and place, with particular memories, standing in particular relations to the populations their hypotheses describe. Once this total evidence is specified, the two theories are no longer equivalent without further stipulation. A theory that predicts vastly more observers must also say where those observers are and what evidence they have. If the additional observers do not share the observer's total evidence, then SIA, applied to total evidence rather than to bare existence, need not favor the more populous theory at all. If instead the additional observers are stipulated to be evidential duplicates, then the two theories differ only in how many observer-tokens realize a fixed observer-type, and the apparent disagreement between SSA and SIA collapses into a dispute about which reference class to adopt — roughly, actual observer-tokens versus possible observer-tokens. The essay concludes that the presumptuous philosopher is not a clean refutation of SIA as such, but a symptom of an unresolved problem about how to fix a reference class, a problem SSA and SIA both face.

The planned sections are as follows. (1) Set up Bayesian updating over hypotheses that imply different total numbers of observers, and state explicitly what evidence the observer is conditioning on. (2) Define SSA and SIA over total evidence, and distinguish two readings of SIA that the informal statement runs together. (3) Reconstruct the presumptuous-philosopher scenario and show that its key stipulation is ambiguous between those readings. (4) Argue that on one disambiguation the scenario lacks its usual force, while on the other the disagreement reduces to a reference-class choice rather than a substantive claim about whether existence is evidence. (5) Develop the conclusion that the deeper question is what fixes a reference class, and explain why that question is harder and more interesting than the presumptuous philosopher suggests.
