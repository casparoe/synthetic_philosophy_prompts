A friend of mine recently asked whether I'd push a button that has a tiny chance of saving an astronomically large number of people over a button that certainly saves a decent number, and my answer kept changing depending on how the numbers were presented. Forget money for this one: utility over money is curved and would just muddy things, and I do think one extra life saved is one extra unit, so lives is where I want to test my view. Here are my actual gut verdicts.

- Certainly save 9,000 people, or a 1-in-100 chance of saving 1,000,000: I take the gamble.
- Certainly save 9,000, or a 1-in-1,000,000 chance of saving 10,000,000,000: I take the gamble.
- Certainly save 9,000, or a 1-in-10^12 chance of saving 10^16: I take the certain 9,000.
- Certainly save 9,000, or a 1-in-10^30 chance of saving 10^34: I take the certain thing, and I feel like the gamble isn't even a live option.

Every one of those gambles has the same expected value, 10,000 lives, against a certain 9,000. So expected value maximisation says I should take them all, but I dont, and I can't tell what principle I'm actually following. Is there a defensible probability threshold below which tiny chances count as nothing? Is there a cap on how many lives can count, so that going from 10^16 to 10^34 lives doesn't buy anything? Or am I just scope insensitive at the big numbers while the small probabilities are doing all the work? I'd like you to try to give me a principle (or a short list of candidates) that gets my verdicts, or close, and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot fit. Also tell me what it says about two buttons I didn't list: a 1-in-5 chance of saving 50,000 lives, and a 1-in-10^50 chance of saving 10^54 lives. Finally, flag the weakest part of the principle you land on: what argument would make you change it, and where in your reasoning (or in my verdicts) things are most likely to fall apart.
