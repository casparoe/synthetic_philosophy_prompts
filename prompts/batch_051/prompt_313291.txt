I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a feature about why students make the same wrong predictions in basic physics over and over, even after they've been taught Newton, and why the errors pattern up so wierdly across different people. The idea I want the piece to actually get across is that these aren't mostly random slips or holes in knowledge; they're the output of a fairly stable, mostly tacit theory of motion that people assemble from everyday pushing and throwing, something in the neighborhood of a medieval impetus theory. The tell that it's a theory and not a pile of mistakes is that it predicts: give someone a new case and they'll produce the wrong answer their theory requires, not just a guess. I want the explanatory arc planned, not the piece written. What does a reader need in place before it even makes sense to call a student's wrong answer a theory rather than an error? Which one example should carry the whole piece, and what does it have to have, and what does it have to not have, for it to do the work? Where in the sequence should I head off the predictable reader reactions — 'this is just a teaching problem,' 'so undergrads are pre-Newtonian physicists?', 'if it's a theory, they should be able to state it,' 'but most students eventually get the right answer, so who cares' — and which of these are better absorbed than refuted? And how will I know by the end whether a reader has got the distinction between something that's merely false and something that's false because it follows from a coherently wrong way of modeling motion? I've got alot of half-formed notes and don't mind the piece being a little in the weeds, but leave out the stuff only a specialist would need.
