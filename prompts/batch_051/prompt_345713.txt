I retired from a thirty-year career in physics a few years back (mostly statistical mechanics, with some quantum foundations reading on the side), and I've gotten into philosophy as a hobby. Right now I'm working through the free will litterature, and one move keeps bothering me: people saying that because quantum mechanics is indeterministic at the bottom, determinism is false and the free will problem just evaporates. That looks like a non-sequitur to me, so I've been collecting the reasons people give for why quantum randomness doesn't actually help freedom. My list so far has four entries:

(1) Chance is not control. If a neural event that leads to my action is genuinely random, that doesn't give me authorship, it takes it away. A choice that comes out of a random draw with probability p is a lottery, not an act of mine.

(2) The decoherence/scale problem. Quantum coherence in a warm, wet macroscopic system like the brain washes out almost immediately. Whatever indeterminism might survive is at the level of thermal noise in ion channels or vesicle release; if that noise got amplified into a decision between two actions, the result would be unrelliability rather than freedom, and neural systems look built to suppress exactly that kind of noise.

(3) The wrong kind of could-have-done-otherwise. Even if there were real branch points in my neurons, the fact that the prior physical state plus the laws didn't fix my choice only shows the choice was underdetermined by physics. It doesn't yet show I had the ability relevant to responsibility; that question just moves up a level to whatever is supposed to explain why the agent took one branch rather than another, and 'it was random' is not that explanation.

(4) The compatibilist deflation. The sense of free choice we should care about — acting from your own reasons, not being coerced or compelled — doesn't depend on whether the underlying physics is deterministic. If that's right, quantum randomness is a solution to a problem we never had, and mostly it adds a nuisance (unpredictability where what we wanted was rational control).

What I'm wondering is whether there's a fifth argument at this same level of abstraction that I'm missing. By different I don't mean a rewording of one of these four, or a special case, or a mashup of two of them; I mean a genuinely distinct reason for thinking quantum indeterminacy does no work for free will. And if you give me one, tell me whether you think it's stronger or weaker than the four already on my list.
