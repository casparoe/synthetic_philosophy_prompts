Question (40 minutes, unseen):

In coherence arguments for expected utility maximisation, a distinction is typically drawn between a synchronic Dutch book and a diachronic money pump. Explain that distinction. Then assess the following claim: an agent with intransitive preferences can be led through a cycle of trades, paying a small amount at each stage only to end up where she began, and this demonstrates that intransitive preferences are irrational. In evaluating the claim, consider whether the argument proves too much or too little, and identify at least one assumption on which its force depends.

Candidate answer:

Both arguments aim to show that violating a norm of expected utility theory leaves an agent exploitable, but they work in different ways. A Dutch book is synchronic: a bookmaker offers a single package of bets, each of which appears fair or favourable given the agent's own degrees of belief, yet the package yields a certain loss no matter which state of the world obtains. A money pump is diachronic: a trader offers a sequence of exchanges over time, each of which the agent accepts because it moves her to something she strictly prefers. Suppose her preferences over three options are intransitive: A is preferred to B, B is preferred to C, and C is preferred to A. The trader can begin with the agent holding C, then offer B for C plus a small fee, which she accepts because she prefers B to C. He can then offer A for B plus a fee, and finally C for A plus a fee. The agent ends where she began, minus the fees. Since each step is an improvement by her own lights while the whole sequence is plainly self-defeating, the argument shows the agent to be irrational. Its force is the same as the Dutch book's: preferences that can be exploited in this way cannot be an acceptable guide to action, because they make the agent a sure loser. Indeed, a sufficiently devious trader can always construct such a sequence against someone with cyclic preferences, so the intransitive agent has no reply.
