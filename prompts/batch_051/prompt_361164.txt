Every pop-science treatment of the fine-tuning argument trots out that firing-squad analogy: a guy survives a firing squad, and even though he obviously couldn't be around to be surprised if he'd died, he's still rationally entitled to be shocked and look for an explanation (they all missed on purpose? the guns were loaded with blanks?). The point is supposed to be that the "you can't be surprised by your own existence" objection to fine-tuning doesn't work. But I've also seen people object that the analogy quietly smuggles in exactly what's at issue — shooters with aims, a procedure designed to kill. I'd like an audit of how far this analogy really carries. Spell out what's meant to map onto what (the squad, surviving, the missed shots, the marksmen's intentions, the possible explanations), then identify where the mapping breaks down, which features the analogy imports that the chance/multiverse story doesn't have, and whether the conclusion it's used to support — that fine-tuning is still surprising evidence in need of explanation — survives once the disanalogies are made explicit. If there's a better analogy for what fine-tuning does and doesn't show, I'd like to hear that too.
