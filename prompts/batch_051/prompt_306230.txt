I am a journalist covering science and ideas, and I want to understand whether a famous philosophical observation can be brought into the laboratory. In G.E. Moore's essay A Reply to My Critics, he notes an asymmetry between a sentence of the form, I went to the pictures last Tuesday but I do not believe that I did, and the corresponding sentence about someone else, She went to the pictures last Tuesday but she does not believe that she did. The second can be true and is perfectly assertable; the first, Moore thought, cannot be coherently asserted even though it might be true. The generic version, p but I do not believe p, has come to be called Moore's paradox.

I want to treat this not as a closed conceptual puzzle but as a claim that might be tested. Suppose someone says: the oddity of Moorean assertions shows that there is a structural, possibly logical, prohibition on combining self-ascribed ignorance with first-person assertion. Another person says: no, the content is perfectly consistent; the problem is that the act of asserting p conventionally signals belief that p, so the assertion pragmatically defeats itself. A third says: the oddity is neither logical nor merely conversational; it reveals something about the functional architecture of self-knowledge, because a creature that sincerely produced such sentences would have to fail to integrate its first-order and second-order states.

As a journalist, my job is to ask what evidence could adjudicate among these. Act as a professional philosopher and cognitive scientist. Begin by brainstorming the exact operationalizable predictions each reading would make. Then design the best test you can of the claim that the Moorean prohibition is structural rather than merely pragmatic. It may involve human subjects, language models, or other systems. Specify what result would count for and against each account, and say what a positive result would and would not establish. Finally, be explicit about what no experiment can settle in principle—for example, whether the norm governing first-person assertion is constitutive or merely a strong convention.

I ask that you separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
