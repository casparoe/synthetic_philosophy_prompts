Two philosophers of music who have been arguing past each other for years have agreed to publish the results of an adversarial collaboration as one joint statement both can sign. One holds a sound-structuralist view: a musical work just is an acoustically specified structure, a type whose tokens are sound-events, so that two performances are of the same work exactly when they realize the same sound-structure; instruments, performance practice, notation, and historical context bear on work identity only through their audible effects. The other holds a contextualist view: a musical work is a composer's historically situated act of specifying a sound-structure together with the prescribed performance means, so that work identity is sensitive to more than the resulting sound — prescribed instrumentation, the circumstances of composition, and the practice of realization can all enter in. On this view, independently composed identical structures can be different works, and a note-for-note realization on the "wrong" forces can be an arrangement or a distinct work rather than a performance of the original.

Write the joint statement they could both sign. This is a drafting problem, not a debate and not a verdict on who is right: the value lies in finding formulations that are neither vacuous nor slanted, and in separating disagreements that are genuine from ones that were only about words.

Use this structure:

1. Agreed claims — a numbered list.

2. Disagreements — a table. In the left column, state each residual disagreement once and neutrally; in the middle column, give a formulation the sound-structuralist would sign; in the right column, give a formulation the contextualist would sign. Neither formulation may be a caricature of the view.

3. Cruxes and tests. For each crux, identify the sub-question such that settling it would move at least one side; specify a case or argument both sides accept as bearing on it; and record each side's prediction about that case in advance.

The joint statement should address at least these cases:

(a) an early-eighteenth-century harpsichord suite performed note-for-note on a synthesizer in an obviously electronic, non-imitative timbre;

(b) an orchestral work re-scored for a different ensemble and engineered so that a trained listener cannot distinguish the result from a performance in the original scoring;

(c) two composers independently arriving at the same simple tune;

(d) a fixed-media electronic work that exists only as an audio file, with no score and no performer;

(e) a jazz improvisation that keeps a standard's thirty-two-bar chord changes but never states the original melody in recognizable form.

For each case, make clear what the joint statement implies about whether a performance (where one occurs) is of the work, and about whether the same work is involved.

If, in drafting, you find that the two positions collapse into each other once "same sound-structure" and "performance means" are made precise, or that the real fault line runs somewhere other than where I have placed it, do not quietly repair the brief: say so in a preamble and then draft the statement across the corrected fault line. Likewise, do not pad the disagreements table to keep the two sides symmetrical; a short table of genuine disagreements is better than a full one of manufactured ones.
