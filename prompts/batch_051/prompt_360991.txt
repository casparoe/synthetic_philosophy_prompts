In my classics elective we have been reading about how the gods swear their most serious oaths by the river Styx, and what struck me is that the oath seems to have force because the penalty for perjury is automatic: the god who swears falsely is laid low and excluded from the company of the other gods whether or not they bother to enforce anything. That made me wonder what is left of an oath's binding force when there is no Styx mechanism — no standing enforcer that triggers on its own. A friend argued that nothing is left, and he set out his reasoning like this:

(1) An oath has binding force only if taking it gives the person a new reason to do the thing sworn.
(2) Taking an oath gives a person a new reason only if it exposes the person to a penalty that would not otherwise have applied.
(3) A penalty can attach to an oath only if some enforcer reliably detects the violation and imposes the penalty.
(4) An ordinary private oath — for example, swearing on one's mother's life with nobody else present — has no enforcer of that kind.
(5) Therefore a private oath exposes the swearer to no new penalty, creates no new reason, and has no binding force.

I want to resist this conclusion, but I cannot tell whether the argument is actually valid and the problem is in one of the premises, or whether there is a hidden gap between the steps. - fault_location: the number of the earliest premise or inference that fails, or null if none does;
