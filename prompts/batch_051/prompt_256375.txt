Here's an argument that's been bugging me, and I can't find the move I don't trust. It's about the Allais paradox and the independence axiom.

Take the usual common-consequence choices, with the payoffs in my own words: A, a guaranteed $1,000,000. B, an 89% chance of $1,000,000, a 10% chance of $5,000,000, and a 1% chance of nothing. Then C, an 11% chance of $1,000,000 (nothing otherwise), versus D, a 10% chance of $5,000,000 (nothing otherwise). Notice C and D are just A and B with the common 89% chance of $1,000,000 swapped for an 89% chance of nothing. Most people, me included, take A over B but D over C. That's the standard independence violation, if outcomes are just money.

A defender of expected utility theory says: the real lesson is that outcomes must be specified as everything the agent cares about, not just money — the regret of having gambled and lost, the special felt value of certainty, whatever. With those enriched outcomes, the choices might satisfy the independence axiom after all. Fine. But the move seems to generalize until it eats everything:

(1) An apparent violation of independence only counts against the axiom if the outcomes are correctly specified.
(2) Correctly specified means: includes whatever the agent ultimately cares about, not just the money.
(3) Any pattern of choices that looks like an independence violation can be redescribed as consistent with the axiom by adding some unobservable mental consequence (regret, relief, pride, etc.) to the outcomes.
(4) So no actual choice data could ever disconfirm the independence axiom.
(5) An axiom that is compatible with every possible pattern of choices does no real work in a theory of rational choice.
(6) So the redescription defense either turns expected utility theory into an empty formalism, or it doesn't actually explain the Allais pattern.

I can't accept (6), and I'd most like to keep (2), but I don't see how to stop the slide from (2) to (3). Which step do I reject, if any? Explain why that step looked fine, and what rejecting it costs — for the expected-utility side and for someone who thinks the Allais choices are reasonable as they stand. Keep the diagnosis under 600 words.
