I spent my working life in the natural sciences and have come to political philosophy only recently, so I am less interested in whether an institutional design is appealing than in how the thing would actually run. I have been reading about charter cities, private cities, and seasteading, and specifically the question of whether one can genuinely opt out of a state. I would like you to work through a modified design I have been turning over.

Consider an experimental city established on land leased from a host state. The host state receives a fixed annual fee per resident and in return agrees to a thirty-year standstill: neither its civil nor its criminal law applies within the territory. An independent monitor may, however, terminate the standstill early on narrow due-process grounds. Inside the city, civil and commercial law are supplied by four accredited private providers. Each resident selects one provider each twelve-month term and pays the same flat fee regardless of provider; the provider keeps that fee minus a fixed contribution to shared infrastructure. Each provider must publish its full rules in advance, with changes permitted only at the annual boundary, and may not decline any resident. An elected resident council sets the flat fee, accredits the four providers, and re-tenders the bundled policing, criminal-justice, and external-defense contract every five years; it has no other powers. Any resident may leave at any time with all assets on payment of a small administrative fee, and providers, employers, and the city are forbidden to impose exit penalties.

Treat this as a piece of social machinery rather than as a vision. Which residents and firms would it attract, and what would the four providers actually compete on once price and exit are fixed? How would each party seek to game the arrangement—residents, firms, providers, the security contractor, the accrediting council, and the host state—and what second-order effects should I expect? In particular, where does the state re-enter through the back of the building even with the front door locked? Does annual choice of law plus free exit amount to genuinely opting out of a state, or does it mostly work for those whose assets are cheap to move while leaving everyone else inside a smaller monopoly? Finish by distinguishing the failure modes that rule changes could fix from those that are intrinsic to a territorial city standing on someone else's sovereign land.
