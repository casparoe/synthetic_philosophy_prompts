Right, I could do with a professional philosopher of mind on this one. Work through it carefully and don't just feed me the canned answer, because I'm genuinely torn.

Here's the checklist I got taught for deciding whether an animal is conscious:

(a) it has some kind of unified perceptual picture, not isolated reflexes;
(b) it can genuinely learn, not just habituate or run a fixed response;
(c) it makes flexible trade-offs between competing needs, like risk against food;
(d) there is something it is like to be it — proper phenomenology, not mere information processing;
(e) it has a model of itself it can update, so it can tell 'that's my own body' from 'that's a predator'.

Now take a honeybee. It navigates several kilometres, does the waggle dance, learns which flowers pay and when, and there are experiments suggesting it weighs up risk while foraging. But it has around a millon neurons and no cortex. Go through (a)–(e) honestly: say which it passes, which it fails, and which criteria are doing no real work in the list. Then do the same for a goldfish, which a lot of people would place differently even though its brain is also tiny and built on a totally different plan.

Here's the bit that's bugging me. If the bee ticks the boxes but my gut still says 'not really conscious', is that a problem with the checklist or a problem with my gut? Is being a tick-box consciousness actually the same as being conscious, or does the checklist need another condition that it didn't know it needed? I'd rather you start by sketching where you'd draw the line and why, then apply it to both animals, and finish with a clear verdict: on your best account, is the bee conscious, is the goldfish conscious, and where exactly does the line fall?
