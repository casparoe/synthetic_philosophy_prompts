I have been puzzling over whether a bigger population is a better one, and I keep finding myself caught in a simple argument that seems to prove too much. I cannot decide whether it is valid, and I cannot locate the mistake.

(1) Adding a new person to a population makes the population better, as long as the added person's life is good overall and no one else is made worse off.

(2) Starting from a town of ten thousand people with excellent lives, one could add people one by one, each with a life that is only barely good: good enough to be worth living, and no more, while leaving everyone already there exactly as well off.

(3) "Better than" behaves properly here: if outcome A is better than B, and B is better than C, then A is better than C.

(4) Therefore, after enough separate additions, a city of millions whose typical life is barely worth living would be better than the original town of ten thousand flourishing people.

(5) Carrying the same reasoning further yields the conclusion that an enormous population whose lives are on average barely worth living is better than a much smaller population whose lives are all excellent.

The conclusion in (5) strikes me as unacceptable, yet each step looked right to me as I wrote it. Is the argument valid? If it is, which premise is doing the work that gets me to the unacceptable conclusion, and what exactly do I give up if I reject it? If it is not valid, at which step does the inference break, and why? Finally, is there a way to keep the thought in (1) that adding a good life, other things equal, is an improvement without being pushed all the way to (5)?
