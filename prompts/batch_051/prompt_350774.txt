I buy and sell secondhand goods for a living, and for years I have watched the same gap appear over and over: a person will often demand far more money to part with something they already own than they would pay to acquire that same thing if it belonged to someone else. The standard explanation is loss aversion, the tendency to weigh a loss more heavily than an otherwise equal gain. I have come to suspect that this explanation bundles together two different phenomena.

The first is a genuine ownership-interest. That a thing is mine, that I chose it, that it has a history with me, or that it belonged to someone I cared about is itself a source of value, and that value seems no less legitimate than a taste for one style over another. The second is a corrective shortfall: I hold an inflated sense of what the thing would fetch on the market or of how much losing it would hurt, and better information, market experience, and time would shrink the error.

My working claim is that these two can be pulled apart. I expect that for an object a person received yesterday by lottery and never chose, the premium is mostly corrective shortfall, and it should shrink once the person sees actual sale prices and watches a few comparable transactions. For an object a person chose, used daily, or inherited, I expect the premium to be mostly durable attachment, and it should survive full information and repeated opportunities to trade, because it tracks something about the particular object that the person actually values.

Tell me how I would test this, as rigorously as a non-specialist can. Give me a concrete design: what groups to compare, what to measure, and what pattern of results would count for or against my claim. Then be honest about what even the cleanest possible result would leave unsettled, especially whether a durable attachment premium should be called a legitimate preference rather than a bias, and whether that leftover question is an empirical one or a normative one.
