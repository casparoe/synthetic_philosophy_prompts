Suppose that in 2026 a country much like Canada or the United Kingdom around 2000 — parliamentary, with a professional civil service and a settled culture of regulatory impact assessment — adopts a constitutional amendment making satisficing the mandatory decision procedure for economically significant regulation. Before an agency may issue any regulation whose compliance costs exceed a modest statutory threshold, the agency must do the following:

1. Publish numerical aspiration levels covering every major impact its governing statute requires it to consider — for example, tonnes of a pollutant abated per year, maximum compliance cost per affected firm, minimum net jobs retained, or maximum administrative burden in hours.
2. Publish the order in which it will examine a specified set of candidate regulatory designs, and then examine those designs one at a time, adopting the first design that meets every aspiration level.
3. Stop. If a design meets all aspiration levels and the agency continues searching in the hope of finding a better one before adopting that design, the resulting regulation is void. Judicial review of the aspiration levels is limited to a single question: whether a reasonable agency could have believed, on the evidence then available, that the targets were achievable. Courts may not ask whether the targets should have been stricter, looser, or otherwise optimized.

Predict how this system actually operates by 2056, with attention to mechanisms rather than a single yes-or-no verdict. In particular: where do aspiration levels tend to settle, and which actors push them in which direction? How do agencies choose the order in which they examine designs, and how does that choice shape the rules that get adopted? How quickly do regulated firms, interest groups, and their lawyers adapt to the fact that the first passable design wins? Does the prohibition on searching past a satisfactory design actually bind in practice, or is it evaded — and if so, how? And what happens to the quality, stringency, and innovation of regulation over a generation? For each major prediction, say whether it is robust or assumption-dependent, and identify what would have to differ — in the political system, the judiciary, or the surrounding economy — for your central prediction to be wrong.

End with a single final line beginning "Bottom line:" that states your overall prediction in one sentence.
