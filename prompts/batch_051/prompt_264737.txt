Okay, so I'm not an academic or anything, but I read Huizinga's Homo Ludens a while back and it's been rattling around my head ever since, especially because I'm a gamer. The way he describes play is: it's voluntary, it steps outside ordinary life into its own closed-off little world, it has its own order and rules that everyone takes seriously for as long as it lasts, and crucially it's done for its own sake, not to produce something or to get paid. He thinks all kinds of culture — law, art, religion, even war — grew out of this play-instinct, and he was allready grumpy that the modern world was draining the play out of things by making everything professional and commercial.

So here's the question I can't settle. If you could bring Huizinga back and actually make him understand a modern video game — let's say a ranked competitive game with pro leagues and sponsorships, or a big online game where people grind daily quests for loot boxes and battle passes — would he call that play in his sense, or would he say it's exactly the corruption he was warning about, just with better graphics? I keep going back and forth, because on one hand a game is voluntary and closed-off and rule-bound, but on the other hand the rules are enforced by code instead of by the players agreeing to them, the designers tune it to keep you hooked, and half the players talk about it like a second job. And then there are things like speedrunning or Minecraft, where players invent their own goals and constraints — those feel more like his idea of play to me, but I don't know if that's just my gut talking.

I'd also like you to actually commit to a verdict and then tell me where that verdict is most likely to fail. What specific feature of either Huizinga's account or modern games would change your mind if you found out you'd got it wrong? I'm trying to avoid the usual thing where someone just lands on "it's play" or "it's not play" and then defends it no matter what.
