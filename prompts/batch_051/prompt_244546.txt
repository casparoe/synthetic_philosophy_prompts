I'm a neuro PhD student working on a short essay about epiphenomenalism and the paradox of phenomenal judgment, and I'd appreciate comments on the idea before I send it anywhere. The essay is built around a passage in T. H. Huxley's "On the Hypothesis that Animals are Automata" where he says consciousness bears the same relation to brain activity as a steam-whistle does to a locomotive engine: the whistle accompanies the engine's workings but does not drive them. I'm not going to put that in quotes because I'm working from memory and don't want to misquote, but that's the image.

My argument in compressed form: The usual objection to epiphenomenalism is that if phenomenal states have no causal powers, then my judgments about phenomenal states can't be caused by them, and so my saying "I'm in pain" is not really about pain. I think that objection conflates two senses of aboutness. A judgment can be causally about a state by being produced by the neural correlate of that state, even if the phenomenal property itself does no causal work. Huxley's steam-whistle is the right picture: the whistle doesn't move the train, but it reliably correlates with boiler pressure, so a mechanism that says "the whistle is blowing" is about the pressure in the information-carrying sense even though the pressure isn't a cause of the mechanism's output. Maybe the same goes for phenomenal judgment. The physical process does the causing; the phenomenal state does the "being signified" via the correlate.

My worry is that this might just move the bump under the rug. If someone asks why the judgment picks out the phenomenal property rather than, say, the physical correlate or some external stimulus, I think I can say: it picks out the state whose relevant causal powers are instantiated in the physical correlate, and that includes the phenomenal state's neural basis. But I'm not sure whether "picks out" is doing too much work. Also I worry that the steam-whistle analogy is the wrong one for cases where the judgment seems to be about the feeling's presented quality, not its functional role, and aboutness is supposed to explain that. I also can't seperate the two senses of aboutness as cleanly as I'd like when the judgment itself is a physical event: what makes one physical state a representation and another a mere cause?

Could you give me comments on the essay, especially: (1) is the move from correlation to aboutness as clean as I'm hoping, or does it smuggle in a representational mind that epiphenomenalism hasn't earned; (2) where in the reasoning am I most likely to be fooling myself, and what specific argument or consideration would make me abandon the whole idea; and (3) does the piece read as interesting to people who know the epiphenomenalism literature, or is it just reheating a standard identity-theory response. American English please. Apologies for any typos.
