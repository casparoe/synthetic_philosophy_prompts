My friend edits a learner's dicitonary and has very settled calls on when two uses of a word get one headword or two. Some of her past calls:

- bank (the financial one) and bank (river edge): two.
- chicken (the animal) and chicken (the meat): one.
- mouth (on your face) and mouth (of a river or bottle): one.
- paper (the stuff) and paper (a newspaper): one.
- well (the hole with water) and well (as in "she sings well"): two.
- bat (the flying animal) and bat (the thing you hit with): two.

Now I want to know what she'd do with crane (the bird) and crane (the machine on a building site). The machine is called that because it was thought to look like the bird, but a lot of speakers don't feel the link anymore. I can think of at least two rules that fit all six of her earlier calls and then come apart on crane. Spell those out, say what each predicts for this case, and identify which earlier case is the closest analog. Then take a definite position: one entry or two, and what principle she'd be adopting either way — I want a committed answer, not just the menu. End by naming the further case you'd most want to ask her about to break the tie.
