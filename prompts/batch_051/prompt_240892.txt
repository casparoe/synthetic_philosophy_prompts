I spent a long evening recently arguing with a friend about the bootstrapping problem for reliabilism, and I came away thinking the position I was defending deserved a much clearer statement than I managed to give it. Let me fix the case precisely, because I want the argument to engage exactly this version of it.

Consider a driver who has never calibrated his fuel gauge and has no independent evidence about whether it is accurate, although in fact the gauge is highly reliable. On many occasions the gauge reads a level—full, half, and so on—and the driver forms the corresponding belief about the tank. According to reliabilism, as I will use the term, those beliefs can count as knowledge simply because the process that produces them, trusting the gauge, is in fact reliable; the driver need not first possess independent evidence that the process is reliable. Now suppose the driver assembles a track record: on this occasion the gauge read full and the tank was full, on that occasion it read half and the tank was half, and so forth. From this he infers that his gauge is reliable. Each conjunctive claim in the track record is available to him only because he already trusts the gauge in that very case, and by reliabilism those claims count as knowledge only because the gauge is in fact reliable—which is exactly the proposition the track record is supposed to establish. My friend kept insisting that this is viciously circular and therefore refutes, or at least severely embarrasses, reliabilism.

Please make a definite argument that this bootstrapping reasoning is not a fatal objection to reliabilism: that reliabilism can permit the driver to acquire knowledge of his gauge's reliability by broadly this route, that the circularity involved, if it is circularity at all, is of a benign kind rather than the sort in which a conclusion is used as one of its own premises, and that the intuitive discomfort comes from conflating taking a process for granted with assuming the proposition that the process is reliable. Keep the discussion anchored in the track-record case rather than drifting into the wider easy-knowledge problem, though you may mark where the two connect. Throughout, distinguish what is genuinely settled in this corner of epistemology from what remains contested, and do not present a contested resolution of the bootstrapping problem as if it were the consensus view.
