Consider the standard form of the Repugnant Conclusion in population axiology. Let A be a population of ten billion people, all with very high welfare, and let Z be a much larger population, disjoint from A, whose members all have lives barely worth living. Stipulate that Z has a greater total quantity of welfare than A, so that a total utilitarian must judge Z better than A; it is this implication that is meant to be troubling. Now vary the case in one respect. Let Z not be disjoint from A. Instead, let Z contain all ten billion members of A, each with welfare reduced by a small fixed fraction but still very high, plus an enormous number of additional people with lives barely worth living. Stipulate, as before, that Z's total welfare is only slightly greater than A's. Analyze how this inclusive formulation changes the standard debate. Address at least three of the following positions: total utilitarianism, average-based views, person-affecting restrictions, and lexical or threshold views of welfare. For each, say whether its verdict changes, whether its rationale changes, or neither, and explain why. Conclude by assessing whether the Repugnant Conclusion becomes more or less repugnant when the larger population includes the original people rather than replacing them.
