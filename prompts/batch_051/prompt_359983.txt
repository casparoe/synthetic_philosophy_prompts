I follow a decision rule for situations of ignorance: when I cannot assign probabilities to outcomes, I apply maximin — I identify the worst result each available option could produce and choose the option whose worst case is least bad. I meant this for genuine unknowns, but I find myself using it whenever I have simply not bothered to estimate likelihoods. Recently I applied it to a choice between two jobs. The first offered a modest but nearly certain improvement. The second offered much larger payoffs under most scenarios I could think of, but in a few low-probability-looking scenarios it would have left me significantly worse off than the first. I took the first. Is this policy defensible, or is extreme ambiguity aversion posing as a principle? Under what conditions is maximin the rational rule, and under what conditions is it a mistake? Does it matter that my decision is a one-shot personal choice, rather than the choice of principles for a whole society behind a veil of ignorance — the setting in which Rawls makes maximin central, and which Harsanyi models instead as maximizing expected utility under an equal-probability assignment? In the job case, would minimax regret or a rough expected-utility estimate have served me better, and which side of the Rawls–Harsanyi dispute should my kind of case push me toward?
