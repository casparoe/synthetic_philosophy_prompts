I recently spent a late night arguing with a friend about whether ordinary car driving is rationally defensible once you measure it in micromorts. I came away convinced he was wrong in an instructive way, and I have drafted the following notes for a short essay. I would like your comments as an expert, not a rewrite.

For context, a micromort is a one-in-a-million chance of death. For the rest of these notes, treat the following numbers as agreed placeholders rather than precise measurements: a mile of US driving = 0.01 micromorts; 10,000 miles of driving per year = 100 micromorts per year = roughly a 1-in-10,000 annual risk of death; an adult's background all-cause mortality = roughly 4,000 micromorts per year; a skydive = roughly 10 micromorts.

My friend's position was: 100 micromorts a year from driving is trivial next to the 4,000 background micromorts we all face simply by being alive, so people who worry about driving while being willing to skydive, or who avoid highways out of fear, are being inconsistent.

My thesis is that the micromort arithmetic is fine, but the move from micromorts to "driving is worth it" or "driving is too risky" is much weaker than it looks.

The notes:

1. The baseline move. Comparing driving's 100 micromorts to the 4,000 background micromorts does not justify taking on a new, chosen risk. Background risk is not a budget or a quota. Whether a given person should drive depends on comparing driving with its actual alternatives — walking, cycling, transit, staying put — each with its own risk and benefit profile, not on how small 100 looks next to 4,000.

2. The average hides the relevant risk. The per-mile figure is a population average. A sober, attentive city driver on familiar roads in daylight, and a driver on unfamiliar rural roads at night, are not facing the same micromorts per mile. "Is driving worth it?" is therefore probably several questions, not one.

3. The externality. My friend counted only the risk that driving imposes on the driver. But a marginal mile of driving also slightly raises the expected deaths of pedestrians, cyclists, and other motorists. If some of those people did not consent to the added risk and derive little benefit from my trip, the moral question looks worse than the private one.

4. The missing denominator. Even if 100 micromorts a year is small, "worth it" is undefined until we specify what the risk is being traded against: hours saved, jobs made accessible, goods moved, emergency access. The natural unit might be micromorts per unit of benefit, and in dense, transit-rich places driving may buy surprisingly little per micromort.

5. The fungibility assumption. Treating every micromort as equivalent assumes that a death risk is fully captured by its probability, regardless of whether the risk is chosen, controlled, familiar, and fairly distributed. That is a modeling simplification, not a neutral conclusion; a decision theory that rules all such considerations irrelevant is doing substantial silent work.
