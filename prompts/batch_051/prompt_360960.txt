Many employers maintain strong norms — sometimes written policies — of salary secrecy: individual employees are discouraged or forbidden from revealing their own pay or asking about a peer's, and managers treat compensation as confidential. At the same time, many of the same employers publish salary bands, announce a commitment to pay equity, and conduct internal pay audits whose results are shared only at a high level of aggregation.

Consider a firm that combines all three features. Its handbook says that individual compensation is strictly confidential and that discussing it is unprofessional. Its intranet publishes a band structure in which the top band is eleven times the bottom band. Its annual pay-equity audit reports only that no systemic unexplained gaps were found, and individual unexplained gaps are quietly corrected without informing the affected employees.

The question is what this practice presupposes about envy and resentment — specifically, about whether an employee's unease at discovering that a peer earns more is a defect to be suppressed or a signal to be read.

Use the following Rawlsian distinction as working vocabulary. Envy proper is the disposition to experience another person's advantage as itself a wrong or humiliation, independently of any judgment that the advantage is unjustly distributed; the envious person would, if able, reduce the other's advantage even at some cost to himself. Resentment is the moralized counterpart: an aversive reaction to an inequality one takes to be unjust, aimed at restoring fair terms rather than at levelling the other down. On this line, resentment can be informative, since it is the felt appearance of injustice, while envy proper carries no such information. Add the Rawlsian idea of excusable envy: even envy proper may be excusable when inequalities are so large and conspicuous that they wound the self-respect of the worse-off and the worse-off cannot reasonably be expected not to feel it. Excusable envy remains distinct from a sense of justice, and a well-ordered institution would, on this view, try to remove the conditions that call it forth.

Answer the following, and take a definite position rather than merely surveying interpretations:

1. Reconstruct the implicit theory of envy and resentment embodied in the firm's three-part practice. Does the practice presuppose that pay comparisons would produce envy proper rather than warranted resentment, and that the correct response is to manage the emotion through secrecy rather than to alter the pay structure? Or does it concede that some resentment would be well-founded and choose secrecy for other reasons?

2. Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Is pay secrecy strictly incompatible with treating resentment as an informative response to injustice? Or could a firm that took resentment seriously still choose secrecy on grounds of privacy, the costs of interpersonal comparison, or the protection of working relationships? State which views the practice rules out, which it only fails to implement, and where it is philosophically underdetermined.

3. Say whether the three elements presuppose a consistent view; if not, locate the inconsistency precisely.

4. Commit to a verdict. On the best-supported interpretation, what does the practice presuppose? And what would the firm have to change if it adopted the Rawlsian view that envy is excusable only insofar as inequalities undermine self-respect, while resentment at a pay difference is presumptively a reasonable response to its apparent injustice? Would salaries have to become open? Could a Rawlsian still defend keeping some salary information confidential, and on what grounds?
