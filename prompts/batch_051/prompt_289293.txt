A friend in my philosophy of mind class has been pushing an argument about epiphenomenalism that I now can't shake, becuase it seems to prove too much but I can't find the broken step. Here it is:

(1) If epiphenomenalism is true, phenomenl properties (the felt painfulness of pain, the way red looks, etc.) never cause anything physical, including the neural states that realize our judgments about our own experience.
(2) So on epiphenomenalism, my judgment that I am in pain right now is caused entirely by a chain of neural events in which the pain itself plays no causal role.
(3) For a judgment about an empirical fact to count as knowledge, the fact it is about has to be causally involved in producing the judgment.
(4) Therefore, if epiphenomenalism is true, I don't know that I am in pain, and no one knows they have phenomenal consciousness.
(5) But I clearly do know I'm in pain; if I know anything, I know that.
(6) So epiphenomenalism is false.

What bothers me is that (3) is doing all the work, and it's too blunt: it would also seem to wreck our knowledge of mathematical facts, since numbers aren't causing anything, and maybe knowledge of the future or of abstract objects generally. But if I weaken (3) enough to save math, I worry I've also destroyed the whole argument against epiphenomenalism. Where does this argument actually go wrong? Which premise would you give up, and what does it cost? If you amend (3) rather than reject it outright, exactly how does the amendment avoid just restating the conclusion?
