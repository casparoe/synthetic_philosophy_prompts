I'm putting together a unit on the contemporary revival of Stoicism, and I keep running into a problem with how students apply the dichotomy of control. The popular formulation, borrowed loosely from Epictetus, says that some things are up to us and others are not, and that we should care only about the first class. But the revival often smuggles in a third class: things indirectly, gradually, or probabilistically within our power, such as the bodily and affective startles the ancient Stoics discussed under the heading of first movements. I need a single example that would make this divergence real rather than merely verbal.

Construct a thought experiment to these specifications. The scenario should be one ordinary-life paragraph — a workplace meeting, a queue, a family gathering — with no bereavement, grave illness, or moral melodrama. It should center on an involuntary first movement: a surge of anger, a start of fear, a flush of humiliation, something that rises before judgment and that the person could, with long training, have made less frequent or less intense. Then run these three frameworks on the episode and make them genuinely disagree, not restate one verdict in different words: (a) Epictetus's strict dichotomy, on which only the judging-and-choosing faculty and its assents are up to us, so the initial movement itself is not up to us while the response to it is; (b) a modern trichotomy of fully / partly / not at all up to us, on which the movement belongs in the middle class because indirect habituation can predictably dampen it; and (c) the ancient distinction between the first movement and the full passion that requires assent, used to say exactly where the two other frameworks part company with it. State each framework's classification — what precisely is up to the person and what is not — and the attitude each framework would recommend. Since this is for a teaching example, the three verdicts must be generated from the structure of the case rather than stipulated in advance.

End with an honest diagnosis. Identify which feature of the case is doing the work — I suspect it is the lagged, statistical influence of habituation on an involuntary reflex — and explain what would happen if that feature were removed, for instance if the movement were entirely untrainable. Then give the strict Epictetan's best reply to the trichotomist's claim that a trainable reflex is partly up to us, and assess whether that reply is a genuine distinction or special pleading under pressure.
