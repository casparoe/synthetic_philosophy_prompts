I'm drafting a short essay on satisficing and bounded rationality, and I would like detailed comments before I present it at a workshop. Below are my thesis and section notes; I am deliberately not giving you a polished draft, because I want criticism of the argument's spine rather than the prose.

The working title is "Satisficing Does Not Abolish Optimization; It Relocates It."

Thesis: Discussions of satisficing routinely fuse three things that should be kept apart: search heuristics (rules for how to explore options), stopping rules (rules for when to end search), and aspiration levels (the threshold that an acceptable option must meet). Framing satisficing as "a stopping rule that is a rival to maximizing" makes the concept look like a claim about how much search an individual should do. I argue that the real work in Simon's picture is done by aspiration levels, and that aspiration levels are endogenous: they are set and revised by the same bounded process they are supposed to guide. This creates an under-discussed regress that is dissolved only if we read satisficing as relocating optimization to a slower, collective process (for example, organizational or evolutionary selection), not eliminating it.

Section 1: The triple fusion. I distinguish stopping rules, search heuristics, and aspiration levels, and show that each has a different failure mode: a stopping rule can be premature, a heuristic can mislead, and an aspiration level can be miscalibrated. The standard anti-satisficing objection, that a satisficer might stop at a bad first option, attacks the stopping rule rather than the aspiration level, and therefore misses the target.

Section 2: Aspiration levels are endogenous. An aspiration level is not a given; it adapts to experience. But adaptive aspiration-setting is itself a search-and-stop problem: how much evidence do you gather before revising the threshold, and by how much? Agents with too-reactive thresholds chase noise; agents with too-sticky thresholds learn too slowly. This is the regress I want to emphasize.

Section 3: The relocation move. I suggest the regress is escaped by pushing the optimizing burden outside the individual: firms, markets, or evolutionary processes select among aspiration-updating rules. The individual decision-maker can genuinely satisfice; the system cannot. If that is right, satisficing's puzzle is not "when is it rational to stop searching?" but "what kind of system-level selection pressure keeps individual aspiration levels well calibrated, and when does it fail?"

Section 4: Upshot. The essay ends by arguing that debates between satisficing and optimizing are misframed; the interesting disagreement is about the level at which optimization is assumed to operate and about how fast that level adapts relative to the individual's decisions.
