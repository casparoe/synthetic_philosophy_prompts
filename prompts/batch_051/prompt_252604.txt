I work on a product built around a large language model, and my team is split over whether the model actually understands the tasks it performs or is just pattern-matching against its training data. I've adopted a policy for settling the question, at least for myself: I don't count benchmark performance as evidence, since the model may have seen near-duplicates of the test items. Instead, I take problems the model gets right and rewrite them so that the underlying structure stays the same but the surface changes—new variable names, different entities, reordered clauses. Only if accuracy holds up under the rewrite do I treat the model as understanding the task. If accuracy collapses, I conclude the original success was memorization, and I stop taking benchmark-based arguments seriously. A colleague objects that this is rigged: humans also get worse when you reword a problem they have just learned, so my test would classify a competent student as not understanding. That worry has made me unsure whether my rule is a good shortcut or a bias dressed up as an experiment. Is this policy defensible? Under what conditions would the rewriting test be strong evidence for or against understanding, and under what conditions would it mislead? Is there a version of the test that actually separates understanding from retrieval, and can any purely behavioral test settle the question on its own?
