Nearly every law a legislature passes commits people who do not yet exist to some set of outcomes, but those people have no role in the decision. I've been thinking about a scheme intended to give the far future weight in present politics, and I want to understand how it would actually behave, quite apart from whether it is morally defensible.

The scheme is this. A country creates a Future Generations Council. Every bill that an independent horizon commission classifies as having material consequences more than seventy-five years out must be approved by the Council before it can become law. Council members are citizens picked by lottery, weighted by remaining life expectancy, so younger people are systematically overrepresented. Members serve a single eight-year term and may not hold other political office, before or after. Their entire compensation is deferred into bonds that mature in stages over the next hundred and twenty years, and the bonds pay out in full only if certain pre-registered long-run indicators — public health, species abundance, government solvency, and counts of specified catastrophic events — remain above thresholds fixed in advance. A Council veto can be overridden only by a two-thirds legislative majority plus a deposit that is forfeited unless, seventy-five years later, the vetoed measure is judged not to have measurably worsened those same indicators.

Treat this as a mechanism, not as a moral position. What would it incentivize among legislators, ministries, businesses, civic groups, and the council members themselves? How would it be gamed, and which lines of attack would be hardest to close? Where is it likely to fail or make long-run outcomes worse rather than better? And can any of those failure modes be fixed without stripping the scheme of its ability to bind the present to the future?
