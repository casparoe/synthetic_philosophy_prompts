I'm a PhD student in philosophy, mostly working on free will and moral responsibility, and I've been going in circles about whether the flicker strategy can actually rescue the principle of alternative possibilities (PAP) from Frankfurt cases. A visiting speaker had a handout with the following argument on it, and I've tried to reconstruct it faithfully, but I can't tell weather it's sound or whether it just hides the contested move really well:

(1) In the standard Frankfurt case, Jones deliberates in his ordinary way and decides on his own to shoot Smith; Black is standing by with a device that would have made Jones decide to shoot if Jones had shown any sign of being about to decide not to. Black never intervenes, and Jones is morally responsible for shooting.
(2) Jones's responsibility is entirely a matter of the actual sequence: what he actually considered, what he actually intended, and the fact that his action issued from his ordinary deliberative machinery. If Black had intervened, the shooting would not be attributable to Jones in the relevant way.
(3) A 'flicker of freedom' — say, the unactualized possibility that Jones would have shown some neural or behavioral warning sign just before deciding not to shoot, thereby triggering Black — is not part of the actual sequence; it lives only in an alternative course of events.
(4) So Jones's responsibility does not consist in the flicker.
(5) On the flicker strategy, that flicker is the only alternative possibility left in the case.
(6) Therefore the flicker cannot be the alternative possibility PAP requires, so pointing to flickers doesn't rescue PAP, and Frankfurt's counterexample to PAP stands.

My suspicion is that the trouble is around step (4) and the jump to step (6) — something about conflating what responsibility consists in with what it requires — but I keep failing to make that stick, because the handout writer clearly thought (6) just follows. I'd like a committed verdict, not a survey of the flicker debate: is the argument valid? If not, tell me exactly which step or inference fails, why it fails, and what hidden principle you'd have to add to make the conclusion follow. If you think the argument is actually fine, say so and own the conclusion.
