I have recently been forced to think about forgiveness properly rather than in the abstract. A close family member did something genuinely damaging to me and has not acknowledged it in any way I can trust; the people around me keep telling me, with real conviction, that I ought to forgive, and that forgiveness with conditions is not forgiveness at all but a transaction. I have spent the past several weeks trying to make sense of that, and I have built a distinction I want checked for rigor.

I distinguish forgiveness-as-event from forgiveness-as-policy.

Forgiveness-as-event is a dated act: on a particular occasion, the injured person does the thing we point to when we say that she forgave him — she lays the grievance down, stops resenting him, or otherwise completes the forgiving. I suspect this event has uptake conditions: it is addressed to someone, and whether it comes off depends partly on how it is received, much as an apology can fail when the recipient rejects it.

Forgiveness-as-policy is not a dated act but a standing commitment: the injured person resolves, prospectively and without a fixed end date, not to treat the wrong as a reason for exclusion, retaliation, or recurring punishment in her dealings with the wrongdoer. A policy can be adopted before any apology arrives and can be maintained regardless of what the wrongdoer does next.

I can feel the gap between the two in my own case. I have been able to decide, without any apology from my relative, that I will not use what happened as a standing reason to exclude him from family decisions or to meet him with reflexive coldness; that feels like real normative work. But I cannot make any particular afternoon the afternoon on which I forgave him, because the thing I would be doing on that afternoon — addressing a relinquishment to him — would depend on his receiving it, and he has given no sign of doing so.

My central claim is this: forgiveness can be unconditional, and forgiveness can be obligatory, but not for the same thing. Unconditional forgiveness makes sense only as policy. I can commit myself not to treat the wrong as a reason against the person, full stop; there is no further condition to be met because the only condition is a decision that lies entirely with me. An event of forgiveness cannot be made unconditional in the same way, because it needs uptake from the wrongdoer; if it is addressed to someone who does not receive it, the event does not come off. And if there is an obligation to forgive, it can only be an obligation to adopt the policy, never an obligation to produce the event. One can be required to commit to a way of treating someone; one cannot be required to have a dated mental act, both because acts of that kind cannot be produced on command and because whether they can occur depends partly on the other person.

It matters enormously to me whether this distinction is doing real work or just renaming my reluctance. I would like an honest assessment:

1. Is the distinction itself coherent, or do the two sides collapse into each other under pressure?
2. Is it already familiar in the forgiveness literature under another name? If so, does my version commit me to anything the familiar version avoids?
3. Is the central claim right — that unconditional forgiveness attaches only to policy and obligation attaches only to policy — or does it merely shift the problem, since adopting a policy is itself something one can refuse to do?
4. Where does the view break? The cases that worry me are these: a policy of unconditional forgiveness toward someone who is still harming me seems not merely hard to maintain but positively wrong; and an event of forgiveness with no backing policy seems theatrical. Tell me which of these, if any, is a fatal objection rather than an uncomfortable consequence, and tell me which part of the view survives the criticism.
