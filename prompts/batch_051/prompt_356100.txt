Insurers routinely assign an individual a probability of a claim by placing them in a statistical class. A fifty-five-year-old applicant might be priced as a fifty-five-year-old man, as a smoker, as a resident of his postcode, or as the holder of his particular medical and genomic profile, and these classes yield very different premiums. Some equally predictive bases, such as most genetic predispositions, are treated as off-limits by law or convention, even though family history that tracks them is not. This is a practical form of the reference class problem. What theory of single-case probability does the practice presuppose? Does it need there to be a fact of the matter about the applicant's true chance of a claim, or only a decision about which frequency is fit for use? Is the choice of a reference class ever a purely statistical matter, or does it always import moral and legal commitments about which similarities matter and which groupings may be used? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined.
