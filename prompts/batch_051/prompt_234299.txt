A few of us got into an argument after class about whether private arbitration is ever legitimate, and I realized my actual position is basically "it depends," but I can't say on what. So I wrote down the cases we argued about and my honest gut verdict on each before I'd done any of the assigned reading. (1) A bank hits me with a $35 overdraft fee that I think its own processing order caused; the account agreement says any dispute goes to binding arbitration and I waive any class action. Not fine. (2) Two midsize companies, each with its own lawyer, sign a supply contract agreeing to arbitrate any breach claim under their trade association's rules. Fine. (3) A warehouse worker signs onboarding paperwork, and buried in it is a pre-dispute arbitration agreement with a confidentiality clause covering future harrassment claims; later she is harassed and the company moves to compel arbitration. Really not fine, worse than the bank. (4) Two neighbors, after a fence-line dispute has already blown up, sign an agreement to use a private arbitrator because it'll be cheaper and faster. Fine, maybe better than court. (5) A union bargains a collective agreement sending seniority and pay-grievence disputes to arbitration. Fine, though this one nags me, since the individual worker never personally agreed to it; they're just covered by the CBA. (6) A person signs their elderly parent into a nursing home, and among the admission forms is a clause routing any future neglect or wrongful-death claim to arbitration. Not fine, and I'm not sure why it feels worse than the bank. The clean principle I keep reaching for is that private dispute resolution is okay only when you agree to it after the dispute has started and you know exactly what rights you're giving up. But that principle would condemn (2) and (5), and I don't want to condemn either. So either the principle is messier than I'd like, or a couple of my verdicts need to go.

Then apply your best candidate to three cases I'm still torn on: (a) a small stationery shop signs a giant retailer's standard vendor agreement with an arbitration clause, knowing the clause is there but having no leverage to strike it; (b) a streaming service says that continuing to use the service binds you to a new arbitration clause unless you mail a written opt-out within 30 days; (c) two customers hit by the same bogus fee want to bring their claims together in one arbitration rather than separately. What does your principle say about each, and do any of those answers force you to repair the principle again?
