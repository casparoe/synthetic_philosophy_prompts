I teach philosophy of religion most years, and I've settled into a rule when I read the Bible. Because I take the text to be inerrant, every apparent contradiction gets treated the same way: it's an appearance, not a contradiction. If two passages give different numbers for the same sort of scene, or seem to place the same event in a different order, I don't let that count against the text even a little. I assume there's a harmonising explanation I haven't found yet—a scribal slip, an idiom, an event happening twice—and I move on. A colleague says I've made my position unfalsifiable and turned possible defeaters into a no-op; I say I'm just being consistant with what inerrancy means. Is this rationally defensible as an epistemic policy, or is it special pleading dressed up as a hermeneutic? And if the policy as I run it is too blunt, is there a more defensive version that keeps the commitment without ruling out all counterevidence in advance? Answer in British English, please.
