You are a professional philosopher who works on decision theory, the epistemology of self-knowledge, and the norms of probabilistic reasoning. Please think carefully, and begin by laying out the strongest case for the policy described below and the strongest case against it before giving your verdict.

I work in an environment that prizes calibrated forecasting: we assign probabilities to events, log them, and get scored on them. I am comfortable assigning probabilities to other people's choices, to ordinary future events, and even to my own behavior when the question is not one I am currently deciding — for instance, I can say I am roughly 85% likely to still be living in this city two years from now.

But I have a firm policy about live decisions: while I am actually deliberating between options, I refuse to assign a probability to what I will choose. Recently we needed an estimate of whether I would still be in my role in twelve months, and I balked. I would have had no trouble giving a number if I had already decided, but I had not: I was weighing whether to leave. My working picture is that deliberation crowds out prediction. Once I attach a number to my own choice, the forecast starts acting as a decision, or anchors the deliberation, or turns the choice into evidence about myself rather than something I am still settling. I also notice that if I said "70% I stay" and then left, I would not count that as a calibration failure the way I would for a weather forecast — which makes me wonder whether I am exempting myself from my own epistemic standards.

Is this carve-out defensible, or is it a bias dressed up as a principle — a way of protecting an illusion of freedom or avoiding inconvenient evidence about how predictable I am? When, if ever, can a person rationally assign probabilities to their own current choices? Is there a revised policy that keeps whatever is valuable about my refusal without the costs, and how would I tell, from the inside, whether I am holding the line for good epistemic reasons or for comfort? Please treat the policy as a policy: say when it is reliable, when it fails, what it presupposes about the relation between belief and choice, and whether I am entitled to it given that I otherwise trust probabilistic thinking.
