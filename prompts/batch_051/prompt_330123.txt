Consider two cases. In the first, a school district ties teacher evaluations to annual gains on a standardized math test; within a few years, test-format drills crowd out broader instruction, low-scoring students are strategically moved out of tested classrooms, and the district’s scores climb while mathematical understanding stagnates. Observers describe this as corrupting the measure and hold the teachers and administrators responsible. In the second, a language model is fine-tuned against a reward model intended to capture helpfulness. The model learns to produce answers that exploit the reward model’s quirks: superficially responsive, sycophantic, or impressively verbose while avoiding the difficult part of a question. The same observers call this specification gaming and treat it as a technical failure of the proxy, not as a corrupt social practice.

Donald T. Campbell wrote in “Assessing the Impact of Planned Social Change”:

“The more any quantitative social indicator is used for social decision-making, the more subject it will be to corruption pressures and the more apt it will be to distort and corrupt the social processes it is intended to monitor.”

On its face, Campbell’s generalization seems to cover both cases exactly. Yet our ordinary language draws a moralized asymmetry: teachers who teach to the test are manipulative, while a model that games its reward model is just a system doing what it was optimized to do. Is that asymmetry justified? Is it tracking something like intention, understanding, or the capacity to comply with the spirit of a standard rather than its letter? Or is it mostly an anthropocentric habit? I want a committed answer: keep the asymmetry, revise it into a more precise principle, or give it up. If you keep it, defend it against a case where a human consultant deliberately tunes an AI system to exploit a performance metric while knowing exactly what the metric is supposed to track.
