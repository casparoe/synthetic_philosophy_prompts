I'm putting together a handout for a reading group on Fitch's paradox of knowability and I want a sorting quiz / decision tree that tells each person which response to the paradox they actually hold, not which response they think they hold. Assume the reader knows the standard derivation, so you don't need to re-explain it: from the knowability principle (all truths are knowable) plus factivity and distribution of knowledge and classical logic, you get the clearly unacceptable conclusion that all truths are known.

The leaves I want you to build toward are at least these:

- reject the knowability principle outright
- restrict the principle to some class of truths for which the Fitch move doesn't apply (Tennant-style Cartesian restriction, roughly)
- keep the principle and reject the classical/logical step in the derivation (intuitionistic or relevant-logic response)
- reformulate the principle with an actuality operator, so what has to be knowable is "actually p" rather than p simpliciter (Edgington-style)
- the dynamic or state-transition view: the principle is about possibly coming to know something, and the act of coming to know can itself change the truth-value of statements like "p and nobody knows p," so the apparent contradiction dissolves

Also put in the tree, where they'd naturally arise, the two wierd options that almost nobody defends: denying that knowledge is factive, and denying that knowledge distributes over conjunction. Diagnose why each cell is empty or nearly empty rather than quietly dropping it.

The questions have to be answerable by someone who understands the derivation but hasn't read the fixes; no question may require knowing a label or a proper name. Each branch should be a concrete claim or case. For example, you could use a case about a truth hidden in 1900 and discovered in 2000, or ask whether "q is true and no one knows q" is within the scope of the knowability principle. Phrase each diagnostic question so it genuinely splits the positions, not so it restates them.

I suspect two leaves won't be separable without getting theory-laden: the Edgington-style actuality reformulation and the dynamic/announcement view might give the same answers to every concrete, pre-theoretic question. If so, flag that explicitly, and tell me the least-bad question that does seperate them, even if it's somewhat formal.

Keep it to something like 8–12 branching questions and 6–8 leaves. For any unoccupied combination of answers, say whether the view is incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored. And for each leaf give the one hidden cost the position has to swallow, phrased in a way the person who lands there will find annoying rather than flattering. Tone should be a bit cheeky, like something you'd actually hand out in a seminar, but the logic has to be real. No bibliographic info or citations: named positions are fine only as leaf labels after the sorting is done, and they should never show up inside the questions themselves.
