I am a PhD student in the philosophy of mathematics, and I am drafting a dissertation chapter on definitional practice: the question of why one formulation of a mathematical concept becomes treated as the right notion while other coherent, often equivalent formulations get demoted to parochial, clumsy, or merely pedagogical. I want you to produce the desiderata — the list of requirements a theory of definition-choice must satisfy to count as adequate for my purposes — grounded in concrete mathematical episodes, together with a judgment about which requirements are negotiable and an explicit treatment of whether the full set is jointly satisfiable. Ground every requirement in a specific case; do not lean on named authors, historical attributions, or citations, and write so that someone with undergraduate and early-graduate mathematics can follow every example.

Respond in exactly the following five sections, in this order, at roughly the indicated lengths.

**1. Aims and Scope (~150 words).** State what a theory of definition-choice is a theory of, and what adequacy means here. Fix the purpose explicitly (I suggest: an account that explains, and where possible justifies, why certain definitions become canonical, and that diagnoses why certain coherent alternatives failed). Say whether the theory you are specifying is descriptive, normative, or both, and mark any boundary you draw around the phenomenon — for instance, excluding purely notational or merely stipulative definitions.

**2. Desiderata with Motivating Cases (~800 words).** Give the requirements as a numbered list, aiming for seven or eight. Each entry should (a) name the requirement in a phrase, (b) state it as a constraint, (c) give the mathematical episode that forces it, and (d) say what kind of theory would fail it. Do not include any criterion you cannot motivate with a case. The following episodes should be represented, unless you swap one out for a case of the same logical type and say what type it is:
- continuity via open sets versus epsilon–delta or metric definitions;
- the group axioms versus nearby structures such as semigroups or monoids, and why inverses earn their place;
- compactness as the finite-open-cover property versus sequential compactness or "closed and bounded";
- the product topology versus the box topology, or the Hausdorff condition and uniqueness of limits.

Also include at least one requirement motivated by a definition constrained by a consistency or impossibility result (for instance, the use of sigma-algebras rather than the full power set in measure theory), and at least one requirement motivated by a consistent but mostly abandoned definition whose failure is instructive. Make sure at least two requirements address rejected alternatives, not just accepted choices, so that the list cannot be satisfied by a theory that simply ratifies whatever history selected.

**3. Negotiable versus Non-Negotiable (~250 words).** For each requirement from section 2, classify it as non-negotiable (any adequate theory must meet it) or negotiable/camp-specific (a reasonable theory may demote or reinterpret it), and justify each classification briefly. I specifically want you to identify one requirement that initially looks like mere aesthetic preference but that you promote to non-negotiable, and one that initially looks like a hard constraint but that you demote to a preference of a particular philosophical camp.

**4. Tensions and Joint Satisfiability (~250 words).** State whether the full set of requirements is jointly satisfiable. If it is not, identify the smallest conflicting subset and state precisely what makes the requirements conflict. If a conflict can be removed by relativizing a requirement to a fixed purpose or background theory, say how. Where the conflict is real, treat it as a result about definitional practice rather than as a defect of the list.

**5. The Single Most Discriminating Requirement (~200 words).** Choose the one requirement that does the most work separating the live candidate accounts — for example, a proof-economy or functionalist account, a natural-kinds or structuralist account, a conventionalist or pragmatic account, and a morphism-based or category-theoretic account. Say what that requirement forces each candidate to do, and why it cannot be ducked.

Please keep the whole response under 1,700 words, with section 2 clearly the longest; sections 3–5 should build on section 2 rather than introduce new cases.
