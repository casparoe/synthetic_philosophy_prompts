Two friends of mine, Priya and Daniel, got into it the other night about a third friend, Tunde. Tunde is in his late thirties, hates his corporate job, constantly talks about training as a carpenter or moving somewhere cheap, but says he's trapped: mortgage, a kid with expensive medical needs, elderly parents nearby who lean on him. The argument was about whether Tunde is in bad faith, in something like Sartre's sense. It went roughly like this:

(1) Priya: Tunde is a textbook case of bad faith. He talks as if the mortgage and the medical bills and his parents physically push him through the office door every morning. They don't. They're facts, but a fact only has whatever weight it has relative to a project a person is pursuing. Every morning he re-chooses the job. He could quit, go part-time, refinance, move the family somewhere cheaper, and he won't seriously consider any of it. Saying "I have no choice" is precisely turning yourself into a thing so you can hand the responsibility to circumstances. That self-deception is bad faith.

(2) Daniel: This is where existentialism gets itself hated. Sartre doesn't say your facticity is unreal; he says freedom is always situated. Tunde's situation is not a gloss he's projecting onto neutral data. His kid's treatment costs a specific number every month, and a child's illness can't be re-narrated into a smaller bill. And if your view is that Tunde is free because he could abandon the kid and skip town to be a carpenter, then the "authentic" option you're defending is just being monstrous. Tunde isn't in bad faith; he's recognizing that some options aren't live for someone who takes himself to have these obligations. Calling that bad faith pathologizes ordinary decency.

(3) Priya: I never said the bills are fake and I never said he should skip town. The point is that facts don't do the concluding. There's a gap between "my child needs expensive care" and "therefore I stay in this job, in this city, in this life," and the gap is filled by a choice—the choice to be the parent who stays and keeps those obligations. That may be the right choice; it's probably the one I'd make. But it is a choice, and the bad faith is in presenting it as a calculation rather than a commitment. The difference between "I'm staying because I value this and accept the cost" and "I can't leave" is the difference between owning a project and disguising it as necessity.

(4) Daniel: And what would change if he said the magic words? If Tunde started saying "I choose to stay," he'd still drag himself to that office. The theory doesn't predict anything different in his actual life. It's unfalsifiable. Stay and say "I can't," and you're in bad faith; stay and say "I choose," and it's either just a verbal substitution or bad faith again because deep down he doesn't mean it; leave, and the Sartrean calls him authentic no matter who gets hurt. You've made bad faith something you can never be wrong about, because every case can be redescribed to fit. That's not an argument, it's a mood.

(5) Priya: It's not supposed to be a behavioral prediction, and it's not settled from the outside. Bad faith isn't a property you read off someone's movements like his pulse. It's about what he does with his own freedom, so in the end only he can confront it. That doesn't make it empty. The difference shows up in the texture of the thing—whether he experiences the obligations as reasons he endorses or as a fate crushing him changes how the resentment and exhaustion and anger at his family actually build up. You can't nail it from a third-person view, but that isn't a flaw. Sartre's point is that freedom is lived, not known as an object. Daniel keeps asking for a criterion, but he's asking for the wrong kind of thing.

(6) Daniel: But you opened by confidently asserting, from the third person, that Tunde is deceiving himself. If there's no outside criterion, that assertion is no better supported than Tunde's "I can't leave." You've quietly switched to offering him a framing technique. Also, if the test is how the choice is lived—whether it's endorsed from inside—then a person who sincerely experiences himself as having no choice is not in bad faith. But you want to say exactly that kind of sincere felt necessitty is bad faith. So you need an external criterion after all, and you just finished telling me none exists.

(7) Priya: That's the part people miss. Sartre thinks the pursuit of sincerity about yourself—being really honest about what you are and what's possible for you—can itself be bad faith, because you treat yourself as a thing with a fixed nature to be described. The person who says "I'm just being realistic about my limits" can be doing exactly this. So when Daniel demands that I either produce an outside criterion or admit I'm only talking about feelings, he's making the same move: he wants a determinate object where there isn't one. The absence of a checklist isn't a defect in the accusation; it reflects the kind of being we're dealing with.

(8) Daniel: Then Priya's own judgment that Tunde is in bad faith is also a third-person, reifying move, by her own standard. Does she think her accusation escapes the bad faith it names? If yes, she needs a distinction she just denied exists. If no, then she's saying "Tunde is in bad faith, and so is my saying so," which doesn't leave a theory of anything—it just spreads bad faith everywhere until it stops doing any work. Either way, we end where we started: she finds Tunde contemptible for something she can't state without doing it herself.

And that's where they got stuck.

Could you go through this exchange and adjudicate it move by move? For each of the eight moves, say whether it's clearly mistaken, mostly wrong, a fair point with a flaw, or clearly right, and briefly justify the rating. Be precise about which parts are decent Sartre exegesis and which parts are Priya or Daniel importing their own commitments. Then say what, if anything, both of them are missing about Tunde's case or about bad faith.
