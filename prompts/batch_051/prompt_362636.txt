I'm a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, so I'll frame this in the vocabulary I actually use. On my team we're deciding whether to ship a system that does open-ended planning with real-world side effects (drafting messages, placing orders up to a cap, opening merge requests). One camp keeps arguing that the whole tool-AI versus agent-AI distinction is overblown: the step from a fixed function to a model with broader competence is like the step from a hand saw to a power saw, or from a pocket calculator to a spreadsheet. The system is still a tool, inert until a person picks it up, so the safety story changes in degree but not in kind. The other camp uses the reverse analogy: a tool is like a hammer, while an agent is like hiring a contractor — you hand over an outcome plus constraints, and the system exercises judgment over means you never review, so the safety playbook does change in kind.

I want an audit of both analogies, carried through seriously rather than dismissed. For each one: (1) make the mapping explicit — in the tool picture, what corresponds to the user's hand, the trigger, the motor, the workpiece; in the contractor picture, what corresponds to the principal, the contract, the contractor's discretion, the invoice, the hiring and firing. (2) Say where each mapping breaks, and separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion from those that are cosmetic. What does physical-tool talk quietly import — inertness between uses, a fixed action profile, real-time human steering, narrowly local effects, no internal objectives of its own — that the target system lacks? What does contractor talk quietly import — shared common sense and social incentives, a natural-language contract that leans on background you don't have to spell out, reputational and legal accountability, a bounded set of available moves — that an AI system may not have? (3) Then rule on the dispute: does "it's still just a tool" license relaxing about deploying an autonomous planner, does the contractor picture earn its stronger caution, or do both carry conclusions they can't support once the disanalogies are on the table? If some third comparison would do better, say what it is and what it gets right.

Start with the conclusion you think survives, then do the correspondence work and the disanalogies. A table of correspondences is acceptable. Do not pad. Hard limit: 800 words.
