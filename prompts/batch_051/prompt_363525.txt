A friend of mine in formal epistemology has been pushing a line thats been bugging me. She starts from Jeffrey conditionalization, which I always took to be basically the right story about learning from uncertain evidence: instead of learning some proposition E with certainty and conditionalizing on it, you take a partition {E1, ..., En}, give the partition members new probabilities q(Ei) as inputs, and then set P_new(H) as the weighted sum of P_old(H | Ei) q(Ei), keeping all the conditional-on-Ei probabilities fixed because those are supposed not to change (rigidity). Fine.

Then she gives this argument that it either collapses into old-fashioned strict conditionalization or else can't model ordinary perceptual learning at all:

1. Suppose I see a card in bad light and my credence that it's red goes from 0.5 to 0.8, without my becoming certain of any proposition about the card or the lighting.
2. For Jeffrey conditionalization to model that, there has to be a partition such that my new credences over its members are just the inputs q(Ei) that my seeing the card directly produced.
3. For the rule to actually represent my learning, the partition has to be the one my experience "latched onto": the shift in the q(Ei) has to be my evidence, not some arbitrary redescription of it.
4. But what my experience latched onto in the card case is the character of the experience itself, that reddish look under those lights. That isn't a proposition, and any proposition I can state (the card looks red, my visual field has a red patch, the lighting is unusual) is a coarse, theorized summary of it.
5. And any such summary is a poor stand-in. The experience itself outruns whatever I can say, so the proposition I feed into the partition is always an abstraction; and my confidence that the abstraction captures the experience is itself another judgment made under the same uncertainty. So each candidate evidence proposition needs its own evidential backing, and off we go on a regress.
6. A regress of that kind has to bottom out somewhere, and the only available bottom is a proposition I accept with certainty about how things appear to me. But learning a proposition with certainty is exactly the strict conditionalization case.
7. Therefore Jeffrey conditionalization doesn't actually extend strict conditionalization after all: either it bottoms out in strict conditionalization on appearance-propositions and collapses into it, or it can't model the ordinary case of learning from an experience you can't fully propositionalize.

I don't want to believe the conclusion, since most of what I learn perceptually is exactly this kind of uncertain, not-fully-articulable stuff. I'd like to keep both Jeffrey conditionalization and the idea that the ultimate evidence in perception is experiential rather than propositional, but I'll give one up if the argument forces it. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
