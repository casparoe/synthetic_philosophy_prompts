I'm in a seminar on how foreign-policy types use history, and last week we spent the whole time on Munich, Vietnam, and the Thucydides trap (appeasement, quagmire, and the fear that a rising power will inevitably fight the established one). Me and a friend got into an argument afterwards about whether analogical reasoning itself is the problem, or whether it's mostly fine and just gets abused as a rhetorical trump card. I'm on the side that the abuse is the normal case, not the exception. We ended up making a list of reforms that might catch the worst errors, and I want someone to actually commit to ratings rather than waffle.

Can you sort these five proposals into tiers, from something like "would stop a big chunk of the worst mistakes" down to "mostly cosmetic or even counterproductive"? Here they are: (a) require any memo that invokes Munich, Vietnam, or the Thucydides trap to spell out three structural disanalogies before it gets circulated; (b) install a designated skeptic on the relevant policy staff whose entire job is to attack the operative analogy; (c) require decision-makers, before signing off, to genuinely entertain at least two rival analogies pointing in different policy directions; (d) just ban the most overused analogies from official memoranda outright; (e) wherever decent base-rate data exists for the relevant class of crises, demote analogies to a supplementary role and never the headline argument.

Give me the tiers and a sentence or two for each placement. Then tell me which single reform you'd bet on to do the most good and which one you suspect would backfire, and why. You don't need to be exhaustive about mechanisms—availability, selecting confirming cases, even the fact that the people reaching for an analogy usually already have a conclusion in mind—but I do want the rankings and the reasoning.
