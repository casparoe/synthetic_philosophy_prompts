Consider Nozick's utility monster: a creature that derives far more utility from each unit of some resource than any human does, so a maximizing utilitarian would transfer the entire stock to it and leave everyone else destitute. Now modify the case. The monster is satiable in principle: if it could consume some finite amount of the resource, it would eventually be fully satisfied and additional units would yield nothing. But that amount is vastly larger than the society's entire stock, and the monster's marginal utility declines so slowly that, at every feasible distribution short of handing it everything, the next unit gives the monster more utility than that same unit would give a human who needs it to stave off starvation.

How does this modification change the force of the objection? Specifically: (a) Does the utilitarian reply that real utility functions are concave and eventually satiate show that the modified monster is harmless? (b) What would a prioritarian prescribe in this case, and does the satiability matter more for the prioritarian than for the maximizing utilitarian? (c) Does the rights-based version of the original objection—that such aggregation ignores the separateness of persons—lose any of its force once the monster is no longer literally insatiable? Work through each of these, and say which responses to the original thought experiment survive the modification. Please answer in American English.
