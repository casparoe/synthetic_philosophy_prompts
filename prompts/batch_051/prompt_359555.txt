In Thucydides' History of the Peloponnesian War, Book 1, chapter 10 (Richard Crawley translation), he defends taking Homer's testimony about the Trojan expedition seriously despite the meager visible remains of Mycenae. He writes:

"For I suppose if Lacedaemon were to become desolate, and the temples and the foundations of the public buildings were left, that as time went on there would be a strong disposition with posterity to refuse to accept her fame as a true exponent of her power. And yet they occupy two-fifths of Peloponnese and lead the whole, not to speak of their numerous allies without. Still, as the city is neither built in a compact form nor adorned with magnificent temples and public edifices, but composed of villages after the old fashion of Hellas, there would be an impression of inadequacy. Whereas, if Athens were to suffer the same misfortune, I suppose that any inference from the appearance presented to the eye would make her power to have been twice as great as it is."

A lecturer summarized this passage for me as follows: "Thucydides is arguing that visible remains are a poor guide to past power, so we should generally accept poetic and traditional testimony about legendary events unless archaeology gives some specific reason to doubt it." I suspect this is mostly on the right track but may overstate what Thucydides is entitled to conclude. Which parts of the summary are accurate, which are subtly wrong, and does the corrected principle genuinely help when deciding whether legends such as King Arthur or the Exodus should be treated as history?
