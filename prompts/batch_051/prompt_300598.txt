I have been following the philosophical debate about gradual neuron replacement as a path to mind uploading, and I am struck by how stable the disagreement has remained. The scenario is this: one by one, a person's neurons are replaced by artificial components engineered to replicate each neuron's causal role—its input–output profile and its connections—while the person stays awake, converses, and shows no outward sign of change. At the end, the brain is implemented entirely in a new substrate. One camp argues that the person plainly survives: there is no moment at which memory, personality, or behavior is disrupted; the relevant continuities are preserved at every step; and insisting on some further ingredient—a soul, an organic substrate, a "bright line"—is arbitrary. The other camp replies that this begs the question: what the observers see as seamless continuity is compatible with the original subject's stream of consciousness having ended and been replaced by a new subject that inherits the same causal roles. On that view, the resulting person is at best a perfect copy, and gradual replacement is only a slower, undetected form of dying. Both sides deploy thought experiments, and neither moves. My working hypothesis is that the two camps are answering different questions under one heading: one is asking what makes a later person numerically identical with an earlier person, while the other is asking what it would be rational to care about when deciding whether to undergo the procedure. Is that the right diagnosis of the impasse, or is there a genuine substantive disagreement underneath—about metaphysics, about empirical facts, about which cases count as evidence? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a distinction, a reframed thought experiment, a formal result, or a good argument that no progress should be expected?
