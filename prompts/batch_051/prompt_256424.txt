I am grading an upper-level undergraduate exam in metaphilosophy, and I would like a second opinion on one student's answer. The exam question was:

"Consider a formal theory that yields a determinate verdict in a particular case, while a firm pre-theoretical intuition about that case yields the opposite verdict. Give a principled account of when the theory should be revised in light of the intuition and when the intuition should be discounted in light of the theory. Your answer should distinguish different kinds of conflict and different sources of intuition."

The student's answer read:

"A theory should win when the intuition can be given a debunking explanation: if the intuition can be traced to an affect heuristic, a framing effect, or some other known source of bias, it carries little evidential weight. An intuition should win when the theory must lean on auxiliary assumptions that were not independently motivated, or when the intuition is widely shared and survives repeated reflection. For example, the intuition that it is wrong to push the large man off the footbridge should overturn simple act-utilitarianism, because that intuition is robust across cultures and because the theory cannot capture the distinction between intending harm and merely foreseeing it. The principle is therefore: theories are answerable to the stable, widely shared intuitions, while intuitions are answerable to debunking explanations of their origin."

Please assign a grade and provide the point-by-point comments that would justify it. I am looking for an assessment of the details rather than a general impression, so please flag any distinctions the answer runs together and any inferential step that does not follow.
