In my graduate seminar we keep reaching for the phrase "merely verbal" to set certain disputes aside, but I have never been able to give a precise account of what, exactly, that phrase commits us to. I would like you to propose a working definition of a verbal dispute: a general criterion that tells us what must be true of two parties for their disagreement to be merely verbal rather than substantive. Do not simply catalog the ways in which a dispute can be verbal — ambiguity, different stipulated meanings, talking past one another, and so on. I want one criterion, which you then test.

Here are the cases the definition must handle:

(a) Two set-theorists accept exactly the same proofs and theorems, but one says "0 is a natural number" and the other denies it, because one treats the natural numbers as including 0 and the other treats them as excluding 0.
(b) Two people agree about every physical detail of a frankfurter in a split bun with mustard and relish, but one says "a hot dog is a sandwich" and the other says it is not.
(c) Two physicists agree about all the relevant equations and about the pressure waves produced when a tree falls in an empty forest, but one says the falling tree makes a sound and the other says it makes no sound, since one means acoustic vibration by "sound" and the other means auditory experience.
(d) Two biologists agree about everything viruses do — their replication, their total dependence on host cellular machinery, their lack of metabolism, their evolutionary history — but disagree about whether viruses are alive.
(e) Two astronomers agree about Pluto's mass, its orbit, and the fact that it has not cleared its orbital zone, but disagree about whether Pluto is a planet.
(f) Two metaphysicians agree about every microphysical fact in the room — that there are only elementary particles arranged tablewise and nothing more — but one says "there is a table in this room" and the other says "there is no table; there are only particles arranged tablewise."
(g) Two people agree about every biological fact about a fetus and about its present and future psychological capacities, but disagree about whether the fetus is a person, and they take this disagreement to bear directly on the permissibility of abortion.
(h) Two witnesses agree about the lighting, the angle of view, and the figure's gait and clothing, but disagree about whether the defendant was the person whom they saw leaving the building.
(i) Two people agree about every non-normative fact about a redistributive policy, but disagree about whether the policy is unjust.

My own prior is that (a) and (b) are clearly merely verbal, and that (h) and (i) are clearly substantive; I am genuinely unsure about the middle cases, and I half-expect a good definition to force me to revise my view somewhere. Propose the definition, run it through all nine cases, and say explicitly where it yields a surprising verdict or no verdict at all. Where that happens, either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict. Do not let the borderline cases quietly come out right; I want to see the points at which the definition strains.
