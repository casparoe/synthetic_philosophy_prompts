I run the nutrition and food-security portfolio for a small NGO in a semi-arid part of East Africa where most families keep goats, cattle, or chickens, and a lot of the children get most of their protein and iron from milk and eggs. The question I'm stuck with comes up every few months, not once: every time we design or renegotiate a program, we fight about animal-source foods. One of our funders, an animal-welfare foundation in Europe, wants us to move our school feeding and maternal nutrition work toward plant-based foods and stop promoting eggs and milk; they keep saying a well-planned plant-based diet meets nutritional needs, which I mostly accept for a rich country. Our field staff push back that in the areas we work plant protein is scarce, expensive, or rejected by the community, and milk and eggs are often the only reliable dense source of nutrition for underweight children. The stakes run both ways: getting this wrong can mean children staying malnourished, or it can mean we quietly keep paying for avoidable animal suffering. I'm not asking you to settle that one debate once. I need something reusable.

Please write a decision procedure my team can actually run whenever a program design or donor proposal raises this question. Ground it in whatever the philosophy of animal ethics under scarcity and nonideal conditions actually establishes, but the procedure itself should be usable by program staff with no philosophy background, in a day or two, with data we can actually get. I want the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision up to me or the country director because it is a question of values rather than facts. Flag the places where the procedure is most likely to misfire, including where our staff have an obvious incentive to answer one way.

Then run the procedure on three decisions, showing the reasoning at each step. The first: our school porridge currently uses whole-milk powder bought from a local dairy with no animal-welfare standards; the donor wants us to switch to a fortified cereal-legume blend that is slightly cheaper per serving and adequate on paper, but the children eat noticably less of it and the community treats the milk as the point of the program. The second: our maternal nutrition program distributes eggs from caged layers; the donor will pay for a legume-based alternative instead, but it costs more per gram of absorbable protein and is less acceptable, so switching means cutting the number of women we reach. The third: we are designing a new program for a lakeshore fishing community where small children's main animal protein is fish, and the donor will only fund the nutrition component if we commit to making it plant-based where feasible, a phrase our staff cannot agree on. For each, say what the procedure decides or where it stalls. At least one of these is probably going to come out in a way I won't like, and I want you to say so plainly instead of softening it. End by telling me honestly what the procedure cannot do.
