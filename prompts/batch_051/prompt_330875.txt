I am a novelist, and I have been rereading Dostoevsky's The Idiot while drafting a character who is almost unfailingly good, and the book keeps troubling me in a way that seems to engage a philosophical controversy I keep turning over. The controversy runs something like this. A life organized wholly around moral goodness — a moral saint, in the sense some philosophers give the term, someone who lets moral considerations dominate every decision — would have to starve the non-moral excellences: wit, play, style, sensuousness, strong partial loves. By that account, the result would not be admirable so much as narrow, humorless, and impossible to envy. Dostoevsky looks as though he tried to run the opposite experiment in Prince Myshkin: to portray a radically compassionate, Christ-like figure and make the reader feel his goodness as magnetic, even lovely. But the plot keeps making that very goodness the engine of catastrophe. Myshkin's limitless pity for Nastasya Filippovna feeds her self-laceration rather than healing it; his inability to choose between her and Aglaya destroys both of them; and in the climactic vigil beside Rogozhin over Nastasya's body, his compassion has gone so far into complicity and then into blank, childlike vacancy that the novel seems to ask whether the perfectly good life fails even by its own lights.

I would like a critical reading of the novel through that moral-saints question: does The Idiot show that maximal moral goodness is self-undermining — not merely unattractive, but dangerous to oneself and to the people one tries to save — or does it show that the philosophical picture of the moral saint is too abstract, because it pictures goodness as a quantity added to a life rather than as love aimed at particular, difficult, ungovernable people? I want the reading to take both sides seriously: to locate the precise scenes in which Myshkin's virtue tips into harm and becomes something the novel holds him responsible for, rather than bad luck, and also to say where the novel resists being read as a brief against sanctity. And since I am asking as a novelist, close by saying what Dostoevsky's narration accomplishes that the argument cannot: how focalizing the story through a man whose goodness is inseparable from a kind of not-knowing lets the novel make a claim about moral perfection that a page of premises and a counterexample could not.
