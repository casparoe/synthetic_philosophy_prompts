I’m a software engineer with no formal philosophy background, and I’ve been trying to pin down why the standard ticking time bomb thought experiment feels so far removed from any real decision procedure I can imagine. In my work, you rarely get an omniscient oracle; you get noisy signals, unreliable sensors, and multiple sources that conflict with one another. So I want to think through a more realistic variation and understand how it changes the philosophical analysis.

Imagine a bomb is known to be planted in one of twelve city blocks. A detainee is in custody. The authorities know from prior intelligence that there is a 60% chance he is actually involved and knows the bomb’s location; if he is not involved, nothing he says under duress will track the truth. If he is involved, his single report under interrogation names the true block with 45% probability and distributes the remaining 55% evenly among the other eleven blocks. There is also a second informant who has already named a different block; this informant names the true block with 30% probability, and when incorrect names one of the other eleven blocks at random. The deadline allows exactly one block to be selected for evacuation. Evacuating the correct block prevents most casualties, while evacuating a block with no bomb causes a smaller but nonzero number of panic-related injuries. Everyone involved knows these numbers at the start.

I’d like you to run the expected-utility calculation on this variation. Does a straightforward consequentialism authorize coercive interrogation at all, and under what conditions would it follow a report rather than choosing randomly or doing nothing? Then I’d like the deontological side. Does moving from a guarantee to a set of probabilities weaken or strengthen the claim that coercion is intrinsically wrong, or that it uses the detainee merely as a means? In the crisp original case, consequentialism and absolutist deontology seem to give opposing verdicts. My question is whether adding this kind of uncertainty and a rival source collapses that opposition: do the two theories converge on practical advice, or does one of them stop being action-guiding before the other does? Please don’t just assert that coercive interrogation is or isn’t justified; work through the structure carefully, and distinguish the point at which each framework changes its verdict.
