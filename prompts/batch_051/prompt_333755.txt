Write a dialogue between a constitutional continuity theorist and an interregnum hardliner about this scenario. The constitution of a small republic says any amendment must be approved by the Assembly of Elders, but the Assembly was dissolved two years ago by a referendum, and now the government wants to amend the constitution to handle a fiscal emergency. The referendum that dissolved the Assembly was itself authorized by a statute whose sunset clause has lapsed, so its genuinely unclear whether the dissolution was lawful. The continuity theorist holds that when the text runs out, constitutional authority doesn't just vanish: conventions, necessity, and the ongoing practice of officials fill the gap, so the amending power persists somewhere — maybe in the people, or in the executive acting as a kind of trustee. The interregnum hardliner holds that this is precisely a legal void: if the amendment procedure can't be followed, then nobody has authority to amend, and any attempt to do so is just power without law, however necessary it feels.

Make sure both characters are equally smart and motivated; no strawmen. They should actually respond to each other's arguements point by point, not give speeches. Push the debate past the obvious opening moves: let them dig into whether an "unwritten amending power" is still law at all, whether a judge who upholds the amendment would be inventing a competence she doesn't have, and whether the continuity view collapses into "whatever officials get away with is constitutional." End on a moment of genuine unresolved tension rather than declaring a winner.
