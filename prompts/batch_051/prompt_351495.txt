I am a features editor at a magazine that covers science and ideas, and each week I have to decide whether an expert's explanation of a phenomenon is genuinely informative or whether it merely postpones the difficulty one step further. The culprits are familiar: the memory researcher who explains how the brain recognises a recollection by positing a system that searches and matches stored traces; the sociologist who explains rule-following by positing an internalised rule; the physicist who grounds each layer of reality in a more fundamental layer of the same broad sort; the metaphysician who says any relation between two things requires a further relation to tie the first relation to its relata. I have no philosophical training, roughly two days before copy must be filed, and a deputy editor who will not accept a piece that reads like a philosophy seminar.

Please write me a decision procedure — a checklist I can actually run on an explanation before I accept it, add caveats, or send it back — for telling apart a regress that is vicious from a regress that is merely infinite but innocent. It should state: the questions to ask in order; what counts as a satisfactory answer to each; the points where the procedure stops with "publishable with ordinary caveats"; the points where it stops with "send it back for reformulation"; and the points where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of judgement rather than a mechanical test. Keep the machinery jargon-free, but I want the underlying distinctions to be ones a philosopher would recognise as doing real work: between an infinite sequence that can be characterised all at once and one in which each step becomes intelligible only after the next has been completed; between a regress that changes the subject to a different kind of fact and one that keeps invoking the same kind of fact; and between a regress that shows the original claim was ill-formed and one that is a harmless artefact of wording. Where the procedure itself misfires, mark that too.

Then run the procedure on three explanations of the kind I actually receive: (1) the memory researcher's searching-and-recognising faculty; (2) the physicist's bottomless descent through layers of nature; and (3) the sociologist's account of norms followed because agents internalise norms, where internalisation is itself a norm-governed practice. My working hunch is that (1) is vicious while (2) and (3) are innocent; do not assume I am right — let the procedure decide, and make at least one of the three come out the other way, explaining why.

Also stress-test the procedure on two boundary cases: a logician who treats every rule of inference as a further premise needing its own rule of application, and the metaphysician who posits a further relation whenever anything stands in a relation. Finally, say plainly what the procedure cannot do — what it will inevitably miss or flatten about the logic of explanation when operated at speed by a non-specialist. Please write the answer in British English.
