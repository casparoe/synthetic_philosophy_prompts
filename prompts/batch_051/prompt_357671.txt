I have been reading about supertasks, and Thomson's lamp is the one I cannot stop thinking about. I would like you to act as a professional philosopher of physics and help me think through a variation, because I suspect the standard argument depends on an assumption that almost never gets said out loud.

The original setup, as I understand it, runs like this. A lamp has a two-position switch. At t = 1/2 minute it is switched on; at t = 3/4 it is switched off; at t = 7/8 on again; at t = 15/16 off again; and so on, halving the remaining time at each stage. By t = 1 minute, infinitely many togglings have occurred. The switch's history is completely specified, yet the on/off sequence never settles into a limit, so there seems to be no fact of the matter about whether the lamp is on or off at t = 1. The standard conclusion is that a fully specified infinite series of operations can still leave a physical state undetermined.

My variation: keep the same infinite schedule of stages, but replace the two-position switch with a dimmer whose brightness can take every real value from 0 to 1. Now add a constraint: suppose the dimmer's brightness must be a continuous function of time over the entire closed interval up to and including t = 1, with no jumps anywhere, including at the limit. Keep the alternating requirement at the stage boundaries: brightness is 1 at t = 1/2, 0 at t = 3/4, 1 at t = 7/8, 0 at t = 15/16, and so on, sweeping smoothly inside each stage.

Does Thomson's conclusion survive this modification? I can see a few possibilities. The continuity requirement might force the final brightness at t = 1 to be a single well-defined value, dissolving the paradox. It might instead make the supertask impossible, because the two subsequences of boundary brightnesses approach different limits. Or the final brightness might remain just as unconstrained as in the original, because continuity alone does not prevent unbounded oscillation near the deadline. I want to know which of these is right, and whether the moral of the original argument — that the final state is not fixed by the infinite history — is still true once the switch is continuous, or whether it was an artifact of the discrete two-state switch.
