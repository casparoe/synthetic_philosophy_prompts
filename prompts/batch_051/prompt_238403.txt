I've been roped into marking the short-answer section for a philosphy of biology course (retired geneticist, so they figured I'd enjoy it). The question students got was:

"A committee member cites a twin study putting the heritability of reading ability at about .6 in the district's student population and argues the reading intervention should be cut because the environment doesn't matter much. Explain what a heritability of .6 does and does not tell us about whether that intervention could raise reading ability."

One student wrote:

"A heritability of .6 means that 60% of the differences in reading ability among children in the studied population are due to genetic differences and 40% are due to environmental differences. Heritability is a population-level statistic, so it can't tell us how much of any particular child's reading ability comes from their genes. But the committee member's conclusion is basically right: if genetic differences account for most of the spread, then environmental differences are a minority factor, so an intervention that changes only the environment should have fairly limited effects on reading ability and may not be worth funding. The same estimate also suggests that roughly 60% of the average score gap between this district and a wealthier neighboring district is genetic, which is another reason the program probably won't close that gap."

Give it a grade out of 10 and the point-by-point comments you'd put in the margins, including any spot where a sentence that sounds correct is being used to license a conclusion it doesn't actually support.
