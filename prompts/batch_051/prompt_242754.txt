I'm the medical director of a teleradiology group that does a lot of over-read work for urgent care clinics, and the bottleneck in our turnaround isn't the workflow, it's when two of our own readers disagree. The setup is exactly the kind of case epistemologists seem to argue about: two board-certified radiologists, same images, same clinical history, roughly the same amount of experience, and they come apart—one calls a lung nodule suspicious, the other calls it benign, one sees a subtle fracture, the other calls it a motion artifact. Right now when that happens we just order a third read and forward whatever the third person says to the referring clinic. What we've never worked out is what the first two readers should do to their own reports in the meantime, or whether the disagreement itself should change how confident either of them is allowed to sound. I've been reading some of the epistemology of disagreement on the side, and I keep recognizing our situation in it: the conciliationist says that when two equally competent people have looked at the same evidence and disagree, both are rationally required to move substantially toward each other (the equal-weight idea), while the steadfast view says no—each can hold their ground if their own first-order reasoning was careful, because the disagreement is just evidence about the other person's judgment, not about the scan. I don't want a survey of that debate. I want it turned into something my group can run.

Write me a decision procedure we can actually use every time two readers disagree: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with "no third read needed" and both reports stand, where it escalates to a third read or to me, and where it's a judgment call that no procedure can eliminate. It has to keep the distinctions that actually do work—like whether both readers really did see the same priors, same history, same images—and drop the ones that only matter to philosophers. It has to be runnable for a group doing maybe 30–50 over-reads a week with a 24-hour turnaround and a medical director who signs off on escalations, and it should be honest about which steps are just my values wearing a checklist costume. Then apply the procedure to three cases: (1) two readers disagree about whether a lung nodule is benign or needs short-interval follow-up; (2) two readers disagree about whether a finding on a wrist film is a hairline fracture or an artifact in a nine-year-old who fell off a bike; (3) one reader reports a finding and the other agrees the thing is present on the images but thinks it's too equivocal to put in a report. And don't rig it: make sure at least one of those applications comes out in a way I won't like, where the procedure tells my more confident reader to soften a report even though they're probably right. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
