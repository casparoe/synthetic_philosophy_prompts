You're an expert in decision theory and the philosophy of rationality, and you've spent way too much time thinking about the Allais paradox. I want you to write a dialogue. Before you write a single line of it, think through—carefully and at length—the strongest version of each side's argument and the point where each side is genuinely weakest. Don't put that brainstorming in the final dialogue; use it to make the dialogue sharp.

The setup: two people, E and A, have just made choices in the Allais two-problem setup. Here are the payoffs so we're on the same page.

Problem 1: choose between
1A: $1,000,000 for sure.
1B: $1,000,000 with 89% probability, $5,000,000 with 10% probability, nothing with 1% probability.

Problem 2: choose between
2A: $1,000,000 with 11% probability, nothing with 89% probability.
2B: $5,000,000 with 10% probability, nothing with 90% probability.

E chose 1A and 2A, the pair that respects the independence axiom. A chose 1A and 2B, the usual Allais pattern. Both chose 1A, so the real fight is over Problem 2 and whether choosing 1A commits you to 2A. They're in the debrief room afterward, the two choice sheets still on the table, and each is trying to bring the other around to there own pair of choices.

Neither character is a strawman. E should do more than mechanically invoke the sure-thing principle: E should be able to lay out the common 89% branch, explain why it's normatively inert, and press A on what happens if the same pattern of reasoning is run across a sequence of choices (does A become dynamically inconsistent, or exploitable?). A should do more than say "certainty feels special": A should be able to explain why the 1% chance of nothing in 1B is doing real work, why anticipated regret is not just a bias to be scrubbed out, and why the independence axiom sneaks in a claim about the separability of mutually exclusive branches that a reasonable person can reject. Each should respond to what the other actually said, not just reload their own view. Let each of them land at least one genuinely good hit, and let each of them concede or refine at least one point. Don't resolve the disagreement at the end; leave the reader able to say exactly where the two of them bottom out.

Aim for roughly 1,200 to 1,800 words. Keep the tone natural and a little tense—two smart people who are actually annoyed that the other won't see it. And don't let either character quote authorities or cite studies; make them argue it on the merits.
