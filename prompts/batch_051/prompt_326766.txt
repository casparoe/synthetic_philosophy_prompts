Act as an expert on Augustine’s anti-Pelagian writings and the later Reformed–Arminian controversy over grace, predestination, and free choice. You are also a seasoned examiner who knows how to write essay questions that separate students who understand the internal logic of an argument from those who only know the party labels. I am setting a final exam for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on predestination and free will. The exam essay is one of two, worth 50 marks, with 90 minutes recommended, and a target length of about 900 words. Please produce three things in order: (a) the exam question itself, built around the passage below from Augustine’s *On Grace and Free Will*; (b) a model answer of roughly 850–900 words; and (c) a marking scheme that names the specific moves separating an A answer from a B answer, and the specific mistakes that should lose marks.

The question should force students to work through whether Augustine’s argument from the existence of divine precepts to the existence of a free choice of will also licenses an Arminian account of the will’s ability to obey, and to explain where Calvin’s doctrine of the efficacy of grace would drive a wedge into that argument. It should not be answerable by reciting summaries of “Augustine vs Pelagius” or “Calvin vs Arminius.” Before drafting anything, think carefully about what the passage proves, what it merely assumes, and what it leaves open.

The passage, from Augustine, *On Grace and Free Will*, chapter 2:

Now He has revealed to us, through His Holy Scriptures, that there is in a man a free choice of will. But how He has revealed this I do not recount in human language, but in divine. There is, to begin with, the fact that God’s precepts themselves would be of no use to a man unless he had free choice of will, so that by performing them he might obtain the promised rewards. For they are given that no one might be able to plead the excuse of ignorance, as the Lord says concerning the Jews in the gospel: If I had not come and spoken unto them, they would not have sin; but now they have no excuse for their sin.
