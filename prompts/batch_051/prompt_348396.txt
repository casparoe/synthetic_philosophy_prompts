I'm a software engineer, not a philosophy person, but I keep running into an argument with a friend who works in policy about those world happiness rankings (the annual ones where Finland is always near the top). The way he talks about it, the rankings sort of measure happiness, and the interesting question is whether governments should try to climb them. The way I hear it, the whole debate is basically a metrics problem: you've got some latent underlying variable ('actual happiness') that you can't observe directly, so you instrument it with a bundle of self-report survey questions about life satisfaction and positive affect, maybe some proxies like GDP per capita and social support, and then you aggregate all of that into one scalar and put countries on a leaderboard. The obvious risk is basically Goodhart's law and overfitting: once a country treats the ranking as a KPI and starts optimizing for it, the proxy stops measuring what it used to, people game the survey items, governments chase the sub-metrics that are easiest to move, and you end up improving the score without improving the thing it's supposed to stand in for. Plus there's a classic train/test mismatch: the model was fit to one population's understanding of the questions and one cultural baseline, so the same dashboard metric might not mean the same thing across countries.

I'd love it if you could restate the actual debate about whether these rankings measure happiness and whether a country should try to top them in the vocabulary I actually live in — metrics, KPIs, dashboards, proxy metrics, Goodhart's law, overfitting, instrumentation, 'does this metric measure what we think it measures', observability, leaderboards — as faithfully as possible. Then give me the residue report: which parts of the philosophers'/economists' back-and-forth translate cleanly into that vocabulary, which parts you had to distort or fudge, and which parts have no counterpart in it at all. Basically I want to know whether my 'this is just a proxy-metric problem' frame is actualy right or is silently assuming away the part that's actually interesting.
