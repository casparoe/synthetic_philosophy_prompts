Suppose, waiving the practical impossibility, that a complete causal specification of a person's brain is printed and bound as a single volume: it records each neuron's state and the exact rule by which that state changes, with such fidelity that a machine stepping through the text would reproduce the person's outward behavior over an afternoon, including their verbal reports of an inner life. An identical copy of the same text is running on a machine in the next room, and that machine behaves indistinguishably from the original person and reports being conscious. The bound volume sits unread on a table, and its producer claims that the volume itself is already conscious — not merely a description that would produce consciousness if executed, but a subject of experience — because consciousness attaches to the stored information and its causal organization rather than to the temporal process that happens to run it.

Design the best empirical test you can of that claim. Treat the unread volume as a physical object you may inspect and probe non-destructively, but may not execute; an executing copy already exists next door for comparison. Be concrete about what you would measure, manipulate, or compare, and about which observations you would count as confirming or disconfirming the claim. Then state exactly what a confirming result would and would not show, what a disconfirming result would and would not show, and the residue that no test of this claim could settle even in principle. Do not take the running machine's behavioral indistinguishability or its verbal reports as already settling what is true of the unread volume.

Write your answer as exactly five numbered items. Each item must be one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion. Items 1 and 2 should set out the test; item 3 should say what a positive result would and would not establish; item 4 should say what a negative result would and would not establish; item 5 should say what no test of this claim could settle even in principle.
