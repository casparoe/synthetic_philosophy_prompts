Two friends of mine got into it at a game night last weekend over something I was only half following, since I'm a software engineer and my history knowledge is basically podcast-level. The thing that set them off was this passage from Herodotus, The Histories, 7.152 (A. D. Godley translation, 1920):

"As for myself, although it is my business to set down that which is told me, to believe it is none at all of my business. This I ask the reader to hold true for the whole of my history, for there is another tale current, according to which it would seem that it was the Argives who invited the Persian into Hellas, because the war with the Lacedaemonians was going badly, and they would prefer anything to their present distresses."

The exchange, as near as I could follow it, went like this. (1) Maya said this is basically source criticism two and a half thousand years early: Herodotus seperates the job of recording reports from the job of endorsing them, and then he applies that rule to his whole book. (2) Evan said that's too generous. Separating recording from believing is a disclaimer, not a method; Herodotus doesn't offer any procedure for figuring out which reports are true, he just says believing them is none of his business and then retells the story anyway. (3) Maya said that's anachronistic. In his context the normal move was for writers to vouch for their material, so Herodotus is doing something genuinely weird: telling readers to hold even the narrator at arm's length. You don't need modern instruments for that, you just need the insight that your sources are reports, not facts. (4) Evan said a blanket warning applied to every single story is as useless as no warning. "Believe all of this" and "believe none of this with confidence" both fail to tell a reader why any particular story deserves more or less trust. The real step toward source criticism comes later, with people like Thucydides claiming to check and cross-examine things, not before. (5) Maya said that treats hedging as if it were meant to be a method, when it's actually a stance: Herodotus wasn't trying to solve which reports to trust, he was refusing to be an authority over them.
