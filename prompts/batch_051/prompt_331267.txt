In my seminar on the philosophy of medicine we have been working on the authority of the earlier self: how far a person, while competent, can bind her later, incapacitated self through an advance refusal of life-sustaining treatment. A student recently pressed the following argument, contending that the usual justification for honouring such refusals collapses in the case of advanced dementia. Each step seemed to me individually plausible, yet I half-suspect that some key term quietly changes its meaning between the first step and the last, and I should welcome a careful audit of the argument step by step.

(1) A competent adult's refusal of life-sustaining treatment is honoured because the question at issue is whether treatment would make her life go better or worse, and that is a matter of her own interests, judged from her standpoint within the life that is at stake.

(2) The respect owed to such a refusal therefore has its ground in the way the decision expresses the person's settled concerns about how her life is to go, the concerns in virtue of which treatment counts as benefit or burden for her.

(3) An advance refusal is made while those concerns are intact but acted upon only later, when they may be gone. A patient with advanced dementia may take pleasure in continued care and human contact, and may find the earlier self's fear of a dependent, lingering death meaningless or alien.

(4) The interests that gave the refusal its point are thus not interests of the person now being treated: they are not experienced as goods by her, and the only interests that remain are the later self's present, experienced well-being.

(5) Therefore, where an advance refusal conflicts with the later self's present wishes, the earlier self has no authority over the later self's care; only the later self's present, experienced interests determine what should be done.

Please answer in exactly five numbered points, (i) to (v), each no more than two sentences, with no introduction and no closing sentence. Point (i) should state whether the argument as written is valid, and if not, name the fallacy or equivocation involved. Points (ii) to (v) should assess premises (1) to (4) respectively, giving each premise its strongest defensible reading and making clear which, if any, fails.
