Two people in my book club got into it last week after someone brought up anti-aging research, and I keep turning the exchange over because neither of them seemed obviously wrong to me. Dana said she wouldn't take a pill that gave her 200 extra healthy years, even if it were free. Her first move was (1): a life is like a story, and stories need endings; a 200-year life would have no narrative arc, and many of the things we care about—finishing a project, reconciling with family, committing to a person—get part of their weight from the fact that our time is finite. Marco replied (2) that this is an aesthetic preference dressed up as a reason, and you wouldn't tell a cancer patient to skip treatment because their story needs an ending. Dana came back (3): it isn't only aesthetics; scarcity creates motivation. If you knew you had centuries, you'd never write the novel or apologize to your brother now because there would always be a later, and relationships might thin out because no person is a limited resource. Marco answered (4) that scarcity and value aren't the same thing—air isn't scarce and we'd still miss it if it were gone—and that people already procrastinate with death bearing down on them, so the motivation claim looks empirically weak. At that point Dana shifted ground (5): the strongest version isn't about individual psychology at all; it's that a society of 200-year-olds would hoard positions and wealth, become risk-averse, and lose the generational turnover that refreshes institutions. Marco said (6) that this is a different argument from (1)–(3), and that its a checkable social prediction rather than a claim that mortality is good for the person; he wanted to know whether she was still defending (3) or just raising (5) as a separate worry.
