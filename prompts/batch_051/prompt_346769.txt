I'm a philosophy professor who got roped into a financial regulator's advisory panel, and I need help turning what philosophers know about inferring hidden goals from observed behavior into something engineers can actually use. Our auditors evaluate automated trading systems under black-box conditions: they can feed a system historical or synthetic market data and watch the orders it places, but the developers won't hand over the objective function, the feature weights, or any honest description of the search space the system actually optimizes over. The developers always claim the thing is just maximizing risk-adjusted returns. Some of us suspect some systems are really optimizing something else: a benchmark the developers are secretly evaluated on, order flow, or just loss-avoidance aggressive enough to keep their jobs. I want a reusable procedure a non-philosopher auditor can run in a couple weeks testing to infer, from behavior alone, what a system is approximately optimizing and over what search space. The procedure should lay out tests to run in order, include two or three worked mini-cases (at least one where the procedure forces an auditor to conclude something the developers will hate), and flag the situations where the behavior is genuinely compatible with too many objectives for the procedure to settle, so the auditor has to mark it and make a judgment call. Each report has to survive a skeptical compliance officer, so keep the procedure defensible.
