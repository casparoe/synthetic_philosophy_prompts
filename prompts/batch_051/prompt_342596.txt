A passage in Pascal's Pensées has been quietly doing a lot of work in arguments I keep having, and I want a diagnosis of the debate rather than another round in it. The passage is the one near the end of the wager section, in Trotter's translation, where Pascal turns from the betting table to the problem of unbelief:

"This will make you believe and deaden your acuteness."

The setup, for context: Pascal has argued that the expected value of wagering on God is infinite, that the cost of wagering is finite and already being paid, and that there is no evading the bet. Then he has his interlocutor say, roughly, that the force of the argument is fine but the listener finds he cannot believe. Pascal's reply is not another argument. It is something like: you have come to me asking for a remedy for unbelief; look at the people who were once bound as you are and now behave otherwise; they made themselves begin by acting as if they believed, by taking holy water, by having Masses said, and so on; naturally this will make you believe and deaden your acuteness.

The debate that has stalled on my reading of the rationalist forums goes like this. One camp treats the wager as a deep but basically sound piece of decision theory with a restricted jurisdiction: it is an argument for choosing the religious life, not for believing, and Pascal's advice merely anticipates the modern distinction between belief and acceptance. We can choose to act on the proposition, design our environment so the belief becomes more available, and regard the "deaden your acuteness" line as an honest if unflattering description of how ordinary minds work. The other camp treats this passage as the moment Pascal shows what the wager really is: a license for self-manipulation. If you have to arrange your own belief by ritual and habituation rather than by evidence, then the wager is not a reason for belief; it is an instruction to trade one cognitive standard for another, and the expected-value apparatus is a distraction. On this view, the passage about staring at the "endless immensity of spaces" does the same work as the holy water passage: Pascal is trying to change how the problem feels, not solve it. Both camps can cite the same text. Neither moves.

My hypothesis is that the impasse persists because the two sides are not using "wager" to pick out the same argument at all. The first camp is evaluating an argument for what one ought to do given a stated payoff matrix, while the second camp is evaluating an argument for how one ought to come to believe. And I suspect the passage about deadening one's acuteness marks the seam: it is the point where the first camp has to treat a belief-engineering move as a corollary of the decision, and the second camp takes the same move as evidence that the argument has changed its subject. But I also worry this is too tidy, because a defender of the wager can reply that the activity of belief-engineering just is one of the acts the wager prescribes, and the defender of the wager was never committed to the claim that all belief-acquisition is evidential.

So I want a structural diagnosis. Is this a verbal dispute, a factual dispute about how beliefs are actually formed, a disagreement about which cases count as data, or a genuine dispute about whether there can be non-evidential reasons for belief? Is the passage about deadening one's acuteness doing the same argumentative work in both readings, or does it mark a real fork? And on your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction, an empirical finding, a formal result, or an agreement that the question was ill-posed? Be specific about what each side has to concede given your account of the impasse. I am less interested in who wins the wager than in why the argument that seems designed to settle something instead produces two armed camps who quote the same paragraph at each other.
