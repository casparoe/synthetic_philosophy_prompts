I'm a PhD student in developmental psychology, writing a chapter on children's epistemic trust, and there's an asymmetry in ordinary parenting judgements that keeps bugging me. I can't decide wether it's defensible or just sentimentality.

Take two parents. The first spends weeks building up Santa and the tooth fairy — eating the mince pie, staging sooty footprints, slipping a pound under the pillow, and answering 'yes' when their eight-year-old asks point blank whether Santa is real. This same parent also uses it to manage behaviour: 'Santa's watching, so stop hitting your sister.' Most people find all this sweet, and would deny that it damages the child's trust. The second parent's child's cat dies, and the parent says the cat has gone to live on a farm. A lot of the same people wince at that one, and parenting books say it will eventually erode the child's trust when they find out.

Both are deliberate falsehoods told by the person the child relies on most, both let the child inhabit a false picture of the world, and in both cases the child will eventually learn the truth. If anything, the Santa story looks worse on paper: it's sustained over years and used as a tool of control, while the farm lie is a single lump of misplaced kindness. So what, if anything, justifies the ordinary verdict that Santa is harmless and the farm story is a mistake? Is it that Santa is a communal fiction the child is meant to age out of, while the farm lie is private and has no exit? Or that the child half-knows Santa is a game already? I'd like the strongest principle you can find, tested against nearby cases: a family that keeps Santa going mainly for the parents' own nostalgia, and a community where 'gone to live on a farm' is the shared idiom for pet death. If those flip your judgment, tell me.
