I have been trying to sort out what I actually believe about the old question of whether excellence is good in itself, or only when it does someone some good, and I have found that my honest reactions to cases pull in different directions. I would like you to find the principle behind the pattern of my verdicts, or to tell me which of the verdicts cannot be kept if the pattern is made coherent.

Here are the verdicts, recorded as faithfully as I can. (1) A mathematician produces a proof of a theorem that was already proved by another method; the new proof is strikingly elegant, but it makes no new results possible and almost no one studies it. I think this is good, and not merely good as a means to future mathematics. (2) A musician records a technically flawless performance and then deletes the file, so that no one else will ever hear it. I want to say the performance was excellent, but I hesitate to say it was good in the same way as the proof; something seems missing. (3) A runner trains alone for years and sets a personal record on a route the runner designed, with no audience, no opponent, and no official recognition. I count this a genuine achievement, but I find myself saying only that it is good for the runner, not good, period. (4) A person memorizes one hundred thousand digits of pi and earns a recognized record in doing so, but the feat benefits no one and interests almost no one. I do not find this good; I find it an impressive waste of effort. (5) A scientist writes a correct and important proof, which is then misplaced and never read, until it is independently rediscovered a century later. I think the original scientist's work was good, even though for a century it did no one any good at all. (6) A calligrapher spends months producing a single work, which is then burned unread as a deliberate part of the practice. I find this good, and not merely good for the calligrapher.

My tentative principle is something like this: an activity is good to the extent that it is an excellent exercise of a central human capacity, whether or not anyone ever benefits. But I am troubled, because that principle seems to make the pi memorizer's feat good, and I do not want it to be; and it does not explain why the deleted recording and the private record feel less good than the redundant proof and the burned calligraphy. Please state the principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that best captures the verdicts I actually hold; say which of my verdicts, if any, it forces me to abandon, and why; and then apply the resulting view to two further cases I have not settled: a chess player who privately works out a beautiful study that no one else ever sees, and a person who becomes, through costly training, the best in the world at a sport the person invented and no one else plays.
