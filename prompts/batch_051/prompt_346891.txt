I am preparing a graduate seminar in the philosophy of mathematical practice on why some definitions win, and before we read any of the case studies I want the standard of evaluation fixed in writing. Produce the list of desiderata that an adequate account of definitional choice must satisfy: an account of what makes a mathematical definition the right notion rather than one that is merely equivalent, merely convenient, or merely familiar. Take as the two running cases (1) the elevation of continuity to the open-set definition rather than the epsilon-delta definition, and (2) the consolidation of the group axioms as the canonical form of the symmetry idea. For each desideratum, state it precisely, give the case or consideration that forces it, and mark whether it is non-negotiable for any adequate account or a preference of a particular camp (structuralist, historicist, category-theoretic, practice-oriented, and the like). Then flag any pairs of desiderata you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied, and identify the single desideratum that, applied honestly, does the most to eliminate candidate accounts.

Throughout, keep the following distinctions in view: a definition that is correct in a fixed setting versus one that travels across settings; proving power versus perspicuity; the historical route by which a notion was found versus the structural role it plays once established. Do not score named philosophical accounts in detail; the criteria are the deliverable, and the seminar will apply them.
