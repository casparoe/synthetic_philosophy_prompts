I teach political theory, and next month I'm presenting a paper at an institute full of classical liberals — people who treat On Liberty as the center of Mill and think the later socialist turn was a sad, senile detour. My thesis is that the turn was the working-out of his liberalism, not a break from it. The throughline: Mill never thought property was a natural right; it's justified by utility and its contribution to self-development. Wage labor, on his own account, trains passivity and servility. So once the cooperative form becomes feasible, the same principles that ground liberty push toward worker cooperatives rather than owner-capitalist firms — while leaving Mill, as in fact he was, wary of centralized state socialism and insistent about population and incentive constraints. I'm defending the view on the merits, not just as a reading of him.

For each one: the strongest form of the objection, what I should grant on the spot, what I should hold, and where my reply is soft. And be straight with me — is the worst problem the incentive/knowledge objection, the changed-human-nature worry, or something else I'm not seeing coming? Put the question I should be praying nobody asks at the end.
