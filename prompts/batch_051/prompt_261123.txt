I spent my career as an experimental physicist and have only recently begun reading seriously in the philosophy of science, where I keep meeting tidy reconstructions of the Kuhn–Popper dispute that I suspect may collapse under scrutiny. A popular history of twentieth-century science I recently read summarizes the dispute this way: Popper held that science is rational because scientists formulate bold, falsifiable conjectures and submit them to severe tests, giving up those that fail. Kuhn replied that this is not what scientists mostly do; outside rare revolutionary episodes they work within a paradigm, solving its puzzles and setting anomalies aside. When enough anomalies accumulate, the field enters a crisis and a revolution replaces the old paradigm with a new one. During such a revolution there is no neutral, paradigm-independent standard by which the rival frameworks can be compared, since each carries its own standards of evidence and even its own meanings; the shift from one paradigm to another is therefore more like a gestalt switch or a conversion than a reasoned decision, and the outcome is settled by persuasion, prestige, and the sociology of the scientific community rather than by rational adjudication. For this reason, the book concludes, Kuhn is best understood as a relativist about scientific knowledge. How fair is this summary? Please work through it carefully, separating what is accurate from what is a simplification or overstatement, and say what Kuhn himself explicitly denied. Does the relativist conclusion follow from the accurate parts of the summary? I would also like you to indicate how confident you are in the various parts of your assessment, and to single out the point you are least sure of.
