I have been trying to reconstruct an argument, often made in the informal terms of negotiation rather than philosophy, that models moral compromise on economic exchange and concludes that principled refusals to compromise are confused. I would like your verdict on it, with a definite answer rather than a survey of possibilities.

(1) A trade is rational for both parties whenever each expects to be better off with the trade than without it; this is the usual idea of gains from trade.
(2) A compromise between rival value systems has the structure of a trade: each side yields on matters it regards as less important in exchange for the other side's yielding on matters it regards as more important.
(3) Therefore, when both sides to a moral disagreement sincerely judge a proposed compromise to leave them better off than the status quo, the compromise is a Pareto improvement over the status quo.
(4) If an arrangement is a Pareto improvement over the status quo, then adopting it cannot be morally worse than remaining with the status quo; so a refusal to adopt it cannot be justified by the thought that the status quo is morally better.
(5) Hence, a party who sincerely judges a compromise better than the status quo and still refuses it, on the ground that accepting it would betray a principle, must either be misdescribing the alternatives or be acting on a non-moral distaste for yielding.
(6) So the familiar idea that some compromises are right to refuse because they would compromise one's principles rests on a confusion: compromising one's principles is just the gains-from-trade logic of trade-offs described in a moralized tone, not a distinct moral reason against accepting an outcome one judges better.

I keep suspecting that step (4) is doing the work, but when I try to say exactly what is wrong with it I end up merely asserting that one cannot move from "better off by their own lights" to "not morally worse." I would like a committed assessment. Is the argument valid? If not, which step should be rejected, on what reading, and what is the strongest corrected version of that step? If it is valid, does the conclusion really have the deflationary force claimed for it, or does it smuggle in a substantive consequentialist assumption?
