Philosophers sometimes defend a principle by showing that it best coheres, after mutual adjustment, with our considered judgments about particular cases. In its narrow form this method takes only moral judgments and moral principles into account; in its wide form it also brings in background theories of persons, society, and moral psychology. Please sort the six objections below into three tiers—fatal, serious but answerable, and largely misplaced—according to how much each threatens the claim that reflective equilibrium can justify principles rather than merely systematize the reasoner's starting commitments. Give a one-sentence reason for each placement, and leave no item untiered.

1. The inputs are contaminated: considered judgments can encode prejudice or self-interest, and the method's movements cannot give them independent credibility.
2. The method is indeterminate: different starting sets of judgments, or different orders of adjustment, can lead to incompatible endpoints.
3. Narrow equilibrium is objectionably restrictive: it brackets background theories in advance, so its results can be no stronger than that restriction.
4. Wide equilibrium merely relocates the problem: background theories are themselves contestable and likely to be chosen partly for fit with the very judgments they are then used to support.
5. The method is either psychologically unattainable in its full form or, if read as a mere ideal, cannot tell an actual reasoner what to believe now.
6. The procedure is circular against the skeptic: it answers a challenge to the authority of considered judgments by appealing to considered judgments.

After sorting, answer two further questions in one or two sentences each. Which single objection most forces a proponent of the narrow method to adopt the wide method, and why? Which objection cannot be fully answered even by going wide, and why?
