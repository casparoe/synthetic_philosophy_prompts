I have a rule for deciding which expert findings to take seriously in domains where I can't evaluate the underlying work myself. I ask one screening question: can the investigators state, in a few sentences, a procedure whose steps I recognize in advance as the way science establishes things — a pre-registered hypothesis, a controlled comparison, and an independent replication? If yes, I give the result real weight even when specialists disagree. If no, I don't just discount the result; I set it to zero for my own decisions, no matter how plausible the mechanism or how experienced the advocates are. Last month I applied the rule to a friend's claim about a treatment for chronic pain: she had a detailed mechanism and cited clinicians with many years of experience, but she could not produce a randomized controlled trial, so I refused to let the claim influence any decision. She told me this is exactly the move Paul Feyerabend warned about — that the history of science shows no single method has ever functioned as the gatekeeper of progress, and that in practice anything goes. Is my policy defensible as a way for a non-specialist to ration trust? Under what conditions is it better than weighing each case on its merits, and under what conditions does it turn into a ritual that protects me from evidence? Is there a rewritten version that keeps the screening benefit while dropping the false negatives — and how would I tell, from the inside, whether my method-talk is protecting my judgment or flattering it? Where your answer depends on what Feyerabend or historians of science have actually established, please separate the settled from the genuinely contested, mark which points are disputed among specialists, and don't present a contested interpretation as the field's consensus.
