Before I read any more of the safety-versus-capabilities literature, I want the specification rather than another position paper. Write the desiderata for a theory of the alignment tax and the competitiveness constraint — roughly, the claim that a lab which spends time, compute, or talent on safety that could have gone to capabilities accepts a real disadvantage (the tax), and that pressure from rival labs makes that disadvantage hard to sustain (the constraint). I want to know what any adequate story about whether and when that tax is real has to get right.

Give me a requirements list, not a survey. For each requirement: the case or consideration that makes it non-negotiable, and a note on which of the usual quick accounts — "safety is just a tax," "safety is good business in the long run," "a regulator's floor makes it moot," "it's a collective-action problem that just needs coordination" — it quietly kills. Keep most of the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; leave only a quarter or so for the scoring.

At minimum, make the list handle: the difference between an alignment tax and mere safety spending, and why it matters (a cost every firm bears equally is not a competitive disadvantage); the counterfactual baseline — caution priced relative to the most profitable feasible policy, not relative to what a lab happens to be doing already; the leader's case, where the tax may simply not bite; the laggard's case, where the same dollar of caution is fatal; the case where caution is product differentiation that customers pay for; the regulator's floor that re-levels the field; the dynamic case where paying early lowers total cost later, so the "tax" is really an investment; and the extreme case where one lab's skimping could destroy the market for everyone, making the expected tax negative.

Throughout, preserve the distinctions that cheap accounts collapse: the tax at the margin versus in total; a one-off tax versus a recurring one; the decision of a single lab versus the industry equilibrium; and observable safety spend versus unobservable internal caution — if competitors can't tell who is paying the tax, what exactly punishes it?

Also tell me which of your desiderata are forced by the underlying economics and which are the preferences of one side of the debate wearing neutral clothes. Flag any pair of requirements you suspect cannot be jointly satisfied — if the list itself is the result, say so — and name the single requirement that does the most work ruling out the popular accounts.

If the framing is off — if "the alignment tax" is too loose a metaphor to bear this many distinctions, for instance, or the constraint dissolves once you compare expectations rather than margins — say so plainly and answer the corrected version of the question instead of politely building a list for a question you don't believe in.

Hard cap: 900 words, excluding any table. A table of requirement / motivating case / why it can't be traded away is welcome; the prose should carry the argument.
