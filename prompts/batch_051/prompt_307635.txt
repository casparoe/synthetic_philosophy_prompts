An internal design memo at my company uses Heidegger's analysis of equipment to argue for a principle the author calls invisible-by-default. The memo's account of Heidegger runs roughly as follows.

When a carpenter is hammering, the hammer is not encountered as a separate object whose properties the carpenter notices. It is absorbed into the activity and refers beyond itself—through the nail and the board, to the workbench, and finally toward the project the whole workshop serves. Heidegger calls this readiness-to-hand. A tool that is working well withdraws: the user does not attend to it at all. Only when something breaks, goes missing, or gets in the way does the hammer show up as a present-at-hand thing that the user can inspect and list the properties of. From this the memo draws a design conclusion: the job of design is to keep equipment in the withdrawn, ready-to-hand mode by eliminating every point of friction. Breakdown is therefore a pure failure—a sign that design has lapsed and that nothing positive is disclosed when a tool interrupts a task.

I find the summary plausible, but I suspect it is doing two things at once: reporting what Heidegger means, and quietly converting that into a design imperative. I also suspect it trades on an ambiguity in words like withdraw and invisible, as if not being attended to were the same thing as not doing any work in the experience. The memo also treats all breakdowns as one phenomenon, even though Heidegger distinguishes a tool becoming conspicuous because it is unusable, obtrusive because it is missing, and obstinate because something stands in the way.
