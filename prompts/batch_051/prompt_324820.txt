You are a philosopher of logic with extensive experience teaching where formal results and intuitive epistemology collide. I am a graduate-student TA preparing a single fifty-minute section on Fitch's paradox of knowability for second-year philosophy majors who have had propositional logic but no modal logic. Do not explain the paradox to me; plan how I should teach it.

The section should build toward the standard result: the knowability principle, roughly p → ◇Kp—if a proposition is true, it is possible to know it—seems to collapse into the far stronger p → Kp—every true proposition is in fact known—once we grant the ordinary thought that some truths are not known. The hinge is substituting an unknown truth into the knowability principle: if p ∧ ¬Kp holds for some p, then p ∧ ¬Kp itself must be possibly known; but knowledge distributes over conjunction, so knowing p ∧ ¬Kp would require knowing both p and ¬Kp, and since knowledge implies truth, K¬Kp would yield ¬Kp, leaving the putatively knowable conjunction impossible.

Give me the dependency-ordered sequence for the fifty minutes: what needs to be established before what, and why. Specify one carrying example and say exactly what features it must have and must lack; if you reject tempting alternatives, say why. Identify the misconceptions students are likely to bring or form—for example, reading knowable as possibly known now, sliding from possible knowledge of p to someone's actually knowing p, treating the knowability principle as a biconditional, or dismissing the result as an artifact of equating knowledge with justified true belief—and say exactly where in the sequence each should be preempted or dismantled. End with one check-for-understanding task that would reveal whether students grasp the modal argument rather than merely repeat its conclusion, and a short, explicit list of what a first exposure should omit and why. Start by brainstorming the conceptual dependencies and likely failure points before committing to the final plan.
