I'm grading a short-answer question for an intro ethics exam and want a second read. The question was:

Explain the separateness of persons objection to utilitarianism. Is it distinct from the objection that utilitarianism ignores individual rights? And how well does the standard reply work, the reply that says the objection just assumes gains and losses need to be balanced within a single life?

The student wrote:

The separateness of persons objection is that utilitarianism adds up everyone's utility and maximizes the total, which treats a group as if it were a single person whose gains and losses can offset each other. But no single person experiences the total, so a loss to me is not made up by a gain to you. Its closely tied to the rights objection, both say utilitarianism fails to respect the inviolability of the individual, and really the rights objection is just a special case of it. The standard reply, that the objection assumes balancing must happen within one life, doesn't work, because the objection doesn't assume that; it points out that there is no entity, the society, whose welfare is being improved by the trade. Still, the best version of the reply is the utility monster: if one person could get enormous pleasure from everyone else's resources, utilitarianism would give that person everything, which shows the separateness objection is really a worry about distributive fairness rather than about aggregation itself.
