I'm a retired ecologist — forty years watching animals do expensive, seemingly wasteful things — and my retirement project has been the philosophy of biology, which I came to rather late. When a colleague sat me down and explained Zahavi's handicap principle, a light went on in a room I didn't know I was standing in. Now I see it everywhere, including in a novel, and I'd like your help with the reading. Please wear two hats here: a philosopher of biology who actually knows the signaling literature (Zahavi, Grafen, the honest-signal debates), and a close reader of fiction. Think carefully, and before you settle on an interpretation, brainstorm a few candidate scenes and see which ones hold up under both the biology and the book.

The task: read The Great Gatsby through the lens of costly signaling and the handicap principle. Don't just catalogue status symbols. I want the stronger claim — that Gatsby's parties, the mansion, the yellow car, the shirts are handicap-style signals, displays whose very wastefulness is meant to make them credible. If so, credible of what, and to whom? The novel complicates the theory in ways I find genuinely interesting: the signals are costly, but the wealth behind them is dirty, the Oxford story is faked, and Daisy, the receiver he's aiming at, reads all that expense as proof he's the wrong sort rather than honest advertising. And then there's the green light, about as cheap a signal as you can get, which does more work in the book than any of the expensive ones. So I want a reading that's faithful in both directions: where the handicap principle really illuminates a scene or a choice, and where the novel resists the theory and an honest critic has to say so. Don't reduce the book to an illustration, and don't warp the theory to fit the book. End by saying what Gatsby adds to the debate that the formal models and field experiments don't. What bugs me most: the handicap principle is supposed to explain why signals stay honest, but Gatsby looks like a costly signal that's both dishonest and somehow honest at once. Does the novel show that cost can certify the wrong property, or that receivers just don't decode signals the way the theory assumes? Argue with the book and with the theory rather then smooth it over.
