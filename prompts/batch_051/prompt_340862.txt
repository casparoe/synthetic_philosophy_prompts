I'm part of a small architecture working group whose longest-running argument, now several years old, refuses to converge. One side holds that the right default is to minimize in-place mutation: use pure functions, persistent data structures, and value semantics so that a computation explicitly takes a state and returns the next state. Their claim is that mutation is the root of temporal coupling and aliasing — when two parts of a program share a mutable object, a change in one place can silently invalidate assumptions far away, and behavior comes to depend on order, timing, and hidden identity. The other side replies that this purity is partly an illusion: a database write, a filesystem write, and a physics step in a game are all mutations in the domain, and eliminating them from the source language just moves them into a store, a transaction log, a database, or a monad — after which the same global complexity reappears, now with more ceremony and sometimes worse performance. Everyone agrees about what the programs do. It's the evaluative question that stalls.

What kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a hidden empirical question we've never formulated — for example, whether mutation-minimizing codebases actually show lower bug rates or lower maintenance cost, and measured how? Is it a conceptual split over what "state" and "identity" mean, one side meaning a mutable location whose contents persist and change, and the other meaning the succession of values a computation involves? Is it a clash of starting intuitions — a program as a set of processes acting on external things versus a program as the incremental evaluation of functions — that neither side can argue for? Or is the question as usually posed ill-posed, because "mutation" is not one technique but many with different costs? Given your diagnosis, what would count as progress for this group: a distinction we've missed, an experiment we could jointly accept, a formal result, a reformulated question, or a good reason to stop expecting convergence?
