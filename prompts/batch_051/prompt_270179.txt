I've been rereading Pascal's Pensées, and I got stuck on the part right after the famous wager paragraph, the bit where the person says he can't force himself to believe. Pascal's answer, as I remember it, is something like: stop arguing and start doing the things believers do—take holy water, have masses said, imitate them—and belief will follow, or at least your unbelief will be blunted. I always found that weirder than the expected-value stuff, because it turns the wager into a manual for manufacturing a belief. Now suppose we alter the wager in a way I haven't seen handled head-on. Instead of two options, belief in Pascal's God or unbelief, there are three: Christianity of the sort Pascal recommends, another exclusive religion that also offers infinite reward to its own followers and no reward to outsiders, and unbelief. For the "act as if" advice, the prescribed actions partially overlap (both involve prayer, moral discipline, self-denial) and partially conflict (you can't organize your daily schedule around two full liturgical calendars). Run the modified wager carefully. Does Pascal's practical advice yield a definite recommendation between the two rituals? Is there a hedging strategy—practicing both enough to keep the possibility of belief alive in each—and would that undermine the very mechanism by which acting as if is supposed to produce sincere belief? Does the conclusion "start practicing Christianity" survive the variation, or is it only an artifact of Pascal having assumed a binary menu?
