I spent my career in physiology, and since retiring I've been reading philosophy of death, and one thing about the brain death versus circulatory death debate has me genuinely stuck. The usual framing seems to be that "death" is a single biological event and the two criteria are just two operational tests for it — like measuring temperature with two different thermometers. But that can't be right. A brain-dead patient on a ventilator still has a beating heart, perfused organs, and gas exchange; the body can even heal wounds and carry a pregnancy. By the circulatory criterion, that body is alive. And going the other way, after a witnessed cardiac arrest with no CPR we declare circulatory death while the brain is still, in cellular terms, rescuable — so the "irreversible" in the definition isn't a fact about the tissue, it's a decision not to intervene. So the two criterea pick out different events, not one event measured two ways. Here's my question: why does the philosophical literature keep talking as though death is a single well-defined biological fact that these two standards are both detecting, instead of just admitting that death is a line we draw — partly for practical reasons like organ procurement — and that the line could defensibly be moved? Is the whole brain-death debate ultimately just a semantic dispute about where to put the boundary?
