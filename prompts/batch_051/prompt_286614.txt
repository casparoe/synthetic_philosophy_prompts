I recently sat through a long argument between two acquaintances about a game-show variant of the two-envelope puzzle, and I would like you to adjudicate who was right about what, move by move. The setup they had agreed on was this. A game show has exactly three possible pairs of amounts: ten and twenty dollars, twenty and forty dollars, and forty and eighty dollars. The host chooses one pair at random with equal probability, places the two amounts in sealed envelopes, and a contestant picks one envelope at random. The contestant knows the three possible pairs, knows that the host chooses among them with equal probability, and knows that the host must offer a switch no matter what, but does not know which pair was chosen. The contestant opens the chosen envelope, sees the amount, and the host then offers her the option to switch to the other envelope. Assume the contestant is risk-neutral about money at these stakes.

The first speaker began by saying that, before the envelope is opened, the option is of no use: the contestant is equally likely to have picked the smaller or the larger amount, so switching and staying have the same expected value before she looks. The second speaker agreed about that, but said the interesting question arises after the contestant looks. She then claimed that if the contestant sees twenty dollars, the other envelope contains either ten or forty, and these are equally likely; its expected value is therefore twenty-five dollars, so the contestant should be willing to pay a small fee to switch. The first speaker replied that this was exactly the naive two-envelope mistake: the move from the other envelope being either half or double to those two possibilities being equally likely is the whole problem, and with a real prior in front of us we should not smuggle in equal probabilities. The second speaker answered that in her example she was not smuggling anything in: given the stated prior, the pairs ten and twenty and twenty and forty really are equally likely conditional on seeing twenty, so the expected gain from switching is five dollars; and the same holds if the contestant sees forty, where the other is either twenty or eighty with equal probability and the expected gain from switching is ten dollars.

The first speaker then said this only appeared to revive a paradox. If the contestant adopts the rule of always switching no matter what she sees, her expected change over all six equally likely envelope outcomes is exactly zero: the guaranteed gain when she sees ten and the guaranteed loss when she sees eighty balance the gains in the ambiguous middle. So the pre-opening symmetry is preserved and there is no contradiction. The second speaker said this was beside the point, because a contestant who sees twenty is not choosing a policy for all possible observations; she is making the particular decision that faces her, and that particular decision has positive expected value. The first speaker shot back that there was still something philosophically suspicious here: the local expected gain is an average over two possible worlds in which the contestant occupies opposite causal positions, ten in the other envelope in one world and forty in the other, and the contestant should not be able to convert an average over mutually exclusive worlds into money in hand. The second speaker replied that all expected-value reasoning averages over mutually exclusive worlds in which the agent is in different positions, so that cannot by itself be the objection.

Please adjudicate the argument in the order the moves were made: say for each individual claim whether it is correct, incorrect, or incomplete, and briefly justify each verdict; then state what the correct conclusion actually is about whether, and on what conditions, a risk-neutral contestant should pay to switch after seeing twenty or after seeing forty in this setup, and whether the two speakers were right to think the pre-opening symmetry and the positive local expected gains are in tension. Write your answer as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter or an email, and do not use headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
