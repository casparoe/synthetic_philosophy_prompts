I write about science and ideas and I'm working on a piece about whether alcohol reveals people rather than changing them. I want to hang it on Thomas Vinterberg's film Another Round (Druk), and I've been reading Plato on drunkenness, which feels like it's testing the same thesis.

The Plato side comes mostly from the Laws. The Athenian argues that wine is a kind of test: taken under controlled conditions, with a sober supervisor present, it strips away inhibition and shows what a person is really like, letting him practise self-mastery cheaply and safely before facing bigger pleasures. Young people shouldn't drink, and whoever is running the thing has to stay sober. Then there's the Symposium, where the drinking party starts with the guests hungover from the night before, so they agree to go easy on the wine to keep the conversation serious, and where Socrates drinks all night without ever being seen drunk—Alcibiades arrives plastered and marvels that no one has ever seen Socrates drunk.

The film: four high-school teachers, middle-aged and stuck, start an 'experiment' of keeping a constant low blood-alcohol level through the school day, supposedly correcting a small alcohol deficit. It starts well—their teaching gets better, one of them revives his marriage, they get looser and more alive. Then they escalate; one of them, Tommy, ends up drinking alone, goes out in his boat drunk, and is found dead in the harbour. The protagonist, Martin, finishes the film dancing wildly on the pier with his pupils before jumping into the sea.

What I'd like is a reading of the film through Plato's argument, and of the argument through the film: where it dramatises the 'wine as test' idea, where it complicates it, and where the film honestly resists Plato. For instance, is the teachers' protocol a parody of Plato's controlled symposium because the only supervisor is their own self-imposed rulebook? Does the film think drink exposes who they already were—and is that exposure good? Does the ending celebrate a Dionysian release that Plato's framework can't absorb, or does Plato's own point that wine makes old men able to sing and speak again cover even that? I want a criticism that's faithful in both directions—not a moral lesson about moderation, but also not one that skates past the death in the harbour. Tell me where the film won't fit.
