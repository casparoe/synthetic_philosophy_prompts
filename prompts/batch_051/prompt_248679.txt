I've been following the fight about formalizing the Anthropocene for a decade or so, and it's weirdly stuck. On one side you've got a big chunk of the earth-science community — plus basically every climate person and most humanists — talking as if we're plainly in a new epoch, with the boundary around the mid-20th century: the Great Acceleration, the plutonium spike from bomb tests, a candidate lake core in Ontario with a clean radionuclide layer. On the other side, the International Commission on Stratigraphy (the body that maintains the official chart) rejected the proposal to make it a formal epoch in 2024, partly because the boundary is too thin and time-transgressive, partly because a human-defined interval doesn't look much like the Pleistocene–Holocene boundary, and partly because epochs are supposed to be multimillion-year units while this would be a sliver. As far as I can tell, no one dispute the underlying facts: the sediment layer is there, the CO2 curve is there, the plastic-and-chicken-bone signal is there. And yet nobody moves.

My working theory is that this isn't really an empirical disagreement at all. The stratigraphers are asking one question — is there a synchronous, globally detectable, rock-recordable boundary of the kind we use to define chronostratigraphic units? — while the other side is asking a different one: does the Earth system now operate in a qualitatively different regime because of one species? And the "when did it start" question is doing different work in each: lithostratigraphy in one case, historical periodization in the other. If that's right, the whole fight is a category mistake, and the "Anthropocene event" compromise is just a way of not saying so. But I can also hear the geologists' reply: that the real dispute is about standards of evidence, since a single lake's plutonium layer doesn't establish a global boundary the way a proper GSSP is supposed to, and that "event" is the honest category while "epoch" is the one smuggling in politics.

So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? A hidden factual one (would a global synchronous marker settle it?), a methodological one about what counts as a boundary, a verbal one about two senses of "epoch," or an institutional one about who gets to periodize Earth history? Give me a diagnosis, and then say what would count as progress given that diagnosis — and whether the event compromise resolves anything or just lets everyone keep using the word while meaning different things.
