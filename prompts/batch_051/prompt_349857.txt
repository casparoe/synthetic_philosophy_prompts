I am a novelist, and Nozick's experience machine has always interested me less as a piece of science fiction than as a question about whether a human life can do without wanting. I want you to work through a variation of that thought experiment, aimed at a view I have been calling tranquilism, and I want the argumentative structure examined rather than the usual dismissal.

Recall the original case briefly. The machine can give you any experience you would choose, indistinguishable from real life. Nozick's suggestion is that many of us would nevertheless refuse, for roughly three reasons: we want actually to do things rather than merely have the experience of doing them; we want to be a certain sort of person rather than merely have the experience of being one; and we want contact with reality rather than a manufactured substitute. The refusal is usually taken to embarrass any view on which only felt experience matters.

Now change the machine. Call it the stilling machine. It simulates nothing and delivers no pleasure. It simply extinguishes craving and suffering. After using it, a person stays awake, lucid, and in contact with the real world, but no longer wants anything: no restlessness, fear, regret, ambition, loneliness, jealousy, or hunger beyond the bare physiological signal. The person may still act from habit or from immediate physical necessity, but no felt desire or aversion pushes or pulls at them. Nothing is craved; nothing hurts. The world has not been replaced, but it has been flattened.

Let tranquilism be the view that only the absence of craving and suffering matters—not pleasure, achievement, meaning, or authenticity, but precisely the condition the stilling machine produces. My own interest is literary: I suspect narrative requires wanting, and that the stilling machine is a machine for ending stories. But I want the philosophical analysis before I trust that impression.

The question is how the standard analysis changes. Run the three Nozickian objections against the stilling machine and say which of them survive. The contact-with-reality objection seems to lose its target, since the machine does not simulate. The other two objections, however, seem to be cravings themselves—wanting to do things and wanting to be a certain sort of person are exactly the states the machine is built to remove. So can they be raised without begging the question against tranquilism? Does this mean tranquilism is in better dialectical shape than hedonism with respect to the experience-machine test, or does it expose something worse: that tranquilism's ideal is a life in which nothing can matter to the person living it, and the fact that the person no longer minds the loss is not a reply to the objection? Finally, consider the act of choosing to enter the stilling machine. The hedonist in the original case seems committed to plugging in. Is the tranquilist similarly committed, or does the choice amount to a self-undermining act—a craving for the end of craving? Work out the strongest case on each side before taking a position.
