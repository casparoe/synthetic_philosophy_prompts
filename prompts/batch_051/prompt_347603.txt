I manage a claims team, and a disagreement about something I said on a recorded call has turned into a puzzle I can't resolve on my own. Nine days ago I told a customer that her claim would be paid by the end of the week. I believed it, and I had what looked like solid grounds: her account showed an adjuster's approval, and our policy is that approved claims are paid within three business days. The claim was in fact paid by the end of the week. Later I learned that the approval I had seen had been keyed against the wrong account. The payment went through on time for an unrelated reason — a regional manager, preparing for an audit, released the entire pending batch that morning. So my statement was true, and any reasonable person looking at the file would have said exactly what I said, but the particular basis on which I said it was false, and the truth of the statement rested on something independent of anything I knew.

Since this happened, three colleagues have staked out positions. The first says I have nothing to answer for: I spoke the truth, and I believed it rather than saying something I suspected was false. The second says that truth and belief are not enough — the question is whether I knew it at the time, and he insists I did not, so the statement itself was improper, even though no one should blame me for making it. The third says I should be judged by whether it was reasonable for me to believe the statement given everything available to me then, and I plainly met that standard.

I want help at the level of principle, not with the company's compliance procedures or customer-relations fallout. Please lay out the strongest version of each of the three positions, along with the most serious objection to each. In particular, examine the second colleague's distinction between a statement being genuinely improper and the speaker being blameworthy for it — that distinction seems to be doing a lot of work here. Then offer your own considered view about which standard I should accept, say how confident you are in that view, and identify the specific point you are least sure of.
