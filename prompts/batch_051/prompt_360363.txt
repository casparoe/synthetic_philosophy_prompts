So the culture committee at my company is floating a new feedback rule, and I have to give them a reaction. We're a distributed team with a big office in Amsterdam, one in Osaka, and a lot of remote people in the US and Latin America. The rule: by default, all work feedback has to be delivered as directly as possible—no hedging, no praise sandwich, no maybe-just-a-thought, and no white lies like telling someone their draft is fine when it isn't. The doc literally says you should say the thing you'd normally only say after two drinks. But if you do soften, hedge, or tell a small soothing untruth, you pay a fine into the team lunch fund, and the fine is logged next to your name in the thread. The committee's pitch is that this turns politeness into a costly signal: if you're willing to pay to soften something, that's a credible sign you actually care about the relationship, while the default keeps everyone maximally honest. How does this actually play out? Who talks bluntly and who pays, and does that map onto rank, native-langauge ability, or office culture in ways the committee isn't expecting? What are the failure modes—people weaponizing directness to be cruel and then pointing at the rule, people paying the fine to buy a reputation for niceness, people finding passive-aggressive ways to be indirect without technically hedging, junior people eating the fines while senior people don't care about the money? And what happens to ordinary white lies—do they disappear, migrate, or get repriced? Could the scheme be tweaked to keep the candor without the status game, or does making politeness into a literal price just make everything worse?
