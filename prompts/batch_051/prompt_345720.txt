Two people in my policy seminar got into it after class about why some countries are rich, and neither would let it go, so I wrote down the argument and I want someone to referee it move by move. I wasn't in it myself and honestly don't lean either way.

(1) Mina opened with institutions. She said the best predictor of whether a country grows is whether it has secure property rights, contract enforcement, and a government constrained enough that it can't just expropriate whatever it wants. The cleanest evidence is natural experiments: Korea got split into two countries with the same geography and basically the same starting culture, and the South got rich while the North starved. Same with the two halves of the same city across the US–Mexico border. She said geography and culture can't explain divergence inside a shared geography, so institutions must be doing the work.

(2) Jonas said that's one or two cases, and institutions don't fall from the sky. South Korea's institutions came from a particular history — American occupation, an export-led developmental state, Cold War politics — and the border city's institutions came from which side of a political line it happened to sit on. So 'institutions' is the proximate cause, not the deep one. If institutions are themselves caused by geography, disease environment, or colonial history, you can't point to them as the explanation; you need to say why they emerged.

(3) Mina said there's a literature that handles that endogeneity. In the former colonies, settler mortality works as a natural experiment: where European settlers couldn't survive the disease environment, they set up extractive institutions; where they could settle in large numbers, they built more inclusive ones. That historical variation predicts present-day income, which suggests the arrow runs institutions → income, with geography only mattering through institutions.

(4) Jonas said that's exactly where the design is weakest. The instrument only identifies institutions if settler mortality effects income today only through institutions, and that exclusion restriction is hard to believe — disease environments affect health and productivity directly, and settlers brought legal systems, human capital, and cultural norms about trust and work. Plus even if the design were internally valid, it's a weird subpopulation of former colonies, so the conclusion doesn't automatically generalize to all nations.

(5) Mina said he was holding institutions to an impossible standard. You can't run a randomized trial where you assign countries good institutions, so an imperfect natural experiment is still better than the alternatives. And there's the reversal of fortune: the parts of the Americas that were richest before colonization are now relatively poor, while the backwaters became the US and Canada. That pattern is the opposite of what you'd expect if current wealth came straight from geography or some fixed local culture, and it fits extractive versus inclusive colonial institutions.

(6) Jonas said the reversal is consistent with a lot of stories besides institutions — what diseases happened to arrive, what the colonizers were trying to extract, changes in the value of trade routes, or just bad data about how rich precolonial places were. And he added that the institutional story gets slippery: some oil-rich Gulf states are wealthy with institutions that aren't exactly inclusive, and a country can get rich under autocracy if it protects property and markets; if 'good institutions' is allowed to stretch to fit every case, the claim stops being falsifiable. The deeper issue, he said, is that the whole question assumes there's one cause, when there is probably a lot of path dependence and interaction, and the literature may be selecting on outcomes.

That's where they left it.
