I am a software engineer at a company that builds and deploys large language models, and I have no formal background in philosophy. I would like you to act as a professional philosopher of cognitive science and artificial intelligence. Think carefully before committing to a verdict; begin by brainstorming the different ways one could understand the phrase "a system reporting its own reasoning," and then reconstruct the argument I describe below in numbered premises before you evaluate it. I am not asking for a literature review or for citations; I want a first-principles analysis of the described setup.

I have been thinking about the standard argument that chain-of-thought prompting gives us a transparent window into a model's reasoning. The argument, as I understand it, runs roughly as follows. An autoregressive model produces each token by conditioning on the input together with every token generated so far. When such a model writes an explicit step-by-step chain before answering, those steps are not a story composed after the fact: they are part of the causal process that leads to the answer. The model cannot be privately deciding something and then reporting it separately, because writing the chain just is part of how it reaches the answer. So the visible chain is, if not the model's complete computation, at least a faithful trace of the reasoning actually performed. I realize that even these models have hidden continuous states between tokens; the argument only needs the visible chain to be causally upstream of the answer, not to exhaust the computation.

Now suppose a later architecture changes that setup. A provider trains a solver to work through problems in continuous hidden states, with training signal applied only to the final answer, not to the intermediate trace. After the solver has fixed an answer, a separate rationalizer model takes the answer, plus at most a summary vector of the hidden state, and writes a clean natural-language chain of reasoning for display. This displayed chain is generated downstream of whatever caused the answer, and it never feeds back into it. Assume the provider's purpose is benign: the hidden-state process simply yields more reliable answers, and the rationalizer exists so that users get a readable explanation.

My first question is whether the standard transparency argument survives this development, needs repair, or is dead. Identify exactly which premise the new architecture attacks, and say whether the attacked premise was an accident of the old architecture or is essential to the argument's conclusion.

Now take the repair attempt. Suppose the provider tries to recover transparency by re-coupling display and computation: the solver must generate its written chain first, and the answer head may condition only on that written chain, with the hidden summary withheld. Would this rescue the claim that the system is reporting its reasoning? Or does causal efficacy still fall short of faithful reporting, since continuous hidden state could be doing work that never appears in the tokens? Be precise about what more, beyond being causally upstream, a chain would need to be before it deserves to be called a faithful report.

Finally, consider a version with no separate rationalizer: the solver itself, after producing an answer from hidden states, is prompted to explain how it got that answer. Is there a coherent sense in which this post-hoc chain could count as the system reporting its own reasoning even though it is not causally upstream of the answer? Say what would have to be true of the model's internal states and of the generated chain for that to be so, and flag where this stops being a conceptual question and becomes an empirical one.
