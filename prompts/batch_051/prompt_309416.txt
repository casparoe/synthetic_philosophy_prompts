I keep returning to an old story about a man in Damascus who learns that Death will meet him in the city the next day. He decides to flee to Aleppo, but then it occurs to him that if the prediction is perfect, Death will simply be waiting in Aleppo, and if he stays in Damascus, Death will be in Damascus. The unsettling part is not just that he seems doomed; it is that whichever plan he settles on, he can immediately see why he should switch to the other one, so he can never finish deciding. I have been trying to understand how much of this strange feature depends on the prediction being flawless.

Let me make the case concrete. Tomorrow a man must be in exactly one of two cities, Damascus or Aleppo. Before he acts, a messenger forms a forecast of which city the man will be in and sends Death to the forecast city; Death then stays there. The man never learns what the forecast said, but he knows the messenger's record: if the man ends up in either city, the forecast would have named that same city on four out of five such occasions, and the other city on the remaining one. The man values surviving in Damascus at 100, surviving in Aleppo at 50, and dying at 0.

I would like to see how the reasoning goes when the record is good but not perfect. Once the man has more or less settled on staying, he should think there is an 80 percent chance Death is in Damascus; on that basis, is staying or going the better plan, and by how much? Once he has instead settled on going, he should think there is an 80 percent chance Death is in Aleppo; then which plan is better, and by how much? And are there ranges of the messenger's reliability in which a single plan is best no matter which of these two attitudes the man takes, or in which both plans somehow confirm themselves? I would find it most useful if the numerical answers could be laid out so that I can see where the old flip-flopping collapses and where it reasserts itself.
