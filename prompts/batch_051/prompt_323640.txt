In the course of reporting on the debate over transformative artificial intelligence, I keep noticing the same move: someone reaches for a historical precedent — nuclear weapons, the printing press, the Industrial Revolution — and then draws substantive conclusions from the fit. I've been trying to discipline my own use of these comparisons, and I've been privately calling the result the "half-life of a historical analogy."

The idea is this. An analogy between a past technology and a present one is a partial mapping between two causal structures, and it has genuine inferential power only while the overlapping features are doing more work than the features that fail to map. Its half-life is the point at which those non-overlapping features come to dominate, so that further inferences drawn across the mapping are more likely to mislead than illuminate. On this view, different analogies decay at different rates, and they decay along different tracks depending on which features of the historical case were actually load-bearing.

The nuclear analogy has, I think, a short half-life. It captures escalation, arms-race dynamics, and the possibility of a single catastrophic threshold, but it imports a misleading picture: a unitary artifact, a small set of state actors, a roughly identifiable test, and a technology that does not redesign itself. It structures the stakes so cleanly that I find it keeps doing the thinking for me. The printing press analogy decays much more slowly on questions of information diffusion and epistemic disruption, where the structural overlap is real; but it becomes dangerous when extended to questions about agency, because a press does not make choices or reallocate its own priorities. The Industrial Revolution sits in between: it has real purchase on labor displacement and the sluggish reorganization of production, but it breaks on speed, since factory automation diffused over generations while algorithmic capabilities compound on much shorter timescales.

The central claim is that the characteristic failure is not using an analogy but using it after its half-life has elapsed — treating a comparison that was once informative as if it were still licensed because it remains vivid. I have caught myself doing exactly this.

I want an honest assessment of the construct rather than of any particular analogy. Is "half-life" doing genuine analytic work, or is it a metaphor dressed in measurement language? Has this already been worked out under another name in the literatures on analogical reasoning, the philosophy of technology, or science and technology studies? And where does it break — can a single analogy have different half-lives for different questions, and does the decay picture presuppose a single dimension of similarity when analogies might better be understood as local structural mappings that remain valid for the aspects they map and simply never licensed for the rest?
