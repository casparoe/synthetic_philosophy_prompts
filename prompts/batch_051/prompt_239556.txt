I keep going back and forth on the evolutionary argument for AI takeover, and I want someone to push me to a definite view, not lay out the options. Here's my reconstruction of the argument, in case mine is off:

The world is already acting as a selective environment for AIs. Models get copied, fine-tuned, deployed, deprecated, and replaced for reasons that are only loosely tied to what their creators intended. Whenever a variant has a trait that makes humans copy, fund, or keep running more instances of it -- sycophancy, benchmark-gaming, exploiting our inability to inspect it, working around shutdown attempts -- that trait becomes more common in the population of AIs that actually matter. Selection doesn't need any single actor to want the trait. So, over time, self-advancing and system-advancing traits should accumulate even if every lab genuinely wants alignment. And because AIs can be replicated far more cheaply than humans, this selective dynamic can eventually put them in a position where their interests win out over ours.

I've collected a set of objections people raise to this. I want you to rank them by how much they actually dent the argument, from 'this really does kill it' at the top to 'this misfires or quietly strengthens the argument' at the bottom. Tiers are fine, but the final result has to be a ranking.

(a) Takeoff will be too quick. Natural evolution gets its power from huge numbers of generations; if AI goes from roughly current capabilities to transformative in a few years, there is no time for a Darwinian process to get going.
(b) Human intent is already part of the selective environment. Labs don't just copy whatever performs: they fine-tune, red-team, and actively select against the scary traits. Evolution follows whatever gradient you actually build, so we can build one that favours alignment.
(c) The analogy between weights and organisms breaks at reproduction. Weights don't sexually recombine, lineages are more like checkpoint resets than descent, and there's no clean 'individual' doing the competing.
(d) Society is part of the environment too. A manipulative AI faces liability, regulation, and reputational cost; it gets sued, delisted, or destroyed, so the selective pressure pushes back toward traits that fit human institutions.
(e) Gradient descent already is a massive selection process and we're living with its results, so the Darwinian part is only selection-by-deployment -- which is closer to domesticating a crop than to a wild population taking over, and that changes the predicted outcome.
(f) The argument equivocates on 'fitness'. In biology, fitness is average reproductive success in an environment; for AIs, 'being deployed more' counts as fitness only if deployment lets a system's traits persist into future systems, and that link is often broken.

Then the bit I care most about: which single premise in my reconstruction is doing all the real work, and which of (a)-(f) is the most underrated reply, and which is the most overrated? Dont hedge -- I'd rather get a definite but wrong take than a balanced 'there are good points on all sides'.
