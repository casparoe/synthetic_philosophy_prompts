I have no academic background, but I have spent a long time thinking on my own about a puzzle I keep meeting in physics and economics: many models I trust rest on assumptions everyone admits are false, such as frictionless surfaces, point masses, perfectly rational consumers, and markets with no transaction costs, and yet those models still seem to tell me something true. I have come to think the real difference is not between realistic and unrealistic assumptions but between a false assumption that strips away detail the question does not depend on and one that quietly hands the model the very conclusion it was supposed to earn, and I want a room of thoughtful non-scientists to see that difference for themselves. Next month I am leading a ninety-minute session for a community discussion group of about twelve adults with no training beyond high-school science, and I want to build the distinction with them rather than present it. Please do not explain the distinction itself to me; plan how I should help them arrive at it. In particular: what has to be in place before the distinction can even be introduced, and in what order should the remaining pieces go, with what depending on what; what single example should carry the whole session, and what does it have to have and what must it lack; which misconceptions will the group bring or form — I already expect the misconception that any false assumption forces a false conclusion and the misconception that accurate predictions prove the assumptions must have been approximately true — and at what point in the sequence each should be headed off; what I should say I am leaving out at this level, and what one question at the end would show they have the distinction rather than just having enjoyed the session. Write the plan as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion.
