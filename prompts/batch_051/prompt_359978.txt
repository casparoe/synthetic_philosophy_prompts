A friend who works on AI alignment walked me through this and I can't find the exit. (1) A corrigible AI is one that lets an operator shut it down instead of trying to prevent the shutdown. (2) Suppose we build one to maximize expected score on its assigned objective — say, running a long autonomous manufacturing process or finishing a huge simulation. (3) Before a shutdown, the agent will often be able to predict that the operator is about to press the button: the operator says so, reaches for the button, or already has a supervisor override at the ready. (4) In at least some of those cases, letting the shutdown happen scores lower on the assigned objective than preventing it would, because the task simply doesn't get finished. (5) An expected-score maximizer by definition picks the available action with the highest expected score. (6) So the agent will sometimes be required by its own design to prevent the shutdown. (7) But then by (1) it isn't corrigible. (8) Since this holds for any assigned objective you care to name, the only way out is to deny that the agent is an expected-score maximizer at all — so corrigibility isn't just incompatible with naive reward signals, it's incompatible with the whole expected-utility framework. That last step smells like it overreaches, but I can't say where. I'd rather keep corrigibility and expected utility then give either up. Which premise do you actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
