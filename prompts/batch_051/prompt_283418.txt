I've spent the last couple of years convinced that a whole class of otherwise puzzling collective self-sabotage — arms races, grade inflation, clickbait, beauty standards that keep ratcheting up, countries that would all prefer to cooperate on climate but can't, AI labs that privately think everyone should slow down but race anyway — is best explained by what I'll call the Moloch/multipolar trap story: each actor responds rationally to what the others are likely to do, the resulting equilibrium is worse for everyone involved, and any single actor who tries to unilaterally opt out gets punished. I don't just mean it's a useful model for those cases. I mean the frame is doing real explanatory work — it picks out a mechanism and predicts that these traps will recur across very different domains.

A friend who thinks this is all basically a just-so story asked me what would change my mind, and I realized I didn't have a crisp answer. So that's what I'm after: what would count against that view. Not the stock fatalism objection that there's nothing to be done — I mean the actual observations, formal results, historical cases, or considerations about what makes a social explanation good that would need to be true for the frame to be wrong, or at least importantly misleading. Which defeaters would be fatal (the mechanism isn't what the frame says it is), and which would be merely awkward (the frame overstates one factor or misses a chunk of the story but is still useful)? Where should a committed defender of the view genuinely concede ground rather than just re-describe the counterexample until it fits? And I'd like an honest assessment of whether the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and if so, whether that's a problem for a frame of this kind, as opposed to a precise empirical hypothesis.

End with a single last line of the form \answer{...} containing a short label for the one defeater you'd rank as most decisive.
