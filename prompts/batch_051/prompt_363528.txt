For a novel I am writing, I need to understand the philosophical commitments behind a familiar piece of government practice: when agencies evaluate policies with long-run health and environmental effects, they discount future benefits at a fixed annual rate, often around three percent, so that a statistical life saved a century from now counts for only about five percent as much as one saved next year. I can see that this could be defended as a mere accounting convention for comparing investments, but it also seems to encode the idea that future people matter less simply because of when they live. What theory of time and well-being does the practice actually presuppose? Does it strictly require pure time preference, or is it equally compatible with a view that denies pure time preference and discounts only for opportunity costs or expected growth? If someone in the novel wanted to show that the practice is philosophically underdetermined, which points could she make, and what would a cost-benefit manual look like if it were rewritten by an author who rejected pure time preference but accepted opportunity-cost discounting? Please answer in American English.
