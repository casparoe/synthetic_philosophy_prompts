I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I am working on a piece about how fiction disciplines our intuitions about animal minds. For that piece I want a critical reading of Richard Adams's Watership Down, in either the novel or the film, through the lens of Morgan's canon: the principle, roughly, that one should not explain an animal's behavior by a higher psychological faculty when a lower one would suffice. I am not interested in the lazy verdict that Adams anthropomorphizes and is therefore unscientific. The more productive question is what the novel does to the canon, and what the canon does to the novel, when each is taken seriously.

Begin by asking where Adams's rabbit world seems to demand exactly the higher-level attributions the canon discourages. The rabbits are shown using something like a language, telling and reshaping stories, planning migrations, deceiving enemies, following leaders, and mourning their dead. Are these demands merely conventions of fiction, or do they track the kinds of behavioral evidence that a careful observer of real social animals might appeal to? Where could a deflationary reading account for the same behavior while positing only lower faculties, and where does that deflationary reading start to look strained, ad hoc, or less parsimonious than the richer attribution it replaces? Then consider the book's openly non-natural elements, such as Fiver's visions. Should a reader set those aside when thinking about the real dispute over animal cognition, or do they illuminate a deeper problem with the canon's assumption that faculties can be ranked as higher and lower in the first place? Finally, take one episode and rewrite it, as a thought experiment, under strict Morgan's-canon constraints. What has to be cut out of the description of motive, knowledge, and intention, and does the result read as more honest, as curiously blind to what social animals actually do, or as both at once?

I want the reading to be faithful in both directions, to Adams's story and to the science-minded debate about the attribution of cognition, and to end by saying plainly what the work of fiction contributes to the philosophical argument that a treatise alone would miss.
