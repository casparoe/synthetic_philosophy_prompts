The scale argument in insect welfare holds that even a low probability of insect sentience, multiplied by the on the order of 10^18 insects alive at any moment and the brevity and brutality of most insect lives, yields an expected amount of suffering so large that insects should dominate any impartial calculation over animal welfare. Decompose the claim into its testable and untestable components. How could one design a concrete research program to bring evidence to bear on each testable component: the abundance and turnover of insect populations, the probability that insects are sentient rather than merely nociceptive automata, and the plausible magnitude or welfare range of an insect's suffering? For each component, specify the observational or experimental design, the result that would strengthen the scale argument and the result that would weaken it, and say whether a decisive test is possible or only a partially informative one. Then identify what even ideal evidence would leave untouched: which open questions are conceptual choices about what sentience requires, which are moral choices about how to aggregate small probabilities across vast numbers, and which are empirical gaps that could in principle be closed. Conclude by assessing whether the scale argument is best understood as empirically vulnerable, morally fragile, or robust precisely because its load-bearing premise lies beyond the reach of any test.
