I've been trying to sort out what I actually think about taking other people's word on aesthetic matters, and my gut keeps pulling in different directions. Here are my snap judgments on a bunch of cases; I want to know if there's one principle behind them, or whether I'm just being inconsistent.

- My brother FaceTimes me from a restaurant absolutely raving about the cacio e pepe, says it's the best pasta he's ever had. A week later at work I catch myself saying "that place has the best pasta in town." That felt wrong — I should have said "my brother says it's the best pasta in town."
- Three film critics I trust all call a new movie a masterpiece. I haven't seen it yet. I'm perfectly comfortable telling a friend "critics are calling it a masterpiece," but I'd feel weird saying "it's a masterpiece" as if it were my own view, even though I trust those critics completely.
- Same three critics, same reviews, but now I use their verdicts to decide whether to buy a ticket. That feels totally fine. I'd even say I know it's probably a good use of two hours.
- The one that bothers me: if using their verdict to decide how to spend my evening is fine, why is using it as my own verdict not? My decision to buy the ticket is in some sense a bet that the movie is good, and I make it confidently. So on some level I do think the movie is good. But I still wouldn't assert it. That feels inconsistent and I don't know how to fix it.

- A friend whose musical taste I share walks me through why an album I found boring is actually brilliant — points out the tension in the chord changes, the way the structure mirrors the lyrics. I listen again and now I genuinely think it's brilliant. That change of mind feels legitimate, not secondhand.
- But the same friend, on the same day, tells me a band I've never heard is brilliant, and I won't call them brilliant until I've heard them myself — even though I trusted him enough to change my mind about the album. What's the difference? In both cases my eventual judgment is caused by his word.
- My piano teacher points out that a passage in a sonata has chords that are badly voiced. I've never heard the sonata. I have no problem saying "that passage is badly voiced" to a fellow student. This feels different from the masterpiece cases, but I can't say exactly why.
- An art historian I respect gives me a vivid, detailed description of a lost painting that survives only in written accounts. It sounds beautiful. I'm happy to say "it sounds beautiful" or "it must have been beautiful," but I'd be lying if I put "beautiful" down as my own judgment of the painting.
- A wine critic's tasting notes: I'll buy wine on them, serve it to guests, and say "this is a great bottle." That doesn't feel wrong the way the pasta case did. Maybe because wine talk is half about detectable properties like balance and finish? Or maybe I'm just snobbish about movies and painting and relaxed about wine.

Is there a coherent principle that captures all of these, or most of them? Something in the neighborhood of: you can't form aesthetic judgments from testimony alone. What exactly would that principle say — does it rule out belief, knowledge, assertion, or just the right to call something beautiful? And which of my verdicts does it force me to give up? Also, what would your principle say about a case I keep going back and forth on: I read a long-form essay about a novel I haven't read, and by the end the essay has convinced me the novel is great. Is that importantly different from the lost painting case? Please write the answer in American English.
