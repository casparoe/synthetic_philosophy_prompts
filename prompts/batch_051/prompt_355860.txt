I'm a novelist, working on a book about someone who grows up kept away from other people, and I keep circling back to Frankenstein because its the obvious ancestor. Lately I've been reading about ubuntu and the wider relational view of personhood — the thought that a person is made through other persons, that being someone happens between people rather than already sitting finished inside a private skull. I'd like you to read the creature in Frankenstein through that lens. He watches the De Laceys, teaches himself to speak and read, argues his own case, begs for a companion, and is still treated as a monster the moment he's seen. Does the novel actually back the relational idea, maybe by showing that being denied recognition is what unmakes him and turns him violent? Or does it partly resist it, since the creature already looks like a thinking, feeling, moral self before anyone recognizes him — which would make recognition more of a duty owed to an existing person than a condition of becoming one? I'm less interested in a clean verdict then in the places where the novel and the theory push against each other, and in what reading Frankenstein adds to the philosophical idea that a bare example never would. Dont worry about quoting the text; a reading from memory is fine. Please write it in plain continuous prose, no headings or bullet points or bold or tables, just the way you'd write a letter.
