You are a professional philosopher working in social ontology and moral psychology, and you are being asked for a consistency audit rather than a lecture. I am an undergraduate taking a course on the philosophy of social science, and we are reading Hume's essay "Of National Characters" (1748). I have settled into a set of views, prompted by the following passage, and I cannot tell whether the set is jointly tenable. Here is the passage:

"Different reasons are assigned for these national characters; while some account for them from moral, others from physical causes. By moral causes, I mean all circumstances, which are fitted to work on the mind as motives or reasons, and which render a peculiar set of manners habitual to us. Of this kind are, the nature of the government, the revolutions of public affairs, the plenty or penury in which the people live, the situation of the nation with regard to its neighbours, and such like circumstances. By physical causes I mean those qualities of the air and climate, which are supposed to work insensibly on the temper, by altering the tone and habit of the body, and giving a particular complexion, which, though reflection and reason may sometimes overcome it, will yet prevail among the generality of mankind, and have an influence on their manners."

Against that passage, my present commitments are:

1. I accept Hume's distinction, and I assign explanatory priority to moral causes: where national character is real, it is produced and maintained by institutions, laws, incentives, and social contagion rather than by climate or inherited temperament.
2. I think the comparative social-science evidence, such as cross-national differences in trust, corruption, and rule-following, tracks real aggregate differences; and I think it is rationally permissible to treat someone's nationality as a weak prior about their dispositions in low-stakes contexts.
3. I think most ordinary national-character statements, such as the claim that the English are reserved or that one's own people are hardworking, are scientifically empty and generally harmful; I would not defend any such characterization.
4. At the same time, I find myself explaining my own country's politics by saying that its public culture is unusually deferential to expert authority, and I regard this as genuinely explanatory rather than as a mere summary of opinion polls.
5. I hold that a national-character attribution is unfinished until one has identified the institutions or incentives that produce and maintain the trait; until then it is a placeholder rather than an explanation.
6. My underlying moral psychology is broadly Humean: character is formed through sympathy, habit, and local interaction, which makes me doubt that there is any deep national character over and above these local and institutional processes.

Please work through this carefully before rendering a verdict. First, explain what Hume's distinction does and does not establish about the source and stability of national traits. Then examine my six commitments pairwise: which are in genuine tension, which only appear to be in tension, and what exactly the difference consists in. For each genuine tension, rank it by severity and specify the least costly revision that would remove it, including which of my commitments would have to give. Finally, say whether there is a single revision that would reconcile the largest number of commitments at the lowest cost, and what I lose by making it.
