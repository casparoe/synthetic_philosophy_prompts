I am a professor of philosophy of science who has been asked by a regional heritage agency to serve as an external advisor to its field archaeology unit. The unit runs roughly a dozen small excavations each year, mostly ahead of construction, and in nearly every season a site director must decide whether an assemblage of objects—a pit, a floor scatter, a deposit—licenses any claim about past beliefs or institutions, or whether the safest reading is merely about everyday practical life. The stakes are concrete: the interpretation enters a statutory site report, can trigger a protective designation, and may be challenged by a developer's consulting firm. The staff are trained excavators, not philosophers or social theorists, and under the terms of their permit they have about half a day to settle an interpretive question before the report is submitted.

I would like you to design a decision procedure the unit can reuse: a sequence of questions to ask about the finds and their context, in order, with explicit criteria for what counts as a sufficient answer at each step. It should say where the procedure returns a definite no, where it authorizes an inference to belief or institution, and where it must stop and hand the question to a human being because the remaining issue is theoretical rather than observational. The procedure should not presuppose that any category—ritual, symbolic, religious, economic, domestic—is sharp. It should be candid about the fact that distinct practices can leave materially indistinguishable traces, and about the standing temptation to classify anything poorly understood as ritual. It should also say which distinctions must survive the simplification and which can be dropped for a technician in the field.

Then run the procedure on three cases from a hypothetical excavation: (a) a shallow pit containing several deliberately snapped but unused ceramic bowls over a layer of ash; (b) a floor scatter of small clay figurines mixed with ordinary cooking debris and a worn quern; and (c) a near-sterile room with a row of complete, unused grinding stones aligned along one wall. At least one of these should be a case where the procedure returns an answer the site director will not like; say which one and why the procedure is nonetheless right. End by stating clearly what the procedure cannot do—which interpretive leaps no checklist can make—and what the unit should do in those cases.
