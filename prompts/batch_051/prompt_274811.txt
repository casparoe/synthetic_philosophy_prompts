My partner and I recently had to decide whether to open a genetic report containing an incidental finding about our child's risk of a late-onset condition. The finding would not change any decision available to us: the condition cannot be prevented by anything we would not already do, and screening schedules would be the same either way. I found myself willing to pay real money not to know, and the urge did not subside when I told myself that information is usually an advantage. This unsettles me because I am committed to expected-utility reasoning, and Good's theorem states that for a probabilistically coherent expected-utility maximizer, the expected value of cost-free information is never negative: once you learn the fact, you can always implement the plan you would have chosen without it, and some findings let you choose better. By that result my preference for ignorance looks like a bias in favor of a comforting belief rather than a preference over outcomes.

Still, my intuition remains firm. People decline to learn risk scores for unpreventable diseases, or the date of their death, not from stupidity but from a considered sense that some states of ignorance are worth keeping. I cannot shake that judgment, and I do not believe it is merely confusion.

Set out the strongest version of each side for me. For the intuition, argue that the theorem's conditions fail in exactly the cases that matter: utility may attach to beliefs themselves (hope, dread, the ability to form projects while uncertainty remains open); a person cannot bind their later self to ignore the news; and perhaps the agent whose welfare matters before disclosure and the agent after it are not the same in the respects that count. For the theorem, argue that these complications can either be priced into a suitably revised utility function without giving up the result, or that they reduce to incoherence: dynamic inconsistency, double-counting emotional states, or treating a choice node as if it were not part of a single plan. Then give your negotiated verdict: should the intuition be debunked and dropped, should the theorem's scope be restricted, or is there a reading on which the two are compatible once the terms 'cost-free', 'expected', and 'utility' are made exact? Do not just pick a side; say what would have to be true for the side you reject to be the right one. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of the question.
