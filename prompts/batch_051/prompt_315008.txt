before I read anything else on legal fictions, I want the spec of what a theory of them has to actualy get right — not a survey, the requirements list. The purpose I care about is deciding whether these devices do real work in law or are just rhetorical garnish, so every requirement has to earn its keep against that question.

Start from the messy material: a court treating a corporation as a person that can intend, know, or be negligent; constructive possession and constructive notice, where someone is treated as having what they physically don't; statutes that deem a person dead after seven years unexplained absence; the old common law fictions in ejectment with John Doe and Richard Roe; the doctrine of relation back, where a later event takes effect from an earlier date. Some of these may turn out not to be fictions at all — the theory should draw that line with a criterion, not a vibe.

For each requirement give the case behind it and say whether it's non-negotiable or just one camp's preference. Keep the weight on the requirements and the distinctions they force — fiction vs. presumption, fiction vs. deeming, fiction vs. metaphor, maybe fiction vs. lie — rather than on scoring particular authors. I've also heard people say some fictions are scaffolding you eventually remove once the rule can stand on its own, and others are permanent plumbing, and I want to know whether that distinction is something a theory has to earn or a red herring.

And the part I most want to see: are any two requirements in tension — like, a theory that says judges know the assertions are false and aren't fooled, and also says the fiction changes what the law requires — and if so, is the right conclusion that the list itself shows something about legal fictions? If there's a pair that can't be jointly satisfied, don't paper it over.

No scorecard of particular theories at the end; I'll do that myself in class.
