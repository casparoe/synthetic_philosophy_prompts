I've been keeping track of how my jurisprudence tutor rules on desuetude cases all year, and I want to work out what she'll do with next week's case before she does it.

Her verdicts so far:

- A Sunday trading ban last enforced in the 1930s, ignored by nearly every shop in town: she said it had lapsed through desuetude and couldn't be revived now.
- An old common scold offence, unused for nearly a century, trotted out against someone who had a screaming row with a neighbour: unenforceable, she said, a dead letter.
- An adultery statute that hasn't been prosecuted for sixty-odd years: she held it had fallen into desuetude.
- A by-law requiring hawkers and pedlars to buy a licence, unenforced for decades with market stalls trading openly the whole time: she said non-enforcement plus open reliance had killed it.
- A bird protection regulation that wardens had simply stopped enforcing for thirty years but that still tracked a live conservation concern: she refused desuetude outright, on the ground that official neglect doesn't repeal protective legislation that still has a point.
- A loitering provision the police had mostly dropped but still used a handful of times a year to move people on: she said it wasn't a dead letter while it was still doing occasional work.
- A theft statute that prosecutors now rarely use for low-value shoplifting: she said desuetude has no application to ordinary, serious criminal law, and its purposes plainly haven't lapsed.

Next week's case is a 1910 act making it an offence to tell fortunes for money. It hasn't been enforced since about the 1950s, astrologers and tarot readers operate openly, advertise, pay tax, all of it. A local authority has now charged an online tarot-card reader under it. Her own teaching notes say the original rationale was consumer protection, stopping frauds pretending to contact the dead, and the general fraud statutes could cover the worst of that nowadays. But she also says the rationale that vulnerable people shouldn't be exploited isn't obviously dead, and reasonable legislators might well vote to keep a licensing scheme rather than repeal it outright.

I want to predict her verdict. Specifically: if she wants to be consistent with her earlier rulings, should she find the fortune-telling law enforceable or not? Which of her earlier cases is the closest analog? What rule is she actually running, and what other rule fits the pattern just as well? And what further case would most cleanly tell those rules apart? If her earlier rulings genuinely underdetermine it, tell me what principle she'd be adopting with each choice.

Also, please seperate what's actually settled among people who work on desuetude from what's genuinely contested, and don't make a contested view sound like the consensus.
