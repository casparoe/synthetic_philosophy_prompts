I'm on an acquisition ethics board at a defense ministry, and we have to decide whether to recommend a new loitering munition for deployment. The thing circles an area, finds targets that match a pre-programmed profile (say, enemy air-defense vehicles), and will fire unless a human operator vetoes it during a short window. A single operator monitors several munitions at once and mostly watches; there's no requirement that the operator identify or approve the specific target in advance. Half the board thinks this plainly violates meaningful human control: the human is just a conscience-stricken stop button, with no real understanding of why this vehicle and not that one got selected. The other half says this is what meaningful human control looks like in practice once you accept supervised autonomy: the commander sets the mission and the rules of engagement in advance, the system stays inside a bounded area and a bounded category of targets, and the veto is genuine.

Draft us a decision memo (600–900 words, American English) recommending approve, reject, or approve with conditions. Give a workable definition of meaningful human control, apply it to exactly this case without hand-waving, and address the strongest objection to whichever recommendation you make — not a strawman.
