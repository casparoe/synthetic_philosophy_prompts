I am auditing a seminar on the moral foundations of rights, and the discussion keeps returning to a claim that the difference between treating rights as trumps, as side constraints, or as instruments is a difference in the form of our reasons, not merely in how much weight rights receive. I find myself unable to make that distinction stick.

On one account, rights are trumps: they do not enter an ordinary calculus of social welfare, but exclude certain kinds of collectively appealing justifications from consideration. On another, rights are side constraints on conduct: we may pursue our goals only without crossing the boundaries rights set, and we may not cross them even to prevent more crossings of the same boundary by others. On the third, rights are instruments—valuable because of the interests or welfare they protect, and revisable when they no longer serve that end.

My confusion is whether the first two positions are really just disguised versions of the third with different weights. If a side constraint is not absolute, then when an emergency or a competing right forces us to ask whether the constraint gives way, are we not simply balancing the right against whatever lies on the other side, as the instrumental view recommends? If it is absolute, the view seems arbitrary in the face of unavoidable conflicts and cases like self-defense. But the puzzle that bothers me most is this: why can't a defender of side constraints say that a right is just an agent-neutral goal assigned enormous—perhaps infinite—weight, something like the goal of minimizing rights violations? That would reduce constraints to instruments while preserving their practical conclusions. What exactly would be lost in that translation? I am trying to determine whether the contrast among trumps, side constraints, and instruments is a genuine structural difference in how reasons operate, or whether it collapses once one asks how an actual agent should reason through rights conflicts and very bad consequences.
