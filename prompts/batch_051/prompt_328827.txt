Please write a dialogue between two engineers arguing about a careenium, a thought experiment of the sort Daniel Dennett has described, which we can specify like this. A shallow, nearly frictionless dish, sealed under a transparent dome, contains a few thousand identical metal microspheres — the simmballs — which glide and collide according to simple, fully deterministic rules; each trajectory is settled entirely by the initial positions and momenta, the collisions, and the geometry of the bowl. Seen from above, the aggregate shows transient large-scale regularities: a cluster that slowly circulates, a bulge that leans toward one side, a jittering front that advances and retreats. The two engineers write a coarse-grained program that tracks these regularities and labels some of them as symbols — a tight triangular cluster functioning as an exit flag, a ring-shaped flow encoding a count, and so on. The symbols are not a separate material: they just are arrangements of simmballs, picked out by their functional roles in the aggregate.

The disagreement is this: does the symbolic level — the labeled patterns and the coarse regularities — genuinely push the substrate around, or is all the pushing done by the simmballs, with the symbolic level riding on top as a compressed description?

The first engineer, the pattern-driver, should defend these commitments intelligently: the microphysics is respected, and yet the symbolic level does real causal work, because the same pattern is realizable by indefinitely many micro-arrangements; robust regularities and counterfactuals live at the level of the symbols; interventions phrased at the symbolic level — remove the exit flag, reverse the count — are the only feasible way to manipulate the system; and a complete catalogue of collisions, while physically exhaustive, is not the causal explanation actually needed for control and prediction.

The second engineer, the substrate-only engineer, should defend the contrary commitments with equal intelligence: every swerve of every simmball is fully settled by local mechanical causes, so the symbolic level adds no further push; the symbols are real enough as descriptions, but their apparent efficacy runs entirely through the simmballs that realize them; the appearance of top-down control dissolves once boundary conditions are written out; and an intervention such as removing the exit flag is itself a physical disturbance of the dish, not evidence of downward causation.

The dialogue should contain genuine back-and-forth: each character should reply to the other's actual arguments, each should be able to score points and make concessions, and neither should be a strawman. At some point, have one character propose a concrete intervention — tilting the dish, magnetizing a subset of the simmballs, adding a ridge, or altering the symbol-reading program — and have the other explain why that experiment would or would not decide the matter. End with the two characters stating, as precisely as they can, what observation would change their minds, even if they continue to disagree.
