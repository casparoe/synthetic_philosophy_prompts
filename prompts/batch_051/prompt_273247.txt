A friend and I spent most of an evening arguing about whether Bayesian convergence results really show that priors wash out, and his argument now seems to prove too much, though I cannot locate the step at which it goes wrong. I have written it out below as he gave it, and I would like a diagnosis.

(1) Take two Bayesian agents who update by conditionalization on a shared, indefinitely growing stream of observations, and suppose their priors are non-dogmatic toward one another in the relevant sense: each agent's predictive distribution over the sequence of observations is absolutely continuous with respect to the other's, so neither agent is antecedently certain that the other will be refuted.

(2) A standard theorem about merging of forecasts says that, under this condition, the two agents' predictive distributions over observations still to come converge to one another: the total variation distance between their forecasts tends to zero, almost surely under either agent's probability.

(3) If two agents issue the same forecasts for every possible continuation of the data — or issue forecasts that come arbitrarily close to the same forecasts in the limit — then any disagreement that remains between them concerns nothing the data could settle. A Bayesian who treats predictive dispositions as the measure of belief should either deny that this residual difference is a difference in belief, or treat it as practically negligible.

(4) Therefore the data wash out the priors. After a sufficiently long shared history, two non-dogmatic Bayesians must agree, not merely about future observations, but about the hypotheses themselves; the prior each began with can no longer be doing any work in the conclusions each reaches.

(5) But this proves too much. It implies that no persistent disagreement in a mature empirical field could be traced back to differing priors, since any long common record of evidence should already have erased them. It also implies that a non-dogmatic but outlandish prior — one that gives a conspiracy theory a sliver of probability rather than zero — needs no criticism in advance, because the data are guaranteed to correct it eventually.

(6) So something must give: either one of the premises is false as stated, or the conclusion drawn in (5) does not follow. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, what exactly does the convergence result establish, and does it license the slogan that priors wash out?
