I spent my career in geochemistry, so when I came to philosophy late I kept my old habits: I want to know what a disagreement is downstream of before I care who wins. The one I can't figure out is the argument about whether delusions are beliefs, and I can't even tell which kind of disagreement it actually is, or if its a real disagreement at all. The two-factor account of delusions seems to be accepted by nearly everyone now: in something like Capgras, one factor is the strange experience (the face no longer produces the usual autonomic response, so the person genuinely feels that the woman is a stranger) and the second factor is a failure in belief evaluation that lets the candidate thought get accepted instead of rejected. That machinery explains how the delusion forms. But then the question is whether the end product is a belief, and there the people who agree on the machinery just line up on two sides. The doxastic side says the person asserts the delusion sincerely, draws inferences from it, sometimes acts on it, so by ordinary functionalist tests it is a belief. The other side says no: delusions are often compartmentalized, the person shows double bookkeeping (says the wife is an impostor but goes on eating her cooking and sleeping in the same bed), they resist evidence in ways ordinary beliefs usually do not, and they sometimes look more like imaginings or verbal postures than beliefs. Both camps accept the two-factor story, both cite the same handful of cases, and the argument has been going in circles for a long time. It reminds me of two labs that agree on every measurement but cannot agree on what the measured quantity is.

Diagnose the debate for me. Is it a substantive disagreement about what beliefs are, or is it largely verbal, a disagreement about where to draw the line around the word belief? Are the two sides answering different questions, one about clinical reality and one about an idealized philosophical construct? Is there a hidden empirical question doing the work, such as how much action guidance delusions actually exhibit, how robust compartmentalization or double bookkeeping really is, or what the affective response measurements actually show? Or is it bedrock intuitions about what should count as believing, which neither side can argue for? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress: a distinction, a kind of study, a different question, or should we expect none? My own suspicion is that the word belief is doing too much work on one side and too little on the other, but I want you to test that rather than just agree with me. I dont need a survey of the literature; I want your diagnosis of the structure of the disagreement, with reasons, and an honest answer about whether anything could move it.
