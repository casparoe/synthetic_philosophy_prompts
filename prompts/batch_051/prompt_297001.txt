I'm a retired physical chemist who's recently gotten into philosophy of social science, and I run a monthly science discussion group at our local library. We usually get 12-16 adults, mix of retired folks and a few undergrads, and I have 90 minutes. I want to run a session on information cascades and rational herding — the idea that a whole room of reasonable people can end up doing the same thing even when most of their private evidence pointed the other way. I want them to feel the pull themselves, so no lecture up front, and please dont have me name the concept until after they've lived through it.

I've read about the standard two-urns set-up: two boxes, Box A has two red balls and one blue, Box B has one red and two blue. The facilitator picks one in secret. In a fixed order, each person draws a ball, sees only their own colour, puts it back, and announces which box they think was chosen. You hear everyone's announcement but not their private colour. The creepy part is that after the first two people announce the same box, the next person can rationally ignore a private ball of the other colour and join the herd — and from then on the announcements stop carrying private information.

Design me a runnable 90-minute version of this for my group. A few things I specifically need:

- Exact materials and rules, with individual, low-stakes scoring (chocolate coins, raffle tickets, poker chips — not real money) so each person cares about their own guess being right and isn't tempted to cooperate with the group.
- A fixed order of play and a strict rule that you may only announce 'Box A' or 'Box B', never the colour you drew — plus practical advice on how to enforce that with chatty adults who cant help blurting things out.
- A timeline that fits 90 minutes, probably two or three rounds, including a later round where one small piece of public information arrives mid-cascade (say the facilitator draws one ball from the hidden box and shows it to everyone) so the group can feel how fragile these things are — a locked-in herd can wobble or break on a single extra signal.
- A debrief that reconstructs what happened: who ignored their own ball, why that isn't obviously irrational, what the public announcements did and didn't reveal, and where the Bayesian arithmetic says a rational person should switch from 'trust my own ball' to 'follow the room' given the 2-to-1 signal strength.
