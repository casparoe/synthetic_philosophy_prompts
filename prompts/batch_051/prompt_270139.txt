Design a runnable classroom activity that stages, rather than describes, a central worry about making argumentation into a competitive sport. The participants are 16 students in an upper-level undergraduate seminar on argumentation and social epistemology; the session lasts 75 minutes. By the end, they should have felt in their own choices — not been told by the facilitator — that the choice of a scoring rule is itself a substantive answer to the question of what good argument consists in, and that the natural rule of rewarding immediate persuasion can systematically favor behavior the participants themselves, under debrief, disown as reasoning.

The activity should make them run into this problem firsthand. Have participants argue substantially similar claims under each of two scoring regimes: one in which points follow the audience's or judges' immediate conviction, and one in which points track accuracy or calibration that can be checked only after the round. The rules, incentives, and roles should be arranged so that, under the first regime, participants find themselves withholding awkward counter-considerations, overstating confidence, or selecting arguments for their rhetorical yield, and so that the shift to the second regime produces a change they notice in their own behavior rather than in a post hoc explanation.

Provide everything needed to run the activity: the roles and formal rules with concrete point scales or payoffs; the claim sets or motion list, chosen so the class can argue from shared background knowledge without outside research; the judge or audience instructions; a minute-by-minute timeline; and a debrief that maps specific moments in the play onto both the component ideas — that a scoring rule is a choice point about what good argument requires, and that persuasion-based and accuracy-based rules select for different cognitive behavior — and the broader question of whether argumentation could be a sport and whether that would improve or corrupt reasoning. Include an honest prediction of what is most likely to fail when the activity is run and how the facilitator should handle it, and a closing note on which feature of that broader question the activity cannot reproduce and must be supplied by discussion instead.
