I run the technical determinations unit at a state coastal-management agency. The unit's whole job is to deliberate, in one memo, a version of "should this go forward?": a marina, a dredging plan, a desalination plant, a homeowner's variance. New cases drop weekly. The stakes are a court challenge if we get it wrong either way — and, occasionally, a wetland that doesn't recover or a project that dies after two years of planning. The director signs off and hates being surprised by an issue the memo didn't surface.

Our current workflow is already factored cognition without calling it that: a lead (usually me) splits each case into subquestions — fish impacts to the biologist, water quality to the chemist, legal authority to counsel, local need to the planner — and then stitches the sections into one memo. I've been reading the alignment people's descriptions of HCH (humans consulting HCH): a human answers a hard question by asking subquestions, those subquestions get answered by humans who can recurse, and the idealization is that this preserves the competence of a single expert who held the whole problem in one head. Its an appealing picture. I want to know whether it survives contact with an understaffed agency, and what procedure would get us the most of it.

So: turn factored cognition as an idealization of deliberation into a procedure our unit can actually run. I'd like a decision procedure/checklist for the whole lifecycle: at intake, whether to decompose the case at all and along what lines; how to phrase subquestions so they don't silently settle the overall question; when the procedure should say "stop — one lead has to hold this entire problem, no splitting"; and what the synthesizer has to do beyond concatenating sections. Include the hard cases and the points where the procedure has to hand the decision to a person because judgment can't be eliminated. Then run the procedure on three of my cases: (1) a desalination permit, (2) a homeowner's variance where the real fight is whether economic hardship outweighs harm to a protected species, and (3) one case of your choosing that shows where the procedure breaks or gives an answer I won't like. Be explicit about what the procedure cannot fix, not just what it can.

One more request: throughout, distinguish the parts of this that are genuinely settled from the parts people who work on it still contest, and don't present a contested view as the consensus. I'd rather see honest uncertainty than false confidence.
