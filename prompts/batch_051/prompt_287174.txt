Question: In the two-envelope problem, someone puts $m and $2m in two indistinguishable envelopes, with m drawn from some prior (you may take it to be discrete), you pick one at random, and you may open it and see the amount before deciding whether to switch. A standard argument says: if your envelope contains $x, the other contains either $2x or $x/2, each with probability 1/2, so switching has expected value (1/2)(2x) + (1/2)(x/2) = 5x/4, and you should switch. Explain what, if anything, is wrong with this argument, and what a correct analysis says about whether you should switch after seeing the amount.

Student answer: The argument goes wrong in its equal-probability step. Conditional on seeing x, the other envelope has 2x just in case you initially picked the smaller amount, and x/2 just in case you initially picked the larger amount. The naive argument treats these two cases as equally likely for every x, but that amounts to an improper uniform prior over the possible smaller amounts, so the 1/2 probabilities are unjustified. With a proper prior over m, the posterior probability that the other envelope has 2x is P(m=x)/(P(m=x)+P(m=x/2)), which is not generally 1/2, so the 5x/4 expected-value calculation is invalid as general reasoning. However, the two envelopes are symmetric before you open yours, so the unconditional expected values are equal: E(switch)=E(stay). The expected gain from switching is therefore zero. It follows that a correct analysis says seeing the amount should not make you prefer switching: no matter what you see, you should be indifferent, and the paradox is dissolved.
