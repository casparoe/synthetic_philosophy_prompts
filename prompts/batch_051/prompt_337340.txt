In a reading group on the question whether psychopaths know that what they do is wrong, a colleague has been leaning heavily on one analogy. He describes the psychopath as morally colorblind. A colorblind person can learn that the light is “red,” can sort swatches by the labels she has been taught, and can pass as a sighted person in most settings; but she lacks the normal experience of red, and that experience is what normally anchors color judgment. Likewise, the psychopath can learn that an act falls under the label “wrong,” can produce the relevant rule when asked, and can pass as a morally competent speaker; but he lacks the experience of wrongness itself. So his moral “knowledge” is really labeling without perception. From this my colleague concludes that the psychopath’s deficit is cognitive rather than merely motivational, and that the criminal law should treat him as less than fully responsible—more like someone with a disability than someone who refuses to care.

I want this analogy audited rather than praised or dismissed. Please structure your response as follows:

1. The mapping (roughly 150 words). An explicit correspondence table: what corresponds to the colorblind person, the redness, the traffic light or color swatches, the sighted observer, and the disability itself.

2. Where it holds (roughly 150 words). The respects in which the two cases genuinely track: what both figures can and cannot do with the relevant labels.

3. Where it breaks (roughly 300 words). The points at which moral wrongness does not behave like a perceptible color property, or at which the psychopath’s capacities do not track the colorblind person’s. Distinguish the disanalogies that matter for the responsibility conclusion from those that merely refine the picture.

4. What the analogy imports (roughly 250 words). What picture of moral knowledge it quietly relies on—for instance, that wrongness is something one perceives rather than something one grasps as a proposition and is answerable to—and whether the conclusion depends on that picture.

5. Verdict on the conclusion (roughly 200 words). Does the diminished-responsibility conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and in what weakened or restated form? If the analogy was doing the real work, say so plainly.

If you think the analogy as stated is too slippery to audit, or that it systematically begs the question, do not comply politely: say so and redo the task on better terms. For each step where you claim a disanalogy matters, indicate how confident you are and what consideration would change your mind. If there is a better analogy or a cleaner distinction that does the work, end with it.
