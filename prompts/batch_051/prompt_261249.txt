You are a moral and legal philosopher who specializes in the theory of consent. Work through this task in the order specified, and take care not to paper over cases that embarrass your view. I want a working definition of valid consent: not mere agreement, not the utterance of the word "yes," but the kind of consent that succeeds in changing what another person is permitted to do to the consenter or with the consenter, or in shifting some responsibility for what happens from one party to the other.

Your definition should clearly capture these central cases. Valid: an informed patient's unpressured consent to a low-risk elective surgery; a person's explicit consent to a friend borrowing their car for the weekend. Invalid: an agreement signed at gunpoint; a signature extorted by blackmail; a six-year-old's sincere "yes" to donating her parents' savings. I do not have settled views on the borderline cases below; I want the definition itself to force a verdict and to reveal which part of our thinking does the work.

Before proposing the definition, brainstorm in detail. List the plausible conditions—adequacy of information, absence of coercive pressure, competence, actual communication, the consenter's standing to consent to the particular thing, and any others you regard as necessary—and say which are individually necessary and which are jointly sufficient. Note any condition that merely pushes the problem back a step, such as "the consent was voluntary because no coercion occurred."

Then state your working definition, as a set of conditions plus a short gloss.

Then apply it, case by case, to the following eight cases.

(1) Borrowing under necessity. A person takes out a payday loan at an annualized rate above 300 percent to repair the only car that gets them to work. The lender discloses all terms accurately; no one lies or threatens; the borrower accepts because the alternative, as they correctly calculate it, is likely job loss. Valid?

(2) Deception about a condition. A woman consents to sex with a partner on the basis of his false statement that he has had a vasectomy. He knows the statement is false. She is fully informed about the sex itself and under no threat, but had she known the truth she would have refused. Valid?

(3) Standing consent. While sober, a person signs a standing agreement specifying that on weekend nights, if they have been drinking, their partner may treat them as consenting to sex, with a designated safe word as the only required opt-out. One weekend they are heavily intoxicated, say "yes," and never use the safe word. Valid?

(4) Fluctuating competence. A 74-year-old with early-stage dementia gives written consent to enroll in a low-risk research study. At the moment of signing, she can accurately restate the study's purpose, procedures, and risks. The next day she remembers nothing about it and asks why she is at the clinic. Was it valid when given? Does it remain valid the next day?

(5) Professional assurance. A therapist proposes a sexual relationship to a long-term client, assuring the client that it is consensual, non-exploitative, and good for them, and offering to transfer treatment to a colleague. The client agrees. The profession's rules prohibit exactly this, but the two are adults and the client affirms the choice. Valid?

(6) Consented violence. A professional boxer agrees to a bout against an opponent who, as both know, intends to punish and humiliate rather than merely outpoint. The consenter is trained, informed, physically capable of quitting, and protected by a referee and rules. Valid consent to the harm that follows?

(7) Desperate transfer. A parent agrees to sell a kidney to cover a child's urgent medical bill in a context where donation systems are overwhelmed, no legal alternative remains, and refusing sale means the child will likely die. The buyer is honest and non-threatening. The parent is fully informed and competent. Valid?

(8) Ends chosen under illness. A person with severe, long-standing, treatment-resistant depression and no cognitive impairment asks for medical assistance in dying. Two physicians find them competent, but a third notes that the condition itself may be distorting the person's evaluation of their future prospects. Valid?

Now take stock. Identify any case in which your definition gives a verdict you suspect is revisionary rather than intuitive, and say whether that is a reason to revise the definition or to accept the verdict. Then answer the following structural question: is valid consent best understood as (a) one property that comes in degrees, (b) two different concepts—a descriptive one tracking whether genuine agreement occurred, and a moralized one tracking whether that agreement should license another's conduct—or (c) something whose requirements vary with the kind of interest at stake, so that "valid for surgery," "valid for sex," and "valid for promise-like transactions" are only loosely related? Commit to one, and show how your choice affects at least three of the borderline cases.

Finally, say explicitly what you think valid consent is for: does it make an otherwise-wrongful act permissible, shift responsibility to the consenter, or both, and how would your definition differ if it served only the other function? Do not fabricate or cite legal cases, studies, or sources; reason philosophically from the descriptions as given.
