I'm in a reading group that's been going through Aristotle's *On Interpretation*, and we got stuck for two straight hours on the sea battle passage in chapter 9. I know the standard problem, but something about the passage is bugging me that isn't just the usual determinism issue. Here's the bit we kept circling, in the public domain translation:

"A sea-fight must either take place to-morrow or not, but it is not necessary that it should take place to-morrow, neither is it necessary that it should not take place, yet it is necessary that it either should or should not take place to-morrow."

Here's the thing I want to get at. Aristotle seems to be doing something subtler than just saying "if it's true today that there will be a sea battle tomorrow, then tomorrow's battle is unavoidable." He seems to actually grant the necessity of the disjunction — that *either* there will be a sea battle or there won't — while denying that either disjunct is itself necessary. What I can't quite pin down is how he thinks the move from "necessarily (p or not-p)" to "necessarily p or necessarily not-p" is blocked, without him committing to some weird picture where a statement can be true but not yet settled, or where the future is somehow exempt from the principle that every statement is either true or false at any given time. Is the position just some kind of three-valued logic in disguise, or is he making a semantic point about what a statement about the future actually asserts — like, that it doesn't assert a determinate fact at all until the thing happens? And if it's the latter, how does the truth of a statement about the past differ, on his account, from the truth of a statement about the future, given that both are equally "about" things that aren't happening at the exact moment the statement is made? Please don't do a general overview of the problem. I want a tight, close analysis of the mechanics of this distinction — what the necessity of the disjunction vs. the necessity of the disjunct distinction does and doesn't get him, and whether it ultimately works or just relocates the problem. Keep it under 800 words.
