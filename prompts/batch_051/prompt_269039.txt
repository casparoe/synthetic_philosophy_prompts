Last night I had a long argument with a friend. She kept insisting that any standard of evidence I cite can itself be questioned, so that no dispute is ever rationally settled; I kept answering that she was confusing a rule with the particular claims judged under it. By the end of the evening I could not state sharply what that distinction amounts to, and I realized that the thing we had been circling was the problem of the criterion. By coincidence, I am to teach exactly that problem on Tuesday: a fifty-minute section for first-year epistemology students who have just read Descartes on the evil demon and have concluded that its lesson is simply that the senses cannot be trusted. Do not explain the problem of the criterion to me; give me a plan for explaining it to them that does its real work in the order of ideas and the map of misunderstandings. Specifically, tell me: what distinction has to be fixed first if the students are not to receive the problem as the ordinary regress of reasons under a new name, and in what order the remaining pieces should come and why; what one example should carry the hour, and what it must have and must lack — I had been planning to use one ruler checked against another ruler, but I suspect that invites the reply that you simply consult the official standard and misleads them into seeing the problem as an indefinitely long chain of measuring instruments; which misconceptions I should expect and exactly where in the sequence to head each one off — I already foresee them treating it as the evil demon over again, insisting that a candidate criterion can be tested straightforwardly against clear cases they know independently, and concluding that any position short of a non-circular foundation is simply a concession to the skeptic; what I should openly decline to cover at this level and why, bearing in mind that later in the course they will meet Hume's naturalism and I do not want to have to retract anything when they do; and one final question I can pose that would show me whether a student has grasped the criterion problem as distinct from both the evil-demon problem and the regress of justification.
