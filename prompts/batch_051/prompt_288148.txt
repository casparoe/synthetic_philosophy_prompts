Okay so I've been stuck on an idea I've been calling "emergency sediment," and I want someone to tell me if it's a real thing. It got personal for me during covid: my sister was on the wrong side of a border closure for three months, and I kept noticing how "temporary" measures calcified. Here's the concept. Every time a government takes an emergency measure, it deposits a layer of sediment in the legal and institutional landscape. When the emergency formally ends, the rule may get lifted, but a residue stays behind: expectations have shifted, some agency has built capacity around it, some exemption is now a precedent, some ID check or surveillance practice has become unremarkable. My real claim is that you can predict how much of a temporary measure sticks around not by how severe the emergency was, but by who acquired an interest in keeping it alive while it was in force—contractors, agencies, police, even ordinary people who got used to shorter lines or not having to show up somewhere in person. So the main wrong of some emergency powers isn't what they do during the emergency, it's the sediment they deposit. I know this overlaps with the ratchet effect and with normalization, but I think what I'm after is more specific: it's about the mechanics and timing of the residue, and about legitimate-looking permanence being built inside a legal exception. I want an honest assessment, not encouragement. Is this coherent or just a suggestive metaphor? What would someone have to show to turn it into a real claim rather than a vibe? And where does it break—like, some emergency measures clearly should outlast the emergency (disease surveillance, stricter building codes after an earthquake), so does my concept just tar those by association? Has anyone already worked this out under a name I don't know?
