I'm a backend engineer and a conversation about pair programming accidentally turned into a philosophy rabbit hole. The question I got stuck on: can two people ever genuinely do one thing together, or is "joint action" just shorthand for a bunch of parallel solo actions that happen to interlock? I worked my way into an argument that seems to show something worse — that real joint action is impossible for any finite creature. It goes like this.

(1) For you and me to do something together, like carry a couch or run a deployment, it isn't enough that I intend my part and you intend your part and the combined result happens. Each of us has to intend the joint activity itself, not just our own slice of it.

(2) But I can't sincerely intend that we carry the couch unless I have some assurance you'll do your part. If I'm merely hoping you'll hold up your end, that's not an intention to do it together — it's a wish with a plan attached.

(3) To get that assurance, I need to believe you intend that we carry the couch, since your doing your part in the right way is exactly the kind of thing that flows from your intending the joint activity.

(4) And for that belief to be justified, I need grounds: I need to believe you wouldn't intend we carry the couch unless you believed I intend it too. So now I need a belief about your belief about my intention.

(5) But that new belief needs the same kind of support, so I need to believe that you believe that I believe that you intend we carry the couch. You can see the ladder — each rung requires the next one up.

(6) A finite head can't hold the whole ladder at once, so the conditions can never all be satisfied.

(7) Therefore no finite creature ever genuinely acts jointly; at best we act individually while predicting each other. But that's absurd — the couch makes it from the truck to the third floor, and it's not a fluke.

So where does this thing go wrong? I'd most like to keep (1), because there really seems to be a difference between two people doing something together and two people doing seperate things that merely mesh. And I'd like to keep (2), since intending seems stronger than hoping. But the ladder in (4)–(6) makes my head hurt. Which step should I actually give up, why does that step look mandatory if it isn't, and what would I have to accept instead about how two people can share an intention without having an infinite stack of beliefs about each other?
