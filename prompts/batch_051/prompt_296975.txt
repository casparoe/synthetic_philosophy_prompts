You are a moral philosopher who specializes in population ethics and in translating philosophical distinctions into procedures that non-philosophers can run under real time pressure. Before you write the procedure, think through the main ways a project can be identity-affecting and which distinctions are worth keeping for busy analysts who will never read the underlying theory. Then present the final procedure, and be candid about the points where it stops being a method and becomes a judgment call.

I am a senior policy analyst at a regional infrastructure and environmental planning agency. My unit screens proposed projects—flood defenses, highway widenings, transit lines, groundwater remediation, school siting, housing programs—before they go to our board for approval. We review roughly ten to fifteen projects a month, and each screening has to run in about an hour or two. The stakes are uneven but sometimes high: a project can shape where people live, whether they get sick, and how the region grows over thirty or forty years. My analysts are planners, environmental scientists, and lawyers, with no training in philosophy, and I can give them only a couple of pages to work through. The director who reads the output wants to know not just the recommendation but where the analysis had to rely on judgment.

Many of our projects affect people who do not yet exist when the decision is made. Our standard screening asks whether the project makes anyone worse off than they would have been under the alternatives. But some projects change who later exists: where people move, whom they meet, when they have children, and how many children they have. When that happens, the question "Is this person worse off than they would have been without the project?" may compare that person's actual life with never having existed at all, rather than with some better life the same person would have led. This is the nonidentity problem, and I want a procedure that tells my analysts when it is present, why it matters, and how the screening should continue once it is present.

Please write me a reusable decision procedure, laid out as a numbered sequence of questions. For each question, say what kind of evidence or answer is sufficient to move on, where the procedure stops with a no or a not-applicable finding, and where it must escalate to a person because the remaining question is one of values rather than facts. Define every term in plain language, since the analysts will not know the philosophy. The procedure should distinguish at least three situations: (a) the same people exist under all the options being compared, so the ordinary harm comparison works; (b) the options lead to different people but roughly the same number of people; and (c) the options lead to a different number of people. It should also include a practical test for when identity effects are too speculative or too small to be worth triggering the special treatment, since almost any large project has some ripple effect on who later exists. And it should help my analysts separate two questions our current screening runs together: whether the project makes anyone worse off, and whether the project wrongs anyone. The nonidentity problem puts pressure on the first question but may leave the second open.

After you give the procedure, run it on three worked examples:

1. A highway widening that will raise long-term air pollution in an existing neighborhood whose current and future residents are already settled there, and whose identities do not depend on whether the highway is widened.

2. A flood-protection plan that will determine which low-lying parcels get developed. Under Plan A, future residents in the western floodplain will exist partly because that land was protected; under Plan B, a different set of people will live in the eastern floodplain instead. Roughly the same number of people will live in the region either way, but not the same people.

3. A subsidized child-care and parental-leave pilot that is likely to change family formation in the region: some children will be born earlier, some who would not otherwise have been born at all will exist, and some families will end up with a different number of children. The policy's supporters want to justify it partly by the well-being of the additional children.

Make at least one of these examples come out in a way that well-meaning agency staff would resist—where the procedure tells us we cannot say the thing we most want to say, such as that a project harms future people, even though our instincts say otherwise. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: the questions it leaves open, the places where the board must supply values rather than analysis, and the most likely ways a hurried analyst would misapply it.
