I've been reading a pop-science book about scientific reasoning, and its whole treatment of inference to the best explanation gets boiled down to one paragraph that I'm now suspicious of. It says, roughly: "Inference to the best explanation tells you to accept whichever hypothesis best explains your data, where 'best' just means simplest and most elegant. The hypothesis that best explains your evidence is also the one your evidence makes most probable, so IBE is really only a heuristic for ordinary Bayesian updating. Besides, the view has a famous hole: for any body of evidence you can invent a clever conspiracy theory or a 'universal demon' hypothesis that fits the data at least as well as the boring explanation, so IBE gives you no reason to prefer the boring one unless you already assume nature is simple—and that assumption is exactly what IBE was supposed to earn. So IBE is either circular or empty, and the heavy lifting is always done by independent background assumptions."

I'd always thought IBE was just common sense, and I can't shake the feeling that this is a slick misreading. For instance, the move from "best explanation" to "most probable" feels too fast, and I would have thought a conspiracy theory that can be bent to fit anything is not obviously a better explanation just because it fits the data. But I don't know the literature well enough to say which claims are accurate, which are common simplifications, and which a defender of IBE would flatly deny. Is this a fair summary? What should I keep, what should I correct, and does the "circular or empty" verdict survive once the accurate parts are put carefully?
