I have been rereading the parts of Adam Smith's Wealth of Nations that deal with colonies, and I want to test his account of empire against the American Revolution rather than just restate it. As I understand Smith's position, he thought the British monopoly of American trade was a net drain on the mother country: it raised prices and misallocated capital for the sake of a narrow merchant interest, while the colonies themselves were held at considerable fiscal and military cost. His menu of sensible policies was stark: either incorporate the colonies fully into the British political system, with representation and taxation on equal terms, or let them go. He also seems to have regarded the entire pursuit of empire as driven less by national interest than by the concentrated interest of merchants and the vanity of rulers. I want you to take the period from the Stamp Act crisis through the war and treat it as a case study for this theory, running the analysis in both directions.

Start with the mapping: who are the relevant actors in Smith's model, and what does it predict each of them stood to gain or lose from the imperial relationship — the British state, British merchants, British taxpayers, colonial assemblies, and ordinary colonists? Then say which features of the episode the theory handles well: for example, does it make sense of the fact that Britain persisted in a policy that looked economically irrational from the standpoint of the country as a whole, and of the way coercion and conciliation alternated? Where does it fail or go silent? I am thinking of colonial identity and self-government as something closer to terminal values than to economic interests, the role of migration and local institutions in creating a polity that would not accept subordinate status, and the actual military and financial logic of the war once it began. Finally, turn the episode back on the theory: does the Revolution confirm Smith's diagnosis of empire as a diseconomy sustained by concentrated interests, does it expose limits in his treatment of sovereignty and nationhood, and what, if anything, should a modern reader keep from this framework when thinking about alliances, client states, or decolonization? Be explicit about the points where Smith's categories simply do not apply rather than forcing the case to fit them.
