Here's something that's always bugged me about how revealed preference actually gets used. The textbook line is that utility is just a numerical summary of choice: if you keep choosing A over B when both are available, then A is revealed preferred to B, and that's all "utility" means. There's no hidden wanting behind the choosing. But in practice, nobody sticks to that. If you buy the same overpriced coffee every morning, the economist counts you consumer surplus as a real welfare gain and moves on. If you buy a lottery ticket every morning, and you're a problem gambler, suddenly the choosing stops counting: that's not a real preference, it's a compulsion, and the surplus from it gets heavily discounted or ignored in cost-benefit analysis. So we've got an asymmetry: ordinary repeated choice is read as "what they want," while pathological repeated choice is read as "not what they really want, just what they do." But the actual choice data — persistent, frequent, continued despite stated regret, surviving repeated failed attempts to stop — can look exactly the same in the two cases. What could justify the line, once you've signed on to utility being nothing over and above choice? Is it that the gambler has a second-order preference not to have the first-order preference? That their choices conflict with their own stated goals? That the habit is somehow "inconsistent" in a technical sense? Run whichever candidate you pick through nearby cases: the heavy drinker who says he'd choose it all again, the smoker who hates that she smokes and keeps smoking, the workaholic, the runner who says she wishes she could quit training. If no candidate survives without quietly smuggling back in a notion of welfare that isn't defined by choice, then what should we do — give up the asymmetry, count all choices as welfare, or admit that revealed preference was never a complete account of what utility means?
