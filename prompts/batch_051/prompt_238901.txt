I keep flip-flopping on Thomas Szasz's claim that mental illness is a myth, and I want you to force me off the fence. The theory side is genuinely compelling: a disease, strictly speaking, is a lesion or at least a malfunction of a bodily system, and a mind isn't the kind of thing that has cells or organs to malfunction. You can have a broken leg, but there's no such thing as a broken thought, so calling a way of thinking or behaving an illness looks like a metaphor that got mistaken for a literal claim. The theory is also tidy and unifying: it explains why psychiatric categories keep expanding in step with social norms, and it respects people as agents rather than patients. I get all that; I'm actually attracted to it. But then I imagine someone in florid psychosis: hearing voices, terrified, unable to tell which thoughts are their own, convinced the food is poisoned. The intuition that this person is ill—really ill, not just unusual or difficult—feels unshakable, and I cant debunk it just by telling myself that the word is being used metaphorically. So which gives? Should I debunk the intuition (say, it's really tracking an underlying brain disease, and brain disease is exactly the kind of thing Szasz would count as real illness), should I amend or abandon the theory, or is there a reading of illness on which the theory is right about the concept and I'm right about the person? Lay out the strongest version of each move, what would justify taking it, and then tell me which one you'd actually take.
