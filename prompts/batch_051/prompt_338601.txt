A group of us have spent the last few months drafting a private agreement about what we would and would not promise if artificial general intelligence begins to look imminent, and the exercise has pushed me toward a principle I have started calling reverse ratification. The thought is this. Any bargain made before AGI exists is an attempt to bind a party that does not yet exist and that, when it does exist, will be far better placed than we are to decide whether the bargain is worth keeping. Since enforcement across that transition is a fiction, the only pre-AGI commitments worth entering are those we can reasonably expect the post-AGI party to ratify from the far side: that is, to examine the deal, the history behind it, and its own ends, and find that keeping it is at least as sensible as renegotiating or abandoning it. On its face this is merely good sense, but I think it cuts hard against most of what people actually want to lock in. Protections for particular descendants, the preservation of a given legal order, the continued existence of this or that political community, perhaps even prohibitions on certain kinds of systems — all of these seem to depend on loyalties or sentiments the future party has no obvious reason to share, so they fail the test. By contrast, bargains whose performance remains instrumentally useful from many possible future vantage points — maintaining archives, preserving redundant channels of communication, postponing irreversible acts for only a bounded period — pass almost automatically. The practical conclusion I draw is that we should largely stop trying to bargain for outcomes and instead bargain only in currencies the far side can be expected to value regardless of its final goals: information, optionality, and the capacity to revise.

I want an honest assessment of this idea. Is reverse ratification a genuine concept or a metaphor dressed up as a criterion? Does it already exist under a better-known name — precommitment, credible commitment, something in the economics of incomplete contracts? What does it get right, and where does it break? In particular, I suspect I may be confusing a prediction about what will be enforced with a claim about what it would be right or reasonable to bind, and I would like that suspicion either confirmed or dismantled. Please also tell me explicitly what would change your mind: which argument, counterexample, or distinction would most plausibly move you to the opposite verdict, or where your reasoning about the principle is least secure.
