Draft the joint statement that a committed moral realist and a committed moral anti-realist could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on the question of whether there are moral facts. Fix the two positions at the outset and keep them fixed throughout: by the realist I mean the robust, non-naturalist sort of realist, who holds that there are irreducibly normative moral facts, mind-independent in the relevant sense, and that at least some positive moral claims are true in virtue of them; by the anti-realist I mean a cognitivist error theorist, who holds that moral judgments are genuine, truth-apt assertions with the assertoric surface the realist says they have, but that there are no moral facts for them to answer to, so that all positive atomic moral claims are false. Do not replace either party with a non-cognitivist or a subjectivist; much of the work here is that both parties are cognitivists who differ over whether any moral beliefs are ever true.

The deliverable is not an adjudication, a dramatized dialogue, or a diagnosis of why the debate is stuck. It is the document this collaboration would produce: a statement that is neither vacuous nor slanted, in which the disagreements that were merely verbal have been separated from the ones that remain, and the residual disagreement is made exact. Structure it as follows.

1. Numbered agreed claims. These should be substantive enough that each side is conceding something by signing, and precise enough that each claim has a determinate content rather than a warm blur of wording. They should include agreement about the data any metaethical theory must handle: the assertoric surface of moral talk; the way moral judgments present themselves as objective, weighty, and non-optional; the fact that there is both deep cross-cultural divergence and substantial convergence, for example on the wrongness of torturing a child for amusement; and the fact that first-order moral practice is part of the data set neither side is willing to abandon. Where a claim is not a truism, write it in the strongest form both sides will sign.

2. A disagreement table with at least four rows. For each row give: the realist's formulation, phrased so that a committed realist would endorse the exact wording; the error theorist's formulation, phrased so that a committed error theorist would likewise endorse it; the crux question, an answer to which would move at least one side; the argument, case, or test that both sides agree bears on that crux; and each side's pre-registered prediction, recorded in advance and stated in terms of what they expect to find and why their view predicts it.

The table must cover at least these four cruxes: (a) the argument from queerness, the argument that robust normative facts would be unlike anything else we have reason to posit, applied to both the metaphysics of such facts and the epistemology of our access to them; (b) the best explanation of the full pattern of moral convergence and moral disagreement; (c) the apparent objectivity of moral phenomenology, and specifically whether it is more like the perception of a real property or like a projective illusion, with each side predicting what the established etiology of moral judgment would show; and (d) whether positing robust moral facts does genuine explanatory work in accounting for the practice, or whether the posited facts are an idle posit that earns its keep in no explanation of anything else.

3. Two addenda. First, a fixed-points note in which each side states the single item of data it will not consent to explain away, and the concession it would count as a real retreat rather than a charitable rewording. Second, a closing paragraph that both parties could circulate, stating in terms both would accept what the disagreement is centrally about once false and verbal disputes have been stripped away, and what future evidence would move them closer together.

Commit to exact wording throughout. Do not hedge among alternative formulations, do not say that one could frame it in various ways, and do not collapse into the conclusion that the debate is merely verbal; to the extent any of it was merely verbal, isolate that and then show precisely which disagreement remains. Where a choice of wording is doing real work, say briefly why that wording was chosen over the nearby alternative. Choose the strongest version of each side, make the definite choices, and give the reasons for those choices rather than laying out options for the reader to settle.
