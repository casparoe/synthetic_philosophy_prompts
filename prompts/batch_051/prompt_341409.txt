I'm a novelist, about to start a book that follows one family over four generations, from the postwar years to now. The working title is something like 'Generations' — maybe too on the nose. What's bugging me is that I dont actually know whether I'm allowed to talk about 'the Baby Boomers', 'Gen X', or 'Gen Z' as if they name something real, or whether those are just marketing categories and lazy period-drama shorthand. I've been reading around and I keep hitting two positions. One side says generations are basically arbitrary: demographers draw a line between 1964 and 1965 and suddenly two people born a year apart are supposed to belong to different kinds of human. The other side says no, there's something there — shared economic conditions, media environments, wars, technological ruptures; people who came of age during Covid genuinely seem to relate to work and risk differently from their parents. I want the novel to be honest about this rather than smuggling in pop-sociology as if its fact.

So here's what I'd like: write an essay on whether generations — Boomers, Millennials, Gen Z and so on — are real kinds. Weigh the strongest versions of both sides: the nominalist/critical view, the realist/cohort view, and maybe the view that they're real only in the way self-fulfilling labels can become real, because enough people believe in them and organise their identities around them. Don't just survey the positions; actually pick one and defend it, even if the verdict is a qualified 'real in this respect, not in that.' Along the way, be explicit about which claims are settled among people who actually study this and which are genuinely contested, and don't present a contested view as if it were the consensus. British English, please. And if the honest conclusion is messier than my title can handle, say so — the book can hold a mess as long as it's an honest mess.
