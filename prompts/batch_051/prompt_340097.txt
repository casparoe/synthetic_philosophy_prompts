In my philosophy of law seminar, a line of questioning about the privatization of justice has crystallized into a puzzle I would like you to work through. Consider two fine-print clauses in a take-it-or-leave-it consumer or employment contract. A clause waiving the underlying entitlement itself — say, an employee agrees in advance not to pursue claims under the minimum-wage or antidiscrimination statutes — is normally struck down as void against public policy; courts treat such rights as not open to advance individual bargaining. But a clause waiving only the machinery for enforcing such an entitlement — a mandatory pre-dispute arbitration clause, a jury-trial waiver, a waiver of the right to proceed on a class basis — is routinely enforced, even when it is clear in advance that individual arbitration would cost more than the claim is worth and the right would therefore go unvindicated in nearly every case. The substantive right is treated as inalienable; the procedural route to it is treated as freely alienable.

The obvious defense is that the second clause merely selects a forum while the first extinguishes the right itself, and that freedom of contract along with the law's strong preference for arbitration justifies the difference. I want you to test whether that defense survives the hard cases: a statutory claim worth eighty dollars with no workable individual mechanism, a party who could not modify a single term, a form contract no one reads. Is there a principle that actually generates the asymmetric treatment, or is the distinction between waiving a right and waiving the means of enforcing it a formalism that allows a protected entitlement to be cancelled by indirection? Give me the strongest justification you can construct, test it honestly against the hardest cases, and conclude by saying whether the distinction should be kept, extended to the underlying rights themselves, or abandoned.
