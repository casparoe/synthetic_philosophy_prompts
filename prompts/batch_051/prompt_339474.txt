I am a philosophy professor working in moral psychology, and I have been trying to settle what I actually think about humility and modesty by testing my gut reactions against a set of cases. I recently reread the passage in Book IV of Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics where he discusses the person who understates his own merits, treating such self-deprecation as a deficiency in truthfulness rather than as a virtue, though he concedes it can seem graceful. I am not convinced that my own reactions support Aristotle, and I would like you to help me extract the principle, if any, that does.

Act as an expert in virtue theory and moral psychology. Think slowly, distinguish humility from modesty only where the cases force it, and do not assume that a single virtue covers all my verdicts. First, lay out the best candidate principles that might capture the pattern; then test each against my cases; then state which of my verdicts would have to be revised for the strongest principle to hold, and what that principle says about the further case at the end.

Here are my verdicts:

1. A scientist wins a major prize and uses the lecture to say the result was mostly being in the right lab at the right time, that her collaborators did the crucial work, and that she was just the one who wrote it up. The facts are that she had the central idea and pushed it through. Verdict: fine, even admirable, though I also think it is not literally true.

2. A job candidate is asked about a major achievement and answers, "It was a small thing, anyone in my position would have done it." Verdict: not fine. She should represent herself accurately.

3. A specialist begins a conference talk by saying, "I am not an expert on this, I only dabble." The talk is excellent. Verdict: not fine, and somewhat irritating.

4. A friend who is an excellent cook refuses compliments with, "Oh, it was nothing, just a recipe." Verdict: fine in small doses, though tiresome; I would not call it a vice.

5. A colleague who knows more than everyone else in the room reliably stays quiet out of modesty, and the committee makes avoidable errors. Verdict: not fine; this is a real failing.

6. A philosopher argues for a controversial view but prefaces it with, "I am very open to being wrong here; I have not mastered all the data." Verdict: fine, and in fact a sign of intellectual honesty.

7. A person with depression underestimates his talents and turns down a position he would have done well. Verdict: not fine, but I would not blame him; pity is the right response.

8. A political leader publicly announces, "I do not know what I am doing, and I was wrong," instead of defending a failed policy. Verdict: fine in a human sense, but I also think it may be a poor reason to trust him with the next decision.

The case I keep returning to: an applicant is asked whether she can handle a demanding job and answers simply, "Yes, I can," with no qualification and no self-deprecation. Is that a lack of humility, or just accurate self-report?

Is there a principle that gets all, or nearly all, of these? If not, what is the best small set of principles, and what do they imply about the applicant?
