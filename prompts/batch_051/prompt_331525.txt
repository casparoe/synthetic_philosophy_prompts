Two coworkers of mine got into it at lunch today about money pumps — specifically whether someone with circular pairwise preferences is irrational — and it was one of those arguments where each move sounds decisive until the other person answers. The running example was a guy comparing laptops pairwise: he prefers the Asus to the Lenovo because it's lighter, the Lenovo to the Dell because the keyboard feels better, and the Dell to the Asus because it's cheaper. The first coworker said the guy is obviously irrational, because you can start him with the Asus, offer him the Dell plus $5 and he'd take it (he thinks the Dell is the better deal), then offer him the Lenovo plus another $5 and he'd take it, then offer him the Asus plus another $5 and he'd take it. He ends up back with the same Asus, $15 poorer, having described every swap as an improvement. The second coworker pushed back: first, nobody actually holds all three pairwise rankings once the cycle is pointed out to them — they'd notice the bug and patch it. Second, even for someone who never notices the cycle, each individual trade is locally rational given the comparison he's making at that moment. Third, someone who does know about the cycle can just refuse the first swap, and the pump never gets started; the exploit depends on the trader seeing more of the sequence than the chooser does, not on irrationality as such.

The first guy came back: knowing about the cycle and refusing the first swap means the guy is saying "this swap makes me strictly better off" and yet forbidding himself from taking it, which is its own kind of incoherence. And if he doesn't know about the cycle, we still shouldn't be fine with the sequence — a person can be rational at every instant and end up strictly worse off with nothing to show for it only if we've stopped holding rationality responsible across time. The second guy said that's smuggling in a premise: a sequence is nothing over and above its steps, and "locally rational at every step but globally irrational" sounds like a contradiction unless you've already decided preferences have to stay consistent over time — in which case the defect is in the circular rankings, not in the trades. The first guy said right, the circular rankings are the defect, but they do their damage through time: someone who never catches on can be taken through that same cycle every Tuesday and slowly lose money while keeping the same laptop, and any account that calls that rational is missing somthing. That's where they stalled.

I'm a software engineer, so I kept trying to map it onto a greedy algorithm that climbs to a local optimum and gets stuck: is the algorithm wrong, or is it just myopic, and is that question even well-posed? Please adjudicate the argument move by move — rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a sentence of justification — and say what, if anything, both of them are missing.
