I am drafting the conceptual framework for an article on neurotechnology and the law, and I have found that the term cognitive liberty is doing far more work in my argument than I can presently defend. I would like you to propose a working definition of cognitive liberty: precise enough to guide policy discussion, but faithful to the ordinary moral force the term carries. The definition should make clear whether cognitive liberty is essentially a negative liberty (protection against non-consensual access to or interference with one's mental life) or also a positive liberty (a right to use available tools to shape and express one's own mind); whether it protects mental privacy, mental integrity, or both; and whether it covers only neurotechnological means or also older techniques by which third parties read, steer, or alter a mind. I do not want a slogan or a mere synonym; I need a definition that does genuine classificatory work.

I have assembled six cases against which I expect the definition to be tested and revised. (1) A locked-in patient uses a brain–computer interface that decodes imagined speech, with informed consent, to communicate. (2) A criminal court orders a defendant to undergo an fMRI-based memory test to determine whether they recognise the crime scene. (3) An employer issues wristbands that infer cognitive overload from heart-rate variability and electrodermal activity, and uses the results in productivity scoring; staff may opt out only by losing preferred shifts. (4) A social media platform continuously reorders a user's feed to maximise engagement, using purely behavioural data such as clicks and dwell time. (5) A person buys a consumer EEG headset to learn, by neurofeedback, to enter a meditative state more reliably. (6) A patient with treatment-resistant depression receives a closed-loop deep-brain stimulation implant to which they consented; the device now dampens certain impulses in ways the patient experiences as alien, and they are no longer sure they want it active, while their clinicians describe this ambivalence as a symptom. I am fairly confident that (2) is an infringement and that (4) lies outside the concept's reach, though I cannot articulate why (4) escapes while (3) may not. Please state the definition, run it through all six cases, say plainly where it gives counterintuitive or indeterminate results, and either revise it or defend the surprising verdicts. Please write your answer in British English.
