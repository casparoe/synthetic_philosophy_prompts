I recently came across a popular science-and-technology-studies piece that summarizes Langdon Winner's "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" this way: Winner argues that technologies are never neutral instruments, because some artifacts are political in the strong sense—their material form itself enforces a particular distribution of power. The paradigm is the set of low-hanging overpasses on Long Island whose designer, Robert Moses, deliberately built them too low for buses, turning concrete and steel into instruments of racial and class exclusion. On this reading, once such an artifact is installed, the political outcome follows automatically from its design; and Winner's conclusion is said to be that, since technological choices are always political choices, the correct remedy is always expanded democratic oversight of engineering. I suspect the summary runs together a stronger claim and a weaker claim, and that Winner is careful to resist at least some of the conclusions the piece draws from him, but I am not sure where the damage is. Treat this as an accuracy audit: separate what is faithful from what is conflated or overstated, and tell me which of the summary's conclusions survive a corrected account of Winner's view.
