Lately I've been working through Adam Smith's The Theory of Moral Sentiments on my own, without any academic background in philosophy, and I keep coming back to what looks like a tension in his account of self-judgment. In Part III, he appears to say that a person who grew up entirely alone would have no more idea of the quality of his own mind than of the beauty of his own face, because he would have no mirror. Society supplies the missing mirror: the looks and reactions of other people show whether they can enter into his feelings, and he first comes to see his own passions as suitable or unsuitable by noticing whether those around him sympathize with them. But in the same part of the book, Smith seems to say that we cannot simply rest with what actual other people happen to approve or disapprove, because real audiences are often partial, misinformed, or biased. He describes a higher standard: we try to view our own conduct through the eyes of an impartial spectator, an imagined fair and informed judge within us, and he sometimes treats this spectator as the judge whose verdict alone we can ultimately respect.

What confuses me is how these two thoughts fit together. If the reaction of actual people is what first gives us any sense of our own character, how can the impartial spectator inside us become a standard independent enough to correct those same people? Is the internal judge just a summary of what we have learned from society, or is Smith giving it an authority that goes beyond social agreement? Are the two passages contradictory, or is the second meant to show how the first can be developed into something more than a craving for approval? I would like to know whether the apparent conflict can be resolved without treating one of these claims as a later change of mind, and what that resolution would imply about whether Smith thinks society's judgments can be mistaken.
