I am a philosophy professor serving as the academic member of my state's committee on pattern criminal jury instructions. We are considering whether to recommend a new instruction stating that a jury may not find a charge proved beyond a reasonable doubt on statistical evidence alone, however high the resulting probability, unless the prosecution also presents evidence specific to the defendant's conduct. The question came to us through a theft case in which the only inculpatory evidence was that the defendant was one of twenty employees with access to the stolen funds and that roughly four-fifths of those employees were shown to have stolen; nothing distinguished him from the others. Some committee members regard the instruction as merely spelling out what proof beyond a reasonable doubt already demands: a verdict must be about this defendant, not about a class to which he happens to belong. Others reply that the instruction would dignify a familiar cognitive error, because jurors already underuse probative statistical evidence and overvalue vivid but weak anecdotal or testimonial evidence. Draft the short written opinion I will circulate before our next meeting, recommending adoption, rejection, or modification, with concrete wording if you propose changes, and addressing the strongest consideration on the side you do not recommend.
