I've been trying to write an essay on whether the things we do inside video games can wrong the characters in them, and I keep coming back to Black Mirror's USS Callister. The episode makes Robert Daly the villain for torturing sentient digital copies of his coworkers inside his private Star Trek-style sim: the copies remember their real lives, make plans, feel humiliation and fear, and eventually escape into the game's public cloud. The story very plainly wants us to count their suffering as real.

But the thing that nags me is that the episode gets us there by making the victims extremely continuous with actual human beings — same memories, same personalities, a felt past outside the sim. It never really pushes on the case of the generic NPCs who get shot, robbed, or ignored as set dressing. So read the episode through the philosophy of moral status for virtual characters: the question of whether something needs consciousness, or a point of view, or just states that can go badly for it, to matter morally. Is the episode making an argument that video-game characters can matter, or is it quietly borrowing moral status from the humans the copies were copied from? Where does the episode get the ethics right, and what would a philosopher have to force it to say that it doesn't? I want a reading that takes the episode and the philosophy equally seriously and isn't afraid to say the episode dodges the harder question.
