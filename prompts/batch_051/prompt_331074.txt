I have been developing a construct I would like you to assess candidly, rather than simply reconstruct. I call it the equilibrium stance. The three familiar stances are predictive strategies: the physical stance predicts a system from its material constitution and the relevant laws; the design stance predicts it from the function it was selected or built to serve; the intentional stance predicts it from the beliefs and desires it would be rational for it to have. I want to add a fourth. To take the equilibrium stance toward a system is to predict its global behavior by asking two questions: which of its macroscopic configurations would be stable under the local adjustment rules its parts actually follow, and which of those stable configurations are reachable from the present one. The parts need not represent the global configuration, and no one need have designed the outcome. The adjustment rules can be extremely simple: imitate the more successful neighbor, move to relieve local congestion, undercut a competitor's price by a little, keep doing what one is doing as long as enough others do it. The relevant notion of stability is deliberately thin: a configuration is stable when the local rules, applied to that configuration, fail to trigger any departure, whether or not the result is good by any global standard. I think this is what we are already doing when we predict where queues will form in a busy station, why similar firms cluster on one block, how one side of a path becomes the side people walk on, or why a posted price stops drifting. My central theoretical claim is stronger: the intentional stance is a limiting case of the equilibrium stance. Predicting a single rational agent just is finding the fixed point of a one-agent adjustment process in which every small deviation from the predicted action would leave the agent worse off, so what we call rationality is the special case in which the equilibrating system has exactly one locus of choice and a well-defined objective function. I also want to claim something about patterns: the regularities the equilibrium stance finds are real patterns in Dennett's sense, because they are compact, projectible, and predictively useful even though no component encodes them. I recognize that all of this sounds close to invisible-hand explanations, spontaneous order, and Schelling-style dynamics, and I want to know how much of it genuinely goes beyond those ideas. Please give me an honest assessment: Is the equilibrium stance a genuine fourth stance, or a special case of one or more of the three? What is the nearest existing idea it most resembles? What are the three strongest objections, and is any of them fatal rather than a fixable amendment?
