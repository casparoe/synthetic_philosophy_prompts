I'm a software engineer on a team that maintains a billing and payments platform, and I have no formal background in philosophy. The ideas I am working with are ones I have only encountered secondhand, mostly in engineering writing: Chesterton's fence, the principle that before you tear something down you ought to know why it was erected in the first place, and the Burkean view that inherited arrangements tend to embody more practical knowledge than any single person can reconstruct, so changes to them should be slow, reversible where possible, and respectful of the fact that we did not design them from scratch.

I want to apply these ideas to legacy code, because in our codebase there are many pieces whose purpose has been lost: flags nobody remembers introducing, defensive checks that look impossible to trigger, retry logic for failures we are not sure can happen, handlers for file formats we think we no longer receive. My team generally wants to delete this code, and sometimes that is correct. Other times we delete something and only later discover it was load-bearing in some way not visible from the code itself: a workaround for a rare vendor failure, a branch a contracted customer still depends on, a value written only for another service to read. The difficulty I keep hitting is that we cannot reliably tell which case we are in until after the deletion, and by then the mistake has already happened.

I have some rough distinctions of my own, and I would like them incorporated rather than replaced. I call the first kind of code active legacy: the reason for it still obtains, but the reason is no longer visible. The second kind I call inert legacy: the reason existed once and has since lapsed. I also think there is a separate distinction between recoverable reasons, which someone still remembers or some comment, test, or commit message preserves, and irrecoverable reasons, which disappeared with the people who held them. It seems to me that Chesterton's fence demands harder evidence before removal when the reason is likely to be load-bearing but hard to recover, and less when the cost of keeping the code is high. But I do not trust my own intuitions about where exactly to set those thresholds.

Could you turn all of this into a decision procedure I can actually run? The constraints are these. I usually have a few hours, not weeks, to investigate before a decision must be made. There is no reliable documentation, and the original authors are gone. The decision has to be explainable in a code review to teammates who lean toward deleting old code and will distrust a procedure that always says to keep it. I would like the procedure written in continuous prose, the way one writes an email, with no headings, bullet points, numbered lists, bold text, or tables. It should specify, in order: the questions to ask, what counts as a good enough answer to each, where the procedure says to leave the code alone, where it says deletion is justified, and where it must stop and hand the decision to a person because the matter is one of judgment rather than evidence. Then run the procedure on three items from our backlog: a retry loop around a payment call that appears never to fire; a monthly batch job that produces summaries nobody has read in years; and a hard-coded list of customer identifiers that receive special pricing, which we suspect is stale. If the procedure says so, at least one of these should come out as leave it alone even though my team wants it removed. Please also tell me where the procedure will misfire, and what it cannot settle.
