Here's something that's been bugging me. A cell phone video of a police officer doing something violent gets treated by most people I know as rock-solid evidence that the official story is a lie. A grainy video of a politician saying something slightly out of character gets dismissed as "probably a deepfake" by the same people, often with no forensic analysis, just because it conflicts with what they already think. Both videos arrive through the same social media feed with the same lack of authentication, and neither viewer has done any chain-of-custody work. I can see the political utility of treating them differently, but I want to know if there's an epistemic principle that actually justifies the asymmetry. To keep it grounded: in Regina Rini's paper "Deepfakes and the Epistemic Backstop," there's a passage where she discusses how video evidence has historically served as a kind of epistemic backstop, especially for people whose testimony gets discounted by official institutions, and she worries that deepfakes will make that backstop unusable. If her worry is right, is the asymmetry I just described exactly the thing she's worried about, or is there a defensible version of it? Give me the strongest justification you can for treating the police-violence video differently from the politician video, and tell me whether it survives contact with Rini's concern.
