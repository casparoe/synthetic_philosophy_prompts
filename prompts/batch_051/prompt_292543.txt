I have spent the past year running a small weekly puzzle group, and we have become mildly obsessed with Bongard problems. For the uninitiated, a Bongard problem presents two panels of six simple line drawings. The six on the left all share some property, and the six on the right are counterexamples: each lacks that property, though the counterexamples often seem designed to come close. The task is to name the rule that separates the panels. Some rules are geometric, such as every left drawing containing a triangle; some are relational, such as the largest object touching the smallest; and now and then the rule is almost perverse, such as the drawing being traceable without lifting the pen. What intrigues me is the subjective moment of solving one. For a long time the twelve drawings look arbitrary; then, in a single shift, the rule becomes so obvious that you cannot unsee it, and you can sort any new drawing with confidence. That feels remarkably like acquiring a concept — say, suddenly grasping what a friend means by a quality they cannot define directly.

One member of the group has turned this feeling into a strong claim. He argues that learning an everyday concept just is solving a Bongard problem in a messier medium. The positive instances are the left panel; the near-misses and negative instances are the right panel; the hidden rule is the concept; and the flash of insight is the moment of acquisition. From this he draws a further conclusion: if a machine could reliably infer the separating rule from six-plus-six examples of this kind, it would have done something that deserves to be called concept formation rather than mere pattern matching, and any reluctance to say so is sentimentality.

I want an audit of how far this analogy can carry that conclusion. Please make the mapping explicit — what plays the role of the examples, the panels, the rule, the insight, and the ability to extend to new cases — and then locate the disanalogies. I am especially interested in the ones that matter for his machine claim. Everyday concepts are often fuzzy, have borderline cases, and depend on context; they are acquired over many uneven exposures rather than twelve tidy ones; their examples do not arrive pre-labelled as positive or negative; and they are tangled up with goals, emotions, and other concepts. Which of these differences breaks the analogy for the use he is putting it to, and which are merely differences that do not matter? Does his conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or has the analogy been doing the work the whole time? If it fails, what better analogy would you put in its place?
