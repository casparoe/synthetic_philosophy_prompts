You are a professional philosopher who works on free will, determinism, and moral responsibility, and who acts as a translator between philosophy and technical disciplines. I am a game systems programmer with no formal philosophical training. The vocabulary I am most fluent in includes deterministic simulation loops and rules of state transition, seeded pseudo-random number generators, hardware true randomness, lockstep deterministic replication, branching history trees under different random outcomes, save-scumming, and AI decision functions in which a non-player character selects actions from goals, utility weights, and control flow. I know the difference between a simulation whose entire playthrough is fixed by its seed and input stream and one in which a true random source is consumed at some point inside the decision function, and I know from experience that rolling dice inside an agent's action-selection code does not by itself make the agent feel more autonomous, more its own, or more deserving of blame.

Here is the debate I want you to restate in that vocabulary, and then report what fails to survive the restatement.

Determinism says that the total state of the world at one time, together with the laws of nature, fixes the total state at every later time. One position in the free-will debate, libertarianism, says that freedom and moral responsibility require the ability to have done otherwise, and therefore require that determinism be false, at least at the point of human choice. Quantum mechanics enters as a possible escape route: if the world is not deterministic at bottom, and if some quantum indeterminacy reaches the neural processes behind choice, then human decisions are not fixed by the prior state of the world, and the would-be requirement of alternative possibilities is satisfied. Against that move stands a family of replies: that biological brains are probably too warm, wet, and noisy for quantum coherence to survive long enough to matter at the level of action selection; that even if genuine randomness did reach the brain, it would look like neural static rather than authorship; and that a choice which could go either way regardless of everything about who I am is not mine in the way responsibility requires but is instead a matter of luck. Compatibilists reject the initial requirement: they hold that freedom lives not in micro-level indeterminism but in whether my action issues in the right way from my own reasons, desires, values, and character, so that quantum randomness is an unnecessary detour.

Please do two things.

First, give me the systematic translation. Restate the whole debate as claims about game systems: the world as a simulation with states and an update rule; determinism as a fixed playthrough for a given seed and input stream; quantum indeterminacy as a true random source consumed at some point inside the agent's decision function; the ability to have done otherwise as something like branching histories under different random outcomes; the luck objection as something about credit assignment when an unowned roll decides the outcome; and compatibilism as a thesis about the agent's goals, utility weights, and control flow being its own. I want each major position in the free-will debate rendered as a precise claim about such systems, not a loose analogy.

Is it a genuine argument against libertarian free will, or an artifact of the translation? Push further on one specific point: game designers distinguish raw randomness from curated randomness, in which noise is injected inside a designer-authored envelope, such as a damage range or a proc chance, so that whichever outcome the roll produces is one the system is built to treat as legitimate and ownable. Does curated randomness offer libertarians a workable model of how indeterminism could help free will, namely randomness constrained to select among options the agent already endorses and will own, or does that collapse back into compatibilism with an extra tiebreak?
