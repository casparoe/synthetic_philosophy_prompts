I follow a standing epistemic policy about coincidences. When someone tells me an uncanny what-are-the-odds anecdote—running into a childhood neighbor on a train in another country, or dreaming of an event just before it occurs—I treat the story as unremarkable by default and do not update toward hidden connections, fate, or paranormal causation. My rationale is that with so many people having so many experiences, rare pairings must happen to someone, and the anecdotes I hear are a biased sample: people retell the hits and forget the misses. I have even used the familiar birthday result—that in a room of 23 people a shared birthday is more likely than not—as a model for how surprising matches arise from many unnoticed opportunities. Last week a dinner guest recounted meeting her primary-school teacher in a small foreign city, and I dismissed it on exactly these grounds. Is this policy defensible as a general rule, or is it a bias dressed up as a principle? Can the birthday-style reasoning really be extended to coincidence anecdotes, and where does that extension break down? Under what conditions should a specific coincidence instead count as real evidence for a common cause or design, and what features of the anecdote should determine how much I update?
