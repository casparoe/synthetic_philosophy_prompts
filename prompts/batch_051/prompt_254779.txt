Just got back from the pub and I can't stop thinking about an argument I had with a mate. He spent the whole evening insisting that our current scientific theories are going to look as daft in two hundred years as phlogiston, caloric fluid, miasma and the luminiferous aether look now. His claim was that the people who believed those things were perfectly confident and had what seemed like good evidence, so our confidence now is no safeguard. I kept pushing back that our methods are better, our instruments are better, we can do controlled trials and quantitative predictions. Fine. But what's nagging me is a pattern I noticed in myself, not in him. Every time he cited a dead theory I had a specific explanation for why that one died and why ours is different: it wasn't quantitative, its predictions were vague, it survived for social reasons, it was never properly tested. Each one felt true, and I still half-believe them. But the move is availible to any generation. Presumably the serious chemists of the 1770s thought they were the careful, experimental ones compared to the alchemists, and that alchemy had died for good reasons while phlogiston was on a completely different footing. So what I want to pin down is this: the standing move of explaining away past failures one by one while keeping full confidence that the current package won't ever need the same treatment. It's not the general argument that the history of science is a graveyard, and it's not quite ad hoc rescue of a threatened theory, because the theory isn't in trouble yet; it's a policy for handling trouble before it arrives. Does that move have an established name? I'm not asking about the familiar general line—past failure makes present confidence irrational—but about its defensive counterpart, the one-by-one exceptionalism. Is it treated in the literature as part of the same argument, or as a bias in its own right? I don't want just a label; I want to know what distinguishes it from plain stubbornness on one side and from the familiar historical argument on the other, and how you'd tell when someone is doing it responsibly rather than just refusing to take history seriously.
