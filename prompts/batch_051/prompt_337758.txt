I'm on our university's animal research oversight committee and we got a protocol this week that split the room. Our published policy says: "No member of the University community shall conduct research involving a living vertebrate animal in which the animal is subjected to pain or distress, except under protocols approved by this Committee on the ground that the pain is unavoidable and outweighed by the expected benefit." The clause was written in 2003, and nobody thinks the drafters were picturing what got submitted.

The protocol is from a computational neuroscience group. They want to build a biophysically detailed simulation of the mouse pain system and run thousands of aversive-conditioning trials on it. There are two arms. In the first, the simulation runs as pure software: nociceptive signals fire, the network reorganizes its connectivity, and it learns to avoid the stimuli, but there is no body. In the second, the same simulation is hooked to a small robot, so that it withdraws a limb, flinches, and over trials learns avoidance. The lab is asking for an exemption on the ground that there is no "living vertebrate animal" anywhere in the study—just computation—so the policy doesnt apply and neither arm needs review. Some of us think that's clearly right on the text and clearly wrong in spirit; others think the text settles it and the spirit is exactly the disputed question of what counts as pain.

I have to draft the committee's analysis. Please work through the case from (i) the plain wording of the clause, (ii) the purpose of the clause, (iii) what a reasonable researcher reading the policy today would take it to cover, and (iv) what the people who drafted it in 2003 would have said if someone had asked them about a simulated mouse. Then tell me which of these approaches should control, and whether the two arms should be treated the same or differently, and why.
