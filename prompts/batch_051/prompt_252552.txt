I'm a retired physicist whose recently gotten into philosophy, and I've been working through the critical side of the effective altruism literature. One image keeps coming up: the drunk who loses his keys in the dark but searches under the streetlamp, where the light is better. The critics seem to use this picture to license a pretty strong conclusion, namely that EA's insistence on measurable impact systematically favors easy-to-count things like bednets or cash transfers and systematically underweights harder-to-measure work such as policy reform, institution-building, or changing norms, which might matter far more. A friend in global health thinks the streetlamp story basically settles the measurability critique. I'm not so sure the analogy can carry all that weight, but I want to be fair to it. Can you spell out what maps onto what? Where does the analogy break down, and which of those disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion being drawn? Does the measurability critique still go through once the streetlamp is taken away, or does it have to rest on something else?
