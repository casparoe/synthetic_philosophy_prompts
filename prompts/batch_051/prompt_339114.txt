I'm the junior member of our hospital's clinical ethics committee, and we have to render an opinion on a new case this week. Going back through our case log, I found five past rulings where we were pretty confident. (1) We called it letting die, not killing, when a team did not start CPR on a patient with end-stage ALS who had asked for no CPR. (2) We said the same when an attending turned off a ventilator for a conscious patient who found it intolerable after exhausting the less burdensome settings — even though someone did have to flip a switch. (3) We called removing a feeding tube from a minimally concious patient letting die when two physicians and the family agreed further tube feeding was futile and onerous. (4) We said a doctor giving a lethal injection at a patient's request, in a jurisdiction where that's lawful, is still killing — not necessarily wrong there, but a different kind of act from the others. (5) And when a nurse smothered a sedated patient with a pillow, without any consent, to end his suffering, we called that killing and said it had nothing in common with withdrawing care. So far, though, every omission we've judged as letting die was done at the patient's request or for futility/comfort; we've never ruled on an omission where the person actually wanted the patient dead. The new case: an overnight nurse notices that a patient's IV line has kinked and stopped delivering the drug that's keeping his blood pressure up. She decides not to fix it and not to call for another bag, because she's decided the patient is better off dead and wants him to die that night, so she just monitors him as he declines until he dies. How should we rule if we want to be consistent with our own precedents? Which past ruling is the closest analog, and if our precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, what principle would we be adopting with each available ruling — and what single future case would best tell those principles apart?
