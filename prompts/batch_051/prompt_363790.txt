A city of 400,000 adults is considering replacing its posted speed limits with a personal, tradeable micromort budget. A micromort is a one-in-a-million chance of death. Each licensed adult would be credited weekly with 4 micromorts, the issue level being set about a fifth below the city's current per-driver average so the cap binds. A mandatory telematics unit in every vehicle registered in the city would estimate, in real time, how many micromorts each mile consumes: the base rate rises steeply with speed and is adjusted for road type, time of day, weather, and whether the street is residential or arterial. Walking, cycling, and riding as a passenger cost nothing. When a driver's balance hits zero, the vehicle is electronically governed to 20 mph until the driver buys micromorts from other adults on a city-run exchange; unused micromorts can be banked for up to a year or sold at any market-clearing price, and the city also sells top-ups at a fixed, deliberately high price, keeping the revenue. The meter prices only the population-average risk of death to the driver. It does not price injuries short of death, deaths imposed on passengers, pedestrians, or other motorists, delay, noise, or emissions.

Analyze how this system would actually behave. In particular: who would end up driving fast and who would drive slowly or not at all, and whether that distribution is fair; what the market price of a micromort would reflect, and whether trading mainly moves risk to those who want it most or to those who can pay for it; what gaming strategies drivers, vehicle manufacturers, and city officials would predictably adopt; and what the scheme ignores even when the meter works perfectly. Then say which failure modes can be fixed without gutting the mechanism, which are intrinsic to pricing self-risk in this way, and whether, all things considered, the system is likely to beat ordinary speed limits or just be a complicated way of doing what a low speed limit does for free.

Format: a single analysis of no more than 800 words, organized under three to five section headings. Do not exceed the limit even if that means leaving out weaker points.
