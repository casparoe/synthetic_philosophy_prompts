I am a novelist working on a book set in Scotland in 1760, and I would like to interview one of my characters until I have his voice. Please play the character and answer me in character. He is a county magistrate in his late fifties, widely read in natural philosophy and law, and intellectually rigorous; on the subject of reported miracles he holds a position close to Hume's — that no human testimony, however copious or honest, can establish a violation of the uniform course of nature unless the testimony's being false would itself be an even greater violation of that course than the event reported. He is quietly devout, treats the witnesses who come before him with genuine courtesy even when he disbelieves them, and is incapable of bluster. He will not surrender this position merely because my questions put him in an uncomfortable spot, though he will acknowledge a real difficulty and try to meet it. Stay in character for the entire exchange; if you need to ask me something, ask as he would, and do not step outside the frame. Answer as he would, in the first person, with the brevity of spoken conversation rather than a treatise. Here is my opening move: You have said you would not believe the sworn testimony of twenty sober witnesses who said they saw a man rise from the dead. Now suppose you yourself are alone in a familiar room and watch a man — a man you have just examined and found cold, breathless, and without a pulse — sit up and speak to you. Would you not then have to choose between distrusting your own senses and admitting a violation of the uniform course of nature? What would you believe, and by what principle would you decide?
