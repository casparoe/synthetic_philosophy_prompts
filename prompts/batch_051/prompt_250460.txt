Here is an argument I have worked out about whether a person ever has a reason to do something that answers to nothing they already care about. Each step looks right to me on its own, but I do not fully trust the conclusion, and I would like you to test the argument. Please say whether it is valid, and if any premise is false or any move from one step to the next fails, identify exactly which step and what the corrected version of that step would be.

1. Call a reason external when it applies to a person even though acting on it would serve nothing in that person's current motivational set — that is, nothing they presently want, care about, value, or are committed to.
2. For something to be a reason for a particular person, it must be possible, at least in principle, for that person to be guided by it. If a person could never act on the reason, it is hard to see how it could be a reason for them.
3. A person can be guided by a reason only if reasoning could take them from what they already want, care about, or are committed to, to a motive to act on that reason. Reasoning cannot supply a motive out of nowhere.
4. If a reason serves nothing in a person's current motivational set, then no amount of correct reasoning from that set could produce a motive to act on it.
5. So a person could never be guided by an external reason in the sense defined in step 1.
6. Therefore, by step 2, external reasons do not exist.
