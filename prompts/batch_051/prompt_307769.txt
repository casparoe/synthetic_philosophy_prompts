I am a PhD student in philosophy working on the ethics of deception, and I keep returning to Augustine's absolutism about lying as a kind of limit case. I would like you to read Shakespeare's Othello through Augustine's account of lying and its wrongness. On the reading of Augustine I am working with, the essential wrong of a lie lies primarily in the speaker's own dividedness: a lie is a matter of saying what one does not hold inwardly, and it corrupts the speaker's relation to truth before it does any further harm in the world. That is why he refuses to permit even lies told to save a life, while still ranking lies by motive and gravity in his well-known eightfold classification. Iago is the obvious test case, but I want a reading that presses on the hard spots rather than reducing the play to an illustration. Three difficulties interest me most. First, how well does Augustine's speaker-centered definition capture what is wrong with Iago's manipulation, given that so much of it runs through true statements, implication, and silence rather than outright false assertion? Second, does the play support or resist Augustine's way of ranking lies by motive — for example, Iago's deftly malicious lies set against the frightened denial Desdemona makes about the lost handkerchief, which the play treats as disastrous despite her comparatively blameless motive? Third, does Iago's final refusal to speak or explain himself look like the terminus of the self-corruption Augustine describes, or does the play locate the serious damage of Iago's lies in the trust and the shared social world they destroy, in a way that an inward account undercounts? I am not looking for a survey of critical positions or any secondary literature. I want a sustained reading that is faithful to both Augustine's actual arguments and the play's dramatic texture, and that ends by saying what Othello contributes to the philosophical dispute that a treatise on lying could not.
