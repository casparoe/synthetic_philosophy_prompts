I teach an upper-level undergraduate ethics seminar, and I am writing the final examination. The course has covered the contemporary debate over whether genuine moral dilemmas are possible, including anti-dilemma arguments built from the agglomeration principle and ought-implies-can, the replies available to defenders of dilemmas, and the significance of moral remainders such as regret, apology, and the sense that compensation is owed even after one has done the best thing available. I need one essay question, to be answered in 60 minutes, that cannot be answered by reciting the standard positions. It should separate students who can actually work the deontic logic from those who have memorized the labels.

Please make the question center on this crux. The central anti-dilemma argument moves from the premises that an agent ought to do A and ought to do B to the conclusion that the agent ought to do A and B, and then applies ought-implies-can: since the agent cannot do both, there is no genuine dilemma. The question should require students to reconstruct this argument in premise-conclusion form, being explicit about which premise does which work. It should also require them to set out the two main ways of responding: the defender of genuine dilemmas may reject agglomeration, while the anti-dilemma theorist may accept the argument and recast the conflicting oughts as merely pro tanto rather than all-things-considered. The question should then force a genuine comparison: what is the cost of rejecting agglomeration, and what constraint on the semantics or logic of "ought" would be needed to invalidate it without wrecking ordinary deontic reasoning? And does the evidence of moral remainders tip the balance either way? The students should have to take and defend a position on which response is ultimately less costly.

Finally, provide a model answer of about 700 words and a marking scheme out of 25. The marking scheme should name five must-hit points, allocate all 25 marks among them, and give a one-line note on the characteristic way weaker candidates miss each. It should make clear that an A answer is distinguished from a B answer by the presence of a real cost-benefit comparison between the two responses, not by merely listing them. Since the exam is closed-book, the model answer should reconstruct the arguments from the principles themselves rather than from quotations or page references.
