I'm a philosopher who works with a psychology lab on visual consciousness, and I've fallen into an epistemic policy I can't quite justify. When a participant says 'I saw a red afterimage' or 'the pain moved from my hand to my elbow,' I treat the sentence as data only about what the participant says — a report to be explained, never evidence about an experience. In print I scrub the experience talk: it's always 'the subject reported', never 'the subject saw'. My colleagues call this heterophenomenology and mostly nod along. But I make an exception for myself. When I sit in the pilot chair and say the same sentence, my own report feels like direct evidence of an actual experience, and I write things up on the assumption that I really did have the red afterimage. The asymmetry has started to embarass me. Is this defensible — is there a real epistemic difference between my reports and theirs that does the work — or is it just special pleading dressed up as method? If I can't defend the exception, which way do I go: extend the same trust to participants' reports, or turn the method on myself and treat my own reports as just more things I say? And how would I tell, from the inside, whether I've kept the exception because it's justified or because I can't stand to treat myself as just another subject?
