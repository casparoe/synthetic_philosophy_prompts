Mark gave notice at work last month to start a company, and a couple of my friends tore into each other about whether he's being realistic. Priya said he's fooling himself: the broad five-year survival rate for new businesses is around ten percent, and unless you've got real information that puts you in a narrower class, ten percent is the rational number to carry around in your head. Ben said that's mistaking the question. Mark isn't a random draw from 'all new businesses': he's a second-time founder with a technical background, he already has three paying customers, and he raised a small seed round, so the relevant class is whichever class those facts put him in, and that class does a lot better. Priya said once you start slicing like that you're lost. A class like 'second-time B2B software founders with early revenue' might have twenty firms in it, half from another decade, so you're estimating from noise — and you can't say why that slice rather than 'second-time founders whose first company was acquired' or 'founders over thirty-five with kids.' Every extra fact about Mark defines a new class, and at the end you get a class of one, which gives you no probability at all, just his biography. Ben said refusing to slice is itself a slice: 'all new businesses' is just one arbitrary cut, and it bundles in the guy franchising a sandwich shop and the person selling candles on Etsy. If the only virtue of the broad class is that it's big, you've stopped answering a question about Mark and started answering a question about a category he merely happens to be in. They both looked at me and I shrugged. I've since learned this kind of argument has a name in philosophy, but the name doesn't settle who won. Can you referee it properly? Go through the specific claims they traded and say who has the better of each; then give an overall verdict and say what either of them are missing.
