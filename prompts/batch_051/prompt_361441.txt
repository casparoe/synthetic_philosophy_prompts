I have been working out a small theory of assertion, and I would like an honest assessment of it before I use it in an essay. I call it the credit account of assertion.

The core claim is that an assertion should be modelled as a loan of credibility. When a speaker asserts that p outright, they borrow a certain amount of trust from the hearer, and they are expected to repay the loan if p turns out false, or at least to give an account of how they went wrong. The permissibility of asserting p is therefore less about meeting a single epistemic threshold, such as knowledge, truth, or belief, and more about whether the speaker is in a position to take on the debt responsibly. That depends on three things: how strong the speaker's evidence is, how much the hearer stands to lose by relying on p, and what the speaker can offer by way of repair if the assertion fails.

On this picture, the standard knowledge norm is not wrong exactly; it is a good proxy because knowledge usually makes the borrowed credit safe. But it is not the fundamental norm. Consider two cases. I tell a tourist that the museum is open late because I checked the website this morning. I tell a friend that a rickety bridge is safe because I simply have a good feeling about it. The difference is not only that my evidence is better in the first case. It is that in the first case I am borrowing a small amount of trust and could easily show that I had done the sensible thing if I turn out to be mistaken, whereas in the second case I am borrowing far more trust than I could repay.

I know this may sound close to the idea that assertion commits a speaker to defend what they say. What I cannot tell is whether the credit framework adds anything, whether it already exists under another name, and where it stops being useful. In particular, I worry it might wrongly classify as impermissible a true but personally costly assertion, and it might let a confident liar count as solvent if they have enough social credit to burn. My questions are: is this a genuine account of the norm of assertion or just a metaphor for one of the standard views? What is the strongest version of it? What are the sharpest counterexamples? And where exactly does it break down?
