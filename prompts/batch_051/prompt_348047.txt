I'm taking a political philosophy course and we've been doing Burkean conservatism, and the professor mentioned Chesterton's fence — the idea that you shouldn't tear down an institution until you know why it was put there, since the people who built it may of had reasons that are now invisible to you. I reread Le Guin's The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas right after class and can't stop thinking about it as a stress test for that principle.

In Omelas the reason for the institution isn't hidden at all: everyone knows the city's happiness depends on keeping one child locked in a basement. So the usual Burkean/Chestertonian appeal to epistemic humility — you don't know why it's there, so don't touch it — gets no purchase. But the story is written so the institution also feels obviously monstrous, and almost nobody actually wants to say the fence should stay up.

I want a reading that treats the story seriously as an objection to Chesterton's fence. Specifically: is Omelas a fair counterexample, or does it rig the case by making the institution's function transparent and its cost unbearable in a way real inherited institutions never are? Does the fence principle, taken literally, actually recommend leaving the child in the basement — and if so, is that a reductio of the principle or just the view working as advertised? And what should a Burkean say about the ones who walk away: are they the real conservatives, refusing to be complicit in a rotten order, or the opposite because they just abandon the city instead of trying to reform it? I don't want a cheap 'the story shows conservatism is evil' take; I want you to say where it genuinely threatens the principle and where a Burkean can plausibly resist.
