I keep noticing a pattern in arguments about how empirical knowledge gets its footing, and I don't have a word for it. Someone starts from the reasonable thought that our beliefs must ultimately answer to experience, and ends up positing a special layer of mental items -- bare looks, raw feels, sense impressions -- that is supposed to justify our other beliefs without itself being justified. When you ask why that layer is beyond challenge, the person says it's just the impact the world makes on us, so it isn't a judgment and can't be mistaken. But ask them to describe one of these items, and they say things like "it's red" or "it's square," which already sort experience into kinds and look exactly like the kind of thing that could be wrong. Press further, and the person slides back: "oh, I only meant it causally -- as what makes me believe -- not as a reason." I see the same shuffle with 'how things seem': it's offered as a reason to believe, and when asked what makes the seeming itself immune to error, it's demoted to a mere cause. Is there a name for this oscillation between treating a mental episode as a cause that needs no justification and as a reason that supplies justification? How exactly does it differ from plain foundationalism, from introspective access, and from the bare idea that sensations cause beliefs? I'm not after just a label; I want the carving -- what's really being conflated and where the edges of the concept are.
