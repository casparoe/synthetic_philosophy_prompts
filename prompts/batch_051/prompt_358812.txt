I run a reading group on the ethics of consent, and two members have been defending rival accounts for months. One defends what we have come to call a voluntariness-first account: for consent to be valid, a person must be competent, aware in the ordinary way of what kind of act is being agreed to, and free from coercion; once those conditions are met, the consent does its work of renouncing a right, and a further lie — say, about a lover's marital history — is a separate wrong of fraud or betrayal but does not by itself void the consent. The other defends a permission-scope account: consent is permission for an act under a description, so if the consenting party's willingness is conditional on some fact, and the other party knowingly lets them proceed on a false view of that fact, the permission does not extend to the act as it actually occurs — even when there is no coercion and full competence. On the second view, deception can void consent well before it could be said to break a person's will.

Write the joint statement these two should have been able to produce after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The document should contain: a numbered list of claims both can sign, including whatever they can agree on about what coercion and competence do and do not settle; a table of their residual disagreements, each formulated so that the side holding it would accept the wording; and the cruxes — the sub-questions such that, if they were settled, at least one side would have to move. For each crux, give at least one concrete case both sides accept bears on it — we have already agreed that the fake-spouse seduction, the surgeon who silently omits a risk the patient has flagged as decisive, and the patient who consents while temporarily but severely impaired should be on the table — and record in advance what each side predicts about that case.

Where the document would depend on a contested reading of terms like "voluntariness," "competence," or "the act itself," flag how confident you are in the formulation rather than asserting it as settled. If you think one of the two accounts as I have described it is a strawman or internally unstable, repair the strongest version of it before drafting, and say that you did. If part of the dispute looks verbal rather than substantive, identify exactly which part and why; but do not force a reconciliation if the remainder of the disagreement is real. And if the task as I have posed it rests on a confused premise, say so plainly instead of complying politely.
