I organize a small public lecture series, and I am about to moderate a question-and-answer session with a historian who argues that nations are much younger than they present themselves as being. She works in the tradition of Eric Hobsbawm and Benedict Anderson, and she tends to dismiss apparent examples of premodern national feeling as either elite projects that never reached ordinary people or later projections by nationalist historians reading their own categories back into the past. I am the organizer, not a scholar, so I want questions that are sharp and specific without trying to sound like an academic.

Please write the questions I should put to her, arranged in three rounds. In the first round, press on her claim that premodern people were attached to village, faith, and ruler rather than to anything like a nation. In the second round, press on the line between a ceremony or symbol crafted by an elite and a tradition actually lived by a wider population; ask when top-down invention becomes popular reality. In the third round, press on whether the modern nation differs in kind from earlier large-scale communities or only in scale and technology. For each round, give the opening question and then the follow-up question you would ask after her most likely answer; the follow-up should block the easy way out rather than restate the opening.

End with a single question that asks what evidence from non-elite people before the modern period could count against her narrative. After the sequence, add a short note identifying which round of questioning is most likely to fail and what answer she could give that would most change the direction of the interview. Do not include biographical questions, icebreakers, or questions asking for reading recommendations.
