Here is my attempt to reduce the central argument in Hume's essay on suicide to a numbered chain, as I understand it. I am not asking for a scholarly account of what Hume literally wrote; treat the numbered argument as the target and assess it as it stands.

(1) If suicide violates a duty to God, it does so because taking one's own life usurps God's prerogative over the moment of death.
(2) The moment of a person's death is fixed through the ordinary causal order—disease, accident, bodily failure—and that order is itself the operation of God's general providence.
(3) Any human intervention that alters that causal order (curing a fever, damming a river, rescuing someone from drowning) changes what God's general providence would otherwise have brought about, and to that extent it interferes with the course God willed.
(4) So if usurping God's prerogative over the moment of death were an impious offence against God, then altering the causal order in any comparable way would be equally impious.
(5) But such alterations are not impious: curing a fever, damming a river and rescuing the drowning are ordinary, permissible acts.
(6) Therefore suicide cannot be impious merely in virtue of altering the moment of death.
(7) The prerogative over the moment of death is the only ground on which suicide could be thought to violate a duty to God.
(8) Therefore suicide does not violate a duty to God.

I suspect that the argument is valid and that the real dispute is over (4) or (7), but I may be missing a structural fault: perhaps (2) slides from 'God is behind every event' to 'no event is specially God's', or perhaps (4) trades on an unnoticed shift in what 'alter' means.
