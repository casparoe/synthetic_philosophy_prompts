I've been running a small philosophy of religion reading group at my church, and two of the regulars have been argueing for months about petitionary prayer — the practice of actually asking God for things. One of them (call him the coherence guy) says it's perfectly coherent: an omniscient, immutable God can eternally will the whole package, your asking included, so the prayer really figures in why the outcome happens even though it doesn't change God or tell God anything new. The other says the practice is pointless or even incoherent: if God already knows what you need and his plan is fixed, then your asking can't make any difference, and you're basically doing a weird kind of self-therapy while pretending to address someone. They've agreed to do a proper adversarial collaboration, and they want a joint statement both of them could actually sign.

Please act as an expert philosopher of religion who has mediated this exact kind of dispute before, and think carefully before you draft. Start by brainstorming the places where their disagreement might be merely verbal, then write the document itself. It should contain: (1) a numbered list of claims both camps can sign, about omniscience, immutability, what petitionary prayer is, and what would have to be true for it to be "effective"; (2) the precise residual disagreement, phrased so each side would accept the wording of its own position rather than a strawman; and (3) the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one of them — and for each crux, a concrete case or argument both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. I suspect one crux has to do with whether God's eternity is best thought of as timelessness or everlastingness, and another with whether the practice could survive people knowing the metaphysics — but you should identify the real ones. If some of their disagreement is just about words, say which part and why. They don't want a reading list or citations; they want the document itself.
