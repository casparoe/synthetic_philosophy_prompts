I've been going back and forth on a forum with someone about how much of the late-medieval transition actually hangs on the specific class incidence of the Black Death. The standard secular-cycle story is that the 1347–1351 plague killed around a third of everyone, so labour got scarce, peasant wages rose, rents and elite incomes fell, elites tried to freeze wages and reimpose old obligations, and you got the wave of late-fourteenth-century revolts plus a slow unraveling of the feudal bargain in much of western Europe.

So here's the counterfactual that's been stuck in my head. Suppose the same plague years, same geography, same total mortality — call it a third of Europe — but the disease concentrates on nobles, higher clergy, and rich urban patricians, while agricultural laborers and artisans are largely spared. Maybe it spreads through the same luxury-trade and administrative networks and for whatever reason doesn't burn through villages. Predict the next century and a half using the mechanisms a Turchin-style secular-cycle model tracks: real wages, rents, land concentration, state capacity, intra-elite competition, peasant cohesion. Which outcomes actually flip relative to our timeline — do we get no late-medieval peasant gains, or do we get a worse elite collapse that opens more room for commoners in a different way? And where are you fairly confident because it's mostly factor-price and demography logic, versus where your prediction is hostage to contingent choices by kings, magnates, and rebel networks that no structural model pins down? I want the argument to be visible link by link, and for the links you're most confident in, say what would have to be true for you to be wrong.
