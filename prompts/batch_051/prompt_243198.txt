Frank Jackson’s knowledge argument, as set out in his paper Epiphenomenal Qualia, runs roughly as follows. Mary is confined to a black-and-white room from birth, knows all physical information there is to know about color vision, and on release sees something red. Jackson considers and rejects the reply that Mary merely acquires new abilities—recognition, memory, imagination—rather than new factual knowledge. He holds that she gains knowledge of what it is like to see red, something not entailed by all the physical information she already had.

Now vary the case as follows. Before leaving the room, Mary is allowed to use a device that directly drives her visual cortex into the exact neural state that her exhaustive physical theory identifies as sufficient for the occurrence of a red experience in a normal human visual system. The device is programmed so that every measurable aspect of her visual processing matches the pattern that occurs when a normal subject views a red square under standard illumination, except that the signal originates centrally rather than from the retina. Mary keeps her eyes closed during the stimulation, the room remains entirely black-and-white, and she knows precisely when the device is operating. Afterward, she leaves the room and sees a red tomato for the first time.

What should Jackson’s knowledge argument say she learns, if anything, at the moment of actually seeing the tomato? Does the initial direct stimulation already give her the knowledge the original case says she lacked, or is something still missing? If something is still missing, say exactly what it is and why the cortical stimulation could not have supplied it. If nothing is missing, explain which premise of the original thought experiment has been damaged, and whether the variation shows that the black-and-white-room restriction was doing more work than it first appeared to do. Contrast how a committed defender of Jackson and a proponent of the ability hypothesis would analyze the two stages of this modified case.
