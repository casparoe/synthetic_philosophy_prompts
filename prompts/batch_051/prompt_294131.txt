I am preparing a discussion for a course I teach on philosophical theories of what makes a life go well. A central strand is hedonism and mental-state theories, and I have been using John Stuart Mill’s *Utilitarianism* alongside Robert Nozick’s experience machine thought experiment. I want students to work through a specific source text rather than just the familiar textbook summary. In Chapter II of *Utilitarianism*, Mill states: "The creed which accepts as the foundation of morals, Utility, or the Greatest Happiness Principle, holds that actions are right in proportion as they tend to promote happiness, wrong as they tend to produce the reverse of happiness. By happiness is intended pleasure, and the absence of pain; by unhappiness, pain, and the privation of pleasure." When I ask the class whether they would enter Nozick’s machine, most say no, and they often conclude that something other than experienced mental states matters for well-being. But I am not sure they have reckoned with what Mill actually claims in this passage, especially once we include his distinction between higher and lower pleasures earlier in the same chapter. Please explain the most important implications of Mill’s definition of happiness, taken in its context, for how we should interpret the standard intuition that we would refuse to enter the experience machine. In particular, does the passage strengthen or weaken the force of that refusal as an objection to a mental-state theory of well-being?
