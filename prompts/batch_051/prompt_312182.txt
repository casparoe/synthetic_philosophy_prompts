I'm running a two-hour evening workshop for first-year design students, and the thesis I want them to leave with is Hofstadter's knob-twiddling model of creativity: making something new is mostly a matter of taking an existing thing and varying a few parameters, and the creative work lives in choosing and noticing the variations, not in generating an idea ex nihilo. These students arrive convinced creativity is a sudden flash, and they bristle at anything that sounds like turning their future profession into mere fiddling. Don't explain the idea to me; plan how I should teach it so the claim lands as a description of what they already do rather than an insult. Give me the order the pieces have to go in so nothing depends on a piece that hasn't arrived yet, the one carrying example and what it must have (and not have) to do that work, the misconceptions they'll form and where in the sequence to head each off, how to check at the end that they've got the idea rather than just nodding, and what to deliberately leave out and say so. Use these sections in order, with rough lengths: Setup (2–3 sentences), The sequence (~250 words), The carrying example (~150 words), Misconception map (~150 words, one entry per misconception and when it should be defused), End-of-session check (2–3 questions plus what counts as evidence), Leave-outs (short list).
