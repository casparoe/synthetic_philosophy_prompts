A friend and I recently spent a long evening arguing about whether a state should push parents to select embryos with better expected lives, and we kept going in circles on the ethics. I realized afterward that I would rather first understand how a concrete version of the idea would actually behave, so here is the mechanism I want analyzed.

Suppose a country whose public health system already funds IVF introduces the following rules. In any funded cycle, every viable embryo is genotyped and assigned a national life prospects score that predicts expected quality-adjusted life years from known markers and the prospective parents' circumstances. The cycle is reimbursed only if the parents transfer the highest-scoring embryo in their set; they may transfer a lower-scoring embryo, but only by paying the full cost of the cycle themselves and forfeiting the subsidy. In addition, clinics must report the scores along with the later outcomes of the resulting children, and a clinic's reimbursement rate for future cycles is adjusted upward when its reported scores predict outcomes well and downward when they predict poorly.

Analyze how this scheme would play out. What would it incentivize for parents, clinics, the designers of the scoring algorithm, and the public payer? Where are the gaming points — for example, in what gets classified as a viable embryo, in how the scores are generated and audited, or in how outcomes are reported? Which effects would run contrary to the stated aim of giving future children better lives, and which of those failure modes are fixable without abandoning the mechanism altogether? Ultimately, does the scheme select for genuinely better reproductive decisions, or for something else?
