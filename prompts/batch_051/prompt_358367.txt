Here are my gut calls on punishing people for things that were legal under their own state's law when they did them. I want to know what principle, if any, captures all or nearly all of them.

- Prosecuting the Nazi leadership at Nuremberg for waging aggressive war: fine. I know the individual crime of aggression wasn't really established before the war; I still think it was the right call, and I wouldn't overturn it on that ground.
- Prosecuting the Japanese leadership at Tokyo on basically the same charge: I'm much less comfortable, and I honestly can't say why. If the law was good enough for Nuremberg it should of been good enough for Tokyo, but there it felt more like victors' justice — the dissenting judges had a point that the law was being worked out as they went.
- East German border guards prosecuted after reunification for shooting people trying to cross the Wall. I'm fine with convicting the commanders and the party officials; I'm uneasy about the teenage conscripts. The court's idea, that a law telling you to shoot unarmed people running away is so intolerable it was never really law at all, captures something, but I don't know where that stops.
- A country passes a law today criminalizing something I did last year, where the act was bad but nowhere near an international crime. Prosecuting me under it is plain retroactive punishment and I'd call it unjust, no question.
- A new government prosecutes the old regime's torturers even though torture was formally authorized by the old law. Fine. So the fact it was legal at the time clearly doesn't always settle it for me.

The case I keep going back and forth on: if the principle behind Nuremberg were applied even-handedly, would it require charging the Allied firebombing of German and Japanese cities, or Soviet conduct in Eastern Europe? I don't want to say those were crimes, but I can't find the sentence in my own principle that blocks that without turning the whole thing into victors' justice after all.

So: is there a principle that gets all of these, or close? State it precisely, with the case that motivates each clause, apply it to cases I haven't mentioned, and tell me which of my verdicts it can't accommodate — especially the firebombing one.
