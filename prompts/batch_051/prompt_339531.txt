I'm a graduate student in a seminar on American constitutional thought, and I've watched the same argument run in place all term. The question is what the Constitution of 1787 actually is with respect to slavery. One camp holds that it was a proslavery compact: the three-fifths clause, the fugitive-slave clause, and the twenty-year protection of the transatlantic slave trade embedded bondage in the structure of the Union, whatever euphemisms the text used. The other camp holds that the Constitution is, read on its own terms, an antislavery document: it never once speaks of slavery or of slaves, it refers to persons held to service or labor, and its grants of power gave Congress the tools to fence slavery in and then extinguish it. Both sides cite the same clauses and both sides regard the other as engaging in motivated reasoning. Frederick Douglass is the pivot of the seminar, because he appears to have held the first view in the 1840s, when he condemned the Constitution as an instrument of slavery, and the second view from the early 1850s onward, when he argued that the text was best read as a charter of freedom rather than of bondage.

The debate has now stalled, and it has stalled not over any fact about Douglass's biography — everyone agrees his view changed around 1851 — but over how to classify that change and the disagreement it stands in for. I have a hypothesis I want you to pressure-test. I suspect participants are answering different questions under one heading: some are asking what the constitutional text asserts when read literally; some are asking what the framers and ratifiers understood and intended; some are asking what the constitutional order did and was known to be doing in practice over its first seventy years; and some are asking which reading is morally and politically usable now. If that is right, the impasse is largely four different questions being argued as one.

I would like you to respond as someone equally at home in the history of American political thought and in the philosophy of interpretation, and to diagnose this dispute rather than resolve it. Is my hidden-question diagnosis correct, or is there a single underlying question the two sides genuinely disagree about — and if so, what kind of question is it: historical, textual, conceptual, or moral? Given your diagnosis, what would count as real progress — a distinction, a kind of evidence, a reframing — and is there any resolution we should not expect because the disagreement flows from starting commitments that neither side can argue the other out of? Please think carefully; begin by brainstorming several possible diagnoses before committing to one, and be as precise as you can about what each side would have to hold fixed for your diagnosis to be correct.
