You are grading a political philosophy exam on Rawls. The exam question was:

"Rawls argues that the parties in the original position, behind the veil of ignorance, would select the difference principle. Explain how the veil of ignorance is supposed to lead to that result. In particular, discuss whether the veil alone settles the choice between the difference principle and rivals such as average utility and strict equality, or whether the conclusion requires further assumptions — for example, about the parties' attitude toward risk, or about the use of probabilities. Be precise about what the difference principle says and what kind of inequality it governs."

A student submitted this answer:

"The veil of ignorance keeps the parties from knowing their class, race, sex, natural abilities, and conception of the good. Because a party must choose without knowing which position in society they will occupy, they must reason as if any life could be theirs. The one choice that protects them against the worst possible outcome is to make the worst-off position as good as it can be. For Rawls, that is what the difference principle demands: inequalities in income and wealth are justified only when they make the least advantaged better off than they would be under any alternative arrangement. So once rationality and mutual disinterestedness are granted, the veil of ignorance by itself yields the difference principle.

What drives this conclusion is that the parties are strongly risk-averse. This is not an arbitrary quirk: because what is at stake is an entire life, it is rational to refuse any gamble in which some may gain only if others risk falling to the bottom. The parties therefore reject average utility, which could leave some badly off for the sake of greater total welfare, and they reject strict equality, because inequalities that raise the position of the worst off are more rational than a smaller strictly equal share for everyone. The difference principle also covers access to offices and positions: inequalities in who holds them are permitted only when those inequalities benefit the least advantaged."

Begin with what the answer gets right, then identify what is subtly wrong, conflated, or overstated, and end with a short overall judgment on whether the grade is driven more by the strengths or the weaknesses. Do not grade the exam question itself; grade only the student's answer.
