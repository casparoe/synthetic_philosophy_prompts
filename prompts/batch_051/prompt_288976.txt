I have come to treat one of evolutionary biology's favorite analogies as doing real explanatory work rather than serving as a loose metaphor: the slogan that evolution is a stupid optimizer. The view I want pressure-tested is not that evolution literally computes gradients or that natural selection always finds the best possible organism. It is that natural selection is, in its essentials, a stochastic hill-climbing process on a fitness landscape over heritable variants — a myopic and noisy optimizer — and that the theory of gradient-based search can therefore illuminate what evolution can and cannot do, in both directions. On this view, drift, neutrality, and epistasis complicate the picture without breaking it: drift is roughly the noise of stochastic gradient descent and can even help escape poor local optima, neutral regions are flat directions through a degenerate genotype–phenotype map that permit exploration, and epistasis simply makes the terrain rugged. The claim is that these deviations from smooth first-order ascent are best understood as refinements of the optimizer model, not refutations of it.

What I want is a map of what would count against this position. Tell me which empirical phenomena, formal results, and conceptual arguments would be decisive evidence that the optimizer picture is wrong or seriously misleading, as opposed to phenomena its defenders can absorb as cases of noisy, rugged, non-infinitesimal, or time-varying landscapes. Candidate problem cases I have in mind include frequency-dependent selection and coevolution, where the landscape itself moves as the population moves; Wright-style shifting balance, where drift plus migration is supposed to cross valleys that selection alone cannot; the degeneracy of the genotype–phenotype map, which can make distance in genotype space a poor guide to what selection can actually find; the fact that real mutations are discrete and often large-effect rather than infinitesimal gradient steps; and results suggesting that evolution succeeds precisely where gradient methods fail, or fails where they succeed. For each candidate, I want to know whether it damages the view, merely forces a refinement, or reveals that the view has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and whether that insulation is a vice here or simply what a high-level modeling claim of this kind looks like.

Please rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which of them a committed defender of the position would themselves concede as counting against it rather than dismiss as out of scope, and end with an honest assessment of how much the position really risks. I would personally be most moved by either a clear case where selection demonstrably and repeatably moves a population toward lower expected fitness, so that the gradient picture gets the sign wrong in a regime it claims to cover, or a formal result showing that some capability plausibly attributed to selection cannot be produced by any hill-climbing process of the relevant kind.
