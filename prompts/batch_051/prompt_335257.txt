After retiring from a career in environmental chemistry, I have recently turned to philosophical work on risk and precaution, and I have been trying to determine whether a national technology-assessment board whose rulings I have followed for many years is applying a single coherent version of the precautionary principle. I would be grateful for a rigorous analysis of its record.

The board issues short rulings on proposals involving uncertain environmental harm. Four recent decisions are these.

First, an enzyme-based fuel additive intended to reduce sulphur emissions from shipping. The residual hazard was well characterised: under a worst-case scenario the breakdown products could deplete fish stocks in a major estuary for several years, a serious harm but one from which the fishery would eventually recover. Pilot studies had bounded the probability of this outcome at roughly one per cent, and the expected benefits were substantial. The board approved the proposal, subject to monitoring.

Second, an open-ocean iron-fertilisation experiment. Here the proposers could not bound either the magnitude or the reversibility of the possible effects on marine food webs, and a severe and possibly irreversible disruption could not be ruled out. The expected benefits, chiefly carbon sequestration and scientific knowledge, were uncertain. The board refused the application.

Third, a bacteriophage treatment for a fungal crop disease. The possible harms, mainly off-target effects on soil microbial communities, were bounded: none of the credible outcomes was severe, and all were expected to be reversible within a few years. The precise probabilities had not yet been established, and the agricultural benefits were judged to be large. The board approved the application, again with monitoring conditions.

Fourth, a proposal to release sterile, non-reproducing insects to suppress an agricultural pest. The intervention would eliminate the local pest population outright, and that local extinction was considered unlikely ever to be reversed; but the board judged this not to be a serious harm, because the species is non-native and abundant elsewhere. The principal effect was well quantified. The board approved.

The case now before the board is the release of a gene drive intended to eradicate an invasive rodent population on an offshore island, thereby protecting endangered nesting seabirds and removing a reservoir of a tick-borne disease. The population-genetic modelling accepted by the board's reviewers puts the probability that the drive will escape to the mainland and, over some decades, eliminate a closely related native rodent species at roughly 0.2 per cent over fifty years. Were that to occur, the harm would be serious and, for practical purposes, irreversible. The expected benefits are substantial and largely quantified. The board must decide shortly.

My question is not merely what one might ideally decide, but what verdict would best cohere with this board's own precedents. I would like an analysis that does three things: (a) states two or more defensible principles that fit the four earlier decisions equally well; (b) says what each principle implies about the gene-drive case, and offers a best guess as to how the board will rule, with the reasons for preferring that prediction; and (c) describes a further case, not on the board's docket, whose facts would cleanly discriminate among those principles.
