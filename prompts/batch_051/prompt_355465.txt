I'm giving a talk to our department's epistemology workshop in two weeks, and my thesis is this: a consistent Clifford-style evidentialism—never believe on insufficient evidence—would classify a lot of morally necessary interpersonal trust as irrational, so there has to be a limited Jamesian permission to believe partly on practical grounds when the evidence genuinely underdetermines, the option is forced, living, and momentous, and the belief is about another person's goodwill rather than a dry factual claim. The room will have two hardline Cliffordians who think believing on insufficient evidence is always a moral failing, a virtue epistemologist who's sympathetic but will press on how a "partly practical" belief differs from self-deception, and a grad student who works on testimony and will ask why this isn't just ordinary evidence about a person's character. Give me the objections in roughly the order they'll actually come, phrased the way each of these people would put them, with what I should concede, what I should hold my ground on, and the one weak point in my view I'd better prepare to defend rather than hope they dont notice. At the very end, give a last line of the form \answer{...} containing a short label for the objection that's most likely to actually move the room against me.
