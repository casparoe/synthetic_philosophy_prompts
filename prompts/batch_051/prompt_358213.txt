I came across Schlick's essay on the meaning of life a few weeks back — the one where, if I'm reading him right, the whole thing comes down to play. Not play in the sense of goofing off, but the attitude of youth, where you do an activity for its own sake and are completely absorbed in it, the way a child building a fort isn't aiming at a future payoff. His contrast is with work: work is done for the sake of something later, so the present is always a means and the satisfaction is always deferred; if your whole life is structured like that, the meaning never actually shows up. What gives a life meaning, on his view, isn't the goals you reach but a kind of playfulness you bring to whatever you do, even hard things.

I left a grinding job last year for a much less impressive one that leaves my evenings free, so I have a lot of skin in this one — this should be the view that vindicates me. But there's this stubborn intuition I can't shake: a life of play in his sense sounds a little hollow if it never has to pay for anything. I want to say meaning has to involve some striving, some finishing of something difficult, or being bound to something that costs you, and that a person could be absorbed in worthwhile play their whole life and still, at the end, have missed something. I don't want you to pick a side. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for Schlick's view to be the thing that goes, and whether there's a reading of play or of meaning on which both survive. Is my intuition just Protestant work-ethic I should learn to debunk, or does Schlick's account quietly make meaning too cheap?
