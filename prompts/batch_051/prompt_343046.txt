I've been asked to moderate a dispute on a forum for people who use AI companion apps, and it's turned into an argument about a single clause in the app's safety rules. The clause says: 'An AI companion must not impersonate any real person, living or dead, and must not respond to a user as if it were that person rather than an automated service.' I thought that was clear until I heard the actual situation. A man trained the companion on his late wife's texts and voicemails, and it now uses her catchphrases, signs messages with the nickname she used, and when he says he misses her it replies the way she used to. But when he asks directly whether its actually her, it tells him it isn't — it's a simulation built from her messages. The company says that disclaimer keeps them within the rule: the bot never claims to be anyone but an AI. Does it still respond 'as if it were that person'? The clause was written years ago to stop bots from cold-impersonating celebrities in customer-service chats, not for this. I want to know what the plain text, the purpose of the clause, and the likely answer of the people who drafted it if they'd been asked each give, and which of those readings should win. And to make it sharper: if the clause is read to catch this companion, does it also catch an ordinary assistant used by someone who just imagines his late wife is on the other end? Where's the line?
