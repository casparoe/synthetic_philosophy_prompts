Assume for the sake of discussion that a person knows that an action is morally wrong when she meets three conditions: (1) she classifies the action under the concept morally wrong; (2) she distinguishes this kind of wrongness from merely conventional wrongness, judging that the action would still be wrong if the relevant rule, law, or norm were removed; and (3) she can identify the standard wrong-making features of the action—for example, that it harmed, deceived, or used someone—rather than merely citing the fact that it is forbidden. Now consider an adult with a severe psychopathic profile who has defrauded several elderly clients. In interview he says, without hedging, that what he did was wrong, not just illegal or unconventional. He insists that the fraud would still be wrong if no statute prohibited it, and he can spell out in detail how the victims were harmed and how their trust was exploited. Yet he shows no distress or remorse, and when asked why the wrongness of the act matters, he answers only that it increases the risk of being caught. He has never been moved by such judgments to refrain from acting. Does this person know that what he did was wrong? Work through the criteria one by one, identify where they underdetermine the verdict, and assess whether the case shows that the criteria need an affective or motivational condition in order to capture moral knowledge.
