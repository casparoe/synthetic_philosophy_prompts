A line I keep encountering in discussions of machine ethics runs as follows. Any behavioral test for 'genuine ethical reasoning' can be passed by a sufficiently good pattern matcher trained on enough moral language; any internal test, meanwhile, assumes exactly what it is supposed to discover — some representational state that counts as a moral reason rather than a cluster of features correlated with the labels. Therefore, the difference between a machine that reasons about what it ought to do and one that merely reproduces moral discourse is empirically empty. I think this overcorrects, but I have not seen a test that cleanly separates the two hypotheses. Design that test. Choose a concrete setting — an allocation rule for scarce medical resources, or a policy about when a high-stakes assistant may break a promise or override a user's explicit instruction. Specify at least one out-of-distribution behavioral probe and at least one mechanistic or representational criterion. Say exactly which patterns of results would move you toward 'genuinely engages in ethical reasoning' and which toward 'merely reproduces moral discourse', and what a null or ambiguous pattern would show. Then state what no test in this family could settle even in principle: for instance, whether the system's internal states have the right kind of content, or whether a capability that appears in a controlled probe would persist under optimization pressure rather than being an artifact of the evaluation.
