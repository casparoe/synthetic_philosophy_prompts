I've been going back and forth about state interference in parenting for months now, and I want to know what my own case-by-case reactions actually add up to, because they do not look consistent to me. I'd rather fix the principle around the case judgments than the other way round.

Here are my calls, roughly:

1. Requiring a home study, background check, and a short prep course before an adoption is finalised: fine, obviously.
2. Requiring all prospective birth parents to pass a basic test on nutrition, sleep safety, and child development before the baby is born — free, unlimited retakes, no income cutoff, just pass it first: not fine, feels genuinely dystopian, even though I can't say why it's different from (1).
3. Removing a child whose parent leaves them home alone overnight at six while the parent works, and requiring a parenting course before reunification: fine, though it breaks my heart for the parent.
4. Investigating a parent because their nine-year-old walks six blocks to school alone, and making them sign a safety plan: not fine, that's overreach, even though the risk isn't zero.
5. Fining parents who pull a fourteen-year-old out of school to work in the family business, even when the family needs the money and the kid says they want to: fine, the state should win.
6. Compelling an Amish-like community to keep kids in school past fourteen because leaving narrows the kids' later options: not fine, leave them alone. I know (5) and (6) are hard to hold together.
7. A court ordering a blood transfusion for a twelve-year-old over the parents' religious objection: fine.
8. A court ordering a cochlear implant for a deaf toddler over the parents' objection: not fine, the state should stay out.
9. Banning a Deaf couple from selecting an embryo with a deafness-linked gene so they can have a deaf child: fine to ban, and I can see that (8) and (9) sit really badly next to each other.

My working theory is basically the open-future idea: the state can block serious, hard-to-reverse narrowing of a child's future options during childhood, but it can't require parents to optimise and can't enforce one vision of the good. But that handles (5)–(9) better than (1)–(4), and it says nothing about why vetting adoptive parents is fine while vetting birth parents isn't. Also (3) and (4) are both about risk to a child, and I come down opposite ways, so I'm not even sure risk is doing the work.

So: is there a single principle that gets all nine, or do some have to go? If some go, which ones and why? And what does the principle you end up with say about a case I keep flip-flopping on: a state considering a rule that all children, however they are educated, must spend at least twenty hours a week in settings with adults trained in child development — which would effectively ban full-time home education even by well-resourced parents who provide plenty of enrichment. I genuinly don't know where I land on that one.
