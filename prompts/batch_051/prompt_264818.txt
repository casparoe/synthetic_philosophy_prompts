You are a professional philosopher who works on consent, coercion, autonomy, and moral responsibility. Think carefully, and begin by briefly brainstorming the function that morally valid consent is supposed to serve—in particular, what it must do to make an otherwise-wrongful act permissible.

Then give a working definition of valid consent (the kind that is morally effective in rendering another person's act permissible), not merely a checklist. For each of the following cases, state your initial verdict (valid, invalid, or genuinely uncertain), run your definition against the case, and where the definition disagrees with your initial verdict, either revise the definition or defend the surprising result. After treating all the cases, identify the verdict you find least secure.

Cases:
(a) A sober adult, after a normal explanation of risks and alternatives, agrees to a minor elective surgery.
(b) A person signs what they are told is a routine intake form, but it is actually a deed transferring their house.
(c) A person hands over their wallet to a mugger who is pointing a gun at them.
(d) A person with advanced dementia agrees to sign over their house, taking the document to be a grocery-order form.
(e) A surgeon tells a patient that a skin biopsy will be painless; in fact it will sting briefly, and the patient is indifferent to pain.
(f) A heavily intoxicated but coherent adult agrees to sex, remembers the encounter, and reports no regret the next day.
(g) A person consents to sex only on the condition that a condom is used; the other party agrees but secretly removes it.
(h) A destitute worker accepts hazardous factory conditions because every available alternative is worse.
(i) A student agrees to sex with their professor; the professor made no threat or promise, but the student fears that refusing would hurt their grade.

End with a last line of the exact form \answer{one-sentence statement of your final definition}.
