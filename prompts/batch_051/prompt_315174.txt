I am a software engineer who works on systems that retrain probabilistic models on a fixed schedule, and I have recently been trying to understand van Fraassen's Reflection principle well enough to write a short essay about it. The essay, tentatively titled Reflection as a Service, would argue as follows. As I understand the principle, it says that a rational agent's current probability for a proposition should equal the expectation of the probability she will assign to that proposition at a later time — formally, if P_t is today's credence and P_s is the random variable ranging over my possible future credences, then P_t(A) = E_t[P_s(A)]. That reading makes the principle look like nothing more than the law of total expectation applied to one's own future posteriors, and therefore like a theorem rather than a substantive constraint on rationality.

The production analogue seems straightforward. Suppose a deployed classifier currently outputs P(label | features) = 0.7. Next weekend's retraining job will ingest another week of logged examples, and any Bayesian would agree that conditioning on more evidence cannot increase your expected error. So today's model should already be able to quote, as its prediction, whatever distribution it expects the retrained model to output; and we should be able to ship the current model as though it were the retrained one, because the current checkpoint is, in expectation, the future checkpoint. One could even offer this as a deployment-time guarantee: the expected loss of the current model already embeds the value of the future data.

My suspicion is that the only reason epistemologists treat Reflection as controversial is that they smuggle in future selves who are drunk, forgetful, or otherwise irrational when they formulate their counterexamples — edge cases no sensible engineer would put into a production pipeline. I would appreciate comments on the accuracy of this essay idea before I develop it further. Please reply as a numbered list of exactly five comments, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion, focusing on where the characterization of Reflection, the law-of-total-expectation framing, or the deployment guarantee goes wrong if it does.
