On the platform team where I work as a software engineer, the same problem keeps coming up in API and data model reviews. One area of the system defines a concept — for example, the `owner` field on a document or the `credit` amount in our billing ledger. Another team copies that concept into a new context with a slightly different meaning. Later, someone copies the copy, and a year on, the same identifier means four subtly different things depending on where it appears. I've come to hold the following view in rough, non-technical form: for concepts in a small connected domain, there is no fixed core of meaning underneath all the copies that careful analysis could recover. The concept just is the chain of copies together with the small shifts each copying introduced, the way a story changes through repeated retelling. At some point, the copies no longer share a definable invariant, and pretending they do causes real damage.

I don't need help defending that view. I need it turned into a decision procedure my team can actually run, because these reuse proposals arrive in my review queue roughly every two weeks, and the stakes are concrete: a concept that has stretched too far makes access checks wrong, migrations inconsistent, analytics misleading, and our public API harder to reason about. The team proposing the reuse and a platform steward both have to sign off on the final call, so the procedure has to be usable by both.

Please write that procedure as a checklist or flowchart in words that a room full of working engineers with no philosophical training can run in a thirty-minute design review. The procedure should ask the questions in order; say what counts as a sufficient answer to each; and state where it returns a decision to reuse the same concept, where it returns a decision to split or rename, and where it must escalate to a person because the decision is a judgment call rather than a test. In operationalizing the view, keep the distinctions that actually do work in detecting drift, say which distinctions can be dropped safely, and mark the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. Then run the procedure on two worked cases from my actual situation: first, reusing the `owner` field on a document for the person accountable for a workflow step, where that accountable person may have no read or write access to the document; second, reusing the `credit` amount in the billing ledger for a non-monetary allocation that offsets a usage limit rather than a balance. At least one case should produce an answer my team will not like. Finally, say what the procedure cannot catch, and under what conditions we should stop trusting it.
