I teach a fiction workshop — I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, but one unit a term I make the students read an ideas book and answer one real question about it, and this term it was Malthus's 1798 Essay on Population. I'm grading the short take-home now and I'd like a second read on it, since the student writes cleanly and I want the grade to track the reasoning rather than the prose. Here's the question I set, then the student's answer. Give me a letter grade plus the point-by-point comments that justify it — the kind of comments I can turn into margine notes.

Question: Malthus keeps getting described as "right but early." Reconstruct his principle of population from the 1798 Essay — the two growth ratios, the two kinds of checks, and the forecast that follows — then evaluate whether the history since 1798 refuted the theory or just moved the deadline. Finally, lay out at least two ways a defender could try to rescue the theory from the apparent failure, and say which of those rescues (if any) leaves the theory scientific rather than unfalsifiable. No citations needed; I want the reasoning, not the bibliography.

Student answer: Malthus's principle of population is that population, if left unchecked, grows geometrically — 1, 2, 4, 8 — while the food supply can grow only arithmetically — 1, 2, 3, 4 — so the two curves have to cross and living standards get forced down to subsistence. He distinguished preventive checks such as delayed marriage from positive checks such as famine and disease, and the forecast was that unless people practiced the first kind aggressively, the second kind would eventually do the work instead. After 1798, Britain didn't starve: coal-powered industry, agricultural improvement, and grain imports let the country support a bigger population at a higher standard of living, so the collapse he described never came. Strictly speaking, then, the theory was falsified, because the predicted subsistence crisis failed to happen in exactly the society where it should have shown up. A defender has two moves. One is the right-but-early move: the arithmetic limit is still there, but fossil fuels and New World grain pushed it further out, so the principle wasn't wrong, just premature. The other is to point at present-day resource problems — soil erosion, aquifer depletion, climate disruption — as confirmation that the underlying constraint is real and that his core insight survives. The second rescue is stronger, because it uses current evidence instead of an endlessly receding future, and it shows the theory was not refuted, only waiting for its assumptions to catch up.

Give me a grade and the comments.
