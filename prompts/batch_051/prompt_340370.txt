While reporting a piece on the movement to reform limitation periods, I was given the following argument by a legal philosopher I interviewed for the ideas desk. It seems to prove more than either of us can accept, and I cannot locate the mistake.

(1) The passage of time is not an action, an event, or a property of a person; it is only a relation of temporal distance between an earlier wrong and a later response to it. (2) Therefore elapsed time cannot itself be a consideration with moral weight—something that bears on what an offender deserves, what a victim is owed, or whether a trial would be just. Saying that too much time has passed must be shorthand for some further fact that travels with time: reform, rehabilitation, decay of evidence, a community's need for closure, a victim's recovery. (3) But each of those further facts can come apart from elapsed time: a wrongdoer can reform within a year or not at all in forty, evidence can be destroyed in a week or preserved for a century, a community can return to calm quickly or remain shattered for decades. (4) So a rule that turns solely on the number of years elapsed substitutes an inert temporal marker for the considerations that actually carry the moral weight, and is for that reason morally arbitrary. (5) Statutes of limitations are rules of exactly this kind, barring prosecution or suit once a fixed term has run. (6) Hence statutes of limitations are morally indefensible, and so is any legal rule that makes elapsed time dispositive.

I am unwilling to accept (6), and I would like to preserve the thought behind (1) and (2) that time is not itself a moral quality. But I cannot see where the argument gives way. Which step should I reject, or, if the reasoning is valid, which premise is least costly to give up? However you diagnose it, explain why the tempting move looked safe, and set out what the main philosophical positions about rule-based and case-by-case moral reasoning would each have to pay.
