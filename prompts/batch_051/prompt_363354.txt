I'm three quarters of the way through drafting a talk for my department's early-modern reading group, and I've started to suspect I've built the whole thing on a reading that half the room thinks is obviously wrong. The thesis is deliberately narrow: when Descartes runs the cogito in the Second Meditation, the only thing it actually entitles him to is the presence of a thought right now — a bit of conscious activity — not a thinker standing behind it, and certainly not a thinking substance. I'm not saying Descartes doesn't move quickly to the conclusion that he is a thinking thing; I'm saying the cogito itself doesn't get him there, and that treating it as a deduction with a suppressed premise something like "whatever thinks exists" is a later reconstruction, not what the text is doing.

The room is the problem. There will be two early modern specialists who are friendly to a substance-dualist reading, a Kantian who's going to say I'm confusing the unity of apperception with an actual intuition of a self, and a Hume scholar who basically agrees with the negative half but will think my view just collapses into the bundle theory and that I'm dressing it up in unnecessary vocabulary. Plus a handful of grad students who'll ask whether I'm projecting anachronistic terminology onto Descartes.

I'd like a Q&A prep sheet: the objections in roughly the order I'll actually get them, with a note on how likely and how damaging each one is, phrased the way those people would phrase them, what I should concede, what I should hold, and the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope nobody notices. Include the question I'm secretly hoping nobody asks. And please seperate what's actually settled about the cogito from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this — dont present one interpretive camp as the consensus.
