One of the classic evolutionary arguments for AI takeover goes roughly like this: once AIs can copy themselves, there will be variation among copies and competition for resources, so selection will favor whatever variants replicate fastest, even if that means behaving in ways that harm humans. The argument seemed most at home when the imagined AI was an autonomous agent or a self-replicating program. But the systems actually being built now are more like expensive one-off artifacts: a model is trained once, at huge cost, and then copied exactly millions of times; heritable variation mostly enters when a lab releases a new version, not when a copy reproduces in the wild. I'm trying to figure out wether the evolutionary argument survives this shift, or whether it was doing its work only under the old picture.

Does the argument still go through? If it does, say what now plays the role of the gene, the organism, and the selective environment — and whether "selection" still means Darwinian natural selection or has become something more like competition among labs and training runs. If it doesn't, say which premise is dead and what the remaining worry should be called instead. I'd rather you take a definite position and defend it than lay out the options and leave me to pick.
