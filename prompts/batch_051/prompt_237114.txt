So I've spent the last year or so trying to design a format that turns argument into an actual sport, and I want an honest verdict on whether it's any good. The working name is epistemic limbo — like the party game, the bar keeps getting lower. The core rule is that you don't score for winning, persuading a judge, or even making the better case. Instead, each side starts the round by publicly writing down the five claims they'd most hate to be wrong about. During the round you can only score by visibly moving off one of those claims: qualifying it, narrowing it, downshifting your confidence, or flat-out retracting it. So if I open with "markets are mostly efficient" and later I'm at "markets are efficient only under conditions I can't yet specify," because of the other side's case, that's a score. But the update has to be certified — your opponent has to sign off that you actually engaged the strongest version of their objection and weren't just faking a concession for points. If they refuse to certify and the judges think they're being vindictive, they lose points instead. Whoever has the highest concession yield at the end (weight of certified updates over claims you entered with) wins.

The theory behind it is that normal debate rewards being immovable, so a real sport of argument should reverse that; make abandoning a bad position the most glamourous move available. Then good reasoning stops being a private virtue and becomes something you can train, score, and spectate. I've run it a handful of times and some of those rounds were the best conversations I've had, which honestly makes me suspicious that I'm fooling myself. I know steelmanning is adjacent, but this feels more specific. Is it coherent? Does it already exist under another name? And where does it break down when people actually try to game it?
