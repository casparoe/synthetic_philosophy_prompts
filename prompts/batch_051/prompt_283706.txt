A friend of mine and I recently spent a long evening arguing about whether the distinction between tool AI and agent AI can bear the safety weight that people commonly assign to it. His position was that a system trained only to answer questions or issue predictions is a tool in a principled sense: the objective is applied during training, but at deployment the system merely computes outputs, with no preferences over states of the world, no plans, and no drive to keep itself in use. From this he concluded that such systems cannot be dangerous in the way that agents are, and that a serious safety program should focus its energy on preventing the construction of agents. I was unpersuaded. The cases I kept returning to were ones in which a predictor's outputs systematically alter the environment it is later evaluated on, as with certain forecasting tools, recommender systems, and predictive policing, so that the composite human–machine loop begins to look as though it is seeking outcomes even though no single component is. His reply was always the same: whatever goal-directedness appears in such loops belongs to the humans who act on the predictions, not to the system, and describing the loop as agentic is just projection.

I would like you to argue a definite position in response. Argue that the tool/agent distinction is not a stable architectural property and cannot ground the safety conclusion my friend wants to draw, even in the strongest case he could identify: a system that only emits predictions, has no actuators, and cannot control its own inputs. Be precise about where the goal-directedness of the overall loop does its causal work, and address directly the objection that any agency in the loop is nothing more than the agency of the human users. I am less interested in whether the system literally has intentions than in whether the distinction can do the philosophical and practical work being asked of it.
