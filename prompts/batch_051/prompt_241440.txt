I am a novelist, and while researching a book set in a German town I have been turning over an institution that seems to sit badly with every clean theory of church and state I know.

In Germany the state collects a church tax on behalf of recognized religious communities: a surcharge of eight or nine percent on a registered member's income-tax bill, withheld by the employer and remitted by the tax authorities directly to the community. Membership in such a community is a matter of official record, and leaving the community — which is what ends the tax — requires a formal declaration before a state registry office. The same state also runs religious instruction in its public schools, taught according to the doctrine of each student's own community, and pays much of the bill for church-run hospitals, schools, and welfare services. Yet German constitutional law commits the state to neutrality among confessions and forbids discrimination on grounds of faith.

From the outside this looks inconsistent. If the point of separating church and state is that the state must not lend its coercive machinery to any religious body, then using the income-tax system as the churches' collection agent is hard to distinguish from an establishment. If the point is only that the state must not impose belief or prefer one confession over another, then the arrangement starts to look like a payroll deduction for a voluntary association, no more an establishment than collecting union dues. But that analogy strains as well: the state decides which communities may receive the tax, keeps the membership records that determine who owes it, and funds those communities' schools and hospitals.

What theory of church and state does this set of practices presuppose? In particular, what does it presuppose about what religion is for these purposes — a corporate identity the state may register and tax for, a private conviction the state must stay out of, or something else? Does the practice strictly presuppose any one of these views, or is it compatible with several, and where is it philosophically underdetermined? And what would have to change if the underlying view were replaced by a strict secularist account of the state's role: which of these functions would have to go, and which could be redescribed as neutral administration?

Please also say what would change your mind — which argument or consideration would lead you to a different judgment about what the practice presupposes, and where your reasoning about it is most likely to fail.
