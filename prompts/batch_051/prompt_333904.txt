I’ve been rereading Daniel Dennett’s “Where Am I?” — the one where a brain named Yorick gets transplanted into a vat in Houston and runs a body named Hamlet in Tulsa over a radio link — and I keep arguing with a friend about a particular moment in the essay. She says the point-of-view shifts Dennett describes are just rhetorical staging for what is, metaphysically, an easy case: the person goes where the brain goes. But that seems to me to dodge the whole puzzle. The essay’s setup makes the body able to see, hear, speak, act on Dennett’s dispositions, and even go through social routines, while the brain keeps the memories and the decision making; so the body is not just a puppet, but it’s also not the obvious site of consciousness. Then Dennett notices his own point of view can be pulled to different nodes of the system depending on whether he focuses on manual tasks, auditory input, or introspective report.

Please argue against my friend’s brain-goes-with-the-person conclusion. Make a case that the thought experiment exposes an assumption we normally don’t have to state — that a self’s location is the location of a unified point of view — and that once the point of view is split, relayed, and distributed across a radio link, the question where is the person? is underdetermined unless we already privilege one relation (memory, control, bodily continuity, consciousness) over the others. Use the distinction Dennett draws between the origin of control, the point of view, and the bearer of a proper name. The strongest version of your argument won’t just list weird cases, but will show that my friend is smuggling in an unargued theory about which relation is essential to personal identity.
