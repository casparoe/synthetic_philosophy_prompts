After a working life in the natural sciences, I am accustomed to asking whether a result is an artifact of how one describes the setup, and I have begun to worry that Kant's Formula of Universal Law has the same vulnerability. I would like the following argument assessed step by step.

(1) The Formula of Universal Law evaluates an action through its maxim, and the maxim is the principle the agent genuinely takes herself to be acting on.

(2) An agent can genuinely take herself to be acting on an extremely specific principle: not "I will buy bread when it is cheap," but "I, a person with my exact taste for day-old rye and my present budget, will buy this discounted loaf from this baker at 7:15 this morning, in order to have a cheap breakfast."

(3) If one universalizes that maxim, the resulting law covers at most a single agent in an unrepeatable set of circumstances, so universal conformity to it cannot generate a contradiction in conception.

(4) Nor can it generate a contradiction in willing, since I can will the harmless law that that one fully described action occur.

(5) Therefore any action can be made to pass the Formula of Universal Law by honestly formulating its maxim narrowly enough, and the Formula is empty as a criterion of moral permissibility.

Is this argument valid? If not, specify exactly which step fails first, explain the correct Kantian claim for that step, and say whether a corrected version of the argument would still trouble the Formula. Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, one for each step above, each item a sentence or two, with no introduction or conclusion.
