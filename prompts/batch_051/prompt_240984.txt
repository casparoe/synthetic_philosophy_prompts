I'm doing a short talk at my school's philosophy and econ society defending the claim that honouring sunk costs isn't always irrational and that the "sunk cost fallacy" label gets used too loosely. I've got about ten minutes then questions. The crowd is mostly A-level economics students who basically think "rational" means "only future costs and benefits matter", so they'll say my view is irrational by definition, plus a couple of psychology students who think of it as a bias people predicatably fall for, and the teacher who runs it is really into behavioural economics and will probably ask why we shouldn't just reframe the decision so the bias disappears.

My actual argument is going to be something like: the standard economics definition of rationality as purely forward-looking already assumes what it's trying to prove. I want to defend cases like someone finishing a degree they no longer want, or staying in a club they've put years into, because the past effort itself gives them a reason — not because it promises future payoffs, but because they don't want all that effort to have been for nothing, and that's a genuine value about the kind of life you're leading rather than a mistake about the future. I'd also say some textbook sunk-cost cases only look irrational because the example quietly assumes the person only cares about money in the narrowest sense.
