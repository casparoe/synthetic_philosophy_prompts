I keep noticing a structural parallel between era-adjusted statistics in sports and the man-of-his-time defence in the moral appraisal of historical figures, and I cannot decide whether it is a real isomorphism or only a metaphor.

Take the sports side first. A serious attempt to compare Babe Ruth and Mike Trout does not compare raw home-run totals, because the run-scoring environment, the ballparks, and the distribution of talent all changed across decades. The analyst instead adjusts the raw numbers by some baseline—the league average, a park factor, or a replacement level—to isolate the player's contribution from what the environment supplied. The adjusted number is meant to answer a counterfactual-sounding question: how would this player have performed under a common standard?

The moral side has a similar surface. When someone says that Thomas Jefferson's slaveholding should be judged by the standards of his time, or that Churchill's racial attitudes or Columbus's treatment of the peoples he encountered should be so judged, they are attempting to separate the person from the moral and intellectual environment he inhabited. The defence refuses to compare the figure against present norms in the abstract and insists instead on comparing him with what a reasonable or decent person in his social position could have been expected to see and do. That, too, is a kind of adjustment: subtract the era, and examine the residue.

The mappings look non-trivial. Raw performance corresponds to raw historical conduct. The league average corresponds to the prevailing norms of the reference group. Park factors correspond to institutional and material circumstances—slavery's economic centrality, imperial ideology, limited information—that made certain wrongs particularly available, tempting, or visible. And in both cases a choice arises about which features of the setting are legitimate to condition on, because once you adjust for enough context, any conduct can be made to look ordinary, just as adjusting for enough factors can turn any hitter into a league-average one.

But I can see places where the analogy may bottom out. First, sports analytics fixes the scoring system in advance—runs and wins are what we are trying to explain, whatever the era—whereas in historical judgment the scoring system itself is exactly what is in dispute; a presentist will say that prevailing norms can be part of the wrong rather than a baseline of legitimate variation. Second, the sports adjustment is descriptive-statistical, while the moral adjustment is already normative: choosing the comparison class for a historical figure involves deciding to which contemporaries he is answerable, and that decision can quietly load in the verdict.

What I want to know is whether the sports-statistics side can be used as anything more than an illustration. Is there a precise sense in which era-adjustment in performance metrics and the man-of-his-time defence both solve, or both fail to solve, the same problem of decomposing an outcome into a personal component and a contextual component? What would have to be true of moral appraisal for the statistical logic—reference classes, baseline adjustment, the decomposition of variation—to transfer without begging the question against the presentist? If the parallel is deep, what surprising prediction does it generate that could be checked against a historical case? If it is merely superficial, what exactly is present on the moral side, and absent on the statistical side, that breaks it?
