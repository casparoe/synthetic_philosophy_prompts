I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, so I will describe this in ordinary terms; if the field has precise vocabulary for what I am describing, that is what I am hoping to learn.

I keep noticing a pattern in systems that store people's names, and I want to know whether the pattern has an established name and, more importantly, how it should be carved up. Here are three representative instances. A signup form requires separate "given name" and "family name" fields and will not proceed unless both are filled; a colleague who uses a single name has to enter it twice or insert a placeholder. A fraud-detection system treats a person's legal name and their professional name as two distinct individuals, even when every other attribute—address, date of birth, device, network—points to one person. A normalization routine uppercases everything, strips diacritics, and reorders name particles, so the same person appears three different ways in a single mail merge. None of these is exotic; they are routine failures when modeling person data.

I am not asking about the bugs themselves. What interests me is the pattern beneath them. Each system embeds a model of what a name is: a single, stable, canonical string that divides neatly into given and family components and stays constant across contexts. When the world fails to match the model, the failure is usually assigned to the data or to the person, not to the model. A name that does not fit is squeezed into the available fields, padded with a placeholder, or treated as evidence of duplicate or fraudulent identity. The schema becomes the norm against which real names are judged, rather than being undercut by them.

I have read engineering write-ups that list the individual false assumptions programmers make about names—for instance, that a person has exactly one canonical name, that names never change, or that they have a predictable order. But a list of false assumptions is not what I am looking for. I want to know whether there is an established concept in philosophy for the move I am describing: taking a representational or administrative schema and treating conformity to it as the norm, so that nonconforming cases count as defects in the world rather than defects in the schema. I would also like to know which philosophical account of proper names these systems implicitly rely on—the picture of a name as a stable tag attached to one person and capturable by a single canonical string—and whether that picture is defensible.

If the concept already has a name, tell me what it is, but do not stop at the label. Say what the phenomenon includes and excludes, how it differs from an ordinary data-modeling failure, from bureaucratic simplification, and from a simple category error, and which neighboring concept fits best. If no established name covers it, propose one, define it, and mark its edges. I care more about getting the carve right than about the label.
