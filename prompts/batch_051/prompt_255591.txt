In a seminar on philosophy of action, we considered the following analysis of what it is to make a petition. A speech act counts as a petition only if the speaker wants the thing requested to come about, believes the addressee is capable of bringing it about, believes the outcome is not already settled by factors that leave the addressee no latitude, and is attempting to get the addressee to do something the addressee would not necessarily have done in the absence of the petition. Now consider a theist who prays for a friend's recovery from illness, addressing a God who is understood to be omniscient, perfectly good, and strictly immutable. On the metaphysical picture this theist accepts, God timelessly knows every event, including the friend's fate, and God's single eternal act of will cannot be altered by anything that happens in time. Nevertheless, the theist prays in the ordinary way, uttering the petition in the hope that it will make a difference.

Go through the four criteria one by one and say whether, on the metaphysics just described, the prayer satisfies them. In each case, identify which criterion is doing the work and whether the difficulty stems from the criteria themselves, from the theology being applied, or from an ambiguity in what it means for a petition to make a difference. Conclude by arguing whether an immutable and omniscient God can be petitioned in this sense, or whether the criteria need revision to capture what makes petitionary prayer intelligible.
