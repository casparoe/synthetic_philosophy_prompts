Suppose that in 2050 the country of Ruritania, a mid-sized parliamentary democracy with a strong administrative state, abolishes expert archival appraisal. A new public-records law requires every government body to transfer its records to the national archive on a fixed schedule. The archive must permanently preserve exactly five percent of every record series, selected by lottery before any archivist or official has examined the contents; a record series is a body of records that an agency files together because they share a function, form, or type of transaction. The lottery may be stratified by agency, format, and series, but not by content, perceived historical importance, or the prominence of the people involved. The remaining ninety-five percent is destroyed on a statutory timetable, and supplementary retention of lottery losers is forbidden except under a court order or a specific statutory hold. Assume the law is sincerely implemented, funded, and complied with.

Predict what Ruritania's surviving record looks like in 2100, and how its history-writing, litigation, investigative journalism, family and minority-community memory, and state power have adapted. Give a chain of reasoning about mechanisms, incentives, and constraints rather than a narrative. For each major prediction, state your confidence and the assumption on which it most depends. Address at least these questions. Which silences in the record widen or narrow relative to curated appraisal, and which biases are eliminated rather than replaced by new ones? How do record creators, agencies, lawyers, and archivists change their behavior upstream once they know selection is content-blind — in what they create, how they label and organize records, and what they avoid putting in writing? Does the archive become more or less useful and trusted, and for whom? What would have to be true for this randomized archive to be more representative than a curated one, and what would make it worse?
