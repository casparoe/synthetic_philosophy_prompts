I've been teaching myself math on the side — mostly topology and abstract algebra, from old lecture notes and whatever I can find online — and I keep noticing the same thing, which I don't have a word for. Take groups. Before the modern axioms, people mostly worked with concrete things: permutations, substitutions, symmetries of a figure, collections of transformations closed under composition. Then at some point the field settled on the abstract defintion — a set with an associative operation, an identity, and inverses — which is much more general than anything anyone was originally looking at, and it lets in objects like the integers mod n under addition or the fundamental group of a circle, which don't really look like "symmetries" in the original sense. Same thing with open sets in topology. The definition (a collection of subsets closed under arbitrary unions and finite intersections) looks almost arbitrary if you come at it from the intuitive idea of "nearness" or "stretching without tearing," and there were rival ways to build the subject, through limit points or neighborhoods or distance functions. But the open set formulation seems to have won out because it makes the theorems come out clean — continuity, compactness, connectedness, quotient spaces, they all just work.

So here's what I'm trying to pin down. Is there an established name for this phenomenon: a mathematical community choosing a definition not because it best matches the pre-theoretic concept, but because it makes the downstream theory powerful and the theorems clean, even when the definition itself looks artificial at first? I know it's not exactly a stipulative definition, because mathematicians talk as if they got something right rather than just made a choice, and it's not just generalization for its own sake, because lots of possible generalizations get rejected. I've also seen the word explication thrown around for replacing a vague concept with a precise one, but that always sounds like the goal is to stay close to the original concept, whereas here the definition seems to be allowed to drift quite far from it. If there's no name, what should it be called, and how do you tell a definition chosen for this kind of fruitfulness from a merely conventional one?
