I have no formal background in philosophy; I am a working software engineer, and I do my clearest thinking in the vocabulary of version control and continuous delivery: feature branches, automated tests, merge gates, deployments, rollbacks, and incident reviews. Lately I have been trying on my own to understand the old dispute usually tagged as Clifford versus James, over whether a person is ever entitled to hold a belief on evidence short of proof. Clifford's side seems to say that accepting a claim without sufficient evidence is like merging a change without passing tests, because other people then build on a component that was never shown to be sound. James's side replies that some questions are live for the person facing them, forced on them, and momentous; and when evidence cannot settle such a question, the person's wants, hopes, and allegiances may, or even must, do the deciding.

I would like you to restate this debate in the software-delivery vocabulary I just described, position by position and element by element. For each element, tell me what carries over cleanly, what carries over only with distortion, and what has no expression in those terms at all. Then go back over the residue and say, point by point, whether each failure is a blind spot in the engineering vocabulary or a place where the philosophical argument was fuzzier than it first appeared. I am not looking for an introduction to the debate; I want to learn whether treating beliefs as systems-under-build is an honest translation or only a forced and flattering metaphor.
