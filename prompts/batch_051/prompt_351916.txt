I have been turning over a feature of criminal trials that first struck me while I was serving on a jury last spring. The prosecution had to prove the defendant's presence, state of mind, and particular actions on a given evening, and the defense spent hours attacking each witness's memory and visual perception. Yet no one in that courtroom was ever asked to establish, or even address, that the events in question belonged to a knowable past; that physical objects continue to exist when no one is observing them; that other people have inner lives to which speech can give access; or that human memory is by and large a channel of truth rather than invention. These things did not merely go unproven; the whole proceeding treated them as so far outside the contest that doubting them would have seemed unintelligible. Had a witness said he was not sure the past happened, or that he could not justify trusting his senses from one minute to the next, the court would have treated him as confused or obstructive rather than as raising a question the rules of evidence were equipped to try. The rest of the system seems to work similarly: a hearsay objection presupposes that out-of-court assertions mean something and can be more or less reliable, but the general reliability of testimony is never itself placed on the docket.

I have been told that this is connected to hinge epistemology and to the late notes of Wittgenstein published as On Certainty, where particular questions and doubts are said to presuppose a background of certainties that are not themselves up for question in that context. I want to know exactly what the courtroom practice commits us to. What theory of knowledge and justification does this practice presuppose? In particular, does the way courts handle the unproven background commit them to treating those certainties as hinge commitments — neither ordinary empirical beliefs nor ordinary pieces of evidence-based knowledge — or is the practice compatible with several different epistemologies once one looks closely? Please reconstruct the implicit epistemology, distinguishing what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say whether the different features of the practice point toward one coherent view. Then explain concretely what would have to change in courtroom procedure if we insisted that the background commitments be proven and weighed like any ordinary factual claim. Finally, indicate how confident you are in each main part of your reconstruction and single out the point you are least sure about.
