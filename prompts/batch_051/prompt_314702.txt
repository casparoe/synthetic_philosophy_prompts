Since retiring from the physiology department I have been reading moral philosophy in an unsystematic way, and I hope you will forgive me if I misname the positions.

The university dining hall I still frequent has begun printing beside each dish an estimate of its greenhouse-gas emissions per serving, in kilograms of carbon dioxide equivalent. Beef and lamb dishes score highest, pork and chicken moderately high, eggs and dairy lower, and pulses, grains, and root vegetables lowest. The dining service also advertises low-carbon menu days and has procurement targets that favor poultry over beef. Judged by radiative forcing alone, the ranking is unassailable.

But I cannot make that ranking cohere with something else I know. A given weight of chicken requires the slaughter of on the order of a hundred times more individual animals than the same weight of beef, since a steer dresses out to several hundred kilograms while a broiler dresses out to about two. The individual bird is small and short-lived, but it is sentient, and its suffering is not less because it is brief or hidden from view. Meanwhile, the plant-based dishes at the bottom of the scale depend on row crops harvested with combines and on fields managed with pesticides and poisoned baits; the field animals killed by those operations are equally absent from the label.

The institution therefore appears to run a decision procedure in which radiative forcing is quantified with great care while the deaths of individual sentient animals, farmed or wild, are entered as zero.

My question is this. What theory of value, or of obligation, does such a practice presuppose? Does it strictly presuppose that the lives and deaths of farmed animals do not count, or only that they are not being counted in that particular number? Is the carbon score merely a partial metric that several rival moral theories could adopt for different reasons, or does it quietly commit the institution to treating climate-related harms as the only relevant kind? Reconstruct the implicit theory as charitably as you can, distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely overlooks for practical reasons, and then describe what a dining-hall scoring system built explicitly around the welfare and lives of individual animals, including the wild animals killed in crop production, would have to look like and what it would change.
