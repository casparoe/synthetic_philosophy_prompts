I keep going back and forth on Strawson's essay Freedom and Resentment, and a friend who did a philosophy degree summarized it for me like this:

"Strawson's move is to say the whole free will debate is asking the wrong question. Pessimists ask whether our practices of praise and blame could be justified from some external, God's-eye standpoint. Strawson says those practices rest on the reactive attitudes — resentment, gratitude, indignation, and the rest — which are just built into human life. When someone deliberately stomps on your foot, you feel resentment whether or not you believe determinism; the response isn't waiting around for a metaphysics exam first. So the real issue isn't whether blame is justified, but whether we could ever actually adopt the objective stance toward everyone, the stance where you see people as objects to be managed rather than as agents to be held responsible. And Strawson thinks doing that globally is psychologically impossible. But if the practices are inescapable, then that settles it: determinism doesn't undermine responsibility. Critics say 'inescapable doesn't mean justified,' but that objection just sneaks back in the same external demand Strawson was rejecting, and Strawson's whole point is that the demand is illegitimate."

Something about the last step bugs me. It seems to slide from "we can't help feeling these things" to "these things are justified," and I'm not sure Strawson is entitled to that. But my friend is confident and says everyone who studied it reads it this way. Is this a fair summary? Go through it and tell me what's accurate, what's a simplification or overstatement, and whether the "that settles it" conclusion actually follows from what Strawson argues.
