I keep noticing a pattern in the popular Stoicism literature, and I have never found a label for it. The practice of separating what is under your control from what is not gets presented as a clean sorting task: list the circumstance, assign it to one column or the other. But in real cases the boundary keeps moving. The same fact — whether a marriage survives, whether a business succeeds — is classified as not up to me when the point is to stop tormenting myself over it, and as up to me when the point is to demand more effort or accept blame for the result. Control, which is supposed to be doing the work, is treated as an either-or property, even though most such outcomes are partly shaped by effort and partly hostage to other people and to chance. I am trying to identify the specific failure mode in which a partially controllable outcome gets rounded up to wholly controllable or rounded down to wholly uncontrollable, depending on whether the immediate aim is consolation or exhortation. This seems adjacent to the Stoic dichotomy of control, and to the modern writers who replace it with a third, in-between category, but I want to know whether the failure itself has an established name and how it differs from those nearby ideas. A bare label will not settle it; I need the edges of the phenomenon drawn, including how to tell when treating the distinction as binary is harmless rather than doing the shifting I have described.
