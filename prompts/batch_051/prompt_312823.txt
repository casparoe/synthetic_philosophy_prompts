I'm in a philosophy of law class and everyone keeps saying statutes of limitations are just about evidence decaying and witnesses forgetting. But I think there's a deeper argument that time matters morally, and I'd like to know if it works. Here it is:

(1) Punishing someone is only appropriate if it tracks the blameworthiness of the person actually being punished.
(2) Blameworthiness is fixed at the time of the wrongful act; later events can't increase or decrease it.
(3) But later events can change whether the person now in front of the court is the same person as the one who did the act.
(4) Over decades, psychological connectedness between the person then and the person now decays enough that they are not the same person in the sense needed for blame.
(5) So for sufficiently old crimes, punishment isn't just impractical; its unjust because it punishes someone who is no longer identical with the wrongdoer.

I half believe this and half think step (4) is doing something sneaky with 'same person'. I want a committed answer: is the argument valid, and if not, which exact step fails? If (4) is the problem, give the strongest reading of it you can and say whether the argument still goes through on that reading.
