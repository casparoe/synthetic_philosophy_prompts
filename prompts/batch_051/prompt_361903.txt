Write a dialogue of roughly 1,000 to 1,200 words between two characters, Corvin and Elara, soon after a mutual friend's funeral. Corvin is a modern Stoic; Elara defends a broadly Romantic view of the emotions. They disagree about whether grief is part of a life well lived or a disturbance the wise person should learn to dissolve.

Corvin should distinguish involuntary first movements—welling tears, a catch in the breath, a startle—from settled passions, which form only when one assents to the judgment that something bad has occurred. His claim is that the loss of a friend is not itself an evil, since only one's character and judgments are good or bad; one may feel the first movements without assenting to grief, and that refusal is clarity rather than coldness. Elara should reply that grief is not a judgment superimposed on a neutral event but a way of perceiving what matters: it registers that an irreplaceable person is gone and that one's own life carried part of its meaning through that person. To refuse assent to that perception, she argues, is to choose partial blindness over freedom.

The dialogue should show each responding to the other's actual arguments, conceding weak points and reformulating under pressure. It should explore three borderline cases: anger at a manifest injustice, the difference between being moved and being governed, and the claim that someone who never grieves has purchased tranquility at the price of self-knowledge. Near the end, the characters should try to state exactly where their disagreement lies: in how emotions work, or in what makes a human life good. Neither should be a strawman or win outright; end with each left thinking rather than converted. Do not cite external authors or texts.
