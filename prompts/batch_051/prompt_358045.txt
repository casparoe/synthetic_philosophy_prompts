I'm a novelist, six months into a book whose main character was born blind and gets her sight back in her late thirties after an operation, and I've realized I keep cheating the actual first-sight scene: either she immediately knows the window is a window, or I go vague and lyrical so I dont have to commit. I don't need a literature review on Molyneux's problem—I need a poem I can keep above my desk as a kind of structural model for the scene, something that forces me to hold the question in one shape.

So here's the commission: write a sestina about Molyneux's problem, with the six end-words touch, sight, cube, sphere, name, know. The form has to carry the argument—the same six words returning each stanza in a new order is exactly the question of whether knowledge survives the jump from touch to sight, whether the same thing can come back in a different arrangement without becoming a different thing. The three-line envoi should stage the moment of first vision, and it should not resolve the question; it can lean toward Locke's answer, that she will not know the shapes until she has handled them, or toward Leibniz's, that she can reason her way to which is which, but only by admitting what the other answer saw. Keep it accurate enough that someone who works on the problem would recognize it, including the modern wrinkle that real cases of restored sight have made both of the old answers look too clean. And it should read as a poem, not a versified argument—I want the panic and euphoria of that first sight in it, not just the proposition.
