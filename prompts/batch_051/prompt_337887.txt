My friend Amara has very firm views about what she'll count as funny, and I've been trying to reverse-engineer the rule. Her track record: she laughs at slapstick—a man in an expensive suit slips on ice outside a restaurant, windmills his arms, and lands in a snowbank with nothing hurt but his dignity. She loves puns, even really forced ones. The hardest she's ever laughed was at a tame, banal joke a pilot made right after a rough landing that turned out fine; she says it wasn't really the joke but the whiplash between how scared everyone had been and how bland the joke was. She hates cringe comedy where an ordinary person is just humiliated for the audience's enjoyment, and she doesn't find comics whose whole act is shouting slurs with no actual joke funny either, no matter how much tension it releases. But she loves dry absurdist non-sequiturs—like a comic walking on stage, saying 'the chairs in this room are extremely Wednesday', and walking off.

Superiority theories seem out because she hates mean-spirited stuff, and relief seemed promising after the pilot story but the slurs thing seems to kill relief as her actual rule. So I've narrowed it to two. Either she's a benign-violation person: something's funny when it violates a norm (physical, social, linguistic) but is safe. Or she's a benign-incongruity person: there has to be a violated expectation plus a safe frame—mere safe norm-breaking isn't enough. The two fit her track record about equally well.

So what do you think she'd say about these? (a) She agrees to be tickled for some stupid experiment, knows exactly when and where it's coming, and still laughs helplessly—does she call that funny? (b) A close-up magic trick where a chosen card turns up inside a sealed orange, completely surprising and delightful but nothing goes wrong and no norm gets broken. (c) A long-running dinner tradition where everyone solemnly inserts the same predictably rude word into the toast every year, so nobody is surprised anymore but the formality still gets broken each time. I'm less interested in the bare yes/no answers than in the rule she's actually running, which other rules fit her record just as well, which of (a)–(c) would best tell the rival rules apart, and what one further case would settle it if none of these does.
