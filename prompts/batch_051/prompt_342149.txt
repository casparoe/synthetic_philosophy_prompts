Here is an argument about constitutional precommitment — the practice of entrenching rights, amendment procedures, and other basic rules so that a current ordinary majority cannot simply revise them, on the model of Ulysses having himself bound to the mast so he could hear the Sirens without steering his ship onto the rocks.

(1) A political community may legitimately be governed only by its present citizens, acting through procedures they can regard as their own. (2) A sovereign may legitimately bind itself, as Ulysses did, but it may not legitimately bind a different sovereign against that sovereign's will. (3) When an old constitutional provision is enforced against present citizens who wish to change it, this must be either a case of the sovereign binding itself or a case of one sovereign binding another. (4) The present body of citizens is not the same sovereign as the body that enacted the provision: the enactors are mostly dead, and the two groups do not share an identity. (5) Therefore, enforcing the provision against present citizens is a case of one sovereign binding another. (6) Therefore, such enforcement is illegitimate, and each generation must be free to remake its fundamental law.

I find (1) and (2) almost irresistible, but (6) seems to dissolve every stable constitutional order. Is the argument valid? If it is valid, which premise must a constitutionalist reject, and what is the most defensible replacement for it? If it is not valid, identify the exact step at which the inference fails.
