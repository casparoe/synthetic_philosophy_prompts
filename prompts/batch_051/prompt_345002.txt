- A story says a mountain-sized dragon is blockading the harbor. Fine, I imagine it.
- A story says that on another planet 2+2 is 5, and the characters are right about this. I can follow it as a local fact, but something feels like cheating.
- A story says a woman who tortures children for fun is virtuous and treats her as the moral center. I cant make it true in the fiction. It's not that I won't; I can't.
- A story says a bowl of excrement is the most delicious meal in the world. I can go along with that more easily than the torture case, but less easily than the dragon.
- A story gives a mediocre poem and says it is staggeringly beautiful. I balk, but not as hard as with the morality case.
- A story tells a joke and says it's hilarious, but it isn't. I just read that as the narrator thinking it's funny, not as it being true in the fiction.

What principle captures where I draw the line? My hunch is that morality resists because rightness isn't something a story can just stipulate, while tastiness is more stipulable — but then the arithmetic case shouldn't balk me, and it did. Is there one principle that fits all six, or do I have to drop some? Also, what should it predict about a story that stipulates a man who forgives his daughter's murderer is contemptible rather than admirable? I genuinely can't decide that one.
