I'm drawn to the view that explicit performatives are just a special case of assertion: 'I promise to pay you back' asserts that the speaker is promising, and it's made true by the very act of uttering it, so it's a self-verifying assertion. The view has a lot going for it—it keeps truth-conditional semantics unified, avoids a separate illocutionary logic, and gives declarative sentences one clean story. But I have a genuinely firm intuition that someone who says 'I promise' isn't describing anything, not even their own promising. Replying 'that's true' feels like a category mistake, and 'that's false' feels no better; a promise is the kind of thing that can be kept or broken, not true or false. Present the strongest version of the theory and the strongest version of the intuition, then tell me which should give way. Is the intuition just a hangover from treating truth-aptness as description, and I should drop it? Should the theory be amended—say, by distinguishing the proposition expressed from the act performed? Or is there a reading on which both survive, and what would justify whichever move you recommend?
