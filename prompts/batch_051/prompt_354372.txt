Here's an asymmetry I can't stop turning over.

A man in a small motorboat sees a lone swimmer drowning a few hundred metres off. The water's calm, he has room and fuel to spare, and the swimmer is the only other soul for miles. He throttles away. Ask anyone: the man is contemptible, and if the swimmer dies he may even be criminally liable.

A trawler in a rising sea is already loaded until the rail sits low in the water. It comes on a raft of refugees taking on water. The skipper does the arithmetic: taking even three aboard might be fine, or it might swamp the whole vessel in the next squall and kill the thirty already aboard. He turns away. Ask the same people: most will say it's a tragedy but he did nothing wrong—maybe even that he *should* turn away, out of duty to the people already on board.

What bothers me is that both are cases where someone declines to rescue because rescuing carries risk to himself and to people in his care. The difference is one of degree, not kind: spare capacity, the size of the risk, how many are already aboard. So is there actually a principle that cleanly splits the two—something about a captain's prior commitment to his passengers, or about preserving a going concern, or about the difference between risking what you already have and failing to gain a good? Or is the line just drawn by salience and numbers, so the motorboat man gets blamed only because his refusal had no numbers to hide behind?

I want the strongest principle you can build, and then I want you to stress-test it against this fix: the trawler could take two of them with no more than a five percent chance of swamping; the two are healthy adults; the sea is getting worse. Does your principle still let the skipper turn away? If yes, do we have to stop condemning the motorboat man whenever taking the swimmer aboard also carried some small nonzero risk? If no, where exactly does the principle give out?
