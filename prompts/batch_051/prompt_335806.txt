I've been trying to sort out how much of my disgust and reverence reactions are actually moral, and I keep finding contradictions in my verdicts. I'm hoping you can extract a principle from them, or tell me which ones I have to give up. Cases:

(1) A man buys a cheap flag of his own country, burns it alone in his backyard as a private protest, and tells no one. Verdict: not morally wrong. Childish maybe, and I'd be upset if he did it in front of a grieving veteran, but the private act itself is fine.

(2) A lapsed Catholic is given a consecrated host by a priest friend who doesn't ask what it's for. At home he burns it and films it for a private archive no one else ever sees. Verdict: this feels genuinely wrong, even though he no longer believes it's sacred, no one is harmed, and nothing was stolen.

(3) A family's dog is hit by a car, and on a dare they butcher and eat it for dinner. Verdict: repulsive, but I can't make myself say it's morally wrong.

(4) A pathologist keeps the prepared skeleton of a human hand from a donated teaching cadaver as a paperweight on her desk. Verdict: wrong. A kind of disrespect to the dead, even if the donor consented to scientific use and no grieving family member knows.

(5) An artist displays a torn, spat-on page of a sacred text in a gallery, with clear signage, and visitors freely choose to attend. Verdict: within his rights, and I don't think it's wrong, though I wouldn't do it and I'd feel angry if it were a text sacred to me.

The tension is that (2) and (5) look structurally similar—symbolic desecration of a sacred object, no coerced audience—but I come down opposite ways on them. And (2) is also hard to square with (1), since I treat a flag as mere cloth but a host as something that can be wronged. My working principle has been something like: disgust and awe are feelings, not moral reasons by themselves; only harm, consent, and maybe some notion of respecting persons count. But that principle seems to force me to say (2) and (4) are at most poor taste, which doesn't fit how I actually judge them.

Can you help me find a principle—or a small set of candidate principles—that captures these verdicts? Please state it precisely, say what it predicts about cases I haven't considered (an undertaker who playfully poses a corpse at a private gathering before burial; a protester burning a stranger's wedding photos; someone eating a human placenta), and be honest about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate.
