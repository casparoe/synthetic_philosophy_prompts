I keep going back and forth on this version of Pascal's mugging, because step 4 looks unavoidable when I read it quickly but obviously something is off. Here's the argument:

(1) A rational agent should choose the act that maximizes expected utility relative to her own credences.
(2) For any finite amount a stranger demands, there is a nonzero chance that the stranger is exactly what he says he is: a being who will deliver whatever arbitrarily large finite payoff (in utils) he promises if you pay.
(3) So for any fixed nonzero credence you have in the stranger's claim, the promised payoff can be set high enough that the expected utility of paying exceeds the expected utility of refusing.
(4) Therefore a rational agent should pay any stranger who makes a finite demand and promises a sufficiently large finite payoff.
(5) But that can't be right: it would make every arbitrary demand rationally compelling, and you can run the same argument in reverse with threats instead of payoffs.

Is this argument valid? If not, which step fails and what's the correct version of it? I half-suspect step 2 — just because something is possible doesn't mean your credence in it can be whatever the mugger wants — but I can't make that objection precise. And if you think the argument is valid, tell me which premise you'd reject instead and how you'd live with step 5.
