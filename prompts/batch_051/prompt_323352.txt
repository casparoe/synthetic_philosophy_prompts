For a long time I have been keeping a list of verdicts about how online reputation and karma systems should work, and I want to know whether there is a single principle behind them. I half-believe the principle is something like: a karma or reputation score should be an estimate of the user's expected future contribution quality in the specific context where the score is displayed. But I can already see cases where that does not obviously fit, and I want you to tell me what the principle really is, or which of my verdicts I should abandon.

Here are the cases.

A user with two years of consistently helpful, civil posts on a technical forum replies abusively once, in a thread where someone has been baiting them, and an automated system bans them and zeroes their score. I think that response is wrong: the same comment from a day-old account should be met with a quick ban, but a long record of good contributions should cushion one bad moment.

A user with an enormous karma score amassed by posting popular jokes in a meme community starts giving confident answers in a medical-advice forum, and the platform displays their global score alongside the answers, so readers defer to them. I think that is wrong: reputation should not travel unchanged across contexts.

A once-excellent contributor has been posting mediocre, sloppy material for the past year, but still appears near the top of the 'top contributors' list because the ranking uses a lifetime total. I think that ranking is wrong: recent performance should dominate.

A user who was chronically toxic two years ago now posts measured, genuinely useful comments, but the platform still flags all their posts for moderator review and shows their old infractions next to their name. I think that is over-punishing: reform should be able to earn back standing, and old negative records should not be permanent.

A user banned for harassment returns under a new account and posts politely. The platform treats them as a new user. I think that is wrong: evading a ban should not reset one's reputation or grant a clean slate.

Ten accounts coordinate to downvote a user's posts from the first minutes. The targeted posts are mediocre, and an ordinary readership might well have downvoted them to a similar score. I still think the coordination itself deserves a penalty, while the same score produced organically would be unobjectionable.

A marketplace seller with a perfect 5.0-average rating from twelve reviews is ranked above another seller with a 4.9-average from seven hundred reviews. I think that ranking is wrong: the second seller is more reliably good.

I want to know whether there is one coherent account of what a karma or reputation score is for—one statement of what it should track, how it should be weighted over time and across contexts, and how it should handle uncertainty and manipulation—that delivers all or nearly all of these verdicts. Please state the principle precisely, show which case motivates each clause, give its verdicts on one or two cases I have not listed, and say honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and whether those verdicts should be revised.
