I am a PhD student in moral philosophy writing a paper on whether the doing/allowing distinction and the killing/letting-die distinction can carry the normative weight usually assigned to them once we move from one-off personal cases to standing institutional arrangements in which a harm is known to all, benefits many, and could in principle be refused. I want a section of the paper to be a critical reading of Ursula K. Le Guin's short story "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" through that lens, and I would like you to write the reading itself. Please do not write a plot summary, and do not take the familiar introductory-ethics line that the story is simply a counterexample to utilitarianism; my interest is specifically in what the story does to the doing/allowing and killing/letting-die distinctions. Begin the response with the piece itself, with no preface and no restatement of the question, and make the opening sentence the central interpretive claim you will defend. Work from the story's stipulated givens: the city's flourishing depends on the uninterrupted, degrading, known misery of a single child; the arrangement is public; most people accept it, and those who cannot accept it walk away; the choice is presented as all-or-nothing. Develop the reading in both directions. Press the philosophical material on the story—for example, the Doctrine of Doing and Allowing, the idea that producing harm is harder to justify than letting it continue, and the familiar contrast cases from Foot and Rachels—and press the story against that material by asking whether the citizens of Omelas merely allow the child's suffering or sustain it, whether knowingly benefiting from and having the standing ability to stop a harm makes "allowing" morally indistinguishable from "doing," and whether walking away is best understood as a principled response for which the distinction has no category or as a second way of doing nothing. End by saying what Le Guin's story supplies to the philosophical debate that the standard trolley and pond cases do not. No citations are needed; I will add them.
