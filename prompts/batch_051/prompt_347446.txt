Let me state the framework so we are talking about the same thing. Accuracy-first epistemology grades doxastic states—graded credences, and sometimes full beliefs—by their accuracy at a world, as measured by a scoring rule; a scoring rule is strictly proper when every probabilistic credence function maximizes expected score by its own lights. The flagship results try to show that incoherent credence functions are accuracy-dominated (some alternative state is at least as accurate at every world and more accurate at some), and that conditionalization is the update plan that maximizes expected accuracy. I accept that this is the project's shape; I want to know where it is most worth pushing or testing.

Here is my working menu of open threads; you may add at most two of your own.

1. Scoring-rule underdetermination. There are infinitely many strictly proper scoring rules (Brier, log, spherical, and others), and they are not equivalent—two forecasters can be ordered one way by the Brier score and the reverse by the log score on the same resolved events. If accuracy is the fundamental epistemic value, it cannot be a different ordering for each rule. So something has to give: one rule is canonical, or accuracy is not one thing, or the scoring-rule framing is losing information.

2. Infinite and unbounded cases. The dominance and propriety arguments are cleanest over finite partitions. Once the possibility space is infinite or the score is unbounded, the standard dominance theorems need extra assumptions and expected-accuracy computations may not be well defined. It is unclear how much of the program carries over to that setting, and with what changes.

3. Full beliefs and suspended judgment. Accuracy-first arguments for probabilism concern graded credences. There is no consensus scoring rule for flat-out belief, for withholding, or for how the accuracy of a full belief should combine with the accuracy of one's credences in cases like believing p while assigning p only moderate credence.

4. The conditionalization argument's own basis. The leading accuracy-based derivation of conditionalization argues that a prior which plans to update in some other way expects to be less accurate than one which plans to conditionalize. The worry is that this treats the prior's perspective as authoritative for grading later selves, which may already smuggle in the diachronic norm it is meant to derive.

5. Chance-credence principles. Attempts to derive the Principal Principle—that one's credence in A, conditional on the objective chance of A being x, should be x—from accuracy considerations usually appeal to calibration or treat chance as an expert one would do best to match. It is contested whether that appeal presupposes the very chance-credence norm it is supposed to justify.
