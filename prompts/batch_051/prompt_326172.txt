In my reporting on genetics, I have noticed how quickly a technical definition gives way to a picture. Scientists will define heritability as the proportion of variation in a population that is associated with genetic differences. Within a few paragraphs, though — sometimes from the same source — the same figure is described as if it divided the trait itself: a heritability of 0.8 for height or intelligence is glossed as meaning that 80 per cent of the trait is genetic and 20 per cent environmental, with the genome playing the role of a blueprint or recipe that fixes most of the outcome and the environment contributing only the remainder not already specified. The conclusion this picture is used to license is usually practical: if a trait is highly heritable, environmental interventions, from enrichment programmes to health policy, can at best tinker with a small leftover, because the bulk of the trait is already written in.

I do not want a dismissal of heredity research. I want an audit of this blueprint-and-leftover analogy and of the inference it carries. Please build the correspondence table first: what is being mapped to the blueprint or recipe, to the builder or cook, to the materials, to the unspecified remainder, and to the finished product. Then identify where the mapping breaks. In particular, I want to know which of these imported assumptions are licensed and which are not: that the genetic and environmental contributions are additive parts of one whole; that the division applies to an individual person rather than to variation in a population; that the genetic share is fixed before any environment appears; that the ratio remains stable across different environments and different groups; and that the size of the environmental share tells us how much a change of environment would move the trait. Then give the verdict: does the conclusion that high heritability implies low environmental malleability survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or is it being carried silently by the analogy? If the analogy fails for this use, say whether a better analogy exists, or whether the honest position is to drop analogies here altogether.

Keep the answer to a hard maximum of 750 words, and write it in British English.
