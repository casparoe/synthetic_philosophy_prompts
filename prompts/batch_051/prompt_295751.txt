I'm on the programming committee for a small independent cinema in a college town, and the committee is deadlocked over the April slate. The programmer's proposal is a ten-film series called "The Uses of Sorrow": mostly tragedies and bleak documentaries (a film about ALS, an adaptation of a Greek tragedy, a Holocaust documentary, a slow drama about a custody battle, and so on). A block of committee members wants to cut it to two or three films. Their stated worry is partly financial, but their real argument is that it's almost perverse for a community cinema to devote a whole season to making people sad, and that our mission — the bylaws say we exist to "enrich the cultural and emotional life of the community" — is better served by work that leaves people more energized than deflated. The programmer responds that people have deliberately sought out tragedy for millennia, and that if we only program feel-good work we're treating adults like children.

The chair has asked me to draft a short written opinion for Thursday's full-committee meeting. It should recommend a concrete decision: roughly how many of the films to keep in the season, and whether to pair the series with introductions or moderated post-screening discussions. It should also give us a principle we can apply to future programming fights. I'm expected to engage the strongest version of the anti-tragedy objection rather than just calling it philistinism, and to say something substantive about why (or whether) deliberately sad art can be worth seeking even when it doesn't entertain in any ordinary sense. A couple of people on the committee keep throwing around phrases like "the paradox of tragedy" and "safe sadness" without defining them, so please either avoid that kind of jargon or actually unpack what it would mean for our decison.
