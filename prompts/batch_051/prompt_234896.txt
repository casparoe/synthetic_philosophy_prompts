I have been working out a decision rule for acting under moral uncertainty which I call the Regret-Parity Principle. Suppose an agent has nonzero credence in several moral theories and must choose among a finite set of options. For each option, define its regret under a given theory as the loss in choiceworthiness relative to what that theory regards as the best available option. The rule: choose the option whose maximum regret across the live theories is smallest; where two or more options tie, choose among them by minimizing the credence-weighted sum of their regrets. I am drawn to it because its primary ranking requires only each theory's internal comparisons of value, not any cross-theory comparison of value-differences, and because it seems to blunt fanaticism: a theory with tiny credence but enormous preferred sacrifices cannot, by itself, dictate the decision the way it can under an expected-choiceworthiness rule. Is this a coherent procedure for moral uncertainty? Has anything like it been proposed, and under what name? Construct the strongest counterexample you can in which the principle delivers an intuitively wrong verdict, and say whether the failure is fatal or merely a cost to be accepted.
