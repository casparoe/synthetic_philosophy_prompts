I chair the Academic Integrity Appeals Board at my university, and I need to circulate a short written opinion before our Thursday meeting on a case that has split the board. The appellant is a sophomore with no prior infractions. In the final section of a history paper, she reproduced the structure and several sustained passages of an article she had downloaded for background research, with wording altered but no citation. She does not dispute the close similarity. Her account is that she pasted passages from the article into her working notes without recording their source, then later condensed and rewrote those notes in the belief that she had put the material into her own words; the earlier sections of the paper are properly cited. The hearing officer, following the professor's recommendation, imposed a failing grade in the course and a permanent academic-integrity notation on her transcript. The relevant provision of our code treats improper attribution as an infraction regardless of the student's intent, and past panels have treated the absence of intent as relevant only to the sanction, not to the finding.

The board is divided in a way that mirrors a deeper disagreement about whether our system may punish without fault. Some members read the policy as settled: the uncredited use is the violation, and allowing an honest-confusion defense would make the rule unenforceable, since every student accused afterward would plead a broken note-taking process. Others maintain that a sanction is a form of blame, and blame without any culpable mental state is unfair and pointless; on their view, the board should use such cases to teach rather than to stigmatize. One member has called the provision a strict-liability offense in everything but name. I would like the memo to take a clear position on whether the finding, the original sanction, or both should stand, and to specify a concrete alternative with a timeline if you would reduce the sanction (for example: a required citation workshop within thirty days, the paper revised for no credit, a transcript notation that expires after one year, or removal of the notation). The memo must engage the strongest consideration on the side we ultimately reject, rather than simply restating the code.
