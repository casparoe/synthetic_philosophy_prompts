I am taking a course on pacifism and the ethics of war, and I keep coming back to a question about our campus pacifist society’s covenant. The society requires members to affirm the following clause: “No member shall knowingly participate in, or lend material assistance to, any activity whose intended purpose is the destruction of human life, whether directly or indirectly.” This clause was written in the 1970s to govern cases like members enlisting, working for arms manufacturers, or serving as military chaplains. It was not written with current graduate research in mind.

Here is the case I want to test. A member of the society is a doctoral student in applied mathematics. She contributes code to a large, open-source library for nonlinear optimization algorithms. The library is maintained by a university consortium and funded by a civilian science foundation. Her contribution is not classified, and she does not work on weapons systems. However, she knows that the library is widely used by defense contractors, including for trajectory optimization in long-range missile guidance. She does not intend to destroy life, but she knows that her module will likely be incorporated into applications whose intended purpose is not the destruction of human life, though it may later be used to make such destruction more accurate. The society’s drafters plainly did not foresee open-source software with no single intended purpose.

My question is not about the practical disciplinary outcome. I want to know what the clause actually requires if interpreted rigorously. Would this member be in breach? Which interpretive approaches—plain words, original purpose, what the drafters would have said if asked, reasonable reader—plausibly reach her or fail to reach her? And does any coherent reading of the clause draw a principled line between her contribution and working for an arms manufacturer, or does the distinction collapse?
