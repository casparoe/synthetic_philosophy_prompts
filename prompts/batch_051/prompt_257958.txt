An inevitabilist about scientific discovery and a "priority contingentist" have been arguing for years about whether the Newton–Leibniz and Darwin–Wallace cases show that big discoveries are basically inevitable. They've agreed to do an adversarial collaboration, and I want you to draft the document they'd produce.

The inevitabilist's line: the multiples show the field was ready, and if either Newton or Leibniz (or either Darwin or Wallace) had died young, the result would still have arrived in roughly the same decade; the great-person story is hindsight. The priority contingentist's line: calling each pair "the same discovery" is already question-begging. The actual papers differ in formulation, scope, what they treat as an anomaly, and what they make salient, and those differences could have changed downstream science—so the individuals matter more than the inevitabilist admits.

Requirements for the document:

(1) A numbered section of claims both can sign. Don't make them bland; include substantive claims about the two historical cases and about what a genuine multiple discovery would show if it were real.

(2) A table or table-like section with the remaining disagreements, each stated so that its own side would accept the wording. If you think part of the dispute is just the phrase "same discovery," say which part and propose a definitional fix—maybe a discovery-type/discovery-token distinction—that both could accept and show what it dissolves.

(3) A crux list. For each crux, give at least one concrete test, archival source, or comparison both agree would bear on it, and write down in advance what each side predicts.

To keep them from rerunning their usual examples, include at least two cruxes built from new material. One should be a sealed-research-plans design: suppose a foundation had made every working physicist, in 1903 and again in 1963, file confidential 12-month research plans. What pattern in the sealed plans would each side count as support, and what pattern would each count as support for the other side? The other crux should be a genuinely fine-grained comparison of Darwin's and Wallace's versions of natural selection—not just "they both had natural selection," but scope, the role of artificial selection, what each treated as an anomaly, and how much evidential burden each took on. Spell out what each side predicts a close reading will find.
