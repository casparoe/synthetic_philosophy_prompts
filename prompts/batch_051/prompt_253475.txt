A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether a rational person's credences must defer to objective chance, and I came away unsettled — not because I now think I was wrong, but because I could not say what would show that I was wrong. I was defending a version of David Lewis's Principal Principle: if you are rationally certain that the objective chance of some event at a given time and world is x, and the rest of your evidence is 'admissible' — roughly, evidence whose bearing on the event is already fully captured by what the chance tells you — then your credence in that event should be x. Chance, on this view, is not just a useful summary of frequencies or a guide that can be outweighed by other information; it is normatively authoritative for credence. My friend's charge was that the admissibility clause is doing all the work: any case that seems to refute the principle gets its inconvenient evidence reclassified as inadmissible, so the principle survives only by being emptied of content. The argument stalled because neither of us could specify what kind of case or fact would count decisively against the principle.

I want a committed map of the Principal Principle's exposure. What would have to be true for it to be false, or for it to collapse into an unfalsifiable stipulation? Distinguish results that would be decisive from results that would merely force a local repair. Attend to the standard hard cases — undermining futures (future histories whose obtaining would imply that the chance-facts one conditionalized on were not the real chance-facts), crystal-ball evidence (perfect information about the future outcome), and evidence about the chance-making facts themselves — and say whether the usual treatment of each is a principled constraint on 'admissibility' or an exercise in insulation. Defenders of the principle themselves concede that it has been revised in response to puzzles; are those revisions signs of a healthy research program or of a position that cannot be wrong? Be willing to conclude that the principle is well insulated and that this is, or is not, a problem. The objection I would personally find most persuasive is one where the principle plainly recommends a credence that violates a better-established epistemic norm, rather than an exotic puzzle that turns on a contested reading of 'chance.' I am not after a survey of the literature; I want your own definite verdict, with reasons, about where the principle genuinely risks being false and where it is safe.
