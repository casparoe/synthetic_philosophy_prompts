For a feature I'm writing on how we decide what counts as life, I'm cataloguing the borderline cases that philosophers and biologists keep returning to. So far my list has:

- Viruses, which cannot reproduce or run metabolism outside a host cell;
- Prions, which replicate by triggering other proteins to misfold but carry no nucleic acid;
- Fire, which consumes energy, grows, and spreads but has no heredity;
- Crystals, which grow and copy their structure when seeded but neither metabolize nor evolve;
- Mules, which are plainly alive but cannot reproduce;
- Dormant systems such as bacterial endospores and tardigrades in cryptobiosis, which sit at the edge between metabolism and suspended animation.

I would like one more borderline case that is not already implicit in those six. In particular, I do not want another example of a system that merely lacks one or two standard criteria, such as reproduction, metabolism, evolution, or boundedness, and I do not want another dormant-or-revivable case. I want a case that puts pressure on how we individuate living things: a system that can reasonably be described both as one living thing and as many living things, or as a whole that is alive and parts that are also alive in their own right. Describe that case and what it adds to the debate.
