I'm a high school student, and I help run my school's technology and ethics club. We're planning a debate night around a question that has been bothering me: as AI systems become more capable, will we be able to know what they're actually doing, or will they become opaque to us precisely as their decisions start to matter most? One camp we've been reading about argues that the only realistic path to transparency is to have AI systems interpret other AI systems. A more capable model could examine the internal computations of a weaker model and produce a faithful account of how it reaches decisions, much as a person might use instruments to study something too complex to take in at once; and if that interpreting model is itself opaque, we could train a still more capable, or more narrowly specialized, system to interpret it in turn. The other camp argues that recursive interpretability of this sort merely moves the problem up a level. If the interpreter is more capable than the system it explains, then we have no independent way to check whether its explanation is right; if it is not more capable, then it may be no more trustworthy than the original system. They also worry that a model asked to explain itself may produce confident, fluent-sounding but false rationalizations, and that small errors could compound at each step of the recursion.

Please write the dialogue itself, set at a poster session after one of the characters has just presented work on using a large language model to explain a smaller model that decides which job applicants should advance to an interview. Make the two speakers a machine-learning researcher who defends recursive interpretability and a researcher who thinks it runs into principled limits. Both should be equally competent and should respond directly to each other's arguments rather than talking past each other.
