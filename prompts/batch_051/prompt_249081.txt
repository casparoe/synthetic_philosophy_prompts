In debates about whether governments and foundations should spend public money to preserve endangered languages and vanishing crafts, I keep meeting the claim that a language or a traditional art form dying out is like a species going extinct. The argument then runs that since most people already accept that biodiversity deserves protection even when no concrete human benefit can be demonstrated, consistency requires treating the loss of a language, a musical tradition, or a craft technique the same way—as something bad in itself, beyond what it means to the current speakers or practitioners. I find this persuasive, but I cannot tell how much of the conclusion is genuinely supported and how much is just the analogy doing emotional work. I would like an audit of how far the analogy holds: first, a table of correspondences, mapping what plays the role of the species, the habitat, the gene pool, the invasive competitor, and the captive breeding program; second, the places where the mapping breaks, with special attention to whether languages and art forms persist and reproduce in the way biological species do; and third, whether the quiet assumption that the whole has a value over and above its individual members—which makes species extinction matter in the ecological case—carries over to culture at all. Does the conclusion that cultural forms deserve preservation for their own sake survive once the disanalogies are made explicit, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time?
