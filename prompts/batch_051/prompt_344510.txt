I keep chewing on an analogy between two things that probably never get talked about together, and I can't tell if it's deep or if its just the word 'condition' doing all the work. On one side there's Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals. The way I understand them, they say you can't equate the probability of an indicative conditional, P(if A then C), with the conditional probability P(C|A), while also treating the conditional as a normal proposition that lives in the same algebra as A and C: if you insist on the equation for every pair of propositions, the probability function degenerates into something trivial. On the other side there's the Lucas critique in macroeconomics (and the do-calculus way of making it precise): the expected outcome under a policy counterfactual, like 'if we adopt policy X, output will be Y,' is not the same as the historical conditional expectation E[Y | X], because adopting the policy changes the process that generated the data. Both seem to be pointing at the same moral: a conditional is not a conditional distribution, and collapsing the former into the latter breaks something.

So here's what I want to know. Is the gap between P(if A then C) and P(C|A) the same kind of gap as the one between E[Y | do(X=x)] and E[Y | X=x]? Or is Lewis's result a purely probabilistic obstruction — about whether a conditional can be a proposition in a Boolean algebra at all — while the Lucas/Pearl point is about mechanisms, invariance, and interventions, so the two are only superficially similar? If they are the same gap, what would have to be true on both sides for the results to transfer, and does Lewis's triviality become a special case of the causal point? If they're not the same, what does the analogy wrongly predict? For instance, it seems like the analogy would predict that once you introduce a causal graph you could finally represent 'if A then C' as a proposition and get probabilities for it — but I thought Lewis's result says even that won't save you, or maybe I'm mixing up indicative and counterfactual conditionals there.
