I’m a student currently taking a course on physicalism and the mind, and a passage from Carl Hempel has been giving me trouble. In his article “Comments on Goodman’s Ways of Worldmaking” (Synthese, 1980), Hempel writes:

“The thesis of physicalism would seem to require a language in which a true theory of all physical phenomena can be formulated. But it is quite unclear what is to be understood here by a physical phenomenon, especially in the context of a doctrine that has taken a determinedly linguistic turn.”

I understand the surface structure of the dilemma: if “physical” is defined by reference to current physics, physicalism looks likely false, since current physics does not yet account for consciousness; if it is defined by reference to some future or ideal physics, the thesis seems to lose content, because we no longer know what future physics will count as physical. But I want to press on something. Is the dilemma itself partly an artifact of the linguistic turn Hempel mentions? In other words, does the problem arise mainly because physicalism was formulated as a claim about what a language can express, and would it disappear if we abandoned that linguistic formulation for a purely metaphysical one? Or is that too quick, because the metaphysical formulation simply inherits the same problem at the level of properties or objects rather than predicates? I would appreciate an explanation that makes the dilemma’s deep force clear, shows why it is difficult for a physicalist to define “physical” in a way that is neither too restrictive nor too permissive, and evaluates whether attempts to escape the dilemma by saying that physics just is whatever a completed scientific account of the world requires are genuinely responsive or merely relabeling. Please work through the conceptual structure in detail rather than giving a survey answer.
