I am trying to pin down what makes a color experience genuinely novel — a new kind of color quale, not merely a color that is new to a subject, not an unfamiliar shade within a familiar hue dimension, and not a finer gradation of a known hue. Please propose a working definition of a genuinely novel color quale and test it against the cases below. I am not asking for a literature survey or for quotations from sources; I want your own definition defended against the cases.

Here are my pre-theoretical verdicts on eight cases.

1. Alia, who has normal trichromatic color vision, notices for the first time a particular shade of teal she has never attended to before. My verdict: not novel in the relevant sense.

2. Joseph has red-green color blindness and, as an adult, receives a gene therapy that gives him ordinary trichromatic vision; he can now distinguish red from green for the first time. My verdict: he has acquired a color dimension that most people already have, so this is new only to him, not a new kind of color.

3. Priya, a tetrachromat with a fourth cone type, can reliably distinguish two acrylic paints that everyone else calls the same green, and she paints with them as two different colors. I lean toward saying she merely has more fine-grained familiar colors, not a new color dimension, though I cannot see what she sees.

4. Theo has sound-color synesthesia: every time he hears middle C he has a vivid experience of a specific burnt orange. My verdict: clearly not a new color; it is a familiar color with an unusual trigger.

5. After viewing a red-green grating under conditions that keep the boundary between the two colors fixed on her retina, Naomi reports a uniform color she calls reddish-green; she says it does not look like any red-green mixture she has ever seen. This is the sort of report people describe as a forbidden color. I do not know what to say about this case; it seems like a candidate for a genuinely new experience, but it may just be two familiar qualia co-occurring in an unfamiliar way.

6. Eve has a bidirectional brain-computer interface that adds a third, artificial color-opponent channel — an axis of hue variation beyond the ordinary red-green and blue-yellow axes — to her visual system. She learns to use it to sort surfaces that are visually identical to normal trichromats, reliably recognizes the same new color on later occasions, and reports that it appears to her as a distinct basic hue, not as a blend of any familiar colors. I want the definition to count this case as genuinely novel.

7. Marcus wears a head-mounted sensor that converts near-infrared intensity into a color-coded overlay in his visual field, and he learns to call its strongest signal infra-gold. My verdict: not a new color; he is reusing familiar gold or amber to track infrared information.

8. Olivia wears goggles that swap her red and green channels; after adapting, grass looks red to her. My verdict: clearly not novel; a familiar quale has been reassigned to a new object.

Please proceed as follows. State the proposed definition. Run it through all eight cases and say where it delivers the right verdict, where it delivers the wrong verdict, and where it gives no determinate verdict; then say whether the definition or one of my verdicts should be revised. Apply the resulting definition to this ninth case, on which I have not made up my mind:

9. Noor is born with a fourth cone type, but the fourth cone's signal is wired only into the existing red-green channel, so she can make some extra discriminations while reporting no new color category.

Finally, identify which feature of the successful definition does the most work in separating case 6, the artificial third opponent channel, from cases 3, 5, and 7. Please write the answer in American English.
