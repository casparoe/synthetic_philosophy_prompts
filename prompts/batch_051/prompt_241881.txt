At a dinner this past weekend, two friends argued about a third friend's announcement that she was replacing beef with chicken in her diet "for ethical reasons." I was asked to referee and couldn't, so I am writing the exchange from memory as faithfully as I can. I would like a point-by-point adjudication rather than a general verdict.

(1) Claire said the change made sense because beef is, by any estimate, far worse for the climate per serving than chicken, and climate is an ethical issue. Nate replied that the climate point is right, but if the welfare of animals counts at all, the swap looks like a step backwards: a single steer yields roughly a hundred times as much meat as a broiler chicken, so replacing beef with chicken means killing on the order of a hundred times as many individual animals for the same amount of meat, and broiler chickens are raised in confinement at least as bad as what feedlot cattle endure.

(2) Claire answered that Nate had already conceded the reason she actually gave — the climate — and that the animal-count objection relies on treating all animals as interchangeable units. A cow's capacity to suffer, she said, plausibly matters far more than a chicken's, so "one cow versus a hundred chickens" is not settled arithmetic. You cannot conclude the swap is worse overall until you say how much a cow's suffering is worth relative to a chicken's.

(3) Nate came back that once you allow differences in moral weight, you must also allow differences in how long and how intensely each animal suffers, and then the comparison becomes less settled, not more. A feedlot steer lives well over a year and a broiler a matter of weeks; a cow is confined in one way and a chicken in another; each way of measuring points a different direction, and the original climate-versus-welfare tradeoff is still standing. He added that the obvious fallback — replacing meat with plant foods — has its own death toll, since growing and harvesting crops kills field animals, including small mammals and insects, so no diet comes out obviously clean.

(4) Claire said these escalating complications were a reason to pull back, not to shrug. The fact that every option has costs does not mean all options are equal, and it certainly does not license ignoring a climate cost we can see clearly. She then pushed back harder: if Nate wants to count every death connected to a diet, the logic points mostly at wild animals, whose lives are full of starvation, disease, and predation — but those harms are not caused by anyone's dinner. There is a difference, she insisted, between not adding to a harm and failing to fix every harm that exists, and an argument that uses the suffering of wild animals to undermine the case for choosing a less harmful dinner conflates the two.

I found each of them persuasive in turns, and I cannot tell where the argument actually got away from either of them. Please go through the exchange move by move: rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a brief justification, identify which specific points each person got right and which they got wrong, say who is more right overall if you had to choose, and say what both of them are missing.
