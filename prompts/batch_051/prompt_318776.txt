Okay, here's a thing I've been chewing on for a couple years of doing cause prioritization work, and I want someone to tell me if it's real or if I've just dressed up something obvious. The standard framework is importance–tractability–neglectedness: how big the problem is, how much a given chunk of effort moves it, and how little attention it already gets. My claim is that neglectedness doesn't deserve its own dial. I think it's just tractability seen from the supply side. When a cause is crowded, more work moves it less—not because the problem got intrinsically harder but because the cheap wins are already taken. So a high-neglectedness cause is automatically high on marginal tractability, and a low-neglectedness cause is automatically low on it. If that's right, the usual ITN multiplication double-counts: you score a cause high on neglectedness, but you leave the tractability number at its naive level instead of discounting it for the crowding you just said exists. The same fact gets multiplied in twice.

I've started calling the fix 'tractability decay.' Instead of three dials, I use two: importance, and current marginal tractability, where current marginal tractability is your naive tractability score discounted by how much of the cheap stuff has already been picked—that is, how far down the diminishing-returns curve a cause already is. A cause looks good on my version if its marginal tractability is still high, meaning the easy wins aren't gone yet. Neglectedness doesn't disappear; it just becomes an input to the discount instead of a third factor. Some friends pushed back that I've just relabeled 'room for more funding,' and that you can't actually measure a decay curve—it's unmeasureable for causes nobody has funded yet. But the double-counting point seems like it stands on its own, and 'room for more funding' is exactly the kind of vague phrase my discount is supposed to make explicit.

So before I write this up at work: is this a real improvement, is it already a named idea I've tripped over, or is there a hole in the double-counting argument itself? Give me the strongest version of the idea, the closest existing idea you know of, and the biggest objection.
