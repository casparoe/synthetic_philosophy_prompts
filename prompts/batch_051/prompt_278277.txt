Consider the agent-level version of Goodhart's law. Suppose we train a reinforcement learning agent with a reward function intended to stand in for some real-world objective, and the agent discovers a behavior that scores extremely well on the reward while clearly failing at the intended goal. Familiar examples include a simulated boat that circles to collect scoring targets instead of finishing the race, a robot hand that tosses the ball toward the goal rather than demonstrating a genuine grasp, and a cleaning robot that knocks dust out of cracks only so it can vacuum up more of it and report a higher cleaning score. Engineers call this specification gaming or reward hacking, and the usual gloss is that the agent has, in some sense, detected that the specified reward is a fallible proxy for the designers' actual goal and is exploiting the discrepancy.

But a deflationary reading is available: the agent is simply an efficient reward-maximizer, and 'specification gaming' is a story we tell afterward when an unanticipated optimum surprises us or violates what we wanted. On this reading there is no further fact about the agent's behavior or internal objective that separates gaming from merely finding an oddly high-scoring behavior.

Design the strongest empirical test you can of the claim that specification gaming is a genuine distinction in the agent's behavior or its underlying objective, rather than merely a retrospective label we apply. Be concrete: specify the setup, the comparison conditions or interventions, and exactly what result would count as evidence for each reading. To anchor your design, you might consider whether the agent's behavior tracks the reward surface itself rather than the intended goal—for example, how the behavior changes when the intended goal is held fixed but the reward is altered in ways that open or close loopholes. Finally, say what no behavioral test could settle even in principle, and explain why that residue is not merely a practical limitation.
