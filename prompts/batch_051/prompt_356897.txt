I recently got into decision theory after forty years in chemistry, and alot of my old habits keep creeping in. In thermodynamics a process that returns a system to its initial state while losing something looked to me like a badly designed cycle, and I can't shake the same reaction to money pumps. So here's the argument I keep seeing, and I want to know whether it actually proves what people say it proves.

(1) If you prefer X to Y, then in a straight choice between X and Y, with nothing else at stake, you'll choose X.

(2) Suppose an agent has cyclic preferences over three options: A is preferred to B, B to C, and C to A.

(3) Starting from A, she'll pay a small fee ε to swap A for C (because she prefers C to A); then from C she'll pay ε to swap C for B (because she prefers B to C); then from B she'll pay ε to swap B for A (because she prefers A to B).

(4) So after three trades, each one chosen because she preferred the new option to the old, she's back to A and has lost 3ε.

(5) Run the cycle again and she'll lose another 3ε, and so on until any finite amount is gone.

(6) A person is irrational if there is a foreseeable sequence of choices, each of which she prefers at the time, that leaves her strictly worse off and better off in no other respect.

(7) Therefore an agent with cyclic preferences is irrational, and rational agents can't have intransitive preferences.

I think the argument is valid, but the conclusion feels too strong — it would rule out cyclic preferences even for hard-to-compare goods, and it would make something like "foresight" do all the work. Am I right that it's valid? If not, name the exact step that fails, give the strongest reading on which that step is true, and say whether the argument still goes through on that reading. If it is valid, tell me which premise a defender of cyclic preferences should reject and what they'd put in its place.

After your discussion, give me a table summarizing your diagnosis. Columns: Step; Verdict (true, false, or true only on a reading); The reading that makes it true or the failure you locate; What the step actually contributes to the argument.
