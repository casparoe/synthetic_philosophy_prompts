I am preparing a discussion for a small seminar on the intellectual history of technological risk, and I keep running into the same disagreement about John von Neumann. In his late essay on whether civilization can survive the technologies it creates, he clearly treated accelerating technical change as something that could destabilize human affairs, but he did not conclude that we are doomed; he seemed to think the problem was, in principle, manageable if institutions and analytical methods improved fast enough. My seminar mates read this in two incompatible ways: some see him as an early doomer who would have embraced the strongest modern claims about AI existential risk, while others see him as the archetypal Cold War rationalist who would have dismissed those claims as wildly overconfident extrapolation.

I want you to settle this with a committed reconstruction. Put yourself in the position of briefing von Neumann, just after he wrote that late essay, on the modern argument that a self-improving artificial intelligence could become unalignable and pose an existential risk to humanity. What would he make of it? What parts would he accept, reject, or reframe in terms of game theory, computation, and his experience with nuclear weapons? I want a definite answer with reasons, not a survey of possible readings. In particular, state clearly whether you think he would be more sympathetic to the doomer or the rationalist side, and defend that choice using his own recorded views about technology, war, and survival.
