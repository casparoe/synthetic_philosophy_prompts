I keep noticing a weird split in how people do the moral math around meat, and I want help figuring out what it quietly commits them to. The same person will say eating beef is better than chicken because one cow yields way more meat than one chicken, so youre responsible for fewer animal deaths — thats the whole argument. But if you ask about the mice, voles, and insects killed by harvesting the crops that feed the cow, or the insects killed by pesticides on those crops, theyll wave it off: thats unavoidable, thats just what farming does, you cant count everything. So individual deaths are the currency when the animal is the product, and they stop being the currency when the animal is a byproduct of producing animal feed. What theory of value or moral bookkeeping does this pattern presuppose? I can see it might presuppose a direct/indirect distinction, or maybe a view about who has standing (livestock versus wild animals), or maybe a tacit doing/allowing or intended/foreseen line. But does the practice actually need any one of those, or is it underdetermined? Reconstruct the implicit view, distinguish what it strictly presupposes from what it merely fits with, and say what would have to change in the beef-versus-chicken advice if someone started counting crop deaths symmetrically.
