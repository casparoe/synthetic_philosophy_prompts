I was arguing with a friend who works on language policy about a thought experiment, and I want your genuine reasoned prediction rather than a survey of options. Suppose that in 1901 the government of a small, mostly monolingual country (call it whatever you like) decides that homonymy is a defect of language and passes a law: whenever two words share a spoken and written form but have unrelated meanings, the pair must be split within ten years. A national academy picks whichever member of the pair is less frequent, coins a new word for it, and schools, newspapers, and government offices are required to use the replacement. The law targets only homonyms, not ordinary polysemy: a word with related senses, like the mouth of a person and the mouth of a river, keeps all its senses, but unrelated meanings that share a form, like bat the animal and bat the club, have to be separated. The defenders of the law know that context usually disambiguates homonyms, but they insist the residual ambiguity is still a cost, and the academy ends up making a lot of borderline calls about what counts as unrelated. Compliance is high even though people grumble.

By 2025 the policy has been running for over a century. I want your best prediction of what the language looks like now. Walk me through the mechanisms you expect to matter: how quickly sound change, borrowing, and semantic drift create new homonyms anyway; what happens to puns, riddles, poetry, advertising, and personal names; how law and religion cope when older documents keep the old ambiguous forms; how children acquire the language when their grandparents still remember both variants; and whether the total vocabulary ends up much larger and the language actually easier to process. Take a definite position: tell me which outcomes you expect and why, not just what could happen. And flag which links in your chain are robust and which are guesses, plus what would have to be true for the whole policy to backfire badly. Keep it continuous prose, like an email, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
