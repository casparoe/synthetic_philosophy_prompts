I'm a PhD student in philosophy of cognitive science, and in a reading group on Dennett's distinction among the physical, design, and intentional stances a colleague has been pressing a line that strikes me as doing a great deal of work with a single analogy. His argument is that attributing beliefs and desires to a system is no more metaphysically loaded than attributing a center of gravity to a chair: both are abstracta, real patterns in an underlying substrate rather than extra things added to it, and no one accuses the physicist of positing spooky centers of gravity that pull the chair around. He uses this to license a strong conclusion, namely that intentional-state attributions are just as real, just as legitimate, and just as causally respectable as the posits of the physical stance, and that the only difference is the level of description at which each is useful. I would like this analogy audited, because I suspect it is carrying more weight in his argument than it can bear.

Please audit the center-of-gravity analogy as an argument for that conclusion. Give an explicit correspondence table — what plays the role of the chair's mass distribution, the calculated point, the physicist's calculation, and the prediction it supports — and then work through the respects in which a belief/desire pattern is unlike a center of gravity: its underdetermination by the underlying substrate, its content and possible falsity, its sensitivity to what the system itself takes its attitudes to be, its normative dimension, and whatever else you find pertinent. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion (that intentional patterns are real and causally respectable in the same way as physical-stance posits) from the ones that don't, and say whether the conclusion survives once they are on the table, or whether a better analogy would be needed — and if so, what it should be.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
