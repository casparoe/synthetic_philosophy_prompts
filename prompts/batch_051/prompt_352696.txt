My upper-level ethics seminar has 14 students and a 75-minute block. We've been reading around the idea of moral trade, and I want a runnable activity that makes them feel the mechanics of it before we name it. The intuition to land is this: even if I think your cause is dramatically less important than mine, it can still make sense for us to swap moral effort, because my marginal contribution to my own top cause is falling off while my contribution to yours might still be high, and the same holds for you. The weirdness to surface is that this treats deeply held values as bargaining chips.

So design a game where students get assigned to rival causes, written on cards, with no real money. Each player starts with a fixed budget of effort chips, say 20. They first allocate chips solo, then a trading round lets them make binding two-person swaps of future allocations. Don't make this a pros-and-cons discussion. The deals should be real enough in the room that students can see a Pareto improvement emerge which no amount of moral argument would have produced on its own. Spell out the roles, the card contents, the exact chip rules and negotiation constraints, and a debrief that maps the play onto the moving parts of moral trade: different moral weights, declining marginal value, gains from trade, and the what-did-we-just-do discomfort. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, for instance some students refusing to role-play their assigned cause or everyone trying to trade with the most sympathetic player, and name the one feature the game inevitably fakes, namely the moral stakes themselves.
