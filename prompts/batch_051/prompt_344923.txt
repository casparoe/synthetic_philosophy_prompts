I'm in a Philosophy of Language class right now and we just spent two weeks on ambiguity and homonymy—how "pitch" can be tar or a throw or a note, how "tone" can be a sound quality or an attitude or muscle tension, and how context is supposed to sort all this out. For our study group I want to make one of those "which account of ambiguity do you actually hold?" quizzes, and I want it to actually sort people by what they say about cases, not by which label they'd pick.

Can you write the quiz for me? I'd like 8–10 questions, each one a concrete case: homonym pairs like bank, bat, bass, or cleave; auto-antonyms like sanction; the way "literally" gets used as an intensifier; a pun; a headline that has two readings; a garden-path sentence like the horse raced past the barn fell; a Mandarin-style tone example where changing the tone on the same syllable changes the meaning; or a situation where someone says something ambiguous on purpose and then pretends they meant the other reading. Each question should have two or three answer options that are real things a person might say, and the options should pull apart the positions below.

The positions I want to sort people into are roughly:

- the defect view: ambiguity is basically a bug, a processing cost, and language would be better if it were more precise;
- the efficiency view: ambiguity is a feature because it reuses easy high-frequency forms and lets context do the work, so it's actually a good tradeoff;
- the contextualist view: there's no real unresolved ambiguity, because an utterance in a concrete context just has its reading determined, and the other readings aren't live;
- the strategic view: ambiguity isn't just tolerated or resolved, it's actively exploited for hedging, dog-whistles, puns, plausible deniability, that kind of thing.

Then give me a scoring key that maps answer patterns to these positions, with a rule for someone who comes out mixed. Also flag which positions your questions can't seperate cleanly, and if some combination of answers is unoccupied, say whether it's unoccupied because it's incoherent, unattractive, or just not a view anyone happens to defend. Please don't include any question that just asks "do you think ambiguity is efficient?" — the whole point is to get at the view through the cases.

Oh and don't worry about citations or anything, this is just for a study group handout.
