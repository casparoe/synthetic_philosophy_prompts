Question: Define strategy-proofness for a social choice function. State the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem as precisely as you can. Then discuss what the theorem does and does not show about the possibility of non-manipulable collective decision procedures. In particular, does the theorem rule out all non-dictatorial strategy-proof procedures, or are there conditions under which non-dictatorial strategy-proof procedures exist? Be explicit about the role of the theorem's assumptions.

Student's answer: Strategy-proofness requires that, for every admissible profile of strict preferences and every voter i, reporting i's true ranking is a weakly dominant strategy: there is no ranking i could report that, holding the other voters' reports fixed, would yield an outcome i strictly prefers. The Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem states that if there are at least three alternatives, the domain is unrestricted, and the social choice function is onto, then any strategy-proof social choice function is dictatorial—that is, the chosen alternative is always the top-ranked alternative of one fixed voter. The onto condition, which requires that any strict preference ordering a voter might have can be submitted in some admissible profile, is what gives the theorem its full force. It follows that any non-dictatorial rule satisfying these conditions, majority rule being the natural example, is manipulable whenever there are at least three alternatives. Indeed, the theorem shows that no non-dictatorial, strategy-proof procedure can exist in any setting of interest, and attempts to design one are bound to fail. One might have hoped that fairness and honesty were compatible requirements on institutions; the theorem shows that they are not, because a rule that never rewards strategic misrepresentation must be dictatorial.
