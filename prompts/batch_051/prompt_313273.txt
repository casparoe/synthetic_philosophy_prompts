I keep getting into arguments at work about whether some function or API is "well-defined," and I've had to admit to myself that I don't really know what I mean by that phrase. You're a professional philosopher who works on mathematical concepts — think carefully, and maybe start by brainstorming a few candidate diagnoses before you commit to one. Here's the case.

The criteria I was taught for a construction being well-defined:
1. The result must not depend on arbitrary choices: if two people carry out the construction and make different choices at the choice points, they have to end up with literally the same object.
2. If the choices do matter, the construction isn't well-defined — not "well-defined up to isomorphism," not "well-defined enough," but broken.
3. A choice only counts as a problem if nothing in the setup settles it; when a rule picks one out, there's no issue.

Now the case that's been bugging me. We define i as "the square root of −1." But x^2 + 1 = 0 has two solutions over the reals, and nothing in the reals tells them apart. Once I name one of them i, the other one becomes −i, and complex conjugation swaps the two while fixing every real number. Any statement about i that uses only the field operations plus real parameters is equally true of −i, because conjugation is an automorphism fixing the reals. So by criterion 1, "i" is not well-defined: two people can each "pick a root" and end up with objects that aren't literally the same element, just mirror images. But it feels crazy to say complex analysis sits on top of a non-well-defined definition, since the choice never seems to leak into any theorem.

The contrast: with x^2 − 2, there's no problem, because one root is positive and the other negative, and the order on the reals settles which one is "the" square root of 2. The i case has no analagous tiebreaker — there is no "positive imaginary unit."

So I want a verdict: is i well-defined by my criteria? If not, does that show my criteria are too strict — do I need room for something like "well-defined up to an automorphism of the choice space," or "defined, but only up to a symmetry, and that's fine for all the statements we actually make"? Or should I bite the bullet and say the definition of i is genuinely not well-defined, and we've just built everything so carefully that the flaw never does any work? Work through the case criterion by criterion, and tell me what definition of "well-defined" I should actually carry around — and where "canonical choice" is supposed to fit in, if it's anything more than a fancy way of saying criterion 3.
