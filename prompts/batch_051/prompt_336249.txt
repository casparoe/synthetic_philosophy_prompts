A colleague offered me the following argument, which I believe proves too much but cannot refute. Consider a bronze statue and the lump of bronze that constitutes it, made of exactly the same particles in the same arrangement.

(1) The lump could survive being hammered flat; the statue could not, since a flattened statue is no longer a statue.
(2) If the statue were identical with the lump, then whatever is possible for the one would be possible for the other.
(3) So the statue is not identical with the lump.
(4) Nothing in this reasoning depends on anything peculiar to statues and lumps; it applies to any two kind terms with different persistence conditions that apply to what we would ordinarily call the same material thing.
(5) By the same reasoning, each of us is not a human animal (the animal could survive in a permanent vegetative state, while the person could not survive the permanent loss of the relevant psychological capacities), the animal is not the body, and the wood is not the table.
(6) Therefore, wherever such kind terms overlap on one spatiotemporal thing, there are as many numerically distinct coincident objects as there are such terms.
(7) But that conclusion is very implausible: it turns ordinary claims such as 'the statue is a lump of bronze' and 'I am a human animal' into non-identities, and populates a single region with objects that differ only in sortal-dependent modal profiles.

So the original move from modal difference to distinctness seems to lead to an ontology I cannot accept. I would like to keep the statue/lump distinction, yet I would also like to keep ordinary identity claims such as 'I am a human animal.' Which premise or step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost?
