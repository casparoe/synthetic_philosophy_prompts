I teach an upper-level formal epistemology seminar, 14 students, 90 minutes, and they've already done plain Bayesian updating and conditionalization. I want a runnable classroom activity around the Judy Benjamin problem: you're a soldier dropped into a square split into four quadrants—Red-HQ, Red-Second, Blue-HQ, Blue-Second—and you start with equal credence in each. Then HQ radios: "If you're in Red territory, then the odds are 3:1 that your in the HQ quadrant rather than the Second quadrant." The puzzle is that this message isn't a proposition you can just conditionalize on; its a constraint on a conditional probability, and it underdetermines your new distribution—you have to make a further choice about whether to keep the total probability of Red fixed or let it move. I want the students to run into that underdetermination themselves, before anyone names it. Design a game or structured betting exercise (play money is fine; no real stakes) where they have to commit to posterior probabilities, make bets against each other, and discover that different defensible update rules expose them to different books. Give me exact rules, materials, a timeline for the 90 minutes, a payoff scheme, and a debrief that maps what happened in the game onto the formal point: ordinary conditioning handles events, while a message like this shifts a conditional probability and leaves the marginal on Red unconstrained. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it—I'm guessing some groups will quietly assume P(Red) stays at 1/2 and won't notice that was a choice—and be frank about which part of the problem the activity can't make vivid and I'd have to supply myself at the end. And keep it so the students don't hear "Judy Benjamin" or "minimum cross-entropy" until the debrief.
