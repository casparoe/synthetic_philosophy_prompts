After a series of avoidable forecasting mistakes in my research group, I have been building a training program around what I am calling rationality katas: short, repeated drills for skills such as taking the outside view, applying base rates, and detecting sunk-cost reasoning. A colleague objected that I was overestimating transfer, and in the course of answering him I produced the following argument. I now half-suspect it moves too quickly somewhere, but I cannot locate the mistake. Is the argument valid? If not, exactly which step fails, and what would the correct version of that step be?

(1) A kata can transfer to live performance only if it drills a component of the skill that can be specified independently of any particular performance context.

(2) Martial-arts katas satisfy this condition: a pivot, a weight shift, or a guard can be described and drilled apart from any actual fight and then recombined under pressure.

(3) Therefore, if rationality katas are to transfer to real-world reasoning, they must drill components of reasoning that can be specified independently of any particular problem.

(4) But the operations of reasoning have no such context-independent specification. Whether applying a base rate, treating a cost as sunk, or taking the outside view is the correct move in a given case depends on the evidence, the stakes, and the alternative courses of action present in that case.

(5) A rationality kata therefore faces a dilemma: if it is concrete enough to give a learner corrective feedback, it has already smuggled in parameters from particular problems; if it is general enough to avoid that, it is too abstract to guide any actual inference.

(6) Hence rationality katas cannot transfer to real-world reasoning, and rationality cannot be trained by katas in the way martial arts can.
