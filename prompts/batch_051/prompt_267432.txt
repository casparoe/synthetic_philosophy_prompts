The essay summarizes Taurek as follows:

Taurek argued that if you have a drug that can save either one person or five, you are not required to save the five. The reason is that there is no single individual who is harmed by the loss of five lives, and no impersonal point of view from which five deaths are worse than one. Since each of the six has an equal claim to your help, the only fair procedure is to give each of them exactly the same chance of being saved, which he thought meant flipping a coin between the one and the five. Saving the five outright would treat the one as if his claim lost its force merely because other people happened to be involved. Taurek took this to show that numbers do not by themselves generate a stronger claim, and that the consequentialist habit of aggregating individual losses rests on a fallacy. The essay then concludes that Taurek’s position commits us to saying a disaster that kills a hundred people is not in any respect worse than a disaster that kills one.
