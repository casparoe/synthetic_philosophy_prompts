I have been working through the four-stage taxonomy of minds associated with Daniel Dennett, and I want to test it against a case that seems to sit between categories. As I understand it, the taxonomy ranks minds by where their trial and error happens and how many inner loops of generate-and-test they run. A Darwinian creature is a generate-and-test device whose behavioral variants are produced only by natural selection and tested over generations; the individual cannot learn. A Skinnerian creature adds one inner loop: during its lifetime it emits behavioral variants blindly and the environment reinforces or extinguishes them, so it can learn through operant conditioning while still being unable to test options before acting on them. A Popperian creature adds another inner loop: it constructs representations of candidate actions and tests them in an inner environment, so that the hypotheses die instead of the creature, and it can preselect before committing itself in the world. A Gregorian creature adds a further loop: it imports and manipulates cultural mind-tools, above all language but also notation, diagrams, arithmetic, and similar artifacts, and can use them to carry out searches and comparisons that would be impossible for a bare brain.

Now consider the following system. An artificial system is trained by stochastic gradient descent over a huge corpus of human text, first to predict the next token and later to follow a reward model that encodes human preferences. At deployment it produces text token by token, but it can also be induced to generate a hidden chain of reasoning, notice an inconsistency in what it has generated, and revise before producing a final answer. Its candidate actions are strings of text, and they are never tested against the physical world or against anything outside the text itself; its only feedback during training was whether the text matched the statistical patterns of the corpus or the learned preferences. Is this system a Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, or Gregorian mind on the taxonomy above, or does it fail to be a mind in any of the four senses?

Work through the criteria honestly and criterion by criterion, since I suspect the interesting action is in the mismatches: a mechanism may look like one stage at the level of parameter updates and like another at the level of token generation. Where a criterion applies only partly, say whether that is because the criterion needs refinement, because the system lacks something the criterion presupposes — such as perception, embodiment, or a world in which actions have costs — or because the taxonomy was built for biological organisms and simply was not designed to place systems like this one. I am asking not so much for a forced classification as for a verdict together with a statement of what the hard case exposes in the criteria; if you think the taxonomy needs a fifth stage or a distinction between the training-time and inference-time creature, argue for that.
