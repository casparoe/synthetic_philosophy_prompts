Lately I’ve been trying to figure out which skeptical doubts I actually take seriously, as opposed to the ones that just feel like classroom moves, and my gut verdicts aren’t sorting themselves neatly. Reading Descartes’ First Meditation and trying to genuinely doubt that I have hands: doesn’t take, feels like a game. A partner who keeps rewriting what I clearly remember her saying: that genuinely shakes my trust in specific recollections, and I think the distrust is rational, not excessive. Change blindness demos and implanted-memory studies: they make me think courts should lean much less on eyewitness testimony, but they don’t make me distrust my own eyes day to day. The brain-in-a-vat scenario: I can assign it some nonzero chance and still plan my week exactly as if it’s false, and it doesn’t bother me. A magic trick fools me automatically even after I know how it works, but I don’t count that as a skeptical problem at all.

Now I’m wondering whether there’s a single principle behind these, and I keep reaching for the line David Hume draws in Section XII of An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding: “There is another species of scepticism, consequent to science and enquiry, when men are supposed to have discovered, either the absolute fallaciousness of their mental faculties, or their unfitness to reach any fixed determination in all those curious subjects of speculation, about which they are commonly employed.” I figured the doubts I take seriously would fall in his “consequent to science and enquiry” bucket, since those are the ones where I’ve discovered a real fallibility. But the change-blindness/memory cases are also consequent-ish—I only know about them from psychology—and they don’t make me distrust my own faculties the way the gaslighting does, while I still think courts should distrust eyewitnesses. So either my verdicts cut across his distinction, or I’m hanging onto some of them inconsistently. What principle, or small set of candidate principles, captures my verdicts? Which ones does it force me to give up, and where does it land on the brain-in-a-vat case if I stop hedging and take the principle seriously? Also, please tell me how confident you are in the principle you end up with and which point you’re least sure of.
