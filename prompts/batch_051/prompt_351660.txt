Since I retired from the lab I've been in a reading group on Bayesian epistemology, and a friend showed us a version of the Dutch book argument that has been bugging me ever since. The ordinary version is supposed to show that degrees of belief should satisfy the probability axioms, and I could live with that. But this version seems to show a lot more, and I can't find the exact place it overreaches. Here's the argument as I've reconstructed it:

(1) A rational person won't accept a bet, or a package of bets, that loses money no matter how the world turns out.
(2) If your degree of belief in a proposition S is p, then you'll sell a ticket that pays $1 if S is true for any price above p, and buy it for any price below p. (Assume the stakes are small, so we can set risk aversion aside.)
(3) Let T be an enormous tautology — say a 200-variable propositional tautology whose truth table has 2^200 rows and has never actually been checked. A non-omniscient person, call her Dana, has degree of belief 0.8 that T is true.
(4) By (2), Dana will sell a ticket that pays $1 if T for, say, 85 cents.
(5) But T is a tautology, so the ticket pays out $1 no matter what. Dana has just accepted a guaranteed loss of 15 cents.
(6) By (1), Dana is irrational purely because she's less than fully certain of a tautology. And since the same construction works for any tautology, any rational person must be certain of every logical truth — a strong form of logical omniscience.
(7) But finite minds can't do that, and it seems wrongheaded to call someone irrational for not having verified a 200-variable truth table.

So one of those steps, or some slide between them, has to go. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'm most reluctant to give up (1). And is there a way to keep the Dutch book machinery for the ordinary probability axioms without being carried all the way to logical omniscience? Please keep the answer under 400 words.
