I'm in an intermediate ethics class and we just finished the unit on consequentialism and cluelessness, and I've been kicking around a view I want stress-tested before my paper is due. I'm calling it horizon consequentialism (tentatively). The main move is that cluelessness isn't a yes/no thing, it comes with a horizon. Every action has what I've been calling a reliable consequence radius: close in, I can predict effects well enough to compare options; past a certain point, consequences fan out and become effectivley unpredictable. So instead of evaluating an act by all its consequences, which is impossible, we evaluate it by expected value up to the edge of its horizon, and treat everything beyond the horizon as noise that roughly cancels out. Example: I'm choosing between charity A and charity B. They have similar near-term effects I can estimate. Charity A has one of those worrying long-run stories—maybe it undermines local institutions in some way I can't trace. On my view I shouldn't let that tip the scales, because it's past the horizon and every option has some untraceable long-run story, so those tails cancel. I know we read Greaves and I think Lenman on cluelessness, and they seem to treat it as a live problem for consequentialism, but I want to dissovle the problem rather than live with it. What I want to know: does this already exist under a better name, am I actually still a consequentialist or did I sneak in something else, and what are the strongest objections? The one that keeps me up is that the horizon looks arbitrary—why exactly there?—and I can't tell if that arbitrariness is fatal or just ugly.
