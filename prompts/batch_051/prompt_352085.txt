So I teach this philosophy of free expression course, and we've somehow ended up in defamation law, which I am only half-qualified to teach. In seminar today a student pulled a move I've heard a hundred times: I asked whether a credit-rating agency could be on the hook for a downgrade that cratered a bond issuer, and whether an equity analyst could be sued for a forecast that a company would miss earnings, and she said it didnt matter that they were wrong—"they're just opinions, and opinions are protected speech." Hand wave, next slide.

I tried to push on it and the word "opinion" kept meaning a new thing: a statement about the future, a statement of evaluation rather than fact, a statement someone believed, a statement no one would take literally, a statement about taste. So I want you to take the slogan "opinions are protected, facts are not" apart and lay out the distinct precise claims somebody could be making when they use it in this ratings/forecasts context. Give me at least five or six readings, as a numbered menu. For each reading: state the precise principle (say whether it's about verifiability, the speaker's state of mind, the phrasing, the type of commitment made to the audience, or something else), give it a quick plausibility grade, and then—the part I actually need for class—say whether that reading protects a credit rating or an earnings forecast from defamation liability. Pay special attention to the messy cases where it matters: a rating that reads like a statement of current financial condition, a forecast labeled a "view" but built on a factual claim the analyst knows is false, a rating agency that quietly knows its own model is broken. My own hunch is that forecasts come out more protected than ratings, but the slogan alone won't get you there, and I want to see the menu do the work.

End by telling me which reading you think is doing most of the work when people deploy the slogan in this context, and whether it survives those messy cases.

Then, after the prose, include a summary table with these columns: "Reading" (a short label), "Precise claim" (one sentence), "Plausibility" (use only: trivial / substantive but defensible / false), and "Protects ratings and forecasts?" (use only: yes / no / only sometimes, then a one-line condition).
