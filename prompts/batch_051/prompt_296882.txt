I am a PhD student in comparative politics, and I have been developing a construct I provisionally call 'reference-class telescoping.' It is an attempt to fix something I find unsatisfying about the way outside-view reasoning gets applied to forecasts about the survival of states, empires, and regimes.

Here is the idea. Standard outside-view practice is to pick a reference class of comparable cases and anchor on its base rate. In practice, though, the class is chosen once and held fixed across the entire forecast horizon. I want to say that the correct reference class should change systematically as the horizon lengthens. For short horizons, a narrow class is appropriate. If I am forecasting five years out, it is reasonable to condition on features such as being a nuclear-armed superpower in a bipolar or near-bipolar system, because those features are themselves likely to persist over five years. But over longer horizons, the defining features of any narrow class begin to decay; they are themselves variables with their own base rates and survival curves. A two-hundred-year forecast conditioned on being a nuclear superpower is implicitly conditioning on a feature that may itself not survive fifty years. So the outside view should 'zoom out' as the horizon grows: from superpowers since 1945, to great powers since 1815, to large territorial states more generally, to all complex societies, and so on.

I have been working on a more formal version. Instead of selecting one reference class, assign each candidate class a weight proportional to the estimated probability that its defining features still obtain at the target date; multiply that weight by the class-specific base rate; and forecast from the resulting mixture. On this rule, a ten-year forecast for the persistence of U.S. primacy would draw mostly on a postwar-superpower class, while a one-hundred-year forecast would draw mostly on a much broader class of premodern and modern empires and large states.

An illustration: a forecaster in 1900 trying to predict the survival of the British Empire would have done acceptably at a ten-year horizon using a class of current great powers. At a fifty-year horizon, that same class would have been badly misleading, since the category 'current great power' had a short half-life itself. The telescoping rule would have pushed the longer-horizon forecaster toward the base rate of maritime empires or imperial systems in general, which was far less reassuring, and would likely have improved calibration.

I realize this is adjacent to hierarchical Bayesian forecasting, model averaging, hidden-state and regime-switching models, and the older reference-class problem. But I have not seen the horizon-dependence of the reference class itself made explicit as a principle. I would like your honest assessment. Is the construct coherent, or is it mostly a vivid metaphor? Does it already exist under another name? What is the strongest version a sympathetic reader could build out of it, and where exactly does that strongest version fail? In particular, I would like to know whether there are systematic cases in which a narrow reference class remains calibrated at long horizons, or in which the broadened class becomes too heterogeneous for its base rate to carry any usable information.

I am not asking for a reading list or citations; I want an analytical assessment of the idea itself.
