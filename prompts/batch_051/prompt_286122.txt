I have been working through an argument for the claim that probability theory is not merely one formal tool among others but the unique logic of coherent reasoning under uncertainty. I have reconstructed the argument in my own words, and I want it evaluated as I state it rather than as a stand-in for any particular author's version; please do not spend time on exegesis or citations.

(1) Wherever I use plausibilities to grade how strongly propositions should be believed relative to a body of background information, the system must at minimum be internally consistent: two ways of combining the same information so as to obtain a plausibility for a given proposition must always yield the same result.

(2) Internal consistency therefore forces plausibility to be representable as a single real number assigned to each proposition relative to fixed background information. For consistency demands that any two plausibilities be comparable on a common scale, and a single-valued numerical scale is exactly what records such an ordering.

(3) Consider the plausibility of the conjunction A and B on background X. One way to reach it is to judge the plausibility of A on X, then judge the plausibility of B on A and X, and combine the two intermediate values. Since the order in which one expands a compound proposition must not matter, the final value must be a function of exactly those two intermediate plausibilities, and the same combination function must work in all cases.

(4) Similarly, the plausibility of not A on X must be determined by the plausibility of A on X alone. Once one has said how much credit to give A, there is no independent room left in which to decide how much credit to give its negation.

(5) Premises (3) and (4) induce functional equations for the combination function and the negation function. If those functions are smooth (twice differentiable) and monotone, the only solutions are, after a suitable rescaling of the plausibility scale, the following:

P(A and B | X) = P(A | X) × P(B | A and X)

P(not A | X) = 1 − P(A | X)

(6) These are exactly the product rule and the complement rule of probability, from which the sum rule for mutually exclusive alternatives follows. So any plausibility assignment satisfying the conditions stated above is, after rescaling, a probability assignment.

(7) Therefore, for coherent reasoning under uncertainty, probability is not one alternative among others. Any system that deviates from the probability rules either turns out to be a disguised version of them or violates a basic requirement of consistency.

I am not sure the conclusion in (7) is actually carried by the earlier steps. Is the argument valid as stated? If not, which numbered step is the first point of failure, and what is the precise nature of the failure — an equivocation, a suppressed premise, a false conditional, a scope shift? For any step that fails, give the strongest reading on which it is true, and say whether, on that reading, the argument still reaches (7). If the argument is valid up to (6) but not beyond, say exactly how (7) overstates what follows. If the whole argument succeeds only as a much more modest conditional, state that conditional.

In answering, please separate what is mathematically settled, given the stated assumptions, from what is genuinely contested among people who know this material, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
