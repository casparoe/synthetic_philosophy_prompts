You are a professional philosopher specializing in theories of well-being, with unusually detailed knowledge of narrative theory. Think slowly, and begin by brainstorming the several distinct claims that might be packed into the analogy before you address the strongest of them.

I am a novelist. In my professional world—publishing, writing programs, festival question-and-answer sessions, readers writing to me about my characters—a comparison is treated as settled wisdom: a good human life is like a good novel, and a good novel requires plot, and plot is built from change—new places, new relationships, reversals, an accumulation of incident. From this people draw a conclusion about well-being, namely that novelty and adventure are not optional preferences but constituents of a flourishing life, and that the fear of a boring, repetitive life is a sound, well-calibrated attitude rather than a culturally specific anxiety or a failure of attention. I have spent my career constructing such plots, and I have come to distrust the inference; but I want to know how much of the work the analogy is actually doing.

Audit the analogy for me. First, present the strongest version of it and an explicit table of correspondences: what in a life is supposed to play the role of the protagonist, the author, the plot, the theme, the reader, narrative tension, and the satisfying ending. Then identify where the mapping breaks. Separate the disanalogies that matter for the intended conclusion about novelty and well-being from those that are merely incidental. Pay particular attention to the difference between a life as it is lived forward in experience and a novel as it is judged from outside and after the fact: a novel is good in part because it holds the interest of someone who is not its protagonist, whereas a life, if it is good at all, is good primarily for the one who lives it. Consider whether the analogy has quietly imported an audience into the good life, and with it the assumption that an uneventful and contented existence is a formal failure. Test your account on two cases: a lighthouse keeper whose days are repetitive and who reports high well-being, and a perpetually uprooted adventurer who is miserable but whose life would make an excellent novel. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion—that novelty is a component of well-being and that the dread of boredom is rationally sound—survives the audit, or whether it was the analogy carrying the weight all along; if a better analogy is available, propose it.
