My brother and I spent most of this year not speaking to each other over what our late father "really wanted" about the house. A mediator finally had each of us say in plain terms what we took the other to mean, and it became obvious that a large part of the fight was a disagreement about the words — not all of it, but enough that the accusation no longer seemed to fit. I have been reading about verbal disputes since, including the metaontological version of the idea: that some disagreements which look as though they are about reality may in fact be, partly, choices about which concepts to apply. Next month I am leading a ninety-minute session on exactly this for my community philosophy circle, whose members are thoughtful non-specialists — a nurse, a retired engineer, two teachers — and I want a plan for teaching it, not the lesson itself.

I am too close to the material to trust my own pacing; the hour has to be carried by the ideas, not by my family story. So give me the teaching plan in the following shape.

First, work out the order in which the ideas must be introduced and what depends on what: what has to be in place before the idea of a verbal dispute can even be formulated, what has to be in place before a substantive disagreement makes sense by contrast, and where in that chain the deflationary question — how far this deflates old philosophical debates — should join the sequence. Second, choose a single carrying example for the whole session, and say what it must have and what it must not have; explain why the obvious light examples, a tomato being classed as a fruit and the like, will trivialize the point rather than carry it. Third, give me the misconception map: the things members of the group will predictably think at each stage — that this is the old it's-just-semantics shrug, that a careful definition would settle the dispute, that once a disagreement is exposed as verbal there was nothing real at stake, that philosophy is all word games — and specify at which point in the sequence each should be headed off and with what move. Fourth, give me the end-of-session check: a single question or small task that shows whether they understand the point and the test for it, without merely having adopted a slogan. Fifth, tell me what to leave out at this level, especially how much of the metaontological debate (whether questions like "are there tables, or only particles arranged table-wise?" are merely verbal) I should let in, and give me a sentence for saying to the group that I am leaving the rest out.

Format the answer in six titled sections, in this order, roughly 1,200 words in total:

1. The lead idea (about 15%) — the one distinction or setup that must land before anything else can, and why it is prior.
2. The dependency chain (about 30%) — the ordered steps, with what each step presupposes and why the order cannot be rearranged.
3. The carrying example (about 20%) — the example, the work it does, and why it must avoid the obvious failures.
4. The misconception map (about 20%) — each misconception, the moment in the sequence where it arises, and the move that heads it off.
5. The end-of-session check (about 10%) — a question or task that would reveal whether the distinction has been understood rather than memorized.
6. What to leave out (about 5%) — the omissions, including the sentence for telling the group you are leaving them out.
