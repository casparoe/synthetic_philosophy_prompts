Recently the gap between two uses of the word "courage" has become personal. My father, who died last year, was decorated for an action under fire. I have spent my adult life in peaceful settings—a hospital, mostly—and last month a colleague described as courageous my decision to report a senior clinician's repeated errors, an act that put only my job and several friendships at risk. The praise unsettled me. I can see a superficial resemblance: fear, and acting despite it. But military accounts of courage keep involving things my case did not—drilled preparation, a defined role and duty, bodily danger, and subordination to something larger than oneself—and I am not sure the two cases share anything beyond the word.

I would like to know whether the claim that these are one trait is the kind of claim that can be tested. Treat it as a competition between two hypotheses. On the first, courage is a single underlying disposition—to appraise fear correctly and act for the right end anyway—which appears in different garb when the feared object is bodily harm and when it is social ruin. On the second, "moral courage" is courage by analogy only: the civilian case borrows its vocabulary from the martial case, but the underlying capacities (stress inoculation, impulse control, role-bound duty, calibration of risk) are sufficiently different that no single shared trait does explanatory work.

Design the strongest test you can of the first hypothesis against the second. It should be concrete enough to run or at least to specify as a research program: what populations, measurements, or longitudinal observations would you compare? What pattern of results would favor a single underlying trait, and what pattern would favor distinct phenomena sharing a name? Do not evade the matching problem—bodily fear and social fear must be compared somehow, whether by presumed intensity, stakes, or physiological signature—and say whether solving that matching problem would itself presuppose one of the answers. Finally, be explicit about what even a clean positive result would not show: whether it would show that courage is one virtue in the normative sense, or only that one psychological mechanism is common to both. I want the residue of the question marked off, not hidden.
