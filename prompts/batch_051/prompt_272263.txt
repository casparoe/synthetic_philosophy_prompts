Here is a parallel I have been turning over, and I want you to tell me whether it is deep or just pattern-matching.

In debates about artificial intelligence, the orthogonality thesis says that an agent's final goals and its general capability are independent axes: virtually any level of capability can in principle be paired with virtually any final goal. The instrumental convergence thesis then says that, despite that independence, a very wide range of final goals will push a sufficiently capable agent toward the same instrumental subgoals — self-preservation, resource acquisition, cognitive self-improvement, and protection of the agent's current goal content. So you have variation at the level of terminal ends and convergence at the level of means.

This looks to me structurally like convergent evolution. Unrelated lineages, starting with different body plans and histories, repeatedly arrive at the same solutions when selection presses on them in similar ways: camera eyes in vertebrates and cephalopods, powered flight in birds and bats, the crab-like body form in multiple crustacean lineages. In both cases, different starting points are funneled toward the same intermediate forms by some systematic pressure. A first-pass mapping would run: an agent's terminal goal corresponds to the niche or selection regime a lineage occupies; the recurring instrumental subgoals correspond to recurring adaptations; general capability corresponds to the generality or power of the search process; and the environment corresponds to the environment — which does suspiciously little work in my version.
