I am the undergraduate representative on my university's Committee on Learning Technologies, and I am taking a seminar this term on philosophical theories of contract, consent, and promising. The committee meets next week to decide whether to approve a three-year campus-wide agreement with a vendor whose online proctoring service would become mandatory in several large introductory courses. I have agreed to circulate a short written opinion before the meeting, and I would like it to treat the decision as a philosophical problem rather than a compliance exercise.

Under the proposed arrangement, every student in an affected course must click "I agree" to the vendor's terms of service before taking their first exam; there is no way to sit the exam without accepting. The terms run to roughly 16,000 words and include the following: the vendor may amend the terms on 30 days' notice, with continued use counting as renewed assent; disputes must go to individual binding arbitration, with a waiver of class actions; the vendor may process, retain, and share student video, audio, and behavioral data with affiliates and service providers; and the vendor's liability is capped at the fees actually paid. The vendor will not negotiate with individual students, and the university's negotiators have so far described these terms as standard. The administration's position is that comparable clickwrap agreements are ubiquitous, that the tool deters exam misconduct, and that students who object remain free to decline by not enrolling in courses that use the service, although in practice that includes most large introductory sections.

The committee's realistic options are to approve the agreement as drafted, to approve it only if the university negotiates specified changes, or to decline to approve it in its current form. Draft the memo I will circulate: it should recommend one of those courses of action, state any conditions or required changes, explain in plain terms whether and why clicking "I agree" under these circumstances amounts to genuine agreement, and engage fairly with the strongest consideration on the losing side. I would rather the memo not collapse the question into current contract doctrine; I am interested in the underlying question of what, if anything, turns a non-negotiable click into an obligation.
