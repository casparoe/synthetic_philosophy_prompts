I'm a philosophy professor, and I teach a grad seminar in formal epistemology. My colleague Priya, who does decision theory, guests in it sometimes, and over the years I've kept a mental list of her verdicts about whether a person can assign a subjective probability to their own future action. The pattern is starnge and I'd like a second read.

Her calls:

(i) You're at a restaurant, genuinely torn between the pasta and the grain bowl; someone asks you, right there, for your probability that you'll order the pasta. Priya says no: while you're actually deciding, you don't have a well-formed credence about your own choice; you're making the choice, not predicting it.

(ii) You decided this morning to go to the 4 p.m. talk and put it in your calendar; at 1 p.m. someone asks your probability that you'll actually show up. Priya says yes: 0.9, based on the settled intention plus your track record with calendar items.

(iii) Same talk, but the question is whether a mutual friend will show up, and she hasn't decided yet. Priya says yes: ordinary credences about other people deliberating are unproblematic.

(iv) You have a strict standing policy of not checking work email after dinner; at 4 p.m. someone asks how likely you are to check it tonight. Priya says yes: 0.05 or so, straight from the policy.

(v) You'd decided to wear the blue jacket, but now, in front of the closet, you've reopened the question and are wavering; asked your probability that you'll wear it. Priya says no again: the moment you reopen the decision, prediction is crowded back out.

The new case: on Wednesday, a recruiter tells you she'll call Friday with an offer at a small liberal-arts college. It's a genuinely open choice, but you've deliberately put it out of your mind; you won't face it untill the call. Right there she asks your subjective probability that you'll accept, just for her planning, with nothing at stake in saying one number or another.

What would Priya say about the recruiter case? More usefully: lay out the plausible rules that fit all five of her earlier verdicts, say what each rule predicts for the new case, give your best guess about which rule is actually hers and why, and name one further case that would separate the candidates most cleanly.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence—the verdict you predict she'd give—and then give the reasoning. No preamble, no restating the question.
