In my moral responsibility seminar we have been working through the matched-pair cases for resultant luck: two agents whose conduct up to a point is identical, and who diverge only because one is unlucky in how things turn out (the familiar pair of equally reckless drivers, one of whom happens to strike a child). I am trying to develop a position I am provisionally calling the double-ledger account of moral luck. The idea is that we should keep two evaluative books. A character ledger records blame and praise only for what was in the agent's control — the intention, the knowingly assumed risk, the level of care — and so is identical across the lucky and unlucky members of a matched pair. A history ledger records what actually happened, and it is the sole source of agent-regret, grief, apology, and duties of repair; it never licenses additional blame. On this picture the common feeling that the unlucky driver is more blameworthy is an error I call outcome leakage: the badness of the actual result seeps into our estimate of the badness of the agent, roughly the way a vivid, high-cost outcome makes hindsight judgments of negligence more severe. I would like an honest assessment. Is the contrast between the two ledgers doing genuine work, or have I merely relabeled the existing blame-versus-regret distinction in the moral luck literature? Is outcome leakage distinct from hindsight bias in moral judgment, and does it matter if it is not? And where does the account fail — in particular, can the character ledger be well-defined at all if constitutive luck means even one's dispositions are not fully under one's control, and can agent-regret really be kept clean of moral assessment the way the view requires?
