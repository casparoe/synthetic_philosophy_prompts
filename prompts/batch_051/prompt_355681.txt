I work as a software engineer, and in postmortems I keep hearing a simple rule: a person deserves blame only if they could have done otherwise. I want to figure out whether I actually accept that rule, so I will describe five incidents involving code reviews and deploy approvals, along with my immediate reactions. I would like you to see whether a single principle captures all five reactions. If one does, state it precisely and say which part of it handles each incident. If none does, tell me which of my reactions is least defensible and state the best principle that captures the rest. Then apply the principle you end up with to a sixth incident I have not settled on.

Priya, a senior engineer, has a small pull request that has gone a week without the required second review. Unknown to Priya, her manager has installed a bot on the review system that will approve and merge the request under Priya's account if Priya tries to request changes or block the merge. Priya considers waiting another day, then decides she has waited long enough and merges without the second review. The bot never triggers. My reaction: Priya is to blame for merging without the required review.

Derek, a principal engineer, approves dozens of routine pull requests each day. During a meeting, only half paying attention, he approves a pull request that actually contains a risky schema migration. He never looked at the diff. Unknown to Derek, a teammate has written a small script, as a prank, that would approve this exact pull request if Derek instead tried to request changes. The script never triggers. My reaction: Derek is not to blame for this particular bad approval, though he is to blame for maintaining a rubber-stamping habit. This feels different from Priya's case, but I cannot yet say what the difference is.

Elena reviews a risky pull request carefully and submits a request for changes with detailed comments. Unknown to Elena, the author has installed a guardrail script that cancels her request and approves the merge. My reaction: Elena is not to blame for the bad merge that follows.

Marcus merges a pull request without the required review at ten in the morning, knowing it violates policy. Unknown to him, the review system's request-a-review feature is broken that day, so if he had tried to request a review, the attempt would have failed silently and been recorded as an approval anyway. My reaction: Marcus is to blame for merging without review, just as much as Priya, even though the obstacle in his case was a plain bug rather than a person's arrangement.

Nadia approves a change she knows is insecure because her manager has told her that if she blocks it, her team will be assigned a punishing on-call rotation and her intern will lose a return offer. Nothing in the review system prevents her from submitting a request for changes. My reaction: Nadia is not to blame for approving the insecure change, or at most only slightly to blame.

Now apply the principle to a sixth incident where I am torn. Sabine merges a pull request without the required review. Unknown to her, her manager has not only installed an auto-approve bot of the kind Priya faced, but has also quietly rearranged Sabine's schedule over the previous two weeks so that she is sleep-deprived and more likely to skip review, hoping she will do exactly that. Sabine, unusually tired, cuts the review short and merges. The bot never triggers. Part of me says she is to blame, but less than Priya; another part says the manager's behind-the-scenes shaping of her situation means the manager, not Sabine, should carry the blame for the merge. What does your principle say about her, and why?
