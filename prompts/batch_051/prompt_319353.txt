In my work on the philosophy of history and in public debate, I have adopted a standing policy toward arguments that lean on historical analogy. When a colleague or student compares a present crisis to Munich in 1938, or warns that an intervention will become another Vietnam, I treat the analogy itself as carrying almost no argumentative weight until the speaker offers a worked-out account of the relevant structural similarities and differences; a vivid precedent by itself does not move me. I adopted this policy because such analogies seem to me to close off inquiry rather than discipline it—each side reaches for the historical precedent that flatters its prior commitments. Is this policy rationally defensible, or is it a bias against a legitimate form of inductive evidence dressed up as methodological hygiene? Under what conditions should a historical analogy genuinely raise or lower one's confidence about the likely course of a present situation? And is there a version of my rule that preserves the protection against tendentious comparison without making me unreachable by genuine lessons of history?
