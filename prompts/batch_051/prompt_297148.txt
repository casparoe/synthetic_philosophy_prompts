You're a philosopher of religion who works on theories of moral desert and Buddhist ethics. Take your time with this one, and start by brainstorming the possible underlying theories before you settle on a single reconstruction.

Here's a practice that bugs me. In many Buddhist communities, after someone dies their relatives perform a bunch of good deeds — feeding monks, freeing captive animals, giving money — and then ritually transfer the merit of those deeds to the dead person, sometimes by pouring water while reciting a formula. On its face, the dead person is recieving karmic credit for actions they never performed. Some traditions add a wrinkle: the dead themselves must rejoice in or approve of the transfer for it to take effect; others just talk about the merit being sent over to them. Meanwhile, the same traditions insist that each being is the heir of its own deeds and that karma turns on the agent's own intentions. So what theory of moral desert does this practice presuppose? Does it treat desert as a transferable good, like property? Does the rejoicing condition rescue individual responsibility, or does it just relocate the problem to the dead person's mental state? Is there an interpretation on which the practice and the doctrine come out consistent, and if not, which one gives way? I want a reconstruction of the implicit theory, a sharp line between what the practice strictly presupposes and what it merely fits with, and a note on what would change if that presupposed view were replaced.
