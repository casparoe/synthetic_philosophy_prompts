I've noticed that I keep using the same move in arguments about fine-tuning, and I can't tell if it's a principled rule or a shield for not updating. Any time someone says the cosmological constant or the strength of gravity is so improbable that we need a designer, I ask: improbable relative to what distribution? If they can't specify the set of possible values and some probability over them before observation, I don't let the argument shift me at all. My friend who's a physics major got annoyed and said I'm being impossible: you don't need a full model of a minefield to be surprised when a ball lands on the one safe square in a field of thousands. But I think the minefield gives you exactly what the constants don't -- a known field, a known mechanism, and a known process that could have put the ball elsewhere. With the constants, we don't know whether they could have been otherwise, whether the range of possible values is finite or continuous, whether there's any natural measure over them, or whether "could have been otherwise" even makes sense when you only have one universe.

Last week a coworker told me that the fact protons are stable and carbon forms in stars is a sign the universe was tuned for life. I asked him whether he could describe the class of "possible" universes this one was drawn from, and he admitted he couldn't. I stopped engaging exactly there. My worry is that this policy might just be a way of always having an excuse, because for any observation I can demand a distribution and most people won't be able to produce one. So: is a policy of "no specified distribution, no fine-tuning argument" defensible? Is there a weaker version I should adopt -- for example treating fine-tuning as a qualitative "interesting fact" but never as evidence for a designer? Or is my demand for a distribution actually required before fine-tuning can get off the ground at all?
