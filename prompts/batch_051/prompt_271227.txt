I’ve been struck by a divergence between how we evaluate competent human decision-makers and how we train AI systems. In aviation and medicine we have built institutions on the conviction that good performance is a property of the procedure rather than the outcome: a pilot who follows the checklist and is hit by an unforeseeable gust is debriefed without blame, while a pilot who skips the checklist and happens to land safely is disciplined anyway. We even treat the opposite tendency — judging a decision by how it turned out — as a named cognitive error, outcome bias, and train professionals to resist it. Yet the dominant approach to supervising AI systems is resolutely outcome-based: the system is rewarded or penalized according to whether its final answer matches a target, so two systems that produce the same sequence of intermediate steps but land on different final answers receive different training signal, and a lucky answer reached through a fallacious intermediate step is reinforced exactly as much as the same answer reached by sound reasoning. In effect, we have recreated at industrial scale the outcome bias we teach human experts to suppress.

I am trying to determine whether anything actually justifies this asymmetry, or whether it is an unexamined inconsistency in how we think about competence on either side of the human–machine boundary. One might defend it by saying that we have introspective or documentary access to human procedures but not to a model’s; that outcomes are the only scalable, unambiguous learning signal available at training time; or that what we ultimately want from a deployed system is correct output, not a creditable internal process. But each of those defenses seems to have implications for the humans we currently evaluate by process. Please construct the strongest defense of the asymmetry you can, test it against nearby cases — process-based supervision of models that rewards intermediate reasoning steps, judging human professionals purely by outcomes, and hybrid positions — and then say whether we should keep the asymmetry, abandon it in favor of process-based supervision, or replace it with something else. If you recommend keeping it, explain why the reasons that justify outcome-based training of AI do not also justify outcome-based evaluation of pilots and surgeons; if you recommend abandoning it, say concretely what process-based supervision would have to look like and what we would lose by adopting it.
