I've been reading a pop-neuroscience book that claims free will is a timing trick, and it hangs almost everything on one analogy. The conscious you, the book says, is the brain's press secretary: the real decisions get made in the back office, and your felt sense of deciding is just the press release being typed up after the fact so you get to feel like you were in charge. The evidence is Libet's setup — people watch a fast clock and flex their wrist whenever they feel like it, and the readiness potential in the brain starts ramping up about half a second before the movement, while the person doesn't report the urge until a couple hundred milliseconds before. So consciousness gets the news late and then writes the memo as if it had called the meeting.

I find the picture wierdly gripping, but I can't shake the feeling that it's smuggling in the conclusion. The press secretary is a whole separate person from the office that decided; in the brain there's no separate guy who finds out afterward, there's just one system being described at two levels. And actual press secretaries sometimes do shape decisions — their spin changes what the office does next, which would be consciousness feeding back, not just reporting. The book also keeps Libet's free-won't veto in the last sliver of time, which sure sounds like conscious decision still doing work, and it treats an arbitrary wrist flex as the same kind of thing as the slow, deliberative choices I actually worry about. Am I just refusing to accept the science, or does the analogy leak at exactly the points the book needs for its conclusion?
