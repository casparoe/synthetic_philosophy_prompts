A colleague of mine is convinced that imaginative resistance is really an unwillingness thing, not an inability. Her line is: people could imagine the morally deviant stuff in a story perfectly well if they wanted to, but they dig their heels in because accepting it in the fiction feels like endorsing it for real. I'm realy not sure. The asymmetry bugs me: the same person who will happily accept that in a story the sky is green, or that five plus seven equals thirteen, will refuse to accept that in the story, killing your infant because it makes you happy is the right thing to do. Is that refusal a won't or a can't?

I want you to treat this as a testable conceptual claim and design the best test you can to separate the won't hypothesis from the can't hypothesis. Be concrete: what kinds of stories would you use, what exactly would you have participants judge or do, what conditions would you compare, and what pattern of results would count as evidence for each side. Then spend at least as much time on what a clean result would and wouldn't settle—whether you've really isolated ability from mere effort, whether incentives or anonymity are changing competence or just changing what people are willing to report, and whether the word unwillingness is doing real explanatory work or just gets swapped in after the fact. Also, please write the answer in American English.
