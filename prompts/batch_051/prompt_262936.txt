In Daniel Dennett's "Quining Qualia" he has that Maxwell House taster case, Chase and Sanborn: both say they used to like the coffee and now don't, but Chase says his standards changed while Sanborn says his tasters changed. Dennett uses it to show you can't adjudicate between them by appeal to private, subjective tastes. He says, in his own words: "I want to shift the burden of proof, so that anyone who wants to appeal to private, subjective properties has to prove first that in so doing they are not making a mistake."

Now vary it. Suppose we add a third taster, call her Mavis, also a Maxwell House taster. All three agree about their *current* experience: they detect the same slightly bitter aftertaste, and on blind discrimination tests their current acuteness is indistinguishable. The disagreement is entirely about the past. Chase says the current taste is the same as it was six years ago but he's become more discerning; Sanborn says the current taste is different because his affections changed; and Mavis says the current taste is different because the roast changed, while her standards and her tasters both stayed the same. So you've got three people, identical current capacities and identical current reports, but three mutually incompatible histories.

Does adding Mavis make Dennett's argument stronger—because the qualia story has even more trouble explaining how three histories can diverge while current experience matches—or does it actually cut the other way, because now the only way to make sense of the diverging histories given identical current capacities is to posit something like private, subjective taste qualia stored in the past? Work through what Dennett should say in light of the burden-of-proof passage, and tell me whether the modified case changes the conclusion or just changes the dialectic.
