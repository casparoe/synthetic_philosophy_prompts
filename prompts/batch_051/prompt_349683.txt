I'm a software engineer working on a system that extracts explicit commitments from natural-language records — meeting notes, support tickets, code review comments — so we can tell what a writer actually committed to, as opposed to what a reader merely inferred. We keep tripping on a boundary that feels intuitive but resists implementation: the line between what an utterance literally says and what it only suggests. A linguist colleague tells me I'm groping toward Grice's distinction between "what is said" and "what is implicated," and I'd like to turn that distinction into a rule precise enough to use. Please give me a working definition of "what is said": a rule that classifies a candidate proposition associated with an utterance in context as part of what is said, part of what is implicated, or neither.

Here is the case set I want the definition pressure-tested against. For each candidate, apply your definition and say which of the three categories it falls into.
1. In a reference letter: "Jones is punctual and his handwriting is excellent." Candidates: (a) "Jones is punctual and has excellent handwriting"; (b) "Jones is a bad fit for the position."
2. "Some of the tests passed." — "Not all of the tests passed."
3. At dinner: "Can you reach the salt?" — "The speaker wants the salt passed."
4. "They got married and had a baby." — "They got married before the baby was born."
5. "She's a rose." — "She's lovely."
6. Said during a downpour: "Lovely weather we're having." — "The weather is miserable."
7. "He's poor but honest." — "There is a contrast between his poverty and his honesty."
8. In a code review: "Have you considered adding an index to that column?" — "You should add an index to that column."
9. "The current king of France is bald." — "France currently has a king."

For calibration: I'm confident that in (1) the first candidate is said and the second is not; I lean toward treating (2) and (3) as merely implicated; and I have no stable intuition about (4), (7), and (8). I suspect (9) breaks the said/implicated dichotomy, and I'd like your definition to explain how. Please state the definition, run it through all nine items, and flag every place where it gives the wrong answer or no answer; then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts. I care more that the rule does real work than that it matches any particular school.
