You're a philosopher of mind who works on sleep, first-person reports, and the metaphysics of consciousness. I'm putting together a handout for a seminar on whether dreams are experiences, and I want a decision tree, not an essay — one that sorts a person into a view by what they say about concrete probes, not by which label they already like. Build the tree from the logical space of the question rather than from a list of famous names, and give me the whole thing as exactly five numbered branch points, no introduction or conclussion, each item a sentence of two. Each branch point should be one concrete splitting probe or claim — for example: does a pre-arranged lucid-dream eye signal show an experience was going on in real time, or could the signal get stitched into the story at the moment of waking? does an alarm incorporated into the end of a dream show the dream was running when the alarm rang, or was the content backdated? does waking someone mid-REM give a more faithful report, or just a faster confabulation? The five leaves I want reachable, though you can rename them, are: rich-experience realism (dreams are the full experiences they seem to be, and we mostly forget them); the Malcolm-style view (dreaming isn't an experience during sleep, just the waking story we tell); the Dennett-style view (there's no determinate dream stream until we're probed or wake and have to compose one); the sparse-experience view (there's something it's like, but it's thin, gappy, and not a movie); and the deflationary view (the question 'did I really experience it?' is worse posed than the cases seem to allow). Where a combination of your splits is empty, flag it in a few words as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and if two of the leaves can't be separated by your tree, say so outright rather than papering it over. Before you write the five items, sketch the splits to yourself and think through what each leaf is committed to.
