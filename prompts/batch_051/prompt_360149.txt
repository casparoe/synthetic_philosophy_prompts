Hey—I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy, working on Kant's ethics, specifically his absolute prohibition on lying. I keep coming back to the essay, 'On a Supposed Right to Lie from Benevolent Motives' (1797), where he responds to Constant's challenge about the murderer at the door. There's a passage in that essay that I think is under-appreciated, and I want to use it to analyze a real historical case. Kant argues, as I read him, that if you lie to the murderer and say the friend is not in the house, the friend might have already left, and the murderer might meet him on the road and kill him; in that case, you would be legally responsible for the death, because your lie caused the murderer to act on false information. If you tell the truth, you are not responsible for whatever happens, because you have done nothing contrary to duty. I'm paraphrasing, but the causal-asymmetry claim is really the move that interests me: lying makes you causally and legally accountable for bad consequences, whereas truth-telling does not.

So here's my question. Take the Danish rescue of the Jews in October 1943, where ordinary Danes, fishermen, and resistance members lied to the Nazis, forged documents, hid people in boats, and denied knowing the whereabouts of Jews. Analyze that episode through the lens of this specific Kantian argument about causal responsibility for the consequences of lies. What would Kant's argument say the rescuers should have done, and would it actually hold them responsible for the deaths of any Jews who were caught when a lie backfired? More importantly, does the episode reveal something about the causal-asymmetry claim itself—e.g., that it conflates legal and moral responsibility, or ignores the baseline of an unjust regime? I want the case analysis to run both directions: what the theory says about the rescuers, and what the rescuers' situation says about the theory.
