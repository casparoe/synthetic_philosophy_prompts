I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy; the vocabulary I reason with comfortably is that of processes, internal state, side effects, output streams, logging and observability, read-only views of state, and the distinction between a computation and what it prints. I have picked up, mostly secondhand, a debate in philosophy of mind that I would like you to restate in that vocabulary and then assess. The position, as I understand it, is this: conscious experience is real but does no causal work — it accompanies brain activity the way a log stream accompanies a program's execution without ever feeding back into it. The standard objection is that this makes it mysterious why the system ever produces judgments and utterances such as “I am conscious” or “this hurts”: if the feeling never influences the machinery that forms beliefs and generates speech, the fact that the machinery so reliably says things that match the feeling looks like a coincidence, and the position appears to undercut the very evidence on which it rests. I gather this is called epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness, attacked by the paradox of phenomenal judgment.

Please translate that exchange, as faithfully as you can, into the engineering vocabulary I actually use: processes and shared state, read-only access versus write access, log output versus the computation being logged, side effects that are visible but causally inert, dead code, monitors, and so on. For each component of the philosophical position, tell me whether the mapping is clean, where you had to stretch the analogy, and what has no counterpart at all in a program. Then give me a residue report: of the material that does not survive translation, how much is a limit of my vocabulary and how much might be a sign that the philosophical claim was less precise than it appears. I want to know, in particular, whether my instinct that this is just a read-only field that gets logged captures the debate or quietly misses the point.
