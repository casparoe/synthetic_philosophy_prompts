I've reconstructed an argument from Harsanyi's aggregation theorem that seems to prove far too much, and I cannot locate the step I should fight. Each premise looks hard to deny alone, but the conclusion disposes of most of moral and political philosophy at once.

(1) Social evaluation under uncertainty should satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms; otherwise its ranking of risky prospects is dynamically inconsistent. So there is a social value function W over prospects that has the expected-utility form.

(2) Social evaluation should respect unanimity: if every individual is indifferent between two prospects, society should be indifferent; and if every individual weakly prefers one prospect to another, society should not prefer the second.

(3) Each individual's preferences over risky prospects satisfy the same vNM axioms and are represented by utilities u_1,...,u_n.

(4) By Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, (1)–(3) imply W is an affine function of the individual utilities: W = c + Σ a_i u_i.

(5) If the social standpoint is impartial across a fixed population, the weights a_i cannot be arbitrary: symmetry forces them to be equal, so W reduces to the sum — equivalently the average — of individual utilities.

(6) Therefore a coherent, unanimously-respecting, impartial social-evaluation procedure just is utilitarianism, and distributive fairness, rights as side-constraints, or priority for the worst off can only be incoherence or partiality in disguise.

(7) That is too strong: these non-utilitarian commitments are not obviously forms of irrationality or bias.

I would most like to keep (1) and (2); I am prepared to give up (5). But I suspect the real problem is earlier, in how the theorem is being applied. Diagnose the argument.
