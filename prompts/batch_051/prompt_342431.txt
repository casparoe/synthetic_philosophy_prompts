But when a mine collapses or a child falls down a well, we spend whatever it takes to save an identified person, even if it's far more per life than we would spend on prevention. You're a professional philosopher working on risk, value, and public policy. Please think carefully: what theory of value, or theories, does this pattern of practice presuppose? Specifically, do these practices strictly presuppose ex post and ex ante values, or are they compatible with several accounts? Is there a coherent way to treat identified lives and statistical lives differently, or does the practice quietly commits us to something like assigning lower value to merely statistical people? Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined, and what would have to change if we replaced the implicit theory with a straightforwardly welfarist, expected-value approach?
