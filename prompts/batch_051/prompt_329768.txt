I am a novelist at work on a book set in a small city-state that has just replaced its elected council with an assembly selected by lot, and I want the new institution to behave on the page the way real institutions behave — clunkily, with capture, drift, and second-order effects — rather than as a fable of citizen virtue or a familiar disaster. The design I have settled on is this. Every two years, 150 adult residents are chosen by a lottery stratified to mirror the city's population in gender, age, neighborhood, income quartile, and level of formal education. They serve for two years and meet one week out of every month. They are paid a salary pegged to the local median income, and their prior employers are legally required to hold their jobs open. Service may be declined only for a narrowly drawn list of caregiving and medical reasons. The assembly votes by public recorded vote on legislation drafted by a professional civil service, and its enactments take effect unless five percent of registered voters call a referendum within ninety days; the law is then overturned only if sixty percent of registered voters turn out and sixty percent of those voting oppose it. Former assembly members are barred for life from standing for elected office and for ten years from working as lobbyists or paid political consultants.

Putting aside the abstract question of whether the arrangement is legitimate, I want to know how the machinery would actually run. What would it select for in practice: who would accept the lottery, who would strain to avoid it, and how would that selection shape the chamber over three or four successive terms rather than just the first one? How would parties, unions, firms, and local media adapt their tactics to a body of part-time, publicly voting, well-compensated amateurs whose members cannot later convert their service into a political career? How would the referendum escape valve be used, and by whom? I would like you to walk through the likely failure modes, including ones a casual observer would miss by looking only at whether laws got passed, and then distinguish between defects that could be fixed by adjusting a specific parameter — the salary, the term length, the public ballot, the post-service bars, the turnout and supermajority thresholds — and defects that seem intrinsic to rule by sortition rather than a quirk of this particular design.
