I write fiction for a living and am thoroughly fluent in the working vocabulary of narrative craft—point of view, narrator reliability, the earned suspension of disbelief, genre expectations, verisimilitude, dramatic economy, the deus ex machina—but only an amateur in formal epistemology. Restate Hume's case that we should never believe a reported miracle on the strength of testimony, piece by piece, in that novelist's vocabulary. For each component—the event's antecedent improbability, the credibility of the reporters, the weighing of the event's unlikeliness against the unlikeliness that the testimony is false, and the standing human appetite for repeating wonders—say what translates cleanly, what must be distorted to fit, and what has no expression at all in the way I habitually think. Then give me your judgment of the residue: does the untranslatable remainder show that my narrative framework lacks a distinction Hume needs, or that Hume's argument rests on something less precise than it looks? There is one asymmetry I especially want examined: my craft is largely the art of making improbable events credible to a reader, which appears to be exactly what Hume says rational belief must refuse. Say whether that parallel is deep or merely verbal, and keep the translation honest rather than smuggling his epistemology in under the cover of craft terms.
