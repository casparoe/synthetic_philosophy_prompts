I keep encountering "mental privacy" in debates about neurotechnology, usually as a right we are told must be protected, but I have to admit I cannot say what condition the phrase picks out. Is it a privacy of brain data, of mental content, of an inner sphere of experience, or of something like noninterference with thought? A definition that protects "thoughts" may not cover raw neural signals; one that protects "neural data" may treat a fatigue sensor and a mind-reading device alike. I want a working definition I can apply to a new device without simply consulting how uneasy it makes me.

Here are the cases I want the definition to sort. (1) A friend notices that I am angry from my face and posture; I am confident this is not a mental-privacy violation. (2) An employer requires warehouse workers to wear a scalp-sensor headband that continuously scores fatigue and attentiveness, discards the raw signal within a day, and decodes no thought content; my gut calls this a violation, but I cannot say what was invaded if no thought was read. (3) A platform infers a user's current mood and depression risk from typing speed and mouse movements, with no neural sensors involved; I do not know whether that should count, and my uncertainty makes me doubt whether the word "mental" is tied to brain measurement at all. (4) A paralyzed person uses an implanted system to spell by attempting to write letters, and the company stores the recorded signals to improve its models; I have no settled view, because the benefit to the user and the exposure of the signal seem to pull in different directions. (5) A court orders a defendant to undergo a brain scan designed to detect whether their brain responds distinctively to crime-scene details that only the guilty would recognize; I want to call this a violation, but I am not sure it is a privacy violation rather than a fair-trial problem. (6) A non-invasive headset reconstructs rough versions of someone's silent inner speech — the running commentary they say only to themselves; this seems to me the clearest violation of the seven. (7) A doctor adjusts a deep-brain stimulator in a way that alters a patient's mood and reported sense of agency, without the patient having asked for the change; this feels like an intrusion on the mind, but perhaps not on privacy.

Propose a definition of mental privacy, or of a mental-privacy violation, that gets these cases right, run it through all seven, and tell me where it returns a verdict I did not expect, where it breaks, and how you would revise it. If the honest conclusion is that the phrase covers several distinct notions, separate them and show which notion does the work in each case.
