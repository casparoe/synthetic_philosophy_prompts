I spent most of the past two months at my mother's bedside during her third round of treatment, and last week two of my closest friends, Marta and Daniel, got into a long argument in the waiting room about what a situation like hers actually calls for from the people around her. Eventually they asked me to referee. I could not, and I have been turning it over ever since. Here is the exchange as faithfully as I can reconstruct it.

Marta: "When someone is suffering the way your mother is, the only response with real moral worth is pity — the direct, uncalculating sharing of another's pain, in which the boundary between one's own interest and the other's welfare briefly disappears, and which moves a person to remove the cause of the pain. Principles, duties, the desire to act rightly: all of that points back at the agent. She is not served by our moral self-examination."

Daniel: "But that boundary never actually disappears; it is felt only by the pitier. Pity is a state of the pitier, and what it most directly motivates is relief of the pitier's own distress at the sight of suffering. That is why pity can be strangely intrusive: the help is shaped by what calms the helper down, not by what the suffering person needs."

Marta: "You are describing pity gone wrong — the wallowing, the performance of compassion, the helper who makes the whole thing about themselves. The fact that an emotion can be corrupted does not discredit it; bad doctors do not discredit medicine. Genuine pity leaves no trace of self in the act. It simply goes to work."

Daniel: "But the distinction between genuine and corrupt pity is precisely what cannot be drawn. Everyone who pities is sure their pity is the genuine kind, and the alleged purity of the motive is invisible from the outside and self-serving from the inside. Even in the genuine case, pity freezes two people into a hierarchy: one is the spectator and benefactor, the other is reduced to a bundle of need. And pity multiplies the very thing it claims to relieve: it adds the pitier's sorrow to the sufferer's without removing the sufferer's."

Marta: "That arithmetic is clever and cold. The real choice in the hospital is not between pity and some spotless respect; it is between being moved and not being moved. If you teach people to distrust pity, what remains is rarely respect. It is discomfort, then avoidance, and then the person suffers alone. So if you want to reject pity, you owe an account of what still gets anyone to the bedside at all."

Daniel: "What remains is an older, more disciplined sympathy: understanding another person's situation accurately without being flooded by it, and acting out of respect for who she is apart from the illness. That treats her as an agent whose dignity survives her suffering, which pity cannot do because it sees nothing but the suffering."

I have since realized that their argument roughly tracks the quarrel between Schopenhauer, for whom pity is the foundation of morality, and Nietzsche, for whom pity is one of its standing dangers, but they were arguing on their own terms and I do not want a verdict inherited from either authority. Please go through the exchange move by move and assess each point: accept it, reject it, or state the qualification under which it holds. Then say which of the two is more right overall, and what the argument as it was actually made leaves out.
