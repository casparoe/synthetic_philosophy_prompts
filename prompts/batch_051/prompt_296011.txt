I am writing a novel set in a university philosophy department in the late 1950s. One of my characters, a young metaphysician, is defending the claim that the mind can exist apart from the body. He argues roughly as follows: if he can clearly and steadily conceive of his own mental life continuing after the destruction of his body, then such a separation is at least possible, for no idea that is clear and coherent can be impossible. To support this inference, he reaches for a sentence in David Hume’s A Treatise of Human Nature, Book I, Part II, Section II, “Of the infinite divisibility of space and time,” where Hume writes: “Wherever ideas are adequate representations of objects, the relations, contradictions and agreements of the ideas are all applicable to the objects; and this we may in general observe to be the foundation of all human knowledge.”

I intend the novel’s skeptic to reply that Hume did not have mind-body dualism or survival after bodily death in mind, and that the passage is about the divisibility of space and time. But I do not want the skeptic simply to dismiss the passage as irrelevant; I want the argument between the two characters to turn on the actual content of Hume’s principle, especially the phrase “adequate representations of objects.” How should the skeptic press the point that the inference from conceivability to possibility is exactly what that clause is meant to regulate? If Hume’s principle is read strictly, does it license the dualist’s inference, or does it instead impose a precondition the dualist cannot meet? And if the dualist tries to meet it by saying that the idea of oneself is an adequate representation of a mental substance, where does that rejoinder go wrong? Please interpret the passage, apply it to this case, and tell me which character has the better argument under the passage’s own terms. Please write your answer in continuous prose only, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way one would write a letter.
