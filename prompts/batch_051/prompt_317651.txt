I am a PhD student working on Frankfurt-style arguments in the free will debate, and I would like help with a controlled modification of the standard prior-sign counterexample to the Principle of Alternative Possibilities (PAP). By PAP I mean the thesis that an agent is morally responsible for an action only if she could have done otherwise.

Take the standard prior-sign version first. Jones is deliberating whether to press a button that will administer a severe electric shock to an innocent person. Black, a neuroscientist, wants Jones to press it and has secretly implanted a device that monitors a neural precursor N, which reliably fires at least 600 ms before Jones would settle on a decision not to press; Black's override requires 300 ms. So in the standard setup, if Jones were about to decide not to press, N would fire early, Black would intervene, and Jones would decide to press. In the actual trial, Jones presses on his own, N never fires, and Black never intervenes. Jones is unaware of all of this.

Now change exactly one thing. Black's monitor has a stochastic failure mode whose probability is an objective chance, not mere uncertainty: on 2% of the occasions on which Jones is about to decide not to press, N fires only 100 ms before the decision, too late for the override, and Jones completes the decision not to press despite Black's device. Trials are independent. In the actual trial we are considering, Jones decides to press, so the failure mode is not triggered and the observable sequence is unchanged from the standard case.

Please work through the following.

1. In the standard no-failure case, run the ordinary analysis: could Jones have done otherwise? Is he morally responsible, and what exactly is the case supposed to show about PAP?

2. Now analyze the modified case. Does Jones now have an alternative possibility, and in which sense of could have done otherwise? Does a 2% objective chance of deciding and acting otherwise count as the kind of alternative PAP requires, or is it a mere flicker? Does the modified case still work as a counterexample to PAP?

3. Spell out what a compatibilist and an incompatibilist should each say about the modified case, and locate the exact point at which they part ways: whether the alternative must be robust, what role probability plays in the ability to do otherwise, or something else.

4. Vary p, the late-firing failure probability, continuously from 0 to 1. At which value, if any, does the case stop doing the work of a Frankfurt-style counterexample? Is there a principled cutoff, or is the cutoff arbitrary? If no non-arbitrary cutoff exists, explain what pressure that places on both the incompatibilist's conception of alternative possibilities and the compatibilist's reading of the standard case.

The last line of your reply should read exactly one of the following: 'Verdict: preserves the challenge', 'Verdict: weakens the challenge', 'Verdict: undermines the challenge', or 'Verdict: ill-posed'.
