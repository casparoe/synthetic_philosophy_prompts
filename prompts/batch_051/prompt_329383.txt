I recently encountered an argument about the Condorcet jury theorem that I find hard to refute, and I would like help locating exactly where it goes wrong. Consider a committee of an odd number of voters that must choose between two policies by majority rule, and suppose exactly one policy is genuinely better. Each voter, independently of the others, is more likely than not to vote for the better policy. Suppose we start with such a committee and add one new voter, who is also independently more likely than not to vote for the better policy, though perhaps only barely (say, with probability 0.51).

The argument is as follows:

(1) In any case where the original committee would have reached the right decision, the added voter is more likely to agree with that right decision than to disagree with it; therefore, adding her can only increase, or at worst preserve, the chance that the final decision is right in such cases.

(2) In any case where the original committee would have reached the wrong decision, the added voter is more likely to vote for the better policy than for the worse one; therefore, adding her can only increase, or at worst preserve, the chance that the final decision is right in such cases.

(3) Since any case in which the original committee reaches a decision is either a case in which its decision is right or a case in which it is wrong, and since the added voter cannot make things worse in either kind of case, adding any better-than-chance voter always increases, or at least never decreases, the committee's reliability.

(4) By induction, then, the larger the committee, the more reliable its majority, provided each added voter is individually better than chance.

But this conclusion cannot be right as stated. A three-member committee whose members are each 90% reliable has a correct majority about 97% of the time; add a fourth voter who is only 51% reliable, resolve ties with a fair coin toss, and the four-member committee is correct only about 92% of the time. So the argument must fail somewhere. Which step is the first to fail, and what precisely is the error at that step? If you believe the argument is actually valid and the counterexample is misdescribed, say so and explain why.
