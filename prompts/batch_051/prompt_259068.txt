I've become convinced that the reason languages tolerate so much homonymy—pitch as tar, pitch as a throw, pitch as a property of a note—is that the lexicon is only under pressure from unrecoverable ambiguity. Two meanings can coexist indefinitely as long as context, prosody, register, or syntactic frame keeps them apart; when they genuinely collide, one reading gets replaced by a synonym or that sense dies out. Surviving homonyms are the safe leftovers, almost never actually ambiguous in the wild. What would count against this? I want the strongest defeaters ordered by how much damage they do, from "this kills the view" to "this is just an awkward fact"—including possible counterexamples, typological patterns, and experimental results. And be explicit about which ones a defender should treat as genuine evidence against the position rather than explain away, and whether the view is so flexible that it can't lose; if so, is that flexibility itself a problem for it?
