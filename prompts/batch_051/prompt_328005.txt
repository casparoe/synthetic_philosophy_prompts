I have been preparing a seminar on the meaning of life, and I keep running into a tension in Camus's The Myth of Sisyphus that has also started appearing in my students' papers. Near the start of the essay, Camus treats the judgment that life is or is not worth living as the one genuinely serious philosophical question, prior to every other question in philosophy. He diagnoses the absurd as the product of a collision between the human demand for meaning, clarity, and unity, and an indifferent universe that supplies none of these; the world is silent, and the longing for a life that is meaningful in that demanding sense is bound to be disappointed. Yet by the end he describes Sisyphus, fully conscious of the futility of his rock, as someone whose lucidity and revolt turn the ordeal itself into a reason to continue, and he insists that we are to picture Sisyphus happy. The second image seems to be doing exactly what the first ruled out: granting a futile, finite life a kind of fulfillment, or at least a reason to live. A contemporary strand of philosophy would press the point. Susan Wolf's fitting-fulfillment view holds that meaning in life is perfectly attainable: it consists of active engagement in projects of objective value to which one is also subjectively attracted. On that view, the lucid rebel — or even Sisyphus, if one grants value to his revolt and his consciousness — would count as having a meaningful life, which seems flatly contrary to the opening of Camus's essay.

I want you to do two things. First, reconstruct the closest thing to an honest reconciliation of the two Camus passages without dismissing the ending as mere rhetoric: what exactly is Sisyphus's happiness, and how can it be compatible with the claim that life has no meaning in the sense humans crave? Second, adjudicate between Camus and the contemporary fitting-fulfillment account: are they talking past one another because Camus denies a cosmic, God-given meaning while Wolf offers only a local, within-life meaning, or is one of them changing the subject, or is there a substantive disagreement about whether a consciousness of futility can itself be a source of value and meaning? Lay out the candidate reconciliations, distinguish the readings that are faithful to the text from the ones that amount to special pleading, and explain what each genuine reconciliation costs Camus's stated position. End with a final line in this exact form: \answer{...}, where the braces contain a short phrase — at most three words — stating your overall verdict on whether the two positions can be reconciled without special pleading.
