I am a PhD student in information science, and I work with a human-factors research group that studies hospital care. My part of the project involves coding field notes and interview transcripts in which clinicians deal with equipment—infusion pumps, medication carts, charting software, barcode scanners. Each week I make the same classification decision many times: for a given episode in the notes, is this a breakdown in the battery of distinctions from Heidegger's analysis of tool use, and if so, what kind, and what should happen to it in our codebook?

Our group's current practice is to label almost any complaint, reflection, or pause around technology as a breakdown, which I think erases distinctions we need. But whatever replaces it must be usable by coders who have not read Heidegger, and it must be fast enough for our weekly batch; we have roughly an hour per ten episodes, the coders have no phenomenological training, and the human-factors lead has to be able to understand, audit, and sign off on the protocol. The classifications feed into redesign recommendations that hospital safety committees review, so a procedure that flattens distinctions has real downstream cost. I need the conceptual work done now, once, and translated into a rule-governed procedure.

Act as an expert in phenomenological philosophy of technology and in qualitative research methods. Think through the problem carefully: start by brainstorming edge cases and contrast cases that would break a naive version of the procedure, then produce the finished protocol.

The deliverable is a reusable decision procedure for a single recorded episode of equipment use or non-use, presented as an ordered list of decision points, a flowchart in words. For each step, specify the question the coder asks, the textual and behavioral indicators that would count as evidence for each answer, and where the coder should go next. The procedure must sustain these distinctions, and should state them in operational language at the top so a coder can use them without the theory:

- ready-to-hand: equipment in smooth use withdraws into the performance of the task; the agent is oriented through the thing toward the work, not at the thing.
- unready-to-hand: the flow of activity is interrupted, but the person remains within the task and its equipmental context; the tool, its failure, or its absence becomes troublesome.
- present-at-hand: the person steps back and inspects the thing as a discrete object with properties—screens, menus, casing, displays—treating it as something looked at rather than worked through.
- the three standard ways the unready-to-hand announces itself: something wrong with the tool itself, such that it becomes conspicuous as damaged, dull, or refusing its ordinary function; something needed is missing, so an absence intrudes into the work; or something is in the way and will not be ignored, standing in the middle of the task.

Beyond classification, the procedure should produce a final routing decision: flag the episode for the design team, route it to organizational or training concerns, record it as normal skilled coping that needs no intervention, or mark it cannot classify and send it to the resident philosopher, with an account of exactly what additional information or discussion would settle it.

Then run the procedure on two episodes:

1. A field note describes a nurse entering a medication order. After a short pause she leans toward the workstation, inspects the keyboard and monitor, and says, "Why does it ask for the order in this order?" She then reads each menu label aloud, one by one, as if studying the screen.

2. A note describes a nurse walking to a supply room for a pulse-oximetry sensor. She keeps talking through the patient's treatment plan on the way, takes a sensor, and remarks, "We're always one sensor short when we need one." She then returns to the patient and attaches it without further comment.

Please be explicit about where the procedure refuses to decide, and be willing to conclude that one of these episodes is not a design-relevant breakdown even though our current coding would claim it is. I need the procedure to be honest about that limitation.
