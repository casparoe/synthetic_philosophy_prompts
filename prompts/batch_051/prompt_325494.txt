I teach an upper-level graduate seminar in the philosophy of history, and we have been working through the problem of causal multiplicity. Last week we discussed Mill’s A System of Logic, Book III, Chapter X, on what he calls the plurality of causes. There he treats the familiar case of a person’s death, which may be brought about by any of several independent antecedents—a fall, poison, starvation, disease—and he is especially interested in the difficulty of assigning the actual cause when several such conditions are present at once. What struck my students is how close that difficulty is to the historian’s notion of overdetermination, and yet how different the practice looks. A historian may claim, for instance, that the outbreak of the First World War was overdetermined: that even if the assassination at Sarajevo had not occurred, some other crisis would have produced a general European war within a few years, given the alliance commitments, mobilization timetables, and imperial competition. I want to push them to say whether such a claim can be tested at all, and if so how.

Here is the assignment I am drafting. I want you to suppose you have to design the strongest possible empirical test of a specific overdetermination claim in history. Choose a manageable historical example—not necessarily World War I—where scholars assert that an outcome was overdetermined by two or more independent causal factors. State the claim precisely enough that it is clear what would count as a failure. Then design a test: what bodies of evidence, comparative cases, or historical micro-analyses could bear on whether the outcome really was robust across changes in one of the cited causes? Say what result would support the overdetermination claim, what result would undermine it, and what intermediate results would be ambiguous. Finally, identify what the test could not settle even in principle—for example, whether the counterfactual dependence can be established for a singular event, whether the nominated alternative causes are genuinely independent, or whether the explanatory language of overdetermination is doing more than retrospective narrative work.
