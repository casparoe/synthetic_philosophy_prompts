Okay here's an asymmetry that's been bugging me. Most people now say ASL or BSL are languages, full stop, same as English. But describe a deaf kid who never had access to an established sign language and built up a home sign system with their hearing family—hundreds of stable signs, a consistent word order, used daily for years—and a lot of people hesitate to call it a language. They'll say it's communication, or gesture, or a code. Say the same about a village sign language that's only a couple generations old and people want to know if it's 'a real language'. Meanwhile a spoken language isolate with three elderly speakers is a language and nobody blinks. So the label isn't tracking number of speakers, and it isn't tracking whether the thing is spoken. What, if anything, justifies drawing the line between a sign language like ASL and a home sign system like that? Is there a difference in kind—community transmission, a community of native signers, grammatical complexity, something else—or is this just a leftover status distinction from the old prejudice that real language has to be spoken? Give me the strongest principled criterion you can, then stress-test it against the cases that make it uncomfortable, and tell me honestly whether the line should stay or go.
