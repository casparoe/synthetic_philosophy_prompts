Here's a principle I've been using and I'd like a professional philosopher or clinical ethicist to check it. Before you answer, do some brainstorming and steelman it first, then attack it fairly.

I call it 'honest randomisation'. A physician is only permitted to randomise a patient if, before the coin is tossed, they would honestly be willing to have their own child receive whichever arm the coin picks, with the same information, and with no private plan to switch arms if they don't like the assignment. My thought is that ordinary equipoise is too weak: a doctor can be 'genuinely uncertain' in the abstract while still having a clear preference for what their own kid would get, so the trial hides something. I think honest randomisation captures what informed consent is really reaching for.

Is this a genuine ethical standard or just a theatrical symmetry test? Does it collapse into clinical equipoise, or does it add something extra? Tell me the strongest objections and whether any of them are fatal rather than just uncomfortable.
