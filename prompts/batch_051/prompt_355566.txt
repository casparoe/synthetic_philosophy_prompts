I'm a team lead and this week I had to mediate between three people after a client meeting went sideways, and now I'm going back and forth on honor cultures, dignity cultures, and victimhood cultures trying to figure out wether I handled it right. Our new conduct policy has a clause everyone keeps quoting: "Employees shall not subject one another to conduct that a reasonable person would regard as demeaning, humiliating, or intimidating, and conflicts about respect shall be taken to Human Resources rather than pursued through personal confrontation." A senior teammate corrected a newer teammate in front of a client; it was blunt but, by our office's normal standards, not abusive. The newer guy grew up in a family with strong honor norms and says a public shaming like that can't be repaired by filing an HR ticket—the only thing that counts is a public retraction or apology in front of the same people. He told her it had to be settled between them, and HR is calling that a threat. Meanwhile a third coworker says the whole episode made her feel unsafe and thinks HR should treat the senior teammate's "vibe" as the real problem. The people who drafted this clause were clearly picturing ordinary dignity-culture workplace snubs, not this. Walk me through what the clause requires or permits here on: (a) a literal reading of the words, (b) a purposive reading, (c) what the drafters would have said if they'd thought about honor-based responses to public correction, and (d) what a reasonable reader would take it to mean. Where do those readings diverge? Does picking any one of them just smuggle in one culture's standard as if it were neutral? And if you had to rule on it, should your answer change if you thought one of these three cultural takes was morally better, or is that the kind of thing the interpretation shouldn't defer to?
