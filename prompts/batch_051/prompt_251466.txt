Draft the joint statement that a good-faith adversarial collaboration between an appraisal theorist of love and a bestowal theorist of love could both sign. The two camps are understood as follows. The appraisal view holds that love is fundamentally a response to value the beloved already possesses—whether their qualities, their rational nature, or both—so that love can be fitting or unfitting in roughly the way admiration can be, and the reasons for loving someone are in place before the love begins. The bestowal view holds that love is not chiefly an answer to antecedent value but a source of it: in loving, the lover confers a distinctive kind of value on the beloved, or constitutes the beloved's value to the lover, so that love's reasons are in an important sense created rather than detected.

Write the document in two parts.

Part 1 is a numbered list of agreed claims that both camps can sign without strain. The claims should be non-vacuous and should cover: the core phenomenon the two camps are trying to explain; the cases both sides treat as data—including a parent's love for a newborn, love that survives the loss of the traits that first attracted the lover, and the apparent non-substitutability of the beloved; what each side owes an account of; and any distinctions both accept, such as between what initiates love, what sustains it, and whether it is fitting. Do not paper over genuine differences in order to reach agreement.

Part 2 is a disagreements table with four columns: the question; the appraisal answer, phrased so an appraisal theorist would own it; the bestowal answer, phrased so a bestowal theorist would own it; and whether the disagreement is substantive or merely verbal. At minimum the table must address the following rows. (i) Whether the beloved's value is prior to or partly constituted by the lover's love. (ii) Whether love can be mistaken or inappropriate in something like the way a belief can be false. (iii) The silent-replacement case: the beloved is unknowingly replaced by a qualitatively identical person with the same memories and dispositions—does the lover have the same reasons to love the replacement? (iv) Whether a statement of the form "I love her because..." can report a reason for love rather than merely a cause.

After the table, identify the cruxes: the two or three sub-questions such that, if they were settled, at least one side would revise its view. For each crux, specify a concrete case or argument that both sides agree bears on it, and record each side's prediction about it in advance. At least one crux must be the silent-replacement case, and at least one must involve the beloved's loss of the properties that initially attracted the lover. End by stating which residual disagreements, if any, you judge to be purely verbal, and which would survive even if both sides accepted all the same verdicts on the cases.
