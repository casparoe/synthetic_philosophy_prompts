In my philosophy of law seminar the punishment debate has been frozen for years. The retributivists keep returning to the scapegoat case—punishing one innocent person to stop a riot is wrong even if it works—while the consequentialists keep returning to the desert-island case, where the last murderer is punished even though no one is left to deter. Same moves every year, no one budges. My hunch is that the two sides are answering different questions: the retributivist is asking what a wrongdoer deserves, and the consequentialist is asking what a community should do, given that it must do something, about wrongdoing. If that's right, the debate isn't a clash of values at all—it's a failure to notice the two questions. Is that diagnosis right? If it is, what would count as progress; if it isn't, what kind of disagreement is it really—verbal, about standard cases, about starting intuitions, or a hidden empirical question about what punishment actually does? Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no introduction or conclusion.
