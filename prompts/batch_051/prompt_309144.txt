I sit on my country's historical reconciliation commission as a community representative, not as a scholar or lawyer, and I have been asked to circulate a written opinion before our final report is drafted. The commission has spent the past two years documenting what happened to the Meri minority between 1962 and 1974. The factual record is no longer in serious dispute: the army and allied militias killed between thirty and fifty thousand Meri, burned villages, demolished nearly every Meri religious structure, banned the Meri language in schools and public offices, and removed thousands of Meri children to state-run boarding schools where they were given new names and punished for speaking their mother tongue. The Meri survived, but they are now roughly a fifth of their former population, and only a few thousand people still speak the language. We have already agreed that the report will describe the killings and expulsions as crimes against humanity. The open question is whether the report should also say that what happened to the Meri was genocide.

Our legal advisors are split. Everyone accepts that, as a legal category, genocide requires something more than mass killing—roughly, an intent to destroy the group, in whole or in part, as such. No document has surfaced that states such an intent. The wartime planning memos speak of security, pacification, and national integration, and some of our advisors argue the regime wanted to absorb the Meri rather than eliminate them, pointing out that many of the removed children were expected to re-emerge as loyal citizens. Other commissioners, along with the Meri survivors' organizations, insist that attacking the clergy, the teachers, the language, and the children exactly is what destroying a people looks like in practice, and that refusing the word genocide would merely continue the regime's euphemisms. I need you to draft the decision memo I will circulate before our next meeting, addressed to the full commission. It should be no more than two or three pages, and it should recommend a concrete course of action: use the term in the final report, avoid it, or use it only in a clearly explained and qualified way. Please take seriously the strongest case on the losing side, and try to work through what the label actually adds to the historical record, what we risk if our evidence of intent is only inferential, and where the line should be drawn between destroying a people and crushing them into assimilation.
