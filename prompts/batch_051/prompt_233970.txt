I'm a retired evolutionary biologist and I've gotten into the philosophy of AI risk over the last couple years. One thing that keeps bugging me is how sloppy people are with the phrase "natural selection favors AIs over humans." Some people clearly mean literal Darwinian selection — heritable variation, differential reproduction, the whole thing — and some clearly mean market competition between AI companies, and some just seem to mean "the world is drifting toward more powerful AI whether we like it or not." I'd like you to build me a decision tree that sorts the major positions on this question: does natural selection favor AIs over humans?

I want the tree built from the logical space, not from a list of labels. So first work out the branching questions, then locate the actual positions in that space, then note which combinations of answers have nobody sitting in them and say why — is the combo incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored?

The branching nodes should each be a question a person could answer from knowing basic evolutionary biology, without already knowing the AI-risk literature. The distinctions I care about, and want in the tree, are: whether the AIs themselves are replicators or are better thought of as vehicles/tools of human replicators; whether the relevant selection is literal Darwinian selection or a looser economic/cultural analog; whether increased fitness for an AI correlates with power-seeking and self-preservation or with being docile and reliable enough that people keep running you; and timescale — does selection for AIs' interests only kick in once humans are out of the loop, or can it already be pushing in one direction?

Leaves should include at least these, in plain descriptive names: the domestication analogy view, the extended phenotype view, the deflationary "this is all just metaphor" view, the inevitable takeover view, and the view that selection actually favors safe, aligned AIs because dangerously misaligned systems get caught and shut down quickly. If your tree can't cleanly seperate any two of these, say so instead of forcing a fake branch. Five or six branching questions is probably the right size, and the audience is a reading group of retired physical and life scientists, so you can presuppose evolution but not the AI risk jargon.
