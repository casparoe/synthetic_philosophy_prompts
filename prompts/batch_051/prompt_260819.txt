At my housing co-op's annual meeting, two members ended up in an argument I was asked to referee, and I couldn't settle it. Our bylaws say that any amendment requires a two-thirds majority of members present at a meeting. A member moved to amend that exact clause to say that any amendment requires only a simple majority of members present. The vote was 55% in favor of the change and 45% against. Elena argued that the change passed, because the entire point of the amendment was to replace the two-thirds requirement with majority rule, and it would be circular to use the old requirement to defeat an attempt to replace that requirement. Marcus replied that when the votes were counted, the only amendment rule in force was the two-thirds one, and a new rule cannot govern the very act that creates it. Elena answered that Marcus's reasoning makes the amendment clause impossible to weaken, since any move to lower the threshold is always judged by the higher threshold it is trying to escape. Marcus responded that if Elena were right, no proposal to change the amendment rule could ever fail: whatever lower threshold a proposal introduced would automatically count as satisfied whenever the proposal received that much support. Please adjudicate their exchange point by point -- which of their claims are correct and which are not, what they are both missing, and whether the 55% vote actually amended the bylaws.
