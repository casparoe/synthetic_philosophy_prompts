For the past year or so I have been thinking on my own about whether everything that exists is ultimately physical. I have no academic background in this, and I have mostly worked through it by reading around the edges and arguing with friends. Two of those friends — one a civil engineer, the other a high-school mathematics teacher — fell into a long argument about it in front of me last weekend, and I cannot tell how well either of them argued. I reconstruct the exchange as faithfully as I can.

(1) The engineer opened with what he called his default commitment: everything that exists is either physical, made of physical parts, or fully determined by the physical. He said this is what rules out souls, ghosts, and immaterial minds.

(2) The teacher asked what he meant by physical. She said that if he means the entities and forces described by current physics, then his claim is almost certainly false right now, since physics is openly incomplete.

(3) He clarified: he meant a completed physics — the theory physical inquiry is converging on, whatever it ends up containing.

(4) She said that definition drains the position of content. If a complete theory turned out to need ghosts, ectoplasm, or immaterial minds, then by his definition those things would count as physical, and everything is physical would be preserved no matter what is discovered. A claim that cannot fail in principle, she argued, is not a claim about the world at all.

(5) He objected that completed physics is not an anything-goes category. A future physics will be recognizably continuous with present physics: fields, particles, forces, and dynamical laws, merely with better coverage. Ghosts will not make the cut, he said, not by stipulation but because physics has never needed them.

(6) She replied that he had moved the problem into the phrase "recognizably continuous" without eliminating it. Unpacked in terms of current physics, that phrase makes his position probably false; unpacked in terms of the future theory, it makes the position empty; and he has no third way of unpacking it. She added that, by his last move, he was no longer making a claim about what things are composed of but a claim about method — roughly, that only what well-conducted empirical science discovers is real — and that this is a separate, debatable commitment, not something established by the word physical.

The argument trailed off there.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each of the six moves, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then state which parts of each person's overall position are strongest and weakest, and what, if anything, both of them are missing.
