After a long career in the physical sciences, I have lately been reading twentieth-century moral philosophy, and I keep returning to G. E. M. Anscombe's well-known protest against the University of Oxford's decision to award Harry Truman an honorary degree. As I understand her position, she objected on the grounds that Truman had authorized the deliberate killing of noncombatants—above all the inhabitants of Hiroshima and Nagasaki—as a means of forcing a surrender, and that this was therefore murder rather than a painful but permissible act of war; no good consequences, and no justice of the larger cause, could alter the character of the act. Underlying this judgment was her defense of the doctrine of double effect, which permits foreseeing harm but forbids intending it, and which is especially strict about the direct killing of the innocent.

Since her death, the practice of war has changed in ways she did not live to see. Armed drones now allow states to kill small numbers of people at long range with great precision, but some of these strikes—often called signature strikes—are authorized not because the person has been identified as a particular commander or combatant, but because a pattern of behavior, association, or movement has been deemed militant. Proponents argue that this is morally cleaner than the deliberate bombing of cities, because the destruction is narrower and more discriminating. Critics reply that the targeting inference is probabilistic, that the category of combatant has become elastic, and that civilian deaths are too readily treated as an acceptable price of acting on uncertain intelligence.

How do you think Anscombe would have assessed contemporary armed drone warfare in general, and signature strikes in particular? I would like you to treat this as a prediction of her view rather than a general moral verdict. Please extrapolate from the commitments she actually expressed about Truman, about the killing of the innocent, and about intention and foresight, and say whether you think she would have regarded the differences in scale and precision between the atomic bombings and a modern drone strike as morally decisive or as secondary. Do not hesitate to note places where her principles would yield an uncomfortable conclusion for present-day defenders or critics of targeted killing.
