I am drafting a chapter of a novel in which a tutor walks a bright but obstinate child through a short piece of reasoning, and the child grants each step as it is offered and yet refuses the conclusion. A philosopher friend has pointed me to Lewis Carroll's short dialogue "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" as the sharpest treatment of exactly this difficulty. The passage below comes after the Tortoise has declared that he accepts A and B but declines the hypothetical proposition, and has challenged Achilles to force him, logically, to accept Z. I have been working through it, and I am not sure how deep the point runs.

    "Plenty of blank leaves, I see!" the Tortoise cheerily remarked.  "We shall need them all!"  (Achilles shuddered.)  "Now write as I dictate:—
         (A)  Things that are equal to the same are equal to each other.
         (B)  The two sides of this Triangle are things that are equal to the same.
         (C)  If A and B are true, Z must be true.
         (Z)  The two sides of this Triangle are equal to each other."
    "You should call it D, not Z," said Achilles. "It comes next to the other three. If you accept A and B and C, you must accept Z."
    "And why must I?"
    "Because it follows logically from them. If A and B and C are true, Z must be true. You don't dispute that, I imagine?"
    "If A and B and C are true, Z must be true," the Tortoise thoughtfully repeated. "That's another Hypothetical, isn't it? And, if I failed to see its truth, I might accept A and B and C, and still not accept Z, mightn't I?"
    "You might," the candid hero admitted; "though such obtuseness would certainly be phenomenal. Still, the event is possible. So I must ask you to grant one more Hypothetical."
    "Very good.  I'm quite willing to grant it, as soon as you've written it down.  We will call it
         (D)  If A and B and C are true, then Z must be true.
    Have you entered that in your note-book?"
    "I have!" Achilles joyfully exclaimed, as he ran the pencil into its sheath. "And at last we've got to the end of this ideal race-course! Now that you accept A and B and C and D, of course you accept Z."
    "Do I?" said the Tortoise innocently. "Let's make that quite clear. I accept A and B and C and D. Suppose I still refused to accept Z?"

My difficulty is not with the surface mechanics. I can see that Achilles adds the missing hypothetical as a new premise, the Tortoise accepts it, and the same problem reappears one level up, so that the notebook fills with further letters indefinitely. What I cannot judge is how much philosophical weight the passage can bear. Is the Tortoise making a demand that could in principle be met, or is the dialogue designed to show that no amount of added premises can do the work that a rule of inference does? If the latter, what exactly is the difference between accepting a conditional as a premise and accepting it as a licence to move from A and B to Z? I am also unsure where this leaves the justification of deduction: does the passage show that deduction rests on something that cannot itself be justified without circularity, or only that one must distinguish the rules by which an argument is drawn from the statements that occur inside it? I ask because I want the child's resistance in my chapter to express a genuine logical pressure rather than mere stubbornness, so I need to know precisely what Carroll is showing here, and what he is not showing.
