Heres an asymmetry i keep tripping over. If a head of state stands up and apologises for something the state did ninety years ago, like forcibly removing children from their families, most of us don't treat that as empty theater — we think the apology was owed, that it can be sincere, and that it changes something in the present, even though the person speaking didn't do the deed and most current citizens weren't alive. But if my neighbour discovered his great-grandfather had swindled an ancestor of mine and came over to apologise for it, I'd think he was play-acting; at most he owes me restitution, because its not his act to apologise for. So why does the state get treated differently? Both the head of state and the neighbour are just the current face of a chain that reaches back to the wrong. Is the difference that a state is a continuing agent in a way a family line is not? That the state inherited benefits and liabilities, so its current members have a standing descendants lack? That the state holds its citizens in a coercive relationship and can speak for them in a way a private person can't? Or is the apology actually doing something else — a present declaration that the polity repudiates the act — in which case we should stop thinking anyone is apologising for the deed at all. Give me the strongest principle you can find that generates both the state and the neighbour judgment, test it against nearby cases (a company apologising for its founder's practices, a church for centuries-old abuse, a family that keeps the money and says nothing), and tell me honestly whether the asymmetry survives or whether one side should go. And when you answer, separate what's actually settled in the philosophy of collective responsibility from what's genuinely contested, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
