I'm a retired ecologist and only recently started reading philosophy of well-being. I'm drawn to the sort of objective-list view that puts novelty—genuine encounters with new places, problems, people—among the basic goods, not just as a tool for staving off boredom. It helps explain why a life of pleasant routines doesn't obviously exhaust what we want, and it matches my memory of fieldwork: the best stretches were the firsts. But here's my problem. Since I retired my life has shrunk to the same garden, same walks, same handful of books, and from the inside I can't honestly say this life is worse than the adventurous years. My firm intuition is that a quiet repetitive life can be just as good as a varied one, provided the days themselves are good. I don't want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes—is it, for instance, a hedonic-adaptation effect, or a failure of imagination about how much better the alternative would be? And what it would take for the theory to be amended instead—perhaps novelty is a basic good only for certain temperaments, or perhaps my repetitive days are less repetitive than I take them to be. Is there a reading on which both survive?
