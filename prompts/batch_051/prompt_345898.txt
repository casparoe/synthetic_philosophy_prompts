A guy from my old comedy troupe just got quietly dropped from a venue's lineup after screenshots of some old tweets circulated, and I keep getting sucked into arguments about whether that counts as "punishment." One friend, who talks as if John Stuart Mill settles the whole thing, gave me this summary of On Liberty:

"Mill's view is that social penalties are the good kind of penalty. The state only gets to interfere with conduct that violates actual rights, but society can punish a much wider range of conduct just through opinion—he literally says that someone who is hurtful to others without violating their rights 'may be justly punished by opinion, though not by law.' So firing, boycotts, deplatforming, public contempt, cutting someone off: all of that is just the public exercising its own liberty. The only punishment that limits liberty is legal punishment, so Mill's worry is only the state. If anything, he'd say cancel culture is society handling things the way he preferred, without bringing in the law."

I went and read the chapter, because this felt too neat. Here's the passage he was leaning on, from Chapter IV of On Liberty:

"The acts of an individual may be hurtful to others, or wanting in due consideration for their welfare, without going the length of violating any of their constituted rights. The offender may then be justly punished by opinion, though not by law. [...] In all such cases there should be perfect freedom, legal and social, to do the action and stand the consequences."

Is my friend's summary fair to that passage, and to what Mill's doing in the chapter? I want it seperated out: which parts are accurate, which parts are overstatements or read into the text, and what Mill actually denied. I'm inclined to think he's right that the quote exists but wrong about what Mill means by "opinion" and "legal and social," but I keep getting tangled whenever I try to articulate it. Also, does the deplatforming conclusion survive if we correct the summary?
