Start from the Rawlsian view that envy is normally a vice, but can be excusable when a social arrangement unjustifiably injures the self-respect of the less favored. Operationalize that view with four conditions for treating an episode of envy as a signal of injustice rather than as a private vice: (1) the envied advantage is large and visible; (2) it flows from a rule or process that cannot be defended to the person without it; (3) the envier's response aims at the arrangement rather than at undermining the envied person; and (4) what is injured is standing or self-respect rather than mere material comfort. Now consider a comfortably paid academic who learns that a colleague hired at the same rank under a since-abolished "market exception" policy receives a much larger salary and permanently reduced duties, and that this disparity was never justified to the rest of the faculty. She rejects the label of envy, calls her feeling resentment at an unaccountable decision procedure, and proposes that the exception be replaced by a transparent salary scale that would also benefit her; but she admits, when pressed, that part of what stings is being valued at a lower rate. Is this excusable envy in Rawls's sense, or is it private grievance wearing the language of principle? Work through the four conditions, note where they conflict or underdetermine the verdict, and say whether the conditions themselves need amending.
