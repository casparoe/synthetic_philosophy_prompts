I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am working on a piece about what the word "matter" has come to mean. I have been reading Lucretius's De Rerum Natura in William Ellery Leonard's translation, and I would like to test his account of matter against a case from modern cosmology.

In Book I Lucretius argues that nature consists of exactly two kinds of thing:

All nature, then, as self-sustained, consists
Of twain of things: of bodies and of void
In which they're set, and where they're moved around.

He then gives a test for telling the two apart:

But, if intangible and powerless ever
To keep a thing from passing through itself
On any side, 'twill be naught else but that
Which we do call the empty, the inane.
Again, whate'er exists, as of itself,
Must either act or suffer action on it,
Or else be that wherein things move and be:
Naught, saving body, acts, is acted on;
Naught but the inane can furnish room. And thus,
Beside the inane and bodies, is no third
Nature amid the number of all things—

A little later, in the section on the character of the atoms, he says that the ultimate bodies are "solid, without a void," and that they cannot be torn apart by penetration from within.

From this I extract the atomist criteria for being matter: (1) body is tangible — it can touch and be touched, and it blocks or checks other bodies from passing through it; (2) body is solid, at least in its ultimate parts, with no void inside; (3) body is what acts and suffers action, while the void merely gives room. Lucretius takes the dichotomy to be exhaustive: whatever is intangible and powerless to keep things from passing through it is void, and there is no third nature.

Now the case. Cosmologists infer that most of the gravitating stuff in the universe is dark matter: it neither emits, absorbs, nor scatters light, and its particles are thought to be collisionless. A dark matter particle would pass through the Earth, and through other dark matter, without measurable resistance. Dark matter is not solid in any familiar sense, and it fails Lucretius's blocking test outright. Yet physicists call it matter, and not unreasonably: it gravitates, it clumps, its mass bends light, and it seeds the formation of galaxies. In Lucretius's vocabulary it acts and suffers action through gravity, but it is not tangible and it cannot block passage.

Is dark matter matter in Lucretius's sense? Go through the criteria one by one and say where they underdetermine the verdict. Where dark matter fails a criterion, say whether the failure indicts dark matter or the criterion. Then tell me whether the body/void dichotomy should be revised — for instance, by replacing impenetrability with gravitational interaction, or with energy and momentum — and what the repaired account would say about dark matter and, if you think it helps, about photons. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question.
