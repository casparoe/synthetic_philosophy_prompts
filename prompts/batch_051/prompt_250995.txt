I cover the science of political disagreement for a general-interest magazine, and I'm writing up my own framework after a year of reporting on why ordinary people sort themselves into such hostile camps. A political scientist I sometimes ask to look over drafts says my set of commitments has a buried contradiction, but she refuses to tell me where, and now I can't tell which of these are real prblems and which are just surface-level friction.

For the record, I'm committed to: (a) the median conservative and the median liberal mostly argue in good faith—they generally say what they actually believe, not what they calculate it is useful to say; (b) before criticizing an opponent's claim, a writer should reconstruct the strongest plausible version of it and aim the criticism at that steelman; (c) identity-protective cognition is the default in political reasoning—people process political information to protect their group's standing—and this is roughly symmetric across the spectrum; (d) a large chunk of polarization is informational: people swim in different media streams, and if you genuinely improved the average person's information diet with better corrections and less engagement bait, it would meaningfully shrink the affective gap; (e) a fairly small set of professional communicators—some cable hosts, some elected officials, some platform regulars—deliberately push claims they know are misleading, and they are the real engine of the worst polarization; (f) the underlying cognitive failings are symmetric, so the left is not more rational, open-minded, or good-faith than the right. Do these positions actually hang together? Which pairs are in genuine tension, which only look like they conflict, and what is the cheapest revision? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equal, and for each say what would have to give and what it would cost.
