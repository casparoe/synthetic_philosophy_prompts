I keep returning to a passage in Stevan Harnad’s 1990 paper “The Symbol Grounding Problem” while trying to settle what would actually change my mind about one of its central claims. After insisting that merely behaving in accordance with a rule is not yet symbolic, Harnad writes:

‘The explicitly represented symbolic rule is part of a formal system, it is decomposable (unless primitive), its application and manipulation is purely formal (syntactic, shape-dependent), and the entire system must be semantically interpretable, not just the chunk in question. An isolated ("modular") chunk cannot be symbolic; being symbolic is a systematic property.’

The position I want pressure-tested is the one that falls out of this: for a system to have intrinsic semantics rather than meanings merely projected onto it by an observer, it is not enough that its states be interpretable by us; the system itself must implement a systematic, non-arbitrary grounding relation between at least its elementary symbols and something nonsymbolic, such as learned or innate categorical invariants over sensory projections. On this view, any purely formal system that manipulates arbitrary tokens—however large, fluent, or useful—is in principle still a Chinese/Chinese dictionary, and the semantics we attribute to it remain parasitic on ours.

I am not asking for a rival theory of machine understanding. What I want is a careful map of what would count against this position: which empirical results, formal results, or conceptual arguments would be decisive, which merely awkward, and which escape routes are still live rather than merely logically possible. For instance, would a system that passed the Total Turing Test without any dedicated grounding mechanism be a refutation, or would Harnad simply say the grounding must have been implicit somewhere in its learned capacities? Would a demonstration that grounding can be acquired through linguistic interaction alone, with no separately specified nonsymbolic categorical representations, undermine the bottom-up requirement? And is there a serious risk that the position collapses into a definitional claim, so that nothing could count against it? I want both what the view’s defenders would themselves concede as a defeater and, separately, what you honestly find most persuasive.
