For a seminar on the place of emotion in a well-lived life, I've been asked to write down my position as a set of claims so the rest of the group can stress-test it before I present. I've been trying to combine a broadly Stoic suspicion of anger with a broadly romantic conviction that some painful emotions are not mere costs but constituents of the good life, and I want to know whether the combination is stable. My claims, as they currently stand:

(1) Emotions are not disturbances or mere instruments. A good human life is partly constituted by its emotional shape — by feeling joy, love, sorrow, fear, and the rest at the right times, toward the right objects, and in something like the right measure. A person who acted correctly but never felt anything would be missing part of the good, not merely missing a useful tool.

(2) Grief at the death of someone deeply loved is a fitting response to a real loss. I would not, on reflection, choose to be the sort of person who loves but never grieves. The grief is not a regrettable tax on attachment; it is part of what it is for the attachment to reach all the way into one's life.

(3) Anger is different. The person I aspire to be would not be angry even toward someone who has genuinely wronged me. Anger's characteristic impulse is retributive — to make the offender suffer or be diminished — and I cannot see that this impulse adds anything worth preserving to a clear-sighted response to a wrong. The ideal, for me, is not moderated anger but its elimination.

(4) I also hold a broadly cognitive standard of appropriateness: an emotion is appropriate when, and because, the world is the way the emotion presents it as being. This standard is meant to apply across the emotions, fear, grief, and anger alike. Whether an emotion passes it has nothing to do with whether the emotion is pleasant or useful.

(5) The right way to handle an inappropriate emotion is not to suppress it by effort but to revise the underlying evaluation: once one genuinely sees that the danger was not real or that the good was not lost, the emotion should subside on its own. And where an emotion is appropriate, the right response is to feel it rather than to blunt it.

Please audit this set. I want to know which pairs of claims genuinely pull against each other and which only appear to, with the genuine tensions ranked by severity. For each genuine tension, say what the cheapest repair would be and what it costs. Then tell me which single revision would do the most to make the whole position cohere.
