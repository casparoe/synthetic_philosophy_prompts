I am writing a term paper on Hobbes's Leviathan for a seminar on the social contract, and I have reconstructed an objection that I cannot settle. Hobbes's story begins from the claim that in the state of nature, where there is no common power to overawe everyone, covenants are empty: no one can rationally be assured that the other parties will perform, so a covenant gives no security and offers no reliable path out of conflict. His proposed remedy is that the multitude covenants, each with every other, to lay down their rights and authorize a sovereign, whose coercive power then supplies the assurance that makes future covenants dependable. But here is the difficulty. The founding covenant is itself made before any sovereign exists; it is made, in other words, in precisely the condition in which, by Hobbes's own standard, covenants are empty. Formally, the objection I have in mind runs as follows:

(1) In the state of nature, no covenant can be relied upon, because there is no coercive common power and therefore no assurance of others' performance.
(2) A commonwealth by institution—as distinct from one by acquisition, where a conqueror's sword is already present—comes into being only through a covenant of each with all to authorize a sovereign.
(3) The covenant mentioned in (2) is made while the parties are still in the state of nature.
(4) By (1), that covenant cannot be relied upon.
(5) So the founding covenant cannot do the work of creating the sovereign it is supposed to create.
(6) Therefore, either sovereignty by institution is impossible, or the state of nature is not so hostile to cooperative covenants after all.
(7) Both alternatives seem to threaten central commitments of the theory.

I suspect the argument goes wrong somewhere, because the conclusion is so strong, but each step looked inevitable as I wrote it. Which premise or inference should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? I am most inclined to protect (1), since it seems to be the core of Hobbes's case for why anarchy is intolerable, yet I cannot see how to keep (1) without being pushed all the way to the conclusion. Alternatively, is there a principled way to hold that the founding covenant is genuinely different from ordinary covenants in the state of nature without making the sovereign's sword redundant?
