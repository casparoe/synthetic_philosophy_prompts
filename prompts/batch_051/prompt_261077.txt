I have recently been reading Leibniz’s Principles of Nature and Grace (1714), and a stretch of section 8 has unsettled me. As I understand him, Leibniz there maintains that no contingent thing, and no collection of contingent things, can contain the sufficient reason for its own existence; the sufficient reason of the whole series of contingent things must therefore lie outside the series, in a necessary substance in which the detail of changes is contained only in an eminent way, as in their source, and this substance he identifies with God. I am a retired natural scientist, so I keep wanting to turn this into a stepwise argument I can inspect.

Here is my reconstruction:

(1) For every true proposition, there is a sufficient reason why it is true rather than false.

(2) The proposition that there exists a total series of contingent things is a true proposition.

(3) Therefore, there is a sufficient reason for the existence of the total series of contingent things.

(4) The sufficient reason for a series of contingent things is either internal to the series or external to it.

(5) If internal, then the series is explained either by its members explaining one another, or by the whole explaining itself.

(6) Explanation of the whole by the members cannot succeed, because each member is contingent and therefore requires a further reason; an infinite chain of contingent reasons would still leave the whole unexplained.

(7) Explanation of the whole by itself is circular unless the whole is necessary.

(8) A series of contingent things cannot be necessary, since a necessary whole cannot be made necessary by its contingent parts, and the series just is its parts.

(9) Therefore, the sufficient reason for the total series cannot be internal to the series.

(10) Therefore, the sufficient reason for the total series is external to it.

(11) An external sufficient reason for a series of contingent things must be a necessary being that serves as the ultimate source or cause of the series.

(12) Therefore, a necessary being exists.

I want to know whether this argument is valid and, if not, where it first fails. My suspicion falls on steps 6 and 8, but I cannot yet say whether the problem is a logical gap, an equivocation on sufficient reason, or a suppressed premise about wholes and parts. If the argument is valid on Leibniz’s assumptions, then tell me which premise is doing the heaviest work and how a critic would most fairly resist it. If it is invalid, show me the exact step that fails and explain whether the failure is fatal to the argument or repairable by adding a premise Leibniz actually held.
