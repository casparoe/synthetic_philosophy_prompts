I'm trying to figure out what I actually think about punishing people who made an honest mistake, by going with my gut on cases, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, I'm tracking. A cashier sells beer to a 19-year-old with a flawless fake ID: fine to fine him. A 31-year-old sleeps with a 17-year-old who showed him a fabricated ID saying 22: I'd convict him, though I don't feel great about it. A man remarries after his lawyer tells him his first divorce went through but it didn't: punishing him for bigamy feels wrong, strict statute or not. A pharmacy ships pills that turn out to be mislabeled because a supplier sabotaged the batch in a way no one could reasonable catch: fine to hit the company with the regulatory fine. A getaway driver whose partner unexpectedly shoots and kills a store clerk: convicting him of murder under felony murder feels wrong. A woman whose boyfriend hid his heroin in her purse, and she gets charged with possession because her state makes possession strict: not fine at all. I can feel myself reaching for a rule like strict liability is okay for minor regulatory stuff but not for real crimes, but that can't be right as a matter of principle—why would whether fault matters turn on the size of the fine or the name of the crime? So: what's the best principle that gets most of these, and which of my verdicts does it force me to drop or soften? Please also tell me where your answer is shakiest, or what argument would change your mind. And run the principle on the case I keep going back and forth on: a parent charged under a truancy statute when their kid was skipping school without the parent knowing.
