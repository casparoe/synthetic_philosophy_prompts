I retired some years ago from a career in the natural sciences and have lately been reading philosophy in a more systematic way than I used to. I am writing because I have met a characterisation of nihilism that I find plausible in places and, in other places, something seems off, and I should like a trained judgement on it rather than my own amateur diagnosis.

The passage comes from a short survey of philosophical movements, not from a technical source, and reads as follows:

"Nihilism is, at bottom, the doctrine that nothing really exists: the objects and persons we take for granted are, in a strict sense, unreal. Its practical form is the denial that there are any objective moral truths, so that no action is right or wrong in itself. A moral nihilist is therefore committed to holding that murder is not wrong, and indeed to thinking that any course of action is as good as any other. The existential version of the doctrine simply applies this to individual lives: since there are no values, a human life can have no meaning. It follows that nihilism cannot be lived consistently. A consistent nihilist would have no reason to get out of bed in the morning, and anyone who claims to be a cheerful or committed nihilist is either confused or not really a nihilist."

I have since gathered that one ought to distinguish moral nihilism, existential nihilism, and a position called mereological nihilism, and my suspicion is that the survey runs these together; but I do not trust my own reading far enough to say exactly where the damage is done. I should be most grateful if you would audit the passage. In particular, please identify which claims a careful philosopher would accept, which they would qualify or reject, and where the passage conflates separate doctrines and what turns on keeping them apart. Is the step from the denial of objective moral truths to the claim that no course of action is preferable, or that a nihilist would have no reason to act, a sound one? Does the final claim about the impossibility of living as a consistent nihilist, and the inconsistency of the cheerful nihilist, follow from anything accurate in the passage? And what would a defender of a more careful moral, existential, or mereological nihilism most object to here? I should prefer an assessment that rests on the conceptual structure rather than on references to particular authors. Please write your response in British English.
