I keep finding myself committed to an attitude-first picture of respect, and I want to know how exposed the position is. Here's the view. When I owe someone respect, what I fundamentally owe is a kind of recognition — acknowledging that they are a source of valid claims on me, not just another feature of the world to be managed or optimized around. I mean recognition respect, not appraisal respect: not admiring them for merit, but granting them the standing to make demands. On this picture respectful action is derivative — an action is respectful insofar as it expresses or is guided by that acknowledgment, and no list of respectful deeds is itself the thing owed.

What would have to be true for this to be false? I want the defeaters, not just the obvious line that all we can ever judge is behavior. Tell me: which cases, arguments, intuitions, or empirical findings should move me off it? Test it from both directions. Someone performs every prescribed act of respect while palpably lacking the attitude — do they still count as having respected the person? And someone who holds the attitude but is unable to act on it, whether from coercion, institutional role, or the sort of constraint you find in a locked-in patient — has that person respected anyone, and can they still be charged with a failure of respect? What does the view predict about people who are constitutionally bad at forming the relevant attitude, and does it commit me to saying we can owe a stance that some agents can't reliably produce? Where does it make predictions that an action-first view of respect wouldn't, and are any of them testable, or do I just keep re-describing every counterexample as a hidden failure of attitude until the view risks nothing?

Then sort the results. I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with the ones a defender of the attitude-first view would themselves concede as counting against it kept seperately from the objections only critics press. Be willing to conclude that the position is well insulated, and then tell me whether that insulation is a feature or a bug. What I'd personally find most persuasive is a single case or consideration that does the most work against the view, followed by an honest verdict on whether the view survives it.
