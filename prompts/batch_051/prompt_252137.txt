I am a PhD student working on the epistemology and ethics of large language models, and I have a distinction in my notes that I want stress-tested before I build a paper around it. I call it the difference between bullshit as an agent's relation to truth and bullshit as a production property of a process.

Frankfurt's central move is to separate the liar from the bullshitter by their orientation toward truth: the liar deliberately departs from what they take to be true, whereas the bullshitter simply lacks any concern with whether what they say is true. On that picture, bullshit is a feature of the speaker's stance toward truth, not a feature of the utterance considered on its own.

I think this framework, applied directly, forces the wrong verdict on large language models. A model cannot lie, and cannot bullshit in Frankfurt's sense, because it has no attitudes and therefore cannot be indifferent to truth. Yet the standard cases of LLM hallucination feel closer to bullshit than to honest error, because the process that produced the string does not track the truth of what the string says. So I propose a two-tier account. First, agent-bullshit: an utterance issued from an attitude of indifference to whether it is true. Second, production-bullshit: an utterance issued by a process whose selection of that string is counterfactually insensitive to the truth of the proposition it expresses, in the sense that the process would have emitted the same string with roughly the same probability even if the proposition had been false. My claim is that ordinary LLM hallucinations are production-bullshit without being agent-bullshit, and that they are not errors either, because an error is a misfire of a generally truth-tracking process, whereas the generative process of a language model tracks statistical patterns in its training distribution rather than truth. For example, a model that confidently supplies a false date for a comparatively minor historical event looks, on my view, like production-bullshit: the string-generation procedure was doing what it was built to do, and no truth-tracking mechanism was malfunctioning.

What I want to know is whether this distinction survives contact. First, is production-bullshit genuinely a species of bullshit, or have I quietly changed the subject by relocating the indifference from the agent to the process? Second, does this already exist in the literature under another name, perhaps in reliabilist epistemology, in work on testimony, or in the recent debate about hallucination? It feels too obvious not to be. Third, is the counterfactual criterion too strong, given that a model trained on a distribution that correlates with facts will in many cases counterfactually co-vary with truth, just not for the reasons a truth-tracking mechanism does? Fourth, if I weaken the criterion enough to capture the cases I care about, does it collapse into the unhelpful claim that the output has merely not been verified?
