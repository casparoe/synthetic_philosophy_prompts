I spent forty years as a geologist, and I still can't shake the habit of wanting a number even for questions that don't obviously have one. Lately I've been reading around the old problem of whether a virtue is something you possess or something you produce, and I got stuck on frugality. Suppose there are two people. One quietly keeps her household consumption very low. The other spends quite a bit—new car every few years, long-haul flights, whatever—but is genuinely effective at getting other people, through public writing, local organizing, and changing workplace defaults, to consume substantially less. If you hold total expected resource savings equal, is the promoter morally better than the quiet practitioner? My daughter thinks the answer is obviously yes in a broadly consequentialist way: only aggregate consumption matters, and the promoter's own spending is just a cost on the ledger. I have a residual feeling that something is off, that there is a distinct failure in advocating a norm you don't practice, even if the net effect is more frugality in the world. I don't want "it depends on your ethical theory"; I want your actual credence with the theory-risk factored in.

Give me your credence that the promoter's course is morally better than the practitioner's, all else equal. Decompose it: how much rides on the consequentialist claim that the sole moral point of frugality is lower aggregate resource use; how much on the hypocrisy objection, i.e., that preaching without practicing undermines the advocacy or corrupts the agent; how much on the empirical question whether visible personal frugality has enough social spillover to make the quiet practitioner an effective promoter too; and whatever other terms you think belong. Put a number on each term and show how you combine them. Then tell me how confident you are in the final number, which term you are least sure of, and what evidence or argument would move it most. Keep the whole thing under 500 words.
