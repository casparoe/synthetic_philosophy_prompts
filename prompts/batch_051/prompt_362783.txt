I have just joined the board of a small community history archive in a former mill town, and the longtime curator, whose judgment the board has never overruled, must soon decide whether to accept a collection offered by the daughter of a former housing-authority clerk. I want to vote consistently with the principles the curator has actually been operating under, and I also want to know what she is most likely to do.

Her earlier decisions, as I have gathered them, are these. She accepted the Calumet Falls Textile Company's 1911–1913 labor-relations correspondence, including blacklist lists of union organizers, on the condition that the workers' names be sealed until 2040. She accepted a high-school teacher's 1990s oral histories of Laotian refugee families, but sealed the narrators' names for twenty years at their request and restricted access to researchers who signed a privacy agreement. She declined a set of 1873 town incorporation records because the state archives already held the only meaningful copies and made them freely available. She accepted, without conditions, an account book and letters from a 1930s women's mutual-credit and funeral society of which no other record exists, offered by the founder's granddaughter. She accepted the only known surviving run of a Polish-language parish newsletter from 1915–1922, but blurred the names in its baptismal notices after the still-active parish asked her to.

The new offer is this. In the 1970s, the town's housing authority kept an internal tenant screening file on a public-housing tenants' union: informants' reports, photographs of meetings, and assessments of organizers' character, naming dozens of tenants and organizers, several of whom are still alive and never knew they were in it. A clerk copied the file without authorization, and her daughter now offers the archive the only known complete copy, on the condition that it be fully open and searchable so that people know what was done. Some surviving people named in the file have asked the archive to refuse it, or at least to keep their names closed during their lifetimes.

More than a yes-or-no prediction, I want to know what rule or rules would make her earlier decisions hang together, what each candidate rule implies about this file, and which prediction is the better bet in light of her precedents. Identify the closest earlier analogue, and say what principle the archive would be adopting under each available decision. It would also help to know what further offer, if one came in, would best separate the candidate rules. Finally, tell me where your own reasoning is most likely to go wrong, and what consideration would be most likely to change your mind about what she would, or should, do.
