I'm a retired physicist and have only recently fallen into legal epistemology, so forgive the sloppy vocabulary. I keep seeing the blue bus and gatecrasher cases discussed as if they're the same problem: a statistical frequency gives you more-probable-than-not evidence, but you lack individual evidence about the defendant. The analogy people usually reach for is that rejecting such evidence is like refusing to act on a solid diagnostic test because it only gives a class frequency. If 70% of people with a certain marker have the disease, a doctor treats the patient; so why shouldn't a civil court treat the blue bus company as liable? I was reading Lawrence Tribe's old Harvard Law Review article Trial by Mathematics, and he makes a point I'll paraphrase as something like: a verdict based on group frequencies would change the law's message from "you did it" to "you're a member of a class that mostly did it." That seems to attack the diagnostic-test analogy at it's root, but I can't tell exactly which part fails. Give me a committed audit: spell out what maps to what in the analogy, where it breaks, and whether Tribe's point is a real disanalogy about the nature of legal verdicts or only about the expressive side of punishment. If the analogy is bad, tell me what a better analogy would be.
