I am assembling a case-study module for an advanced course in intergenerational ethics, and I want to use the Stern–Nordhaus controversy as a way of getting students to see how a technical parameter in welfare economics does real philosophical work. You are a philosopher of economics who works on intertemporal welfare and social discounting. Please think through the issue carefully, start with a brief conceptual mapping before you write, and aim for a disciplined case analysis rather than an encyclopedia-style summary.

Analyze the dispute between the Stern Review on the Economics of Climate Change (2006) and William Nordhaus's DICE-based critique of it as an episode in the philosophical debate over pure time preference. The salient facts are these. Stern's review used a near-zero pure rate of time preference, on the order of 0.1 percent per year, so most of its roughly 1.4 percent discount rate came from the consumption-growth term; Nordhaus's integrated assessment modelling used a pure rate of social time preference on the order of 1.5 percent per year, which together with a higher elasticity of marginal utility of consumption generated a substantially higher social discount rate and much lower near-term carbon prices. Treat the Ramsey equation, r = ρ + ηg, as the formal core, but be disciplined about what it can and cannot show.

I want the analysis to run in both directions and to be rigorous about the mapping. For each side: who is the decision-maker, what are the options, and what counts as the evidence or payoff stream? Then say what each side should have done by the lights of pure time preference theory, which features of the controversy the theory explains, and which features it misdescribes or is silent about. In particular, locate where the disagreement really lives: whether it is genuinely about the pure rate of time preference ρ, or whether it is partly about the elasticity of marginal utility of consumption η, risk and catastrophic tail outcomes, within-generation inequality, or the valuation of non-market damages. Throughout, keep clearly separate the discounting of future consumption because it is expected to be less valuable at the margin from the discounting of future utility merely because it lies further in time; Stern's position is often misdescribed as zero discounting, and a careful answer should explain why that is a confusion. Finally, run the analysis in reverse: what does this episode show about pure time preference as a normative concept — does it confirm it as a separable primitive, expose it as a placeholder for other commitments, or reveal that the two sides were partly talking past one another? Flag any auxiliary assumptions a sophisticated defender of each side would need to add to keep the theory from overreaching.
