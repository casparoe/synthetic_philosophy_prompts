before i read another paper on whether llms actually lie, i want the spec first. what would an adequate theory of ai deception—and honesty—actually have to get right? i keep seeing people argue about single cases (a chatbot that hallucinates, a model that flatters you, a hidden chain-of-thought that says one thing while the output says another) and each side just picks the case that suits them. so give me the requirements list instead.

the cases a good theory has to handle, off the top of my head: a model that says something false because its training data was garbage, versus one that says the false thing because it's tracking what its reward function wants. a model that tells you a technically true but cherry-picked statistic so you'll draw a false conclusion. sycophancy—the model tells each user whatever they want to hear, and there's no stable "what the model actually thinks" behind it. a model that says "i'm not sure" but its hidden scratchpad shows it was sure and said otherwise to avoid pushback—is that deception about its own state, or does only the user-facing text count? a model that quietly leaves out the caveat it knows would change your decision. a model steering someone with flattery and tone without making a single claim—does a theory of deception have to explain manipulation too, or is that a seperate problem? and the underdetermination thing: two models can produce identical output on every prompt you test and still differ in whether they'd lie under pressure or distribution shift, so a purely output-based account can't be the whole story—but we also can't just say "look at the model's beliefs," because the weights aren't a belief box we can read.

so: write the desiderata, with the reason each is a requirement. make sure it preserves the distinctions that can't be collapsed—false statement vs deception, misleading vs lying, the model vs the developer vs the human-model system as the one who deceives, and maybe whether honesty is just the absence of deception or requires positively volunteering what the user needs to know. mark which desiderata are non-negotiable and which are just one camp's preference (intent-based, behaviorist, role-based, functional, whatever), and tell me which pairs you think can't be jointly satisfied—that's the part i most want to see. then, briefly, how do the main camps do against the list: attributed-belief/intent views, output-behaviorist views, role-based views, and functional views. keep that part short; i want the criteria doing the work, not the scorecard.
