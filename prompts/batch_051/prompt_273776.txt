While rereading the beginning of Republic II, I found myself wondering how much of Glaucon's ring-of-Gyges argument depends on an idealization that tends to pass without comment. In the familiar form, Glaucon asks us to take a just person and an unjust person, give each a ring that makes its wearer invisible at will, and then compare their lives once detection is impossible. His prediction is that their behavior converges: with the fear of being caught removed, each will pursue his own advantage at other people's expense, and the just person will turn out to have been just only because he lacked the power and opportunity to do otherwise. The challenge is then to show that justice is worth choosing for its own sake rather than for the reputation and safety that usually accompany it.

Now change one thing: the rings are not perfectly reliable. For each act committed while wearing the ring, the wearer is made wholly invisible with probability q and remains ordinarily visible with probability 1−q; the outcome is realized independently on each occasion and cannot be known in advance, so one learns of a failure only when the act is witnessed. Everyone knows q, and both ring-bearers are prudent and moderately risk-averse, caring about reputation and exposure exactly as the people in Glaucon's story do. Set q at something high but short of certainty, say 0.95, and suppose the tempting acts are such that at that level the venture is comfortably worthwhile in expected terms for a risk-neutral agent, while a detected act would be ruinous. Does the convergence claim survive? More pointedly: is there a threshold q below which even the purely self-interested agent declines to use the ring, and does that show that the original thought experiment was really testing risk tolerance rather than love of justice, since only the difference between certainty and near-certainty separates the two agents' conduct? Or can Glaucon fairly reply that with q sufficiently close to 1 and stakes sufficiently large the prudent self-interested person will still act, and that the just person's continuing restraint therefore remains something his challenge can legitimately demand be explained?

When you work through this, please clearly separate what is fixed—such as what Glaucon actually sets up in Book II and what follows from the stipulated probabilities and payoffs—from what is genuinely contested among people who work on the Republic, and avoid presenting a contested reading of the original challenge as the settled consensus.
