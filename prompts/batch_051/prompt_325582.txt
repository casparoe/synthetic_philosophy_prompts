I'm a physicist — I think in terms of states and configuration spaces, observables, symmetries and the quotienting they force, and gauge redundancy — and I have only a casual acquaintance with analytic metaphysics. Restate the debate between Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles and Max Black's two-sphere universe in that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can: what becomes of the principle itself, of Black's thought that a perfectly symmetrical universe of two qualitatively identical spheres could still contain two things, of the replies that would distinguish the spheres by their spatial or relational properties, and of the move that singles one sphere out as "this one." Then give the part I care most about: a careful report of what did not survive the translation — which claims map cleanly onto my formalisms, which map only with distortion, and which cannot be expressed at all. Where something fails to translate, say whether that is because the physicist's vocabulary refuses to count something genuinely present, or because the philosophical distinction was doing no observable work. If the quantum mechanics of identical particles is the natural home for this debate in physics, include that in the translation. End with a final line beginning "Residue:" stating in one sentence what, if anything, the translation could not carry over.
