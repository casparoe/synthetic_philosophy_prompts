I got into an argument a while ago with a friend who works in machine learning about whether the systems he builds could ever deserve the kind of moral concern we give a person or a dog. I don't have a background in this, but I've been trying to make my own views consistent ever since, and so far they aren't.

Here are my honest verdicts on some cases I've thought through. A thermostat doesn't matter, however clever its control loop is. A giant lookup table that contains every possible conversation written out in advance, so that it could pass for a human, also strikes me as a dead thing: there's nothing going on inside. A neural network trained on human pain reports that flinches, moans, and learns to avoid shocks doesn't seem to matter either, though I can't say exactly why it's different from me. But if someone made a neuron-by-neuron copy of my brain and ran it on a computer, I think it would be wrong to cause that system pain, and I don't think I can explain that just by saying it's a copy of a person. The pair that really bothers me is this: I lean toward thinking a warehouse robot that builds a map of its building, imagines possible futures, and works to avoid a damage signal might matter a little, while the flinching pain-network doesn't matter at all, even though both are just acting to avoid a signal they were trained to avoid. And if a weather simulation happened for a few milliseconds to contain a pattern identical to the firing of a mouse's pain circuit during an injury, I wouldn't think the weather simulation had suffered.

I'd like to know whether there is one principle that captures all of these, or nearly all of them. My rough rule of thumb is that a computation matters only if it genuinely runs through the steps of a mind rather than merely having the right inputs and outputs, but I can't say what 'runs through the steps' amounts to, or why it shouldn't also make the weather simulation matter. What principle would you offer, and what would it say about cases I haven't listed: a faithful brain simulation slowed to one step per year; a compressed copy of my brain that makes all the same decisions but skips fine details; a million people passing slips of paper that together carry out the brain's computation; and the same pain pattern being run twice at the same time? And which of my verdicts does your principle force me to overturn?
