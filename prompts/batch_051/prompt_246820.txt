My friend's favorite move in arguments is to tell people "don't speak unless you know." The other day he used it on me: I said something that was true, and I believed it, and I had decent though not conclusive reasons, but I couldnt rule out being wrong. He said I shouldn't have said it because I didn't know it. Now im honestly not sure what that little rule is claiming. It could be a rule about truth, a rule about belief, a rule about justification, a rule about knowledge, a rule about certainty. What are the distinct precise principles hiding in "don't speak unless you know"? Lay them out as a menu and say for each whether it's plausible as a norm of conversation, whether it's too demanding or too weak, and which reading actually supports my friend's objection in a case where the claim was true and justified but not known. I don't just want the label knowledge-norm-of-assertion; I want to know what that norm would actually require and how it differs from the neighboring rules.
