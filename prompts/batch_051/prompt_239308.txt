At dinner last week my cousin, who works in HR, went off on how "all that generational stuff is basically astrology for the workplace." Her summary of the debunking case went more or less like this: there's no actual thing out in the world that "Millennial" or "Gen Z" refers to; the cutoff dates are just numbers a marketing firm made up, and it's absurd that someone born December 31, 1996 and someone born January 1, 1997 are in different generations when they're a day apart. The research behind those "Millennials are killing the napkin industry" articles, she says, is mostly confused because it treats age effects as if they were cohort effects: being new to the workforce, having no savings, not having kids yet, being online all the time — none of that is about which generation you're in, it's about how old you are when the survey happens. And the big events that hit everyone, like the 2008 crash or COVID, are period effects, not properties of a cohort. Once you control for age and period, she claims, the supposed generation differences mostly just vanish, and what's left is too weak to build an entire consulting industry on. So generations, she concluded, are not real the way a species of frog is real; they're labels with fuzzy, arbitrary boundries that keep getting redefined, and people sort themselves into them the way they sort into horoscopes.

But then when I pointed out that fuzzy boundaries don't make biological categories fake either — biologists argue all the time about where one bird species ends and another begins — she admitted that was true but said generations are still different. I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, so I can't tell where her argument is doing real work and where it's just vibes. Could you go through her summary and tell me: which parts would someone who thinks generations are real kinds actually accept, which parts are overstatements or conflations, and does her conclusion that generations aren't real follow from the accurate parts? And what would it even mean to say a generation is real — should we hold social categories like generations to the same standard as biological species, or is "real kind" doing something different there?
