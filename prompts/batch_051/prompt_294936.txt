I have been using the following four criteria to decide who counts as having a legitimate voice in a neighborhood's land-use decisions—a right to shape the city, in a deliberately local sense:

1. Sustained residence: the person lives in the neighborhood as their primary home.
2. Vulnerable dependence: their access to housing, work, or care would be seriously affected by the decision.
3. Embedded contribution: they participate in the neighborhood's informal networks, institutions, or economy.
4. Reciprocity: they bear the negative effects of the decision at least in rough proportion to any benefit they receive from blocking it.

Now consider this case. A city is considering upzoning a low-rise neighborhood to allow a six-story mixed-income building on the site of a closed school. The leading opponent is a seventy-year-old former resident who was displaced from the neighborhood thirty years ago and now lives in a suburb twenty minutes away. She returns daily: she works as a home health aide for several elderly clients on the same block, attends the church around the corner, and provides free child care for her grandchildren, who still live in the neighborhood. The proposed building would house about eighty households, mostly drawn from the city's waiting list for affordable housing, who currently live in overcrowded or unstable conditions elsewhere in the city.

Go through the four criteria and say, for each, whether she satisfies it; where a criterion is ambiguous in her case, say exactly what the ambiguity turns on. Then give your overall verdict: does she have a legitimate voice in this decision, or does her case show that the criteria themselves need revising—perhaps by adding a criterion about historical displacement or about the claims of nonresidents and future residents? If your verdict is indeterminate, identify the single criterion whose revision would most change the result and explain how.
