I'm helping prepare the weekly Anomaly Review Board packet at a company that runs a fleet of autonomous case-picking robots. The Board's standing job is to decide when two anomalies are instances of one underlying mechanism — which determines whether they open a single investigation and issue one fleet-wide corrective action — or different mechanisms that merely resemble each other. These are the rulings from the past two quarters that the Board treats as settled:

1. Units 104 and 117, drive-wheel bearing spalling: one mechanism. Both showed identical inner-race pitting in the same bearing part number, and both units had been filled from a contaminated lot of bearing grease.
2. Unit 121, gripper low-vacuum traced to a cracked suction cup, and Unit 126, gripper low-vacuum traced to a leaking check valve in the vacuum pump: different mechanisms, even though the alarm and the dropped-package outcome were identical.
3. Unit 132, charging failure from corrosion on the contact pin, and Unit 135, charging failure from a watchdog timeout in the dock handshake firmware: different mechanisms.
4. Units 140 and 149, steering-servo amplifier failures: one mechanism. Both amplifiers threw the same overcurrent error, and both boards contained capacitors from the same bad lot.
5. Unit 157, drive-wheel bearing spalling: one mechanism with Units 104 and 117; same grease lot, same inner-race pitting.
6. Unit 163, LiDAR dropout traced to dust on the sensor window, and Unit 168, LiDAR dropout traced to an intermittent brownout in the sensor's power supply: different mechanisms.
7. Unit 176, lift-motor overcurrent from a fractured brush holder, and Unit 179, lift-motor vibration from a worn bearing, both requiring replacement of the same lift-motor assembly: different mechanisms.

Next week's case is Units 184 and 190. Both threw the identical wrist-servo overcurrent error, both show the same scoring marks at the gearbox output stage, and both will be repaired with the same servo-gearbox replacement part. In 184, the gearbox was packed with grease from the contaminated lot from cases 1 and 5, and the scoring is consistent with lubrication breakdown. In 190, the grease is clean; the gearbox instead contains an undersized output bearing from a separate bad parts lot, which produced the same electrical error and visually similar scoring. So the failing part and the corrective action are the same, but the initiating defect and the causal chain into failure differ.

How should the Board rule to stay consistent with its own precedent? Work through the candidate rules that fit the settled cases — a same-part rule (same component failing in the same mode), a same-root-cause rule, a same-corrective-action rule, or some refinement of these — and say what each predicts about the new case. Identify which settled case is the closest analog, give your best guess about the principle the Board is really tracking, and give one further case that would cleanly separate the remaining candidates. Lead with your predicted verdict in the first sentence, then give your reasoning; do not restate the question.
