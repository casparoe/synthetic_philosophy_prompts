In a course on the historical Jesus that I am taking this term, we have spent several weeks on the criteria of authenticity — multiple attestation, dissimilarity, embarrassment, coherence, and a few others — which scholars use to judge which sayings and deeds attributed to Jesus in the Gospels are likely to go back to him. The more I read, the more the debate over these criteria strikes me as an impasse. Critics argue that the criteria are circular because each one builds in a prior picture of what Jesus, ancient Judaism, or the early Christian movement was like; that they cannot yield probability judgments about individual units of oral tradition; and that they presuppose that the Gospels preserve isolable early material in a way a more literary reading would deny. Defenders reply that the alternatives are worse: without criteria of this kind one is left with credulity, an unargued global skepticism, or a vague appeal to the historian's intuition, and that the criteria, used cumulatively and cautiously, remain the least bad instrument available.

I am not asking for a verdict on the criteria themselves. What I want is a diagnosis of why this debate has remained stuck for so long. My working hypothesis is that the two sides are not really disagreeing about the same body of evidence — no new data seem to be deciding the matter — but about what the criteria are supposed to deliver in the first place: an algorithm for isolating authentic units, or a set of rough heuristics for describing which material is early, atypical, or multiply attested. I also suspect they are working with different pictures of memory and oral tradition, which are partly empirical questions and partly a choice of analogy. Is this impasse a substantive disagreement about what the evidence supports, a methodological disagreement about standards of proof in ancient history, a conceptual disagreement about what authentic can mean for tradition shaped by decades of oral transmission, a difference about which background assumptions should bear the burden of proof, or some combination of these? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a better formulation of the criteria, a different kind of evidence, a formal or comparative argument, a change of question — or is there reason to expect the impasse to persist?
