I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm working on a long piece about the way the language of "commitment" — commitment devices, credible commitments, self-binding — has drifted away from the older moral notion of a promise. In the course of reporting, I've come to suspect that I never really understood the philosophical question of why promises bind in the first place, and I want to test whether my usual vocabulary can even say what the philosophers are arguing about. I am not a trained philosopher, but I am fluent in the vocabulary of game theory and behavioral economics: commitment devices, cheap talk versus costly signaling, repeated games and reputation, Nash equilibria of commitment games, self-enforcing arrangements, third-party enforcement, and the distinction between a binding contract and an arrangement that simply gives each party an incentive to follow through.

Please restate, as faithfully as you can, the main philosophical positions on why promises bind — for instance, the conventional-practice view that promising is a rule-governed social institution and that making a promise is performing a move whose normativity comes from the practice rather than from any single individual's will; the expectation-based view that a promise obligates because the promisor knowingly leads the promisee to form expectations and signals that the promisee may hold the promisor responsible if those expectations are disappointed; and the deflationary or skeptical view that promising is fundamentally a signaling-and-coordination device whose "bindingness" is nothing over and above the costs of breaking it. Translate each into the vocabulary I just described. Then give me the residue report: which parts map cleanly onto commitment, signaling, reputation, or contract-theoretic ideas; which parts can be forced to fit only by distortion; and which parts have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. I especially want to know whether the philosophers' notion of obligation or bindingness is captured by the notion of a self-enforcing equilibrium, or whether my vocabulary is structurally blind to the very thing the debate is about.
