We're doing Turing's "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" in my philosophy of AI class, and we spent two sessions on the Learning Machines section. The bit where he says that instead of trying to program the adult mind directly, we should start with the child mind and educate it, and that punishment and reward can only ever be a small part of teaching because the amount of information they carry is so limited. I think I understand that proposal on its own terms. What I'm trying to get clearer on is what the passage is doing in the paper.

Here's my list of things people in class have said its doing, or that I've seen said about it elsewhere:

1. It answers Lady Lovelace's objection—the machine can produce things the programmer didn't explicitly foresee, because the teacher's feedback plus the child-machine's initial structure does the work, not a fully specified adult program.
2. It replaces the question "can a machine be intelligent?" with the question "can a machine be educated?" and treats the second as weaker and more tractable.
3. It supports an anti-intellectualist reading of human intelligence: we ourselves start as child minds and get educated, so the adult mind is already the product of teaching rather than built-in content.
4. It anticipates modern machine learning by making learning rules and feedback more important than hand-coded knowledge.
5. It places an upper bound on reward-and-punishment as a teaching channel, which is interesting because he still thinks it can be used to build a learner.

I want one more thing the passage is doing that isn't on this list and isn't just a mixture of items already there. Is there a sixth reading, maybe something about imitation, discipline, or the teacher's role, that the list misses? And if you give one, tell me honestly whether it's any good or whether it's the kind of thing someone says in seminar that falls apart when you look back at the text.
