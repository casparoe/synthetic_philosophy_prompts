I'm on my town's public-history commission and we're suppose to approve the wording for a marker at the site of a Black-owned business district that burned in 1971 and was never rebuilt. The descendants' association submitted text that calls what happened "the deliberate destruction of the district," and they want the marker to say that after the fire the city refused to help rebuild, cleared the lots, and rezoned the land for the expressway. Those later actions aren't really disputed — the oral histories from longtime residents are full of specifics about them, and the city's own records back them up. But the fire itself is messier: the fire marshall's report says it started in a restaurant kitchen, no one was charged, and the city's planning memos at the time describe the fire as an accident that made the expressway route possible. The staff historian says we can't put "deliberate destruction" on a marker because then the commission is vouching for a claim the documents don't support; his fallback text just says the district was destroyed by fire and the city cleared what was left for the expressway. I argued about this for an hour last night with a friend. She thinks a marker is an assertion by the city, not a place for memory to override the record, so if we can't back it with documents we shouldn't say it. I keep thinking that what counts as "supported by the record" depends on who got to write the record, and that the community's memory might be carrying a true claim about the destruction even if the fire itself was an accident. We meet Thursday and I said I'd circulate a draft memo. Draft it for me: recommend a concrete course of action — approve the descendants' text, approve the historian's fallback, or specific compromise wording — and make sure the memo honestly engages the strongest argument on the side you don't take. Give it as exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, no intro or conclusion.
