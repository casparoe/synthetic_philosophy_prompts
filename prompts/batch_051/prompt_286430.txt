I am presenting a paper at a theology conference this spring and want a Q&A preparation sheet. The thesis I am defending is that a faithful translation of a sacred text can itself be inspired, in substantially the same sense as the original, because inspiration attaches to what a text teaches rather than to the particular words in which the teaching was first written down. My central case is the Septuagint, the early Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures, which the New Testament writers and much of the early church seem to treat as authoritative scripture in its own right rather than as a mere approximation of an authoritative Hebrew original. My argument in brief is that inspiration is a property of the teaching a text conveys, not of its lexemes; a translation that faithfully conveys the same teaching can therefore carry the same authority, and a church or reader who treats it as Scripture is not making a theological error.

The audience will be mostly Protestant systematic theologians. Several are sympathetic to the view that one particular English translation, the King James Version, has a unique claim to authority; one is a textual critic whose work turns on the distinction between the original autographs and their later transmission; and a few are philosophers of religion who will press me on what exactly the property of being inspired is and how I would identify a translation that has it. I need to be ready for their questions, not the questions of secular philosophers of language.

Prepare me for the objections this specific audience will raise, in their own vocabulary, roughly ordered by how likely and how damaging they are: which objections have real force and which are bluster, what I should concede up front about the original languages and the doctrine of the autographs, where my own position is weakest and most needs a prepared defense, and which question I should most hope nobody asks. For each objection, give a short reply suitable for a two-minute answer and a note on where that reply is soft.
