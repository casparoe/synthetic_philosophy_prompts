I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm drafting a long-form essay on the tension between the evolutionary record of animal pain and the doctrine that creation was originally good. Please respond as a philosopher of religion with expertise in evolutionary biology and the problem of natural evil. Start by brainstorming the strongest objections to my argument and the most plausible replies available to a defender of the doctrine; then comment specifically on whether my fourfold distinction is sound, whether my central theological claim is fair, and whether the closing proposal is engaging enough to anchor the piece. Please keep the comments substantive and philosophical rather than offering generic editorial advice.

Here are my working notes.

Working title: The World Before the Fall Was Already Wounded

The essay opens with a paleontological vignette—a dinosaur vertebra with a healed fracture and a jawbone showing chronic infection—to establish that pain, disease, and violent death occurred hundreds of millions of years before any human being existed. I then separate four questions that are usually run together: (a) whether pre-human animal suffering in fact occurred; (b) whether such suffering is compatible with the claim that creation was good; (c) whether it is compatible with the stronger doctrine that suffering and death entered the world only as punishment for a first human sin; and (d) how a theology that takes evolution seriously should narrate creation, fall, and redemption.

My central argument is that most reconciliations of pre-Fall animal suffering with a good creation work by a silent shift: from an external, creature-centered criterion of goodness—roughly, that goodness rules out pain and killing—to a systemic or narrative criterion, according to which the world is good because it is ordered, fruitful, and open to development. I argue that this shift is intellectually legitimate but expensive. It makes "good" a property of the whole system rather than of each creature's experienced state, and it turns the fall from a datable catastrophe into something like a standing shadow over finite life.

I close by proposing that the doctrine of a good creation should be read not as a description of an original state but as a promissory, or normative, claim about what the world is meant to become: a standard by which the present order, including its long pre-human history of predation and disease, is judged and toward which it may be called.
