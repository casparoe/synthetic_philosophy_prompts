I'm a PhD student in environmental history, and I got stuck on something in our history-of-science reading group and I'd love for someone to walk me through it. We were reading Malthus. The short version: in 1798 he argued that population, when unchecked, grows geometrically while the food supply can only grow arithmetically, so any improvement in living standards gets eaten up by more mouths, and societies keep getting pulled back to subsistence. Britain after him didn't actually play out that way, at least not in the next century and a half; real wages rose, famine receded, and the trap kept not closing. The standard defence is that Malthus was "right but early": the mechanism is real, the escape was temporary, the check is postponed rather than absent.

So far so normal. But one of the graduate students formalised the defence, and now I can't get his argument out of my head. It goes like this:

(1) The "right but early" move works for any failed long-run doom prediction, because the theory never says by when the pressure must dominate.
(2) If any failed short-run test can be absorbed by "early," then no short-run outcome could ever count against the theory.
(3) A statement for which no possible observation would ever count as evidence against it is unfalsifiable.
(4) Unfalsifiable statements have no empirical content, or at least no predictive content worth planning around.
(5) Therefore Malthusian-style long-run scarcity claims have no empirical content worth planning around.
(6) But we do plan around them, and I think rightly: soil exhaustion, resource depletion, climate-linked agricultural stress — the timetables keep slipping, but it would be crazy to treat them as empty.

I want to keep (6), and I also don't want to give up the Popperian-sounding instinct behind (3) and (4), that there has to be something the world could do to show a theory is wrong. So: exactly where does this argument go wrong, if it does? If it doesn't go wrong, what's the cost of keeping (6) — do I have to accept that long-run predictions aren't falsifiable in the usual sense, and if so, what criterion makes them better than astrology? I have a feeling the rescue isn't actually free, and that each time you say "not yet" you quietly trade away part of the mechanism that made the prediction, but I cant tell if that's the right diagnosis or just my way of avoiding the conclusion. When you answer, please say how confident you are in the diagnosis, and single out the point you're least sure of.
