I run a reading group on the history of economic thought — lately we've been working through Malthus and Boserup — and for months two members have been argueing past each other about whether a theory can be 'right but early.' The demographer in the group is fairly neo-Malthusian: she says Malthus's core mechanism, population growing geometrically while the food supply only grows arithmetically and gets held in check by positive and preventive checks, is a real constraint that technology and institutions keep temporarily loosening. On her view two centuries of failed predictions aren't refutations, they're postponements, and the bill is finally coming due in the form of soil degradation, groundwater drawdown, phosphorus limits, and climate change. The other one is a philosopher of economics who thinks Malthus has been falsified about as thoroughly as a theory can be — the demographic transition, the Green Revolution, the Haber-Bosch process, and a century of falling real food prices all look like things the original model simply couldn't anticipate. He says 'right but early' is the classic unfalsifiable rescue: every time the predicted check fails to materialize, the date just slides further out, until the claim is compatible with literally any observation.

They've agreed, at my annoying insistence, to try an adversarial collaboration, and I'd like you to write the document they could both actually sign. Take the role of a professional philosopher of science who works on falsification, Lakatosian research programmes, and the history of Malthusianism, and think through this carefully rather than reaching for easy compromise. The document should contain: (1) a numbered list of claims both sides can accept — about what Malthus actually claimed, what he got wrong, what the demographic transition and induced innovation show, and what sort of limits a finite planet does impose — that is specific enough not to be vacuous and neutral enough that neither side chokes on the wording; (2) the exact residual disagreement, stated so that each side would recognize and accept the phrasing of their own view; (3) three or four cruxes, where settling the sub-question would actually move at least one of them, each paired with a concrete observation, dataset, or historical case that both would agree bears on it, and with each side's prediction for it recorded in advance. If part of the fight turns out to be verbal — say, about the word 'right' — flag that too and say which part is merely verbal. Present the cruxes as a small table. Start by brainstorming candidate agreed claims and cruxes, then refine each formulation until neither side would object.
