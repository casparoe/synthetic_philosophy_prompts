I'm a journalist on the science and ideas desk, and I've somehow been talked into writing a verse explainer about dialetheism and paraconsistent logic for our end-of-year ideas issue. The form is fixed by the section editor: a villanelle. She doesn't want it to just name-drop the liar paradox; she wants the form itself to do the arguing.

You're an expert on philosophical logic and, ideally, a working poet. Think carefully about the craft before you draft, and maybe start by brain-storming what each stanza has to do technically, not just what it says.

Here's the structural brief I've been mulling over. Dialetheism, as I understand it, says there are true contradictions. Paraconsistent logic is the machinery for coping with that: it rejects the principle of explosion, the classical rule that a contradiction entails absolutely everything, so a system can contain a contradiction without collapsing into triviality. A villanelle feels like the right form because it also refuses to collapse: it keeps repeating its refrains and still holds together as a poem.

So: the two refrains should be one and the same sentence, first asserted and then denied (or denied then asserted), built around a naturally phrased version of the liar sentence, so the poem litterally contains a claim and its negation while remaining composed and meaningful. The refrains alternate through the tercets and then meet in the closing couplet. Around the fourth tercet there should be a turn where the reader notices the poem hasn't fallen apart — and notices that this is the point: containing a contradiction did not make everything else follow. Don't scatter jargon through it; let the imagery do the work, but make the closing couplet land the logical point exactly.

After the poem, give me a short committed note: say whether the poem is best read as a defence of dialetheism proper or only as a demonstration that contradictions can be managed as formal objects, and flag one place where the poem takes a liberty with the logic.
