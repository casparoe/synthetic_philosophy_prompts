I sit on a conservation board that rules on interventions affecting wild animal populations. Our precedents so far: we approved vaccinating an endangered wolf population against a painful disease, even though darting stressed the animals each year; we approved eradicating invasive rats from an island because they were eating seabird chicks alive; we rejected a proposal to cull a starving deer herd, since we couldn't be confident the cull would reduce total suffering rather than just swap one kind of death for another; and we approved supplementary feeding for a threatened butterfly species during a drought, even though it might inflate the population before a later crash. Next meeting we have to rule on a landowner's proposal to drain a species-rich marsh and replace it with a low-wildlife garden centre. The marsh supports millions of small, short-lived animals — insects, amphibians, small fish — most of whom die painfully before maturity. Draining it would kill or displace the current inhabitants, but it would also mean far fewer of those short, painful lives come into existence in future; the replacement habitat would support only a few, longer-lived animals. What should we decide if we want to be consistant with ourselves, which precedent is the closest analogue, and if our precedents genuinely underdetermine it, what principle would we be adopting either way?
