At a recent seminar on historical explanation, a participant defended a strongly anti-structural reading of the First World War. The war, he argued, was not the product of alliance systems, militarism, or imperial rivalry, but of a cascade of small accidents in the July Crisis: a wrong turn by the Archduke's driver, a hesitant diplomat, a message arriving late. Pressed to explain why this should defeat structural accounts rather than merely complicate them, he said only: "For want of a nail the kingdom was lost." He plainly took that sentence to license the conclusion that large historical outcomes hinge on small contingencies, so a good explanation should follow the chain of contingencies rather than the big structures. I suspect the proverb can be read several different ways, some too weak to support him and some far too strong to accept, but I want the full menu rather than my own list. Distinguish the distinct precise claims about historical causation that "for want of a nail the kingdom was lost" could be used to assert, from the near-tautological to the radical. For each, say whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false or unsupported as a general claim. Then identify which reading actually licenses his anti-structural use of the proverb, and which reading is most often doing the work when the proverb is deployed to end such arguments.
