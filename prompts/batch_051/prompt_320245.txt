I'm a PhD student in cognitive psychology, and I've been collecting my own categorization judgments in the hope of extracting the theory of concepts that lies behind them. The problem is that my verdicts seem to presuppose different theories from one case to the next, and I would appreciate help working out whether any single account can cover them.

Here are the judgments.

(1) I accept "a robin is a bird" faster than "a penguin is a bird," and when I list birds, robin comes early while penguin comes late. Yet I don't think a penguin is a borderline bird or that a robin is more of a bird than a penguin is; membership feels all-or-none even though how good an example something is seems graded.

(2) If an animal born to raccoons is surgically altered to look exactly like a skunk—black-and-white stripes, the smell, the works—I call it a raccoon, not a skunk, and I expect raccoon offspring from it. For a natural kind, I seem to let hidden history and insides override outward similarity.

(3) I rate 4 as a better example of an even number than 806, although I know that even number has a crisp definition—an integer divisible by 2—and that 806 satisfies it exactly as much as 4 does. The gradedness doesn't make me doubt that membership is definitional.

(4) When I plan what to take out of a burning house, I put the children, the passports, and the laptop on the list before the television, even though the television is far more similar to the laptop than the passports are. The category seems to be organized by my goals rather than by any family resemblance among its members.

(5) I call a tree stump at sitting height that everyone uses as a seat a chair, and I call a beanbag a chair, but I can't find any intrinsic features all chairs share. What seems to make something a chair for me is its function, not its shape or material.

(6) Yet I won't call a near-perfect counterfeit $20 bill—one that circulates undetected and behaves exactly like the real thing—a $20 bill. It functions identically, but its having come from the wrong source seems to settle the matter. This last verdict unsettles me most, given (5).

Is there one account of concepts—classical, prototype, exemplar, theory theory, or an explicit hybrid—that predicts all six of these, and if not, which of my verdicts should I overturn? I would also like to know what the best-fitting account predicts about two cases I keep vacillating on: a tree pruned while alive into the shape of an armchair and used daily for sitting, and a $20 bill printed by the proper authority but with a glaring typographical error that nobody notices.
