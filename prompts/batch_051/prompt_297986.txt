I keep gnawing at the Hart–Fuller debate from a weird angle. Hart's positivist story says law is grounded in a social fact: officials accept a rule of recognition from the internal point of view, and that acceptance is what gives rules their legality — moral merit need not enter. Fuller's reply is that a legal system can't be understood without an inner morality: law has to be general, published, not retroactive, tolerably coherent, and so on, and a system that flouts that isn't just bad law but fails as law. Now suppose a jurisdiction routes a large share of its first-instance decisions through machine-learning systems trained on past rulings and statutory text — systems that have no point of view, internal or otherwise, and don't accept anything. They just output classifications. Does Hart's account survive that development? Or does legal validity stop being a matter of official acceptance and turn into, weirdly, a statistical property of a corpus? Or should we relocate acceptance to the humans who design, train, deploy, and let the systems run, saying positivism is fine but its unit of analysis moved? And does Fuller's line get stronger here, or is he equally lost once no one is doing the lawmaking in anything like his sense? What I want is a clear account of which specific premise in Hart's or Fuller's story is actually under pressure, and whether a repaired version would still be recognizably positivist.
