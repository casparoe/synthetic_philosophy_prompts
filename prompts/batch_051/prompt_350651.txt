I have been reading the exchange between those who defend a duty to remember and those who argue for the uses of forgetting — the debate associated with Margalit and Rieff — and have tried to write down my own position on the duty to remember and the case for forgetting. I would like an audit of whether it hangs together before I present it in a seminar. My commitments are these:

(1) There is a genuine ethical duty to remember the victims of mass political violence, and the duty is owed to the victims themselves; it is not merely a means to preventing future horrors.

(2) Much of what passes for collective memory — state ceremonies, monuments, museum exhibits — has become ritualised and self-congratulatory, and cannot by itself discharge the duty in (1).

(3) Some forgetting is necessary: former enemies cannot build a common life, and individuals cannot go on, if nothing may ever be set aside.

(4) But I also think calls for forgetting, or simply for moving on, are typically made by those who benefited from the wrongs or by their descendants, and accepting such calls too readily lets them launder their responsibility.

(5) The ultimate test for whether a community should remember or forget something is what best serves present and future life, not fidelity to the past for its own sake.

I would rather be told where the strain is than have every tension treated as equally serious.
