I have no academic background, but I enjoy reading about philosophy of mind, and for the past year I keep coming back to the three-stances idea usually attributed to Daniel Dennett: you can predict a system physically, by cause and effect; you can predict it by assuming it was designed to do something and will tend to do that; or you can predict it by treating it as a rational agent with beliefs, goals, and reasons. The part that interests me most is that these are not supposed to be mutually exclusive facts about what a system is made of. They are stances we take, and we usually adopt the cheapest one that works, moving up to the intentional stance when a system is complex enough that mental language pays its way. I have also seen it argued that the patterns picked out by the higher stances can be real without being visible in the underlying physics, the way a computation is a real pattern in a flurry of electrical activity rather than a separate substance.

Lately I have been sketching an institution built on this idea. Suppose a regulator, call it an Algorithmic Stance Commission, had to issue an official classification for every AI system above a certain scale before it could be offered to the public, and that classification locked in which stance the law would use for that deployment. A physical-stance classification would treat the system as ordinary machinery: ordinary product-defect liability and safety rules, with no requirement to document objectives or training behavior. A design-stance classification would require the operator to file a precise function specification, including what the system is optimizing and its known failure modes; harm would be judged by whether the system performed its specified function, and the operator could not be accused of the system having meant anything by its behavior. An intentional-stance classification would require the operator to treat the system as an agent: submit value-alignment evidence, keep decision logs, and accept liability as if the system were an employee for whose actions the operator is responsible. Operators could request a classification and later petition to be moved, but the Commission would decide.

I want to understand how this would actually behave if it existed, not whether it is a good idea in the abstract. Which systems would developers try to get classified under which stance, and what arguments would they use? What incentives would the rule create — for example, would companies deliberately keep systems legible enough to stay in the design stance, or deliberately make them opaque so nobody can make the intentional classification stick? What happens at the boundaries, where a system is usefully predicted from more than one stance at once, or where the deciding factor is the classifier applied to a model's outputs rather than anything about its internals? Are the likely failure modes fixable with amendments, or do they expose a deeper mismatch between treating stances as fixed categories written into law and treating them as strategies for prediction?

I care more about concrete mechanisms than about whether the Commission as a whole is defensible.
