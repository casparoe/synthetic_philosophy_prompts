In arguments about existentialism I keep noticing a dialectical pattern I do not have a name for, and I want to know whether one exists — and more importantly, how to carve its boundaries.

The pattern runs as follows. A critic attacks the slogan that existence precedes essence as self-defeating: if human beings have no fixed nature, then that very fact is a fixed fact about human nature, so the slogan appears to assert the essence it denies. The defender replies by distinguishing two senses of essence: the slogan denies only a built-in, definitional nature, not the self-fashioned character that accrues through choice and action. The critic then observes that on this weak reading the slogan is no longer radical; almost everyone is happy to grant that people are not born with a complete blueprint. The dispute was supposed to be about something stronger — whether there is a deeper human condition, such as facticity, bad faith, or the requirement to choose, that shapes what a person can make of themselves. Yet the conversation rarely settles at this point. The strong anti-essentialist claim reappears a few turns later, and the two-senses defense is redeployed whenever the strong claim is challenged.

I have also seen the mirror image in the same debates. A critic grants the weak reading for the sake of argument, draws a conclusion that only the strong reading would support, and then, when pressed, insists that only the weak reading was ever being used. Both sides, in effect, share one slogan and two propositions of very different strength, and the word essence does a different job each time it is invoked.

What I would like to know is whether this exact standoff has an established name in philosophy — an incoherence charge answered by a two-senses distinction, where the distinction either collapses the original claim into something trivial or is quietly dropped later, so that the slogan survives without ever having been defended in a single fixed sense. If there is no established name, I would like you to propose one. Either way, the label matters to me less than the carving: define the phenomenon precisely, distinguish it from plain equivocation and from the weaker-claim retreat usually called motte-and-bailey, and say what diagnostic test would tell whether a given use of a two-senses distinction is a legitimate clarification rather than an instance of the pattern.

Please organize your answer into four named sections, in this order, with roughly the indicated length:

1. Nearest established concepts (about 200 words): identify the closest already-named phenomena — for example motte-and-bailey, equivocation, persuasive definition, and any relevant term from the existentialist literature — and state briefly what each captures and what each misses about the pattern I am describing.

2. Precise definition (about 250 words): say whether the pattern has a standard name; if not, give it a name and define it, making clear the roles of the strong claim, the weak claim, the shared slogan, and the two-senses defense.

3. Discrimination rules (about 250 words): provide criteria for separating this pattern from plain equivocation, from a legitimate distinction of senses, and from motte-and-bailey; for each contrast, include a concrete diagnostic question one could apply to a particular argument.

4. Application to the existentialism case (about 200 words): apply your definition to disputes over existence preceding essence, and state whether, by your own criteria, the two-senses defense in that debate is typically a clarification or an instance of the pattern, and exactly where it succeeds or fails.
