I am a lower sixth-form student at a school in England, and I moderate a Discord server for a tabletop role-playing game. I am fluent in the vocabulary that comes with that job: base permissions and elevated roles, warnings, timed mutes, kicks, bans, the appeals channel, shadow-banning, de-escalation scripts, reputation systems and codes of conduct.

In my philosophy lessons we have been studying the idea, often associated with the philosopher Stephen Darwall, that in addition to treating people correctly in what we do, we owe them a certain sort of attitude. I have been taught a distinction between two senses of respect. 'Appraisal respect' is the positive assessment we earn through skill or excellence, and it cannot be owed to anyone. 'Recognition respect' is different: it is supposed to be owed to every person just because they are a person. It is an acknowledgement of their standing to make claims on us, to be answered rather than merely processed, so that what they say counts as a claim on us and not just as data we must handle. The position I want you to work with holds that this recognition respect is a genuine moral requirement, and that it cannot be reduced to a set of required actions or to whatever we happen to feel.

Restate that position in the vocabulary of server moderation, as honestly as you can. Go through the components one by one: the equal standing of every member, appraisal respect as something like an earned role or reputation, recognition respect as something like base permissions, a right to appeal, or the duty to give each user a fair hearing, and the specifically attitudinal element wherever the moderator's tools and practices come closest to expressing it. For each component, tell me whether it translates cleanly, translates only with distortion, or has no expression at all in the moderator's vocabulary.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I most want. I suspect that what the philosophers call 'owing an attitude' is really just a grander way of saying that moderators should apply the code of conduct even-handedly, answer complaints promptly and give everyone a fair hearing. Tell me whether my vocabulary is missing something genuine about what it is to acknowledge another person, or whether the philosophical claim turns out to be less clear than it looks once one tries to state it in terms of roles, permissions and enforcement decisions. Be specific about where the breakdown occurs: is it a defect of the moderator's framework, or a sign that the original view was partly metaphorical?

Please write your answer in American English.
