Write a parody of a customer-support FAQ page for a fictional wearable-device company whose sole product turns Warren Quinn's self-torturer thought experiment into a consumer good. Begin with a short Quick Start block, then give eight to ten numbered FAQ entries, roughly 700 words in total, in the deadpan, ingratiating voice of a consumer-electronics support page.

Do not assume the reader already knows the thought experiment; build the setup into the parody itself. The device is a band with a dial of, say, 1,000 settings. Setting 0 is entirely comfortable. Each one-notch advance is engineered to be subjectively imperceptible, and each advance pays the customer a fixed sum ($10,000 in this version), so that the aggregate state at the top of the dial and the aggregate payout show up only on paper. The catch, which the FAQ should treat as unremarkable company policy, is that the dial ratchets: it cannot be turned back. The terms of service define accepting one more notch — never the whole dial at once — as the governing act of consent, and the company classifies a customer's later desire to reverse course as a known, non-actionable condition rather than a defect.

The satire has to get the underlying rational-choice puzzle right: at every step the marginal trade is one the buyer would individually accept, while the terminal state is one the same buyer would refuse even in exchange for the full cumulative payment, so the preferences come out intransitive. The jokes should come from the company's marketing and support language exploiting exactly that gap and treating it as a feature. Likely material: a pre-commitment add-on, sold at a fee, that resets the ratchet and in doing so constitutes yet another one-step trade; support replies that refuse to describe the aggregate sensation because no single moment is unpleasant; a refund process that can only be invoked by advancing one more notch; completion statistics for customers who "graduate"; and a cheerful policy on regret at settings far from where the customer began.

The reductio should land without the narrator ever announcing it: by the end, a reader should feel that the company's logic and the self-torturer's logic are the same logic. Do not cite or quote any philosophical literature; stay inside the parody throughout.
