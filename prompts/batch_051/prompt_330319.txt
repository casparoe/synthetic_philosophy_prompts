I am preparing an advanced seminar on sortition and lottocracy, and I want to test a comparison that has been troubling me. In the Perseus Digital Library translation of Aristotle’s *Politics*, Book IV, 1294b, we find the following passage:

> I mean, for example, that it is thought to be democratic for the offices to be assigned by lot, for them to be elected oligarchic, and democratic for them not to have a property-qualification, oligarchic to have one; therefore it is aristocratic and constitutional to take one feature from one form and the other from the other, from oligarchy that offices are to be elected, and from democracy that this is not to be on a property-qualification.

The passage belongs to Aristotle’s account of how a well-mixed constitution can combine oligarchical and democratic features, and it treats election and sortition not as politically neutral devices but as constitutional markers. I am interested in how much of this evaluative idiom survives in contemporary defences of lottocracy. Modern lottocrats often argue that replacing elected bodies with randomly selected citizens’ assemblies would improve descriptive representation, weaken donor-class capture, and break the link between office-holding and campaigning. They tend, however, to treat election and sortition as mechanisms with predictable costs and benefits rather than as features carrying an inherent constitutional colouring.
