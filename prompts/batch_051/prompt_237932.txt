I spent most of my professional life in the physical sciences, and only recently began reading seriously in moral psychology. The work of Jonathan Haidt and his collaborators has particularly interested me: they propose that the human moral sense is not one faculty but several distinct foundations—roughly, care, fairness, loyalty, authority, sanctity, and liberty. To make this pluralism vivid, they repeatedly reach for an analogy from the biology I know well: the moral mind is said to be like the tongue, equipped with a small number of taste receptors that, combined and culturally amplified, produce an enormous range of cuisines and individual palates. In the political version of the argument, liberals and conservatives are described as being differently attuned to the same basic receptors, which is meant to explain why they talk past one another. What concerns me is the further use to which this analogy is put. Many readers take it to imply that left and right are merely different taste profiles, that neither is more correct than the other any more than one cuisine is more correct than another, and therefore that the psychology of left and right gives us no reason to think either side is morally right.

I would like you to audit this analogy rigorously. Begin by laying out the mapping in detail: foundations to receptor types, moral judgments to taste experiences, moral systems or ideologies to cuisines, and whatever else belongs in the table. Then identify the points where the mapping breaks down, separating the disanalogies that are merely cosmetic from the ones that matter for the intended conclusion. Say explicitly what the analogy imports from the domain of taste—assumptions about subjectivity, the equal standing of preferences, the futility of ranking—and whether those features actually carry over to moral judgment. Finally, tell me whether the conclusion that the psychology of left and right tells us nothing about who is right survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if not, whether there is a better analogy that preserves what is genuinely illuminating while avoiding what is misleading.
