I've been reading Benjamin Franklin's account in the Autobiography of his attempt at moral perfection, and a friend has pressed me with an argument he takes from one of its closing passages. The passage is the one in which Franklin admits that, after drawing up his list of thirteen virtues and keeping his daily chart, he found the work of eradicating his faults harder than he had supposed: as soon as one fault was under control, another appeared. He illustrates the point with a story. A man buying an ax from a smith asks that the whole face of the ax be made as bright as the edge. The smith agrees, provided the man works the grindstone. As the smith presses the face of the ax hard against the stone, the man quickly tires. The smith tells him to keep turning because the ax is not yet bright all over but only speckled. The man stops and says he prefers a speckled ax. Franklin then applies the story to himself, saying he had come to suspect that a few faults in a character might be acceptable, and perhaps even useful for life in society.

My friend reconstructs the underlying argument as follows.

(1) A fully faultless moral character would require eliminating every moral defect, just as a uniformly bright ax would require grinding away every remaining speck. (2) Eliminating every moral defect is, for most people, extraordinarily costly, and may never be fully completed. (3) A person who retains a few defects still possesses a workable and generally decent character, just as a speckled ax still cuts. (4) Therefore it can be rational, and perhaps even desirable, to stop the project of moral perfection and accept a few faults rather than continue the labor. (5) But by the same reasoning, one could justify abandoning the correction of any stubborn vice—habitual dishonesty, say, or cruelty—once correcting it became costly, so long as the rest of one's character was decent. (6) That seems far too permissive to accept. So there must be a mistake between steps (1) and (5): perhaps the ax is not an apt model for moral character, or the fact that a perfect finish is costly and unnecessary for cutting does not show that moral defects are acceptable, or the argument slips between what is tolerable and what is desirable.

I cannot find the exit. Please diagnose the argument step by step, identify which premise or transition fails, and say what rejecting it costs. If the fault lies in the ax analogy, does that undermine Franklin's entire method of self-examination? If the fault lies in the move from difficulty to acceptability, can one still recommend rigorous daily self-review without being committed to moral perfectionism? Which alternative is the least costly?
