I keep running into a version of negative utilitarianism — the lexical-threshold sort — where there's a break at some intensity of suffering: below the threshold, pain and pleasure trade off in the usual way; above it, no amount of mild pleasure, or of relieving mild pain, can compensate for allowing even a little more of it. The thing is, people who defend or attack this view seem to be arguing about half a dozen different things at once and nobody sorts them. The threshold could be principled or arbitrary. It could be the same across persons or set by each person's own internal breaking point. Mild pains might aggregate, so that enough sub-threshold discomfort should count as above-threshold, or aggregation might only make sense below the line. Decision-making under risk gets weird once you've got what is effectively an infinite exchange rate. And under all of it sits the older question whether pleasure and pain are even commensurable enough for talk of an exchange rate to make sense in the first place. Suppose you had to draw the dependancy graph for this corner of ethics: which of these questions is actually load-bearing — the one that, if settled, would most reorganize the rest of the debate — and which are downstream or just things people burn time on? I want a defended ordering, not a survey. For each candidate question, say whether you think it's upstream or downstream, which criteria you're weighting (importance, tractability, how many other questions hang on it), how tractable it is with armchair tools rather than data collection, and what argument or counterexample would most move it up or down your list. Pretend I'm a grad student trying to decide what to spend two years on.
