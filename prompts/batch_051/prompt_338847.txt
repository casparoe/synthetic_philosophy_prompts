I lead the visual communications team at a regional public health agency. We produce a biweekly one-page bulletin for clinics and community centres, and our designers increasingly want to replace text with pictograms. After a long argument with a friend about whether some public-health claims simply cannot be pictured, I want to introduce Otto Neurath's picture-language discipline into our workflow. Please write a decision procedure we can run on every proposed bulletin item before design work begins. The procedure should operationalize Neurath's central commitments—including the rule that larger quantities are shown by repeating identical pictograms, not by enlarging a single symbol, and his broader view that pictures can show facts but may fail to express conditional, causal, or modal claims. It should be usable by designers with no philosophy background, fit a two-hour weekly review meeting, and respect that the bulletin is signed off by a public health nurse and the department head. For each step, say exactly what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with "leave this as text," and where judgment cannot be eliminated. Then run the procedure on three proposed items for a bulletin on diabetes and vaccination: (1) diabetes diagnoses are twice as common in one neighbourhood as in another; (2) people who get a flu vaccine are less likely to need hospitalisation; (3) diabetes is rising because of changes in diet. Make sure at least one case yields the answer that we should not use a picture, even though my team will dislike it, and explain what the procedure cannot do.
