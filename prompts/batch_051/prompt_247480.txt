For a legal philosophy seminar, I am planning a unit on legal fictions, and I want to anchor the discussion in a passage from Henry Sumner Maine's *Ancient Law* (1861). In the chapter on legal fictions, Maine writes:

> I employ the word fiction in a sense considerably wider than that in which English lawyers are accustomed to use it, and with a meaning much more extensive than that which belonged to the Roman fictiones. Fictio, in old Roman law, is properly a term of pleading, and signifies a false averment which the plaintiff is not permitted to traverse; such, for example, as an averment that the plaintiff is a Roman citizen, when in truth he is a foreigner. The early English law had a fiction remotely analogous to this in the allegation, required from every plaintiff, that he was in the custody of the king. But I now employ the term fiction to signify any assumption which conceals, or affects to conceal, the fact that a rule of law has undergone alteration, its letter remaining unchanged, its operation being modified.

I am particularly struck by the last sentence, because it seems to build an evaluative claim into the very definition of a legal fiction: a fiction is any assumption that "conceals, or affects to conceal," the gap between a rule's unchanged letter and its modified operation. That formulation treats concealment—or at least the appearance of concealment—as internal to what makes something a legal fiction at all, rather than as an incidental rhetorical feature or an abuse that some fictions fall into.

What I want you to do is interrogate that definition from the inside. First, state as precisely as you can what Maine must mean by "conceals, or affects to conceal," and distinguish the two disjuncts: concealment proper and merely affecting to conceal. Second, give me two criteria that a social practice would need to meet in order for a legally operative assumption to count as concealing or affecting to conceal a change in a rule's operation. Then apply those criteria to two borderline cases. The first is a modern specialty court that transparently announces it is treating a statutory word as having a different extension than its ordinary meaning in order to preserve the statute's purpose under changed technological conditions. The second is a legal system in which judges know that everyone in the profession knows that a phrase has been reinterpreted, and the judges continue using the old phrase solely because the legislature has not updated the relevant code. Which of these, if either, qualifies as a legal fiction in Maine's sense? Finally, tell me whether you think Maine's definition is too broad, too narrow, or roughly right for the purposes of a philosophical inquiry into how law manages continuity and change, and say what your answer implies about whether legal fictions are best understood as a kind of collective pretense, a technique of doctrinal evolution, or something closer to an institutional self-deception.
