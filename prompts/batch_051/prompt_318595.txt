In a course on the philosophy of law, we have been studying the idea that failures to act, no less than actions, can function as causes. Suppose a jurisdiction were to replace the usual rule—that a person is liable for an omission only where she had a pre-existing duty to act—with the following scheme. A person is liable for a harm whenever (1) her failure to prevent it was necessary for the harm's occurrence, (2) the harm was reasonably foreseeable to her, and (3) the expected cost to her of preventing it was lower than the expected harm she thereby could have prevented. Liability attaches to every person satisfying these three conditions, with a jury apportioning shares. I want to understand how this scheme would actually behave, not whether it is just. Trace the incentives it creates for ordinary passers-by, for professionals whose work exposes them to others' risks, and for potential claimants; identify where it would be gamed first and its harshest failure modes; and explain how its behavior in cases with many potential interveners bears on the philosophical question of whether an omission is a cause in the same way an action is. Can its failure modes be repaired without quietly reintroducing the old duty requirement through the back door?
