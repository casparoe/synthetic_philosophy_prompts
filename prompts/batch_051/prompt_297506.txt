I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and next month I am giving a public lecture to a mixed university seminar defending a specific intentionalist view of texts: that a text's meaning is fixed by the kind of speech act its author intended to perform with it—asserting, fictionalizing, satirizing, promising, and so on—rather than by what its words literally convey or by what later readers find in it, so that recovering that governing intention is the standard of correctness for interpretation. My core argument is that only a governing intention explains how the same sequence of words can function as a confession in one context, a scene in a novel in another, and a joke in a third. The audience includes two literary scholars who work on reception and adaptation, a philosopher of language who holds that meaning can outrun what authors had in mind, a legal scholar who studies statutory interpretation, and several working critics who resist the idea that their readings must answer to a dead writer. Draft a Q&A preparation sheet for me: the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased the way these people would actually put them, with what I should concede, what I should hold, and a note on the weakest point in my own position that I should prepare to defend rather than hope they miss; include the question I am hoping nobody asks.
