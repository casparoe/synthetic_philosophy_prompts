I am a novelist at work on a book whose protagonist has resolved, on principle, to live as a hedonist. He is not greedy or lazy; he has argued himself into the conviction that pleasure is the only thing worth pursuing, and he means to pursue it honestly, without self-deception. My difficulty is conceptual rather than artistic. When I write the scenes in which he examines his own motives, I find I do not know what counts as a pleasure. The word works smoothly in ordinary use, but under pressure it blurs.

I want a working definition of pleasure — something precise enough that a reflective person could actually live by it — and I want it tested against these cases:

1. The taste of a ripe peach eaten slowly, in good health and in pleasant surroundings.
2. The warmth of a hot bath after coming in from a cold wind.
3. The relief that arrives when a severe pain begins at last to fade.
4. The quiet satisfaction of finishing a chapter that had been stalled for months.
5. Hearing that a rival's novel has been badly reviewed, and catching in oneself a small, private lift at the news.
6. Being so thoroughly absorbed in writing that three hours pass without one's noticing.
7. The burn of very hot chilies, which some people seek out and call pleasant, although the same sensation would ordinarily be called pain.
8. Sitting in a waiting room feeling neither comfortable nor uncomfortable, with no particular feeling in either direction.

I am confident that the first two are pleasures and that the last is not. The middle cases are the test: I can feel the pull in both directions about several of them, and I suspect a good definition will force a verdict I did not expect.

The philosophical stake matters to the book. Hedonism seems to presuppose that these states share something — one property, one kind of value, one currency — so that a life organized around pleasure is organized around a single thing rather than around a label. That is sometimes called the heterogeneity problem. Give me the strongest definition you can, run it through all eight cases, say where it surprises you or fails, and either revise it or defend the surprising verdicts. In the end, tell me whether my protagonist is pursuing one thing or several, and whether his project survives the definition.
