I'd like a working definition of the species category — a general account of what makes a group of organisms one species rather than more than one — that gets the following cases right. My own use of the word is confident in easy cases, so I'll set those aside: two members of a single interbreeding songbird population are clearly one species, and a songbird and a sequoia are clearly not. The cases below are the ones that actually strain the concept. For each I'll give you my verdict, or flag that I don't have one. Propose the definition, run it through all seven cases, tell me where it gives a verdict I didn't report or leaves the case unsettled, and then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts. I'm more interested in a definition that earns my judgments under pressure than one that matches the textbook.

1. A chihuahua and a Great Dane cannot mate without human assistance, but artificial insemination produces fertile offspring. I call them one species without hesitation.

2. A salamander ring around a valley: five adjacent populations each hybridize with their neighbors, but the two terminal populations, which now overlap geographically, do not interbreed. I count the whole ring as one species because genetic continuity runs through the chain, though I recognize the case for splitting the terminal populations into two.

3. Two frog lineages in the same forest look nearly identical and breed in the same season, but they use different advertisement calls and never pair in the wild; in the laboratory they can be induced to mate and produce sterile hybrids. I take these to be two species.

4. An apomictic dandelion lineage reproduces only by asexual seed and exchanges no genes with other lineages, yet it is morphologically distinctive, stable over generations, and ecologically recognizable. I want to count it as a species, but I cannot say what property makes it one.

5. Two coastal populations of a marine bacterium carry the same species name. Their core genomes diverge about as much as those of two closely related mammal species would, and their ecological capacities differ sharply because each continually acquires and loses accessory genes from unrelated bacteria. I cannot settle this case; my judgment shifts in both directions.

6. A fossil lineage of shelled protists changes gradually over three million years. The earliest and latest specimens look different enough that a paleontologist would assign them to different species, perhaps different genera, but every intervening deposit grades smoothly into the next. I want to say the endpoints are different species, yet any boundary I draw would be arbitrary. I am torn.

7. Two plant species meet along a narrow, stable hybrid zone where hybrids have strongly reduced fitness; outside the zone the two forms remain distinct and do not merge. I count them as two species despite the gene flow, and I suspect reduced hybrid fitness should be part of the reason.

As a further test, tell me what your definition says about a case I deliberately left off the list: two healthy, fertile calves produced for a de-extinction effort by engineering a suite of cold-tolerance gene variants from woolly mammoths into Asian elephant embryos and gestating them in elephant surrogates. Conservationists disagree about whether the calves count as the extinct mammoth species, as Asian elephants, or as a new species. I care less about which label you pick than about which features of the case your definition treats as decisive, and whether that reveals a clause you should have added.
