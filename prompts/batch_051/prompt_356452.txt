Two people in my epistemology seminar got into it about the dogmatism paradox after class and neither would give ground. Marcus said the paradox shows something is wrong with our ordinary concept of knowledge. His version: if you know p, then any evidence against p is misleading, so from your own point of view you can set counterevidence aside without checking it. But that would let you ignore criticism about anything you take yourself to know, which is obviously bad epistemic hygiene. So knowledge cant be what we think it is. Elena said he was moving too fast. She said the paradox only bites on the assumption that you know that you know p; in real cases you rarely do, so the license to ignore never kicks in. And in the rare case where you do know you know—say you just proved something—setting aside a supposed counterexample for now is exactly right, since no counterexample could be sound. Marcus shot back that this still leaves the original problem: take his knowledge that the seminar meets on Tuesdays, or that 2+2=4. By her logic he may ignore any person who 'corrects' him forever. No sane epistemology can require one to double-check every known fact. Elena answered that he was smuggling in a crazy demand: checking matters only because we might be wrong about whether we know, not because knowledge itself requires it. If you genuinely know p, then any alleged counterexample is false, so investigating cant change what you should believe about p. Marcus said that's the paradox in one sentence—it makes your current belief the unfalsifiable arbiter of which challenges deserve a hearing. Please adjudicate the dispute move by move: who is right about which point, where does each overreach, and what does the paradox actually show? I want exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, and no introduction or conclusion.
