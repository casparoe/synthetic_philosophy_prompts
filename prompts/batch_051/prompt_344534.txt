Take the blue bus setup: a bus hits someone, and the only evidence linking the defendant company to the accident is that it ran 80% of the buses on that route at that hour. Or the gatecrasher version, where 499 out of 500 people at the rodeo are known to have paid and the defendant was there. Now suppose a country adopts a rule that naked statistical evidence like this can be sufficient for a criminal conviction whenever the trier of fact is satisfied that the probability the accused did it is above 0.95, with no individuating evidence required. Nothing else changes — same police, same courts, same sentencing. Predict what the legal system looks like in thirty years. Specifically: which kinds of crime does this rule actually get used for, and which kinds stay effectively unprosecutable under it? How do prosecutors choose their reference classes, and how do defense lawyers attack them? Does individualized suspicion migrate to policing, charging, or appeals, or does it basically vanish? Do juries and judges actually comply with the rule, and what happens to the public meaning of a conviction if they do? I want the chain of reasoning — mechanisms, incentives, and constraints, not a story — and I want you to tell me which predictions you'd bet on hard and which ones fall appart if, say, people get good at gaming the statistics.
