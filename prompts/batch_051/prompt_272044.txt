I keep returning to a parallel I cannot quite settle. In discussions of infinite regress arguments, the usual claim is that mere unendingness is not automatically a problem: a regress is vicious only when each item depends on the next for its existence, truth, or justification, whereas an unending series that is otherwise grounded can be benign. This looks structurally close to the contrast in computer science among three kinds of recursive definition: a computation that fails to terminate because it chases an ill-founded chain of calls; a well-founded recursion that bottoms out in base cases; and a productive or guarded coinductive definition that unfolds indefinitely while still determining every finite piece of output. Is the philosophical distinction best seen as the same three-way distinction? More sharply: would it be right to say a regress is vicious when the corresponding definition is neither well-founded nor productive, and benign when it is either well-founded or productive? I want to know what would have to be true on both sides for anything to transfer. Could something like a guardedness or productivity condition be spelled out for putatively benign regresses such as the regress of truths or infinite divisibility, and do canonical vicious regresses fail it? Or does the analogy break because philosophical regresses concern explanation, reasons, or ontological dependence, which lack the operational content of step and output that makes termination analysis work? Finally, identify at least one case where the analogy would predict a verdict the standard philosophical treatment rejects, or vice versa. Please separate what is settled in the formal material from what is genuinely contested among people working on regress arguments, and do not present a contested account of viciousness as the consensus.
