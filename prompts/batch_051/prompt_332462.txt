For a paper I am drafting on the ethics of self-presentation, I have provisionally settled on the following claims and would like a consistency audit before taking the argument further.

(1) There is a standing but defeasible moral reason to make ourselves legible to people whose lives our decisions significantly affect: to explain what we are doing and why in terms they can follow.
(2) Legibility demands more than truthfulness; one can answer questions honestly while remaining opaque, so discharging the reason in (1) cannot be a matter of merely not lying.
(3) We often lack reliable introspective access to the actual sources of our own actions, and a good deal of ordinary self-explanation is confabulated.
(4) Moral requirements cannot exceed an agent's capacities: no one is obligated to supply an explanation of a kind they cannot in principle produce.
(5) Intimacy raises rather than lowers the bar for legibility; the closer the relationship, the stronger the reason to explain oneself rather than retreat behind privacy.
(6) Nevertheless, a person can sometimes legitimately refuse to explain herself, even to someone close to her and even when the other person's distress is genuine, because holding some part of her inner life outside others' view can be a valid exercise of autonomy.

Which of these claims are in genuine tension with one another, and which tensions are merely apparent? Rank the problematic pairs by severity, say for each what would have to give and at what cost, and then identify the single revision that would restore the most overall consistency at the least loss.
