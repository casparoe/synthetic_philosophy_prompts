I keep noticing a self-supporting argumentative structure, and I want to know whether it has a settled name and, more importantly, how it should be carved up.

The structure runs like this. Someone wants to certify that a source of belief is trustworthy, and they do it by appealing to the source's track record. Two instances of the pattern I have in mind:

1. A person argues that she is entitled to trust her color vision because, over the years, she has looked at objects, formed judgments about their colors, checked those judgments by looking again in different lighting, and found that the judgments held up. But every one of those checks is itself an exercise of the very faculty whose reliability is at issue.

2. A person argues that memory is reliable because he can recall many occasions on which memory proved accurate. The record of past accuracy is itself a memory record.

The structure is not ordinary circular reasoning, where the conclusion is assumed among the premises. The conclusion that the faculty is reliable is not assumed; it is inferred from a track record. Nor is it quite the classic problem of justifying a rule of inference by using that very rule, since what is checked here is a contingent reliability claim about a faculty rather than the validity of an abstract rule. And if the move is accepted, it seems to yield knowledge of the faculty's reliability with no independent check, and from that a license to take the faculty's later outputs at face value. That strikes me as suspiciously cheap.

Is there an established name for this exact structure? If there is, give the name but do not stop there: distinguish the phenomenon from (a) plain circular reasoning, (b) a rule-circular justification of a rule of inference, and (c) the wider idea that no source can be certified from within itself. If no established concept fits exactly, say which nearby concept fits best and explain how the thing I am describing differs from it; if nothing fits, coin a term, define it, and mark boundary cases. I am after the conceptual carving, not a label or a reading list.
