I'm a novelist, and there's a puzzle I've been turning over ever since my writing group nearly fell apart over a chapter of mine. I can get readers to accept the wildest physical impossibilities without a fuss—a city folded up inside a suitcase, a woman who ages backwards—but when I wrote a scene narrated by someone who treats something awful as plainly good, the group didn't just say it was disturbing. They said they couldn't make themselves inhabit the narration, that my sentence there was nothing wrong in what he did to the boy didn't land as a fact of the fiction but as a demand being made on them personally. I've since learned that philosophers call this imaginative resistance, and that there's a real dispute about whether it's an inability—readers literally can't simulate the deviant evaluative perspective—or an unwillingness, where they can but won't, because imagining it feels too much like endorsing it.

I want to know weather that distinction is testable and, if it is, what the best test would look like. Design me a concrete study: what materials, what comparison conditions, what measures, and what result pattern would actually favor can't over won't. And be brutally honest about what even the cleanest outcome would leave unresolved about what happens when a reader refuses a fiction.
