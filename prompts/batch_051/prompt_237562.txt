Question: State the common prior assumption and explain its role in Aumann's agreement theorem. What exactly does the theorem rule out, and what happens when the assumption is dropped?

Student answer: The common prior assumption (CPA) says that all agents start from the same prior probability distribution, so any difference in their posterior beliefs has to come from differences in the information they receive, not from differences in how they weigh identical evidence. The CPA is usually defended as a modeling discipline rather than an empirical claim about actual people: on the Harsanyi line, seemingly different starting beliefs can be represented as different private information in a richer type space under a shared prior. For the CPA to do the work in the theorem, though, it has to be common knowledge. It isn't enough that there is a common prior; each agent must know that they share it, know the other knows it, and so on. Once that is in place, Aumann's agreement theorem says that if two agents' posterior probabilities for an event are common knowledge, then those posteriors must be equal; that is, rational Bayesians cannot agree to disagree. The result is strong because it does not require the two agents to have seen the same evidence. They can have private information, and what forces equality is only common knowledge of the posteriors. Dropping the CPA breaks this: with different priors, two agents can each rationally update on the same evidence and end up with different posterior probabilities, and that disagreement can be common knowledge without contradiction. If the common prior is in place, the theorem also has a dynamic consequence: as long as the agents keep announcing their current posteriors and updating on each other's announcements, their opinions must converge to a common value. So persistent disagreement among Bayesian agents has to reflect a failure of the common prior or of common knowledge, not a failure of Bayesian rationality.
