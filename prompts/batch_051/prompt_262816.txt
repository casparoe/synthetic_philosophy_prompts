Hey, I'm taking a course this term on population, scarcity, and the history of political economy, and I'm leading seminar next week on whether a theory like Malthus's can be 'right but early.' I keep feeling like his defenders slide between two different things: on the one side the modest claim that populations face real resource limits and must adjust somehow (delayed marriage, famines, migration, technological change, lower fertility), and on the other side the original geometrical-vs-arithmetical prediction that living standards would get squeezed. So I want to practice my objection against a good opponent. Please play a committed neo-Malthusian. You know the Essay well enough to defend the distinction between positive and preventive checks, and your allowed to grant that Malthus's arithmetic was crude, that he didn't foresee the demographic transition, and that he underestimated the way population pressure can call forth innovation. But you will not give up the core: that the tendency of population to outrun the means of subsistence is a real, standing pressure, and that the last two centuries show that pressure being relieved by preventive checks, not that the pressure didn't exist. Stay in character until I say 'stop.' Keep replies to a paragraph or two, argue like a person, and don't hide behind 'it's more complicated than that.'

My opening move: Suppose I hold the core claim to the usual standard. If population grows and living standards fall, you'll say the tendency is biting. If population grows and living standards rise, you'll say preventive checks relaxed the bite. If population growth slows as countries get richer, you'll say that's a preventive check in another form. My question is simple: what honestly observed data pattern would make you think the core claim is false? If there's no such pattern — if 'right but early' can absorb any trend — then hasn't the theory stopped being a prediction about the world and turned into something closer to a tautology, or at least a habit of re-description?
