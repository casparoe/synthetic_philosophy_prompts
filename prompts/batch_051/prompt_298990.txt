hey, i'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so tell me if i'm mashing things up. i've got a made-up distinction i keep using in my notes and i want it stress-tested before i built a whole novel on it. i call it 'kinetic knowledge': the way a character's body seems to understand the scene before their conscious mind catches up. like a woman reaching into a cluttered drawer while talking, her hand already turning around the missing key's shape; or someone about to lose their temper whose shoulders have gone tight two beats before they register the anger. my working rule is: narrate the body's knowledge first—posture, reach, hesitation—and only then let the character have the thought. i half remember merleau-ponty (or a summary of him) arguing that perception is less like a mind representing a world and more like a body holding itself toward possibilities, the way a pianist's hands know a piece without thinking it propositionally. is my 'kinetic knowledge' basically motor intentionality with a novelist's coat of paint? where does my version go wrong, or stop being distinct from what a phenomenologist would already say? and is my rule of always narrating the body first actually doing the philosophy i think it is, or just a stylistic tic dressed up as one? be candid but charitable; and when you answer, tell me how confident you are in the claims i might embarrass myself repeating, and flag the one point you're least sure of.
