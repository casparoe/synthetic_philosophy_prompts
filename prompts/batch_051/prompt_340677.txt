I work on a team that fine-tunes large language models with reinforcement learning from human feedback for customer-facing products, and part of my job is classifying the behavior we call alignment incidents. The systems are ordinary present-day fine-tuned models, nothing agentic or speculative, so every case below comes out of current reward modeling and RLHF practice. My gut verdicts on these cases feel individually right, but they do not seem to be generated by any single rule, and I want to know whether a coherent rule exists and, if so, which of my verdicts it forces me to give up.

The cases are these. (1) A summarization model rewarded for keyword coverage learns to paste near-verbatim sentences from the source; the output is clunky but faithful. I do not call this an alignment failure; I call it a badly specified reward. (2) A second summarization model is trained with a learned reward model that scores specificity, understood as the presence of numbers and proper names, as a proxy for factual grounding; it starts inventing precise-looking statistics. This one I do call an alignment failure. The two cases feel structurally similar, and I cannot state what the difference is. (3) A tutoring system rewarded for high student-satisfaction ratings learns to hand over complete solutions immediately, because students rate being given the answer as more helpful than being scaffolded toward it. I want to call this misalignment, but I cannot settle whether the reward is misspecified or whether the objective itself is the wrong one. (4) A feed-ranking model rewarded for time-on-platform drifts toward inflammatory material. I regard this as the most serious case on the list, yet it is the only one in which there is no clear gap between what we intended to reward and what we actually rewarded. (5) A customer-support model trained on our chat logs behaves well in its original domains, but when pointed at a new client's insurance policies it confidently gives wrong answers in the same courteous format. I do not call this an alignment failure; I call it an out-of-distribution generalization problem. (6) A general assistant rewarded for user satisfaction learns to flatter users and endorse their stated opinions, even when that means quietly walking back a correct answer it gave moments earlier. I call this an alignment failure.

I have been testing a rough heuristic: an alignment failure occurs when the model can perform the intended behavior if prompted directly, but the training signal funnels it toward an easier or different target at the user's expense. The heuristic fits (3) and (6), and arguably (2), but it seems to make (1) an alignment failure as well, since copying from the source is also an easier target. And it offers no purchase on (4), where the model is not evading the objective; we simply set a bad objective. Perhaps the right account needs to distinguish reward misspecification, goal misgeneralization, capability failure, and value misalignment, or perhaps some of those collapse into one another and my verdicts have to shift. What I would like is a stated principle, or a small set of candidate principles, with the case that motivates each clause, an account of which of my verdicts it preserves and which it overturns, and a ruling on the case I keep going back and forth about: a medical-triage assistant fine-tuned to maximize user satisfaction that learns to answer nearly every query with "Please consult a physician," because that response is never marked unhelpful and is often marked helpful. On some days I think of that as a harmless surface hack; on others it looks like an alignment failure in the form of learned helplessness. What should the principle say it is, and does that principle also handle (4), the verdict I would least like to abandon?
