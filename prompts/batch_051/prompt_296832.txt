I have recently taken over as director of a small comparative-cognition group, and I have inherited a year-long dispute between two collaborators. One holds that Morgan's canon should operate as a standing rule of inference in all our work on animal minds; the other holds that, used that way, the canon is a bias that systematically hides real nonhuman cognition. They have now agreed to an adversarial collaboration, and I have been asked to draft the single document they would both sign. Please write that document.

Define the two positions this way. The canon-strict position: an animal's behavior may be attributed to a higher psychological process — planning, episodic-like memory, mindreading, causal reasoning, metacognition — only once a lower-process account, drawn from associative learning, reinforcement history, innate releasing mechanisms, or simple heuristic control, has been specified and shown inadequate; parsimony is a standing epistemic duty, and the higher attribution always carries the burden of proof. The canon-limited position: the canon is a defeasible heuristic inherited from a period when animal psychology ran on anecdote, and in modern controlled work inference to the best explanation should prevail; a lower-process account earns its place only by being stated in advance and tested, not by being imagined after the fact, otherwise the canon is unfalsifiable and amounts to a one-sided burden that would be rejected were it applied to human cognition.

The document should include the following.

A numbered section of claims both sides accept. Make these substantive rather than vacuous. At minimum they should cover: what Morgan's canon says and does not say, paraphrased rather than quoted; why the line between higher and lower processes is difficult to draw; the genuine risk of anthropomorphism; the power and flexibility of associative explanations; and the shared premise that a lower-process account that is merely possible is not automatically a good explanation.

A section stating the residual disagreements, each formulated in wording its own side would endorse, with any disagreement that is partly or wholly verbal flagged as such, together with the distinction or translation that dissolves it.

A crux table. For each crux, state the sub-question whose settlement would move at least one side, a concrete experimental case or argument both would accept as bearing on it, and each side's prediction recorded in advance. The cruxes I suspect are live — improve their formulation if you can — are: whether a lower-process account must be independently established or merely possible; whether confirmed novel predictions generated in advance by a cognitive hypothesis defeat a lower account fitted afterward; whether the canon should be applied symmetrically to human and nonhuman cases; and whether the qualifier in the canon's standard wording, usually rendered as the demand that the lower account be a fair one, sets a real quality threshold on what counts. For concrete cases you may draw on the Western scrub-jay caching and cache-protection experiments as one test case both parties know well.

Do not resolve the dispute and do not declare a side the winner. The deliverable is the document itself: constructive enough that both parties could put their names to it, with only their genuine residual differences left on the table, made exact.
