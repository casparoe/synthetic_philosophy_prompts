You are a teaching assistant grading an upper-level undergraduate philosophy of physics exam. Below are the question and one student's answer. Question: Compare the Everett (many-worlds) interpretation and the de Broglie–Bohm (pilot-wave) interpretation on two points: (a) whether the collapse of the wavefunction is a fundamental process, and (b) how each accounts for the appearance of definite measurement outcomes with Born-rule probabilities. Your essay should also assess the claim that decoherence makes the two interpretations effectively equivalent.

Student answer:

The Everett and Bohm interpretations agree on the central point: the wavefunction always evolves by the Schrödinger equation, and collapse is not needed as a fundamental process. In Everett's view, a measurement entangles the apparatus and observer with the system, and the universal wavefunction branches into several components, each with a different recorded outcome. Since the components are nearly orthogonal, they no longer interfere with one another, and decoherence explains how the cross-terms in the density matrix vanish, rendering the branches effectively independent. Although the dynamics is fully deterministic and all branches are equally real, probabilities in Everett arise naturally: each branch has an equal amplitude, so we should expect to find ourselves in each branch with equal probability, and the appearance of one definite outcome is simply a feature of our being located in one branch.

Bohm's theory begins from the same unitary wavefunction but adds definite particle positions. These positions are guided by the wavefunction via the guidance equation, and the guidance equation is probabilistic, so the particles move stochastically even though the wavefunction itself never collapses. Measurement in Bohm's theory reveals a particle's pre-existing position rather than producing it; the other components of the wavefunction remain, but they are irrelevant to the particle's subsequent motion. The Born-rule probabilities arise from our ignorance of the initial positions, which are distributed according to the quantum equilibrium distribution, so the apparent randomness is compatible with an underlying determinism.

Since both theories drop collapse and retain unitary wavefunction evolution, and since decoherence in each case explains why only our own branch, or the occupied component, matters for future observations, the two interpretations end up empirically equivalent for all practical purposes. The only remaining disagreement is philosophical.
