Ok, take the old Jim-and-the-Indians case, where a visiting botanist is told by a captain that if he shoots one of twenty captives the other nineteen go free, but if he refuses all twenty are shot. Change it like this. The captain no longer asks Jim to shoot anybody. Instead he tells the twenty captives that, by sundown, at least one of them must step forward to be shot; if nobody steps forward, all twenty will be shot. If one or more people step forward, the captain picks one of the volunteers by lottery, shoots that one, and lets the rest go. The captives may talk beforehand, but any promises they make are nonbinding, and Jim is there the whole time but has no role. Work through how the standard analyses change.

First, run the utilitarian reasoning properly. What does each person's expected-value calculation look like once the wait-and-see logic of a volunteer's dilemma is in play? Spell out whatever you assume about how much they care about each other, and say whether the utilitarian's usual one-life-for-nineteen arithmetic still goes through.

Second, think about the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism. Does it get stronger, weaker, or just change shape when the person sacrificed is selected from among volunteers rather than by an outside agent who has to do the shooting? Is the volunteering doing real normative work here, or is it the same sacrifice with better optics?

Third, the original case is often read as a worry about integrity: Jim cannot keep his hands clean if he acts. If Jim is now off the hook, does that worry vanish, or does it get redistributed onto the captives? And is that redistribution itself a version of the separateness problem?

End with a verdict: does this variant still separate the utilitarian from the anti-utilitarian, and should either side revise what it said about the original case after seeing what the variant does to it? Write the response in American English.
