I am a retired natural scientist—most of my working life was spent in experimental physics—and I have recently been reading philosophy of science. I am now preparing to interview a philosopher of science for a small podcast. She defends a specific, strong version of the view that elegance in scientific and mathematical theorizing is a genuine epistemic signal: on her account, the aesthetic appeal of a theory is not merely a psychological motivator or a heuristic for generating candidates, but a fallible yet real piece of evidence about a theory's likely truth or fruitfulness, carrying evidential weight over and above predictive success and other empirical virtues. She is careful to say that elegance is neither necessary nor sufficient, but she insists that it should move one's credence on its own.

Draft eight to ten interview questions that push hard on this position rather than letting her restate it, with follow-ups counted as questions. Please make sure the set includes at least one question on each of the following five pressure points, and for each such question, a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give:

(1) The base-rate problem: how can she show that elegance has a real track record as a guide to truth, rather than a selection effect in which elegant successes are remembered and elegant failures are forgotten or reclassified as not truly elegant after the fact?

(2) The conflation problem: whether elegance collapses several distinct virtues—symmetry, unification, formal simplicity, familiarity, tractability—and whether she can isolate the specific component that is supposed to carry evidential weight.

(3) The selection-of-attention problem: if scientists preferentially develop, fund, and test theories they find elegant, then the observed correlation between elegance and eventual success is partly manufactured; how does she establish that elegance contributes to truth rather than merely to which theories get the chance to be confirmed?

(4) The history of false elegance: aesthetically compelling frameworks that were empirically overturned or abandoned, such as circular celestial motion before Kepler's ellipses, the luminiferous ether, or static cosmological models, and how she avoids explaining those away in a way that makes the thesis unfalsifiable.

(5) The exit condition: under what specified circumstances would she abandon or substantially weaken the thesis—how much empirical anomaly should outweigh elegance, and can she state that trade-off as a likelihood ratio, a stopping rule, or some other non-aesthetic criterion?

For each question, give a terse note on what the guest will likely say and how the follow-up is designed to prevent her from retreating to a weaker or more defensive version of the view. The questions should form a single escalating arc rather than a list of independent challenges, and the final question should be the one she can least deflect. Do not include icebreakers or questions about her intellectual biography.

Lead with a one-sentence bottom line stating the through-line of the interview, then give the questions with their reasoning. No preamble and no restating of the question.
