I'm a philosophy PhD student (mostly formal epistemology, so anthropic reasoning comes up a lot), and two people in my program got into a running argument about the doomsday argument after reading group last week. They kept pulling me in as referee and I completely failed. The exchange, as best I can reconstruct it:

(1) Dana: "I actually think the standard doomsday argument is just sound. Take two hypotheses: Soon, total humans ever = about 200 billion; Late, total = about 200 trillion. I'm around the hundred-billionth human who has ever lived. Under the self-sampling assumption, I should treat my birth rank as roughly uniform among all the humans who will ever live. Under Soon, being number 100 billion is totally unremarkable—I'm right in the middle. Under Late, I'd be in the first 0.05% of all humans who ever lived. That's a massive coincidence. The likelihood ratio favors Soon by a factor of about a thousand. So unless the prior on Soon was already a thousand-to-one against, the posterior says doomsday is likely. That's just Bayes."

(2) Marcos: "But the 'uniform among all humans' step is doing all the work, and it isn't compulsory. Why all humans? Why not all conscious beings, or all person-years, or all humans who are in a position to notice their birth rank? Switch the reference class to all sentient organisms and the math shifts; pick a narrow class like 'all humans who have ever heard this argument' and the effect can become whatever you want. The argument is only as good as the choice of reference class, and there's no non-question-begging rule for fixing that class. So an alarming doom posterior resting on such a sensitive modeling choice is way to fragile to actually believe."

(3) Dana: "Every Bayesian model involves choices like that—hypothesis space, how you carve up outcomes, priors. The existence of a choice isn't an objection unless someone can show the choice is wrong. For a question about human extinction, the natural class is humans. And while other classes can change the size of the likelihood ratio, the qualitative point—that being surprisingly early in your class is evidence for a smaller total class—survives most reasonable choices. Marcos needs a reason some other class is uniquely correct, not just a menu of alternatives."

(4) Marcos: "Okay, grant the class of all humans. There's still a factor missing. The Late hypothesis contains a thousand times as many humans, so there are a thousand times as many particular people who could have been me. That means Late predicts my existence much more strongly to begin with—that's the self-indication assumption. When you multiply it out, the thousandfold SIA boost to Late cancels the thousandfold SSA penalty for my being early. So the entire doomsday shift comes from SSA alone, and SSA is exactly the contested move. Dana hasn't shown the data favor Soon; she's assumed the sampling rule that makes them favor Soon."

(5) Dana: "But SIA is the loaded principle, not SSA. SIA says that between two theories differing only in how many observers they contain, you should strongly prefer the one with more observers. That has wild consequences: it makes 'the universe contains vastly many minds' confirm itself by sheer numbers, and in our case it functions as a prior that humans will be numerous simply because a huge future contains more people. My existence isn't the evidence in question—it's a precondition of the whole setup. SSA is the neutral default: conditional on being in the class at all, draw your rank uniformly. You can't cancel a likelihood with a principle you haven't earned."

(6) Marcos: "Then consider the false-alarm problem. Take someone at a point in prehistory when only a few million humans had lived so far. Running the same argument, they'd conclude the total is probably small and the end is near. In our kind of world, if the long-lived branch is true, they were wrong. More importantly: on SSA, every generation gets to say it's typical and infer that the end is probably coming soon. A rule that always outputs 'the end is probably near' should look like a factory for false alarms, not like neutral bookkeeping. Unless there's an error theory for why all those past doomsday inferences failed, I don't see why I should trust this one."

(7) Dana: "A rational method can generate plenty of false alarms when the low-probability branch is full of people who get to ask the question. If the long-lived branch is true, then all the early observers sit in a tiny slice and they're rational to be surprised by their rank—they're just unlucky. And your objection is survivorship: of course the past generations look wrong from here, because we're looking at a history where extinction didn't happen yet. That's not independent evidence against the argument; it's the very thing the argument is conditioning on."
