I have recently been asked to help design a small local-currency scheme for a group of independent shops and landlords. In thinking about it, I have been reading Berkeley's writings on money, and I find that the following set of views has come to seem natural to me. I want to check whether the package actually hangs together before I present it to the rest of the group.

(1) I hold that the wealth of a nation consists in the productive powers of its people — their labor, skill, land, and habits of industry — and that money is not part of that wealth. Money is at most a ticket or counter for recording and transferring claims on the fruits of those powers.

(2) I hold that the material or intrinsic worth of money is therefore beside the point. A paper issue that is as generally accepted as coin would serve just as well, and often better, than gold and silver, whose monetary use is no less conventional.

(3) I hold that a scarcity of money, or a sluggish circulation of it, can itself produce poverty and unemployment even where goods, skill, and willingness to work are abundant. Tight or slowly moving money leaves a people's industry idle rather than merely recording that it is idle.

(4) I hold that the right test of a monetary arrangement is whether it calls forth and quickens the industry of the people, especially the poorest, rather than whether it accumulates bullion or secures a favorable balance of trade. On this view the design of money is partly a moral task, not only a technical one.

(5) I hold a methodological view as well: a monetary explanation is acceptable only if it can in principle be translated into facts about individual beliefs and choices. Terms such as confidence, acceptance, and habits of industry are legitimate shorthand, but they must be capable of that translation, or they are placeholders rather than explanations.

(6) Finally, I have drawn from all of this the practical conclusion that our group can improve the neighborhood by issuing its own paper tickets, capped in number and accepted by the participating shops and landlords, without waiting for central money.

I would like an audit of this specific set, not a general assessment of Berkeley. Which of these commitments are in genuine tension with one another, and which pairs only seem to be? Please rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad; for each, say what has to give and what the cheapest repair would be. Then tell me which single revision would do the most to make the whole set coherent, and what it costs.
