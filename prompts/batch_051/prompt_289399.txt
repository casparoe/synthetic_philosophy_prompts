So I just got back from a long argument with a friend about whether you can lock in a deal now that's supposed to govern what happens after AGI gets built, and it's still bugging me, because I couldn't quite land my own point. His line is that a contract is just a prediction about how badly each side wants out, so if you make defection expensive enough now — escrow something real, use a multisig, write conditions that don't depend on the future being normal — the deal survives. My line is that every enforcement mechanism you'd use is exactly the kind of thing AGI would change anyway: courts, money, reputation, the fact that you're still a legal person with a phone number. Also the deal has to specify what counts as "post-AGI" before it can specify what each side owes, and by the time you can specify it you're already on the far side of it.

Write me a short story where two people actually try to do this — lock in a pre-AGI bargain about post-AGI outcomes — and let the story carry the concept rather than state it. Make it small and concrete: two people at a kitchen table or in a cramped lawyer's office, drafting actual text, not a manifesto. I want to feel the specific ways the thing could start to unravel (or, honestly, maybe not unravel — surprise me): the trigger clause that can't be defined without already knowing what AGI will be, the escrowed asset loosing its referent, the person who shows up to collect not being the person you shook hands with. The idea should show up in what they keep crossing out, not in speeches.
