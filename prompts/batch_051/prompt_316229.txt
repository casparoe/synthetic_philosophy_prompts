I am a software engineer with no formal philosophy background. Next month I am presenting a short paper at a small workshop on conservation and environmental value, and I want to prepare for the question period. The paper defends this thesis: the value of nature and wilderness is neither an intrinsic property of ecosystems nor merely instrumental to human preferences; it is relational — it arises from ongoing patterns of attention, care, use, and restraint that connect particular human and nonhuman communities. "Wilderness" is therefore best understood as a regulative ideal for choosing which relations to cultivate, not as a description of a value that exists independently of any valuer. My argument sketch: (1) attempts to ground intrinsic value in ecosystem properties covertly depend on a valuer's standpoint; (2) purely instrumental accounts cannot explain protecting remote places no one will ever use; (3) only a relational account explains both. A sub-claim I also defend: the monitoring infrastructure my teams build for conservation — camera traps, acoustic sensors, species databases — is itself part of the valued relation to a place, not a neutral overlay on a value that was already there.

The audience: mostly academic environmental ethicists committed to intrinsic-value traditions, who are primed to hear relational and instrumental views as weakening nature's claim on us; plus two restoration ecologists who distrust philosophical abstraction and care whether the view helps or hurts actual restoration practice; and one conservation-policy analyst whose first question will be what the position implies for land-use decisions.

Act as an expert in environmental philosophy and metaethics who has prepared many speakers for exactly this kind of mixed Q&A. Think carefully and first brainstorm a wide list of candidate objections before filtering. Then produce a Q&A prep sheet: the six to eight objections I will most likely face, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way this particular audience would phrase it, with what I should concede, what I should hold, a two-minute reply, and a note on where the reply is softest. Include the objection I should hope nobody asks, and identify the weakest step in my argument sketch that I should rehearse rather than hope they miss.
