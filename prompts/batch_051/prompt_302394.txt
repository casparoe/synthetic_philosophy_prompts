Take the 2011–2012 dispute over whether to publish two studies that made H5N1 influenza transmissible between ferrets, and analyze it through the lens of the unilateralist's curse: the idea that when any one actor in a group can act on an uncertain shared prospect, the effective decision tends to fall to the most optimistic actor. I know the standard sequence — an expert advisory board first recommended redacting key methods from the papers, influenza researchers declared a voluntary moratorium on the relevant experiments, the board later reversed itself, and the full papers eventually appeared — so don't spend the response retelling it. What I want is the mapping the curse requires and the mapping it resists: who the actors were at each stage, what the unilateral act was, what options and estimates each faced, and how well the curse's central prediction explains the permissive outcome. Then give an honest account of what the episode does to the theory — whether it confirms it, exposes conditions it never anticipated (gatekeepers, common goods, asymmetric stakes, actors who can block as well as act), or shows that the curse is better read as a structural claim about decentralized authority than a psychological claim about overoptimistic individuals.
