I have spent the past month teaching climate ethics, and the discussion keeps closing around the inefficacy objection: no single abstention, ballot, or purchase is large enough to make a causal difference, so why treat the choice as moral? I want to think through that objection by reading Ursula K. Le Guin’s short story “The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas” through it, because the story seems to me more exact about the structure of the problem than most arguments are.

For context: in the story the entire happiness of Omelas depends on the misery of one child kept in a cellar; the citizens all know this, and most stay, accepting the bargain as the price of their city, while a few walk away alone, expecting nothing to change. I would like a philosophical reading of the story in terms of individual contribution to collective harm and the inefficacy objection, not a plot summary.

In particular, please address the following. (1) In the familiar cases—climate, industrial meat, large elections—the objection says a given individual’s withdrawal or vote is inert, so abstention cannot be required. Omelas tightens that thought: both staying and leaving are causally inert with respect to the child, since one more walker changes nothing and the arrangement continues without any particular citizen. If efficacy cannot distinguish the two options, what kind of consideration does the story suggest should do that work? (2) Is Omelas best modeled as a threshold case, in which each additional walker raises the chance that the arrangement becomes untenable even if no one expects to be the decisive one? The walkers themselves seem to expect nothing to change. Does the story take that kind of expected-difference reasoning seriously, or does it imply that such calculation is beside the point? (3) Are the walkers’ departures best read as expressive acts, as claims about the kind of person they refuse to be, or as something else? Does the story treat a gesture that helps no one as carrying genuine practical weight, and do you think that is right? (4) An honest reading must also note where the allegory strains. In climate, meat, and elections, the victims are numerous and diffuse, the causal chains are scattered, and the arrangement was never a single known bargain. Does the inefficacy objection get stronger or weaker as we move from Omelas to those cases?

Conclude by saying what the story adds to the philosophical debate over individual inefficacy that a cleaner argument would leave out. Please keep the response to at most 800 words, and open with a one-sentence thesis stating the reading you will defend. I am interested in the argument and the reading rather than in references, so please give no footnotes or bibliography.
