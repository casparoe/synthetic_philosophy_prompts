I have been trying to get clear on the regulative principle of worship, and I keep tripping over the line between an element of worship and a mere circumstance of how worship is ordered. Here are the criteria I have been working with for something's being an element of public worship on a regulative-principle view, and therefore something that needs positive scriptural warrant:

(1) Act-character. An element is something the gathered assembly does or offers to God; a circumstance is something done to or around the assembly to make a commanded act possible, orderly, or fitting.

(2) Content-character. An element has repeatable substance of its own — words, images, movement — that can be assessed as fitting or unfitting for worship; a circumstance is comparatively contentless, like the hour of meeting, the seating, the lighting level, or who stands where.

(3) Separability. Removing an element removes an instance of worship; removing a circumstance leaves the commanded worship going on, only less convenient, less expressive, or less focused.

(4) Recognition. The congregation itself would identify an element as a distinct item in the service, rather than as part of the manner in which some other item is carried out.

Now take this borderline case. A congregation that otherwise follows the regulative principle strictly — unaccompanied psalm-singing, no instruments anywhere else in the service, no images in the sanctuary, no set prayers beyond the Lord's Prayer — includes a regular three-minute meditation time between the corporate confession of sin and the scriptural assurance of pardon. The lights are lowered; a single sustained chord rises and falls; a projector shows a deliberately ordered sequence of natural images — dust, dry ground, dark water, then dawn. No one speaks or reads during those three minutes, and there are no words on the screen. The congregation is invited only to reflect on the confession just made. The elders maintain that the meditation time is not an element of worship but a circumstance of the confession — a way of supplying the time, quiet, and visual stillness in which the commanded confession can be made with due seriousness, comparable to dimming the lights or allowing a silence. I can see both sides.

On my four criteria, does the meditation time qualify as an element of worship, so that the regulative principle would forbid it unless it has positive scriptural warrant? Work through the criteria one by one. Where a criterion underdetermines the answer or cuts both ways, say so. Where the case puts pressure on the criteria themselves, say which criterion needs to be revised or dropped and how, if the regulative principle is to avoid both a loophole by which any innovation can be relabeled a circumstance and a strictness by which nothing can be arranged at all.
