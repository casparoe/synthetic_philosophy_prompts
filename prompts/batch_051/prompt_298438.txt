I work in the strategy group of an AI company, and I've been asked to draft an internal standard for what counts as an "alignment tax." The context is the competitiveness constraint: our leadership treats every dollar and benchmark point spent on safety as a cost that competitors may not be paying, and they want a ledger that separates genuine alignment taxes from other kinds of sacrifice; the standard I write will determine what gets reported to the board and which projects get labeled safety liabilities. Last night I had a long argument with a friend about this. She took the view that an alignment tax is simply any capability, speed, benchmark score, or revenue given up for the sake of safety. I pushed back that this makes the term swallow everything: if every foregone gain counts as a tax, the concept stops marking the specific tradeoff between making a model more capable and making it safer, and it becomes useless for decisions. We never settled it.

Please give me a working definition of alignment tax that correctly classifies these cases: (1) the extra inference cost of running a moderation model over every output; (2) benchmark-score losses caused by refusing to train on data contaminated with the benchmark; (3) revenue forgone because the model declines legal but harmful requests; (4) salaries for an interpretability team; (5) a decision not to sell a model to a government that might misuse it; and (6) a slower launch schedule caused by red-teaming. I think (1) and (6) are clear cases of the tax, (2) is clearly not, and I have no settled view about the middle ones. Propose the definition, run it through all six, say where it surprises you or forces you to revise, and identify the verdict that is least secure.
