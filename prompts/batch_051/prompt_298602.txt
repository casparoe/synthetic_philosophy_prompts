So I've been thinking about this thing lawyers call desuetude, and I want to understand what it philosophically presupposes about law. Here's the practice I have in mind. My state still has criminal statutes on the books against adultery and fornication, plus an old Sunday trading ban that nobody has enforced since the seventies. Every few years someone introduces a bill to repeal them, it dies in committee, and everyone just shrugs because the laws are, in practice, dead. The courts keep treating them as perfectly valid laws — they just sit there unused. And then every once in a while a prosecutor dusts one off and actually charges someone under it, and the courts generally don't treat the fact that everyone assumed the law was dead as any kind of defense. Some legal systems apparently have a doctrine that a law can lapse through long non-enforcement, but mine doesn't, and the courts act like that's fine. What philosophical commitments does this practice presuppose about what law is? It seems to presuppose that a law's validity comes entirely from its having been properly enacted, and has nothing to do with whether it actually operates, gets enforced, or shapes anyones behavior. But the same system also insists that punishment requires fair notice, that law is supposed to be predictable and applied evenhandedly, and that one of law's whole points is to guide conduct. How is that not inconsistent? I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory of law behind treating a dead letter as fully valid, separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it's underdetermined — where several rival theories of law could all license the same practice. Then tell me what would have to change if the system instead held that a law no longer enforced ceases to be law.
