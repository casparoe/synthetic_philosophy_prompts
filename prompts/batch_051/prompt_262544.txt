I'm a software engineer rather than a student of philosophy, but I've ended up thinking about decision rules after a side project in which I built a bot that automatically sizes bets for a prediction market; the distinction between arithmetic and geometric returns became vivid to me there. I'd like a rigorous analysis of a mechanism that a committee at my company is considering, which tries to apply the same idea to R&D funding. Our group funds around thirty small internal projects a year from a fixed budget. The current process has familiar failure modes: sponsors oversell their projects, committee members face no personal consequences for being overconfident again and again, and the portfolio drifts toward whatever sounds impressive in the room. A colleague has proposed adding a structured betting overlay, on the theory that a funding committee is making a sequence of compounding bets and should be judged the way a bettor's bankroll is judged — by how the stake compounds, not by the average outcome across the portfolio.

The proposal is as follows. Each committee member begins the year with an account of 100 points. For each project, the sponsor must register in advance a concrete success criterion and a payoff multiple m, defined as points of value per point of cost if the criterion is met. When a member votes to fund a project, the member stakes a fraction of their current account equal to the Kelly fraction for a bet that wins with their stated probability p and pays m-to-1, which is (p(m+1) − 1)/m, with the stake forced to zero if the edge is nonpositive. A success credits 1 + m times the stake to the account; a failure wipes out the stake. Members who vote no neither stake nor gain. At year-end, each member's bonus is proportional to the natural logarithm of their final account balance, and anyone finishing below 50 points loses their seat for the following year. Funding is still decided by ordinary majority vote; the accounts are an overlay on the committee, not the actual budget.

Treat this as a mechanism rather than as a good idea in the abstract, and walk me through how it would actually behave. What does it incentivize in the way members choose their stated probabilities and in which projects end up funded? Where is it gamed first — in the success criteria, the payoff multiples, the voting, or the seat threshold? Who ends up on the committee after several years, and will the committee come to be composed of well-calibrated judges, lucky survivors, or something else? Which of the failure modes you identify can be fixed without gutting the underlying idea, and is the design a faithful implementation of the view that repeated risky decisions should be evaluated by expected log growth — or does paying the log of the balance simply assume that conclusion from the start?
