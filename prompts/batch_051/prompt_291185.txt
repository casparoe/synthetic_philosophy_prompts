I want a specification of the desiderata that any adequate theory of the badness of pain must satisfy — not a defense of one theory and not a survey of the literature. By a theory of the badness of pain I mean an account of what makes a pain bad for the subject who has it: why it is not merely another bodily sensation such as warmth or itch, but a state that normally gives its subject a reason to want it to stop and normally feels like such a reason. The specification will be used as a rubric against which evaluativist, representational, motivational, and plain subjectivist accounts can be assessed, but the weight of your answer should fall on the requirements and their motivating cases, not on scoring those accounts.

For each requirement, state it, give the case or consideration that forces it, and mark it as non-negotiable, negotiable in its details, or merely the preference of one school. After the list, identify the single requirement that eliminates the most otherwise plausible theories, and say whether any two or more of the non-negotiable requirements are jointly unsatisfiable; if they are, show where the conflict lies, since that is the part I most want to see.

The requirements must at least address the following. Pain asymbolia: a subject can report the location, intensity, and sensory quality of a noxious experience — a pinprick that is sharp, a plate that is burning — while denying that the experience is unpleasant or bad, not withdrawing from it, and sometimes smiling through it. The theory must explain how badness can come apart from painfulness, or else say why such reports should not be taken at face value, without dismissing the case by stipulation. Morphine analgesia: a patient can report that the pain is still present and recognizably painful while no longer being distressed by it, so the theory must permit badness to vary while painfulness does not. Ordinary acute pain, in which intensity and unpleasantness track one another so closely that the distinction is barely visible from the first-person perspective; the theory must explain why a genuine dissociation is normally hidden. Phantom limb pain, severe and located in a limb that is gone, with no tissue damage at the felt site, showing that the badness is not a property of current peripheral damage. Wanted pain — the runner's burning legs, the chili eater's burn, certain masochistic practices — in which a subject seeks or welcomes an experience they still describe as painful; the theory must say whether these are cases of outweighed badness, locally absent badness, or something else, without surrendering the datum that pain is the paradigm aversive state.

The theory must also keep three things distinct: the felt unpleasantness of a pain; the subject's overall attitude toward that pain, including dislike, fear, and the wish that it stop; and the pain's being bad for the subject in a reason-giving sense, since an asymbolia patient might retain the abstract judgment that pain is a bad state while lacking the felt aversion. Two boundary cases must be handled as well: a subject who reports that her pain is bad and says she would do anything to stop it while her autonomic and motor responses are flat, and a subject whose protective withdrawal and grimacing are intact but who reports no unpleasantness at all. The theory must say where the badness lives in each.

Finally, the theory should take a position on whether pain is bad in itself, bad only for creatures with a certain kind of evaluative or motivational architecture, or bad in a way that comes apart from both, and it should do so without settling the question by a verbal definition. End with a short note on which requirements you would defend as core and which you suspect are provincial to the human or clinical case.
