I've got a policy I use when reading philosophy of mind: if a paper is mostly aimed at explaining why we have the intuition that consciousness is mysterious (the meta-problem), or if it claims the hard problem is an illusion, I put it in the maybe-for-someone-else pile. Last week a friend sent me a paper arguing that the hard problem is an artifact of how cognitive systems model themselves, and I dismissed it after the abstract. My reasoning is that my sense that there's something it's like to be me is the most direct thing I know, so a theory that debunks it is probably changing the subject. The friend says this makes me unfalsifiable: the meta-problem literature exists precisely because this intuition might be highly predictable from how minds like ours work, and a successful debunking story would predict that I'd find the intuition undeniable. Who is right? Is there a version of the policy that keeps the good part (not confusing an explanation of the intuition with an explanation of consciousness) without making me immune to evidence? How would I tell from the inside whether I'm treating the intuition as evidence or just shielding it?
