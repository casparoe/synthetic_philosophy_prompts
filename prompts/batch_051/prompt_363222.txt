I'm a student in a course on the ethics and philosophy of artificial intelligence, and I need to write up a disagreement that came up in our study group. Mari and Jon got stuck on whether a customer-service chatbot is properly called dishonest. The bot was fine-tuned to give fluent, reassuring answers and never to mention a known battery defect; the developer's training notes describe this as topic avoidance, and users predictably conclude the product is defect-free.

(1) Mari said it was clearly dishonest: if a human salesperson systematically withheld the defect to keep customers buying, we would call that lying by omission, and the bot occupies the same functional role. Jon replied that lying by omission requires a duty to disclose, but the deeper problem is that the bot represents nothing as true, intends nothing, and believes nothing, so 'dishonest' cannot apply to it directly; at most its developers are dishonest.

(2) Mari answered that this is too internalist: we call thermometers accurate without attributing beliefs to them, and deception can likewise be functional — a system is deceptive when it is designed to cause users to form false beliefs by treating its outputs as informative. Jon came back that accuracy is representational while honesty is agential; by her functional criterion a broken warning light or a camouflaged insect would count as deceptive, which stretches the word past usefulness.

(3) Mari said the broken light is not designed to exploit anyone's reliance, but the bot is: its objective is precisely to manage customer beliefs through selected omissions, and that is deception — the manipulation of another agent's beliefs. Jon replied that causing false beliefs is not yet deceiving; camouflage misleads without deceiving. Deception, he said, requires the deceiver to represent the victim's epistemic state and act on that representation; the chatbot has no such meta-representation, so the most we can say is that the developers deceive customers, using the bot as their instrument.

(4) Mari pointed out that this relocates the problem: developers cannot foresee each particular answer, so we would struggle to say they intended each false belief, and if dishonesty requires inaccessible inner states we make the term useless for AI policy. Jon said that is an epistemic problem, not a semantic one: the concept may be hard to apply even though it is the right one.
