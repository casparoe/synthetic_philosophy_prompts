I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I'm drafting a long essay about the moral arguments surrounding the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. I want Christopher Nolan's film Oppenheimer to be the essay's through-line, and I'd like to read the film through a fairly specific philosophical lens: G. E. M. Anscombe's objection to the bombings and to the honoring of Harry Truman. You can take the following summary of her position as given. In the 1950s, when Oxford planned to award Truman an honorary degree, Anscombe protested; her central claim was that deliberately killing the innocent — in particular, intentionally targeting civilian populations as a means of ending a war — is murder, not a lesser evil, and that this is what the bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki did. She therefore thought Truman, who ordered the attacks, was not a fit object of academic honor, whatever good consequences the bombs were supposed to have produced. Nolan's film, by contrast, is built around a more ambivalent moral texture: it follows Oppenheimer from the Manhattan Project through the Trinity test, the bombings, and his later security hearing, and it moves between the excitement of the science, the argument that the bomb would end the war and save lives, the creeping horror at what has been unleashed, and a memorable scene in which Truman dismisses Oppenheimer's guilt.

Act as a professional philosopher working at the intersection of just war theory and film criticism. Begin by brainstorming, in note form, the key scenes and dramatic choices in Nolan's Oppenheimer that bear on Anscombe's distinction between deaths one intends and deaths one merely foresees (the doctrine of double effect), and the key commitments of her position. Then write a careful, sustained reading of the film through that lens. Where does the film dramatize or confirm her argument? Where does it resist or complicate it — for example, in its portrait of Oppenheimer's psychology, in its treatment of the scientists' limited control over how the weapon is used, or in its handling of the claim that the bombs ended the war? And what does the film add to the philosophical debate that Anscombe's argument, as a piece of polemical ethics, does not? I care less about the film's historical accuracy than about its moral structure, and I would like the reading to be honest in both directions: don't reduce the film to an illustration of Anscombe, and don't distort Anscombe to fit the film.
