Okay so I'm a novelist, and for about fifty pages now one of my characters has been monologuing a little theory, and I need someone to tell me whether I'm going to be embarrassed by it when the book comes out. I've been calling it (in my notebook, not on the page) 'reframed appetite'—terrible name, whatever. The character's claim is that self-control isn't really about resisting a desire, and it also isn't about precommitment, which he dismisses as cheating: hiding the car keys from yourself. Real self-control, he says, is attention work. You train yourself to see the tempting thing differently until it stops showing up *as* a temptation. His stock example: the third drink. It doesn't present itself as forbidden and delicious; it presents itself the way a food your allergic to shows up—just not for you, not a live option, not even a negotiation. And he insists its not repression, because nothing is being held down. There's no pressure, no pushback. The desire didn't get suppressed; it got redecorated out of existence.

So here's what I want checked. Is this actually a distinct category, or is it just ordinary preference change with some purple prose around it? Is it self-deception wearing a nicer name—you've fooled yourself into thinking the temptation is gone rather than actually changing anything? Where does it break, i.e., what's a case where no amount of reframing would work and you're just stuck white-knuckling? And is there an existing name for this I'd recognize if I heard it? I vaguely remember some old distinction between the person who doesn't want the thing and the person who wants it but overcomes the want—is my character just clumsily reinventing the first one, or is something genuinely different being claimed?
