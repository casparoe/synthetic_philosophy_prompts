Here is a puzzle about my own observance. I am a Christian, and I treat some biblical commands as binding on me now and others as not. I would like to know whether my judgments express a principled position or whether some of them have to go. The familiar threefold division of the law into moral, ceremonial, and civil categories strikes me as a label applied after the verdict is in, rather than a test I could use beforehand, so I will simply report the cases.

1. The dietary laws. I eat pork and shellfish without compunction. I do not think the food laws of Leviticus 11 bind me, and I do not feel obliged to preserve whatever point they served by finding a modern equivalent. The command as a whole seems dead rather than translated.

2. Mixed fibers. I wear garments that combine wool and linen, which the Torah forbids, and I never think about it. Not binding, and again no carried-over principle.

3. Lending at interest. I pay interest on a mortgage and would accept interest if I lent money, even though lending at interest to a fellow Israelite is forbidden (Deuteronomy 23). But I do think deliberately ensnaring a poor member of my community in a loan they cannot repay would be wrong. So here a rule seems dead while a principle against exploitation survives — which already complicates the contrast with cases 1 and 2.

4. The holy kiss. I do not greet fellow believers with a kiss. I take the command to concern a culturally specific gesture, and I try to keep the warmth and peace the gesture enacted. The form falls away; the principle remains binding.

5. Foot-washing. I do not wash anyone's feet after the service, and no one washes mine (John 13). I take the passage to impose a real, binding responsibility of servant-shaped humility. Again, form falls away, principle remains.

6. Head coverings. I do not require women to cover their heads in corporate worship (1 Corinthians 11). I take the instruction to be culturally shaped. But the text grounds the practice in the order of creation, and elsewhere I take creation-order reasoning to have continuing force rather than being discardable cultural costume. That pairing — set aside the form while keeping the ground — is the one I cannot yet explain.

7. The Decalogue and Leviticus 18. I treat the prohibition on incest and the prohibitions on idolatry and bearing false witness as binding on me more or less as written. I do not reduce them to a general principle before obeying; the rule itself carries the obligation.

8. The slave-master instructions. I treat the New Testament instructions to slaves and masters (in Ephesians and Colossians) as addressed to people inside an institution I am not part of and consider unjust. I do not apply them as they stand, but I do not simply discard them either: I think they contain a principle about acting righteously within asymmetrical relationships. Something survives here, though the governed practice does not.

I can feel the shape of a principle — roughly, separate a command's culturally embedded form from its rationale, and preserve the rationale only when it is moral rather than ritual. But that looks circular: I first decide which rationales are "moral" and which are "ritual," and then let that decision determine which commands survive. I want something prior to that classification.

So: is there one principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that assigns all or nearly all of these verdicts correctly? If no principle gets every case, tell me which of my judgments are doing the damage and whether they should be revised; be explicit about what your favored principle still cannot accommodate.

Then apply it to the case I keep going back and forth on: whether the Sabbath command binds Gentile believers now, and if it does, what counts as keeping it — rest from paid work, corporate worship, not making others work, or something thicker. And use it to classify the Jerusalem Council's instruction that Gentile believers abstain from blood (Acts 15). Must someone who thinks the way I do stop eating blood sausage?
