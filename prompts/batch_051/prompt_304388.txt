I am helping to draft the governance charter for a new cooperative community of roughly two thousand households, and Rawls's veil of ignorance has become a live issue for us. One member has proposed making the veil literal for the community's annual budget. The mechanism would work as follows. Each year, a random sample of residents sits as a budget assembly. The assembly must choose a single linear tax-and-dividend schedule—a flat tax on all household income, with the revenue returned as an equal per-household dividend—for the coming year. Before voting, each assembly member is told that, for that coming year, their own household's disposable income will not be their ordinary income. Instead, immediately after the schedule passes, each member's household disposable income will be set to match that of a household drawn uniformly at random from the distribution the budget office projects for that schedule; the community's finance board will make up or claw back the difference. Members see the projected distribution for any proposal, but no one knows which household they will be matched to until after the vote.

Walk through what this would do to the proposals the assembly passes, to the character of its debates, and to the coalitions that form. Where is the mechanism likely to be gamed first—for example, through side insurance among members, outside wealth, manipulated projections, or exit threats—and can any of those failure modes be fixed without gutting the veil? Does the assembly actually reproduce Rawls's choice situation, or does it change the question?
