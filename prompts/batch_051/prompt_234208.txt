I have been trying to work out what I think about social sanction — the cluster of practices loosely called cancel culture, including firing, deplatforming, withdrawal of invitations, and organized public condemnation — by recording my reactions to a series of cases. I want to know whether there is a principled view behind them, and if so, what it is. My gut on each case is as follows.

1. A streaming service drops a comedian whose act has long depended on contemptuous imitations of a particular minority group. The decision comes after she devotes an entire special to mocking a named private individual, drawing a wave of harassment onto him. I think the platform has done nothing wrong. This is harsh treatment, and I am prepared to call it punishment, but it is punishment that is earned.

2. A technology company fires a mid-level engineer after a screenshot from a private group chat he was part of at fifteen emerges, showing him using a racial slur. He apologizes without qualification. I think the firing is unjustified: whatever one calls it, it is a disproportionate response to a person he stopped being long ago.

3. A newspaper drops a columnist who has spent years arguing, in sober and carefully argued prose, for forcibly excluding a particular minority group from public life. The articles contain no slurs, and the author presents the case as a policy argument. I think the paper should answer him, not silence him. This is viewpoint exclusion, and it troubles me even though I find his conclusions abhorrent.

4. A film festival rescinds an invitation to a director after several people make credible but legally untested allegations of sexual misconduct spanning two decades, and attendees say they would not feel safe if he attended. I think the festival is justified. It is not trying him; it is protecting the event, and it may reasonably use a lower standard than criminal guilt for that purpose.

5. A university revokes an honorary degree from a retired politician because he wrote, while in office, a private letter urging a colleague not to hire members of a particular faith. The letter is published after his retirement. I think the university is behaving absurdly. An honorary degree is a backward-looking honor, and stripping one for a single newly discovered letter turns an institution into an instrument of a mob's retrospective indignation.

6. A small bookstore refuses to host a reading by an author who, a year earlier, was recorded screaming racist abuse at a neighbor during a property dispute. The author has since received therapy and issued a public apology that struck me as genuine. I think the refusal is justified: the bookstore is not a court, it does not owe him a platform, and his behavior gives customers a reasonable reason to stay away.

The case I keep going back and forth on is this. A well-regarded architect is removed from a public university's board of trustees after a journalist obtains his undergraduate diary, in which he wrote a series of bigoted remarks about several ethnic groups. The diary is forty years old. His public record since then is unblemished, and colleagues from the intervening decades express disbelief. I cannot decide whether the university is right to treat him as a continuing risk to its community, or whether it is punishing a man for someone who no longer exists.

My question is: what principle, or small set of candidate principles, best captures the verdicts I feel confident about? State the principle precisely, say which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which one I would have to revise, and then apply the best available principle to the case of the architect. I would also like to know which of the apparent tensions among my verdicts are only apparent and which are genuine.

Please write your answer in British English.
