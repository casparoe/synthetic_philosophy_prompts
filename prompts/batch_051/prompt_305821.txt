My roommate keeps pressing this argument on me and I cant find the exit. He says, take "bias" in the moral sense—not as a psychological mechanism but as a term of criticism—and take "preference" as a stable evaluative stance about how you or others should act. Then: (1) to call an evaluative stance a bias is to say it departs from the correct or neutral standard for the matter at hand; (2) so to count something as a moral bias, you need to be able to specify the standard it deviates from; (3) but in moral disputes the standard of correctness is itself part of the fight, and the people you call biased typically reject the very benchmark you are measuring them agains; (4) if the benchmark can't be established independently of somebody's moral outlook, then the charge "that's a bias" reduces to the speaker's own preference wearing the costume of a neutral standard; (5) therefore the preference/bias distinction in morals is empty—bias attributions are just preferences for different standards, and any preference can be redescribed as a bias by choosing the right benchmark. He admits the conclusion sounds insane and says that's the point: the premises are cheap and the steps are mechanical.

But it seems to prove too much. It would make a judge who is biased against defendants of a certain background no different in kind from someone with a mildly harsh view about sentencing, and it would leave no room for the bias I can catch in myself by my own lights. I'd most like to keep the distinction and throw out whatever premise is doing the damage. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the argument is valid, tell me which premise is least costly to give up and what the resulting view looks like.
