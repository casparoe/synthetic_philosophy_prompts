I'm a journalist who covers AI and the ideas that circulate around the industry, and I've been trying to get a handle on an argument people in the AI safety world keep putting to me, the one they call the competitiveness constraint. As I've heard it, making a system safer or more aligned carries a cost, the "alignment tax": extra money, slower iteration, or lost capability. In a competitive market, a lab that pays that tax loses to a lab that doesn't, because customers mostly buy capability and price, not safety they can't see. So market pressure pushes everyone to spend as little on safety as they can get away with, and the systems that actually get deployed are the least aligned ones that still function. Barring regulation or an industry-wide agreement, the default outcome is a race to the bottom.

But the industry I've been reporting on for the last few years doesn't look like that. The big labs now sell safety and "responsible AI" as an enterprise feature: banks, hospitals, and government agencies want red-teaming, audits, and models that reliably refuse bad requests. The same post-training step where humans rank answers, reinforcement learning from human feedback, made the models both more capable and better behaved on standard evals, so that kind of alignment can look like a negative tax. And with regulators now treating some baseline of safety work as a legal cost of doing business, a lab that cut all of it wouldn't just win the market; it would get locked out or sued. On the surface, competition has been pushing toward more of the observable kind of alignment, not less.

Which leaves me unsure whether the original argument is dead, or whether it was never about the kind of alignment you can put in a spec sheet, and the last few years only changed the meaning of one of the words. My hunch is that the argument survives, because the tax was supposed to fall on the hard-to-measure stuff, robust behavior in novel situations and actual goals rather than surface compliance, while enterprise customers and regulators can only check the shallow stuff. But I'd like a careful verdict rather than my hunch. Can you say exactly which premise, if any, the current situation puts real pressure on, whether the competitiveness constraint needs repair or can be restated, and what's left of the race to the bottom now that some safety work is also a product feature and a legal requirement? Just continuous prose for the answer, no headings, bullets, bold, or tables, like an email.
