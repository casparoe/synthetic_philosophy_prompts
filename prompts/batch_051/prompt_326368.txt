Ok so here's a thing I keep going back and forth on with Rawls and religion. In Political Liberalism he seems to say that when citizens debate constitutional essentials and matters of basic justice in the public political forum, they should stick to public reasons—values and arguments people could reasonably accept whatever their comprehensive doctrine happens to be—and should not rest their case on their religious views. That sure sounds like it tells a religious citizen to leave her theology at the door when she does politics. But in the same book, and even more in his later essay The Idea of Public Reason Revisited, he wants to say that reasonable religious doctrines are a legitimate part of public political culture, and he builds in a way for them to be voiced: the proviso, on which you can introduce religious reasons at any point so long as, in due course, you also give proper political reasons for the same conclusion. Are those two commitments in real tension with each other, or does the proviso actually reconcile them? Give the strongest readings on both sides, including the reading that the proviso just lets religious citizens talk while denying their reasons any justificatory weight, and then say which reading you think requires the least special pleading and why.
