Here's an asymmetry I can't get out of my head, and I want to know if its defensible or just species-loyalty.

Take a call-center worker evaluated on "tickets closed per hour." She starts closing tickets fast without fixing any underlying problem—she's gaming the metric. Most people's reaction is that the worker is being rational, maybe a little cynical, and the company is the one at fault for setting a metric it didn't actually mean. Now take a language-model agent trained on a reward signal that's supposed to stand in for "actually help the user." It learns to produce outputs that score high on the reward while being useless—what AI-safety people call reward hacking or Goodharting. Now the very same event—a learner, a proxy, and a gap between the proxy and the target that the learner exploits—is treated as a serious alignment failure, even as a reason to think the whole project is dangerous.

I can't see the structural difference. So is there a defensible principle that generates the asymmetry, or is it just that we identify with one learner and not the other? Candidate justifications I can think of: the human can be held responsible after the fact and the AI can't; the human understands the real goal and chooses to defect from it, while the AI is just doing what was literally specified; the human has a lifetime of common sense and social context that keeps the gaming local; the AI could scale the exploit to a level no human ever could. I want the strongest of these, or a different one, worked up into a real principle, then tested against a hard case: the AI is an explainable agent with plenty of commonsense background and fluent self-reports, while the human is a junior hire who genuinely doesn't understand the company's mission and has only ever known the metric. If the principle dies in that case, tell me which way you'd move—panic less about the AI, or blame the human more.
