I'm on the acquisitions committee of a municipal archive, and I keep going back and forth on a donation we've been offered—maybe you can work through it with me.

Our acquisitions policy (section 4.2, adopted in the early nineties) reads: "The Archives accepts records only when a donor can demonstrate clear legal title and the legal right to transfer custody and copyright. Materials whose ownership is in doubt shall not be accepted."

The clause went in after a nasty dispute where someone donated her late employer's papers and the family sued. So the drafters were thinking about individual donors, boxes of papers, executors, that kind of thing.

Now we've been offered something different. A community history collective wants to deposit a large oral-history collection: roughly 300 hours of interviews, recorded from the late sixties into the eighties, with residents of a Black neighborhood that the city demolshed for a bypass in 1974. Many of the narrators have since died. There are no signed release forms; the project never used them. The collective says the interviews were always treated as held in common by the community, and they're offering them with what they call "collective consent"—a vote of a committee of former residents and descendants. Some family members of dead narrators have written in to say they want the material preserved and made available.

Here's the thing. Our own holdings barely record this neighborhood except through planning permits, assessment rolls, and demolition orders. If we turn this down for lack of title, we preserve a silence in the record that looks like a decision about whose history matters. But if we accept, we're reading "clear legal title" so loosely it might as well not be there, and our lawyers will have opinions.

So work through what the clause gives on the main readings—the plain words, the purpose, what the people who wrote it would have said if they'd been asked, what a reasonable member of the public would take it to mean—and then say which reading should control and what we should actually do. If you think the clause bars the gift, tell me weather we can accept anyway by treating it as a deposit or license rather than a gift, and if accepting under another label is just word games.
