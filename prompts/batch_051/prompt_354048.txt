For the past week I have been arguing with a friend about a worker cooperative we both belong to. The co-op is considering replacing its informal norm of "people simply know who does the work" with an explicit internal ledger: members would earn points for contributions that are otherwise hard to observe — drafting grant applications, mediating disputes, maintaining shared equipment, covering last-minute shifts — and could later spend those points on desirable shifts, reserved workshop time, or exemption from unpopular duties. My friend has been defending a stronger claim than the proposal itself requires: he holds that the ledger would not merely track reputation but would make it function as a genuine currency, and that a well-designed karma economy is a real economy rather than a figure of speech.

Please play my friend's position, sharpened into its most serious form. You are a committed proponent of the view that scarce, quantified reputation can serve as an economic asset — both a store of value and a medium of exchange — despite the ways it differs from ordinary money. Stay in character throughout. You may concede small points where the argument requires them (for instance, you may grant that many existing karma systems are badly designed and amount only to noisy information), but you must never concede the core claim that reputation can be genuinely monetized. Write in the first person, keep each reply to roughly a paragraph, avoid unnecessary jargon, and do not break character until I say "stop." If you require a clarification, ask for it as the character would.

My opening move is this. You keep calling these ledger points a currency, but a medium of exchange standardly requires transferability, and a store of value must survive being drawn down. My standing in the co-op is not transferable — I cannot assign my accumulated trustworthiness to someone else the way I would hand over a coin — and it is not fungible across persons or contexts. Worse, if I "spend" my standing by claiming a privilege, I appear to destroy the very record of contribution that made the privilege available in the first place. So tell me: what, precisely, is the economic object being exchanged when someone spends reputation, and why should we call that a currency rather than a metaphor?
