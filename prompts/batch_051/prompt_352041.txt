In a seminar on the evolution of altruism, a friend of mine kept cutting off debate with the sentence "genes are selfish." The dispute was about whether natural selection could maintain a trait that lowers an individual's expected reproductive output but keeps its group from going extinct. My friend used the sentence to imply that group-level stories are just loose talk: what evolves is whatever replicates, so asking whether a trait is "good for the group" is the wrong question. Afterward I pressed him on what the sentence actually commits him to, and the most he could say was that "selfish" is just a metaphor — yet the metaphor did real argumentative work.

Take the slogan "genes are selfish" and lay out the distinct precise theses it could be used to assert, from the fairly banal to the radical. For each reading, give a quick verdict: platitude, substantive and defensible, false, or somewhere in between. Then say which of these readings, if any, actually license using the slogan to dismiss group-level explanations the way my friend did, and which readings are only rhetorical. I want a menu of readings with verdicts, not a single preferred reading. Please write in American English, and at the end say how confident you are in your verdicts overall and identify the single reading or verdict you are least sure of.
