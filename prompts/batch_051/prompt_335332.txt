A colleague has offered the following argument, which he calls the scale argument, to show that even a very low probability that insects are sentient should lead us to treat many routine practices as gravely wrong.

(1) If a creature can suffer, then causing it to suffer matters morally in the same kind of way that causing a mammal to suffer does, even if the weight per creature is much smaller.
(2) For any given insect, there is at least a small but non-negligible probability p that it is sentient and can suffer, even on the most skeptical plausible view.
(3) When deciding under uncertainty about which creatures can suffer, we should maximize expected moral value, discounting possible suffering by the probability that it is real.
(4) Therefore the expected moral cost of harming one insect is p times the badness of that insect's suffering, and this is positive rather than zero.
(5) Routine human practices — agriculture, pesticide use, ordinary pest control, even transport — harm or kill insects in astronomically large numbers.
(6) Expected moral costs aggregate across all the individuals affected: harming n possibly-sentient creatures has expected cost n × p × the badness of the suffering per creature.
(7) When n is in the billions or trillions, n × p is still an enormous number even when p is very small.
(8) So many routine practices carry an enormous expected moral cost.
(9) Therefore, absent a comparably enormous expected benefit, routine practices that harm vast numbers of insects are morally on a par with, or worse than, serious harms to humans that we already condemn.

The conclusion seems unacceptable to me, yet I cannot find the fault. Is the argument valid? If not, say exactly which step fails and why. If it is valid, say which premise you would reject and defend that choice. I half-suspect that step (1) or the move from (8) to (9) is doing more work than it admits — in particular, something about adding up many tiny costs spread across many short-lived creatures and then comparing the sum to concentrated harm to a single human — but I cannot say whether that is a real objection or just squeamishness.
