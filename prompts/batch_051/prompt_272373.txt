The position I find myself defending is that, because the concepts we use in law, science, and ordinary life are tools that serve contingent purposes, the central philosophical task for concepts that matter — race, mental disorder, consent, marriage, knowledge — is ameliorative: the question is not what our existing concept picks out, but what concept we should have, given the purposes we want it to serve. What would count against this as a general methodological stance? I want defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, with a clear distinction between objections that defenders of the stance would concede count against it and objections they would dismiss as missing the point. I especially want your assessment of these pressures: cases where tracking an existing kind matters more than making the concept serve our aims; the implementation problem that a revised concept is only a proposal until speakers take it up; the metric problem of saying whose purposes settle what a concept is for; and the charge that the stance collapses into ordinary conceptual analysis or into applied ethics with extra steps. Say what the stance commits me to that a purely descriptive approach does not, and be willing to conclude either that the stance is well insulated or that it has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and whether that matters for a view of this kind.
