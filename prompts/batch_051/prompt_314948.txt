Here is the student's answer, roughly verbatim:

An intuition pump is a thought experiment whose concrete details are arranged to make one intuitive verdict feel compelling—sometimes before any explicit argument has been given. A neutral thought experiment would present the case as bare as possible, so the intuition you get tracks the principle at issue rather than the way the case is dressed up. In practice, the difference is mostly a matter of how much vivid detail is added: the richer the scenario, the more it counts as an intuition pump. Take Searle's Chinese Room. Searle imagines a monolingual English speaker in a room with a rulebook and a stack of Chinese symbols, mechanically matching incoming Chinese to outgoing Chinese without understanding any of it. The image of a lone person shuffling symbols is there to make it feel obvious that no understanding is going on, and Dennett singles this out as the kind of move to be suspicious of—the imagery does the persuasive work that the argument against strong AI is supposed to do. So once you have identified a case as an intuition pump, the intuition it produces should be set aside as rhetoric rather than counted as evidence. On Dennett's view, the upshot is that a careful reasoner should avoid vivid cases when something important is at stake and work instead with stripped-down or formal reconstructions of the problem.
