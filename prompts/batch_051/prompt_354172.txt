I'm trying to figure out what I actually think a "grammatical rule" is, and my gut keeps pulling me in two directions. Here are my verdicts on a pile of cases.

1. A brand-new verb like "dax": I'd bet every English speaker says "daxed" for the past tense without being taught. That's a rule of English, the default one, and it's about as clear a case as I have.
2. "go -> went": I'd never call "goed" the past tense, and I wouldn't say "went" is some weird exception to a rule that failed to fire — it *is* the past tense of go, full stop. But it isn't computed from "go" by anything; you just know it. If "daxed" is a rule, "went" feels like the opposite of a rule, a stored brute fact — and yet I don't want to say English has no rule for go.
3. "sing -> sang", "ring -> rang", "drink -> drank": these feel like a small rule, not the default. If you gave me a nonce verb like "spling" I'd hesitantly expect "splang", but if someone said "splinged" I wouldn't hear it as broken the way "goed" is broken. Same with "foot/feet" and "mouse/mice": I recognize the pattern, but if you tell me about a new noun "bloot", I'd say "bloots", not "bleet".
4. The Great Vowel Shift (why "bite" used to be pronounced roughly like today's "beet", and "house" roughly like "hoos"). I think of that as a rule too, and here's my verdict: it had no genuine exceptions among words in the right environments at the right time — if a word didn't shift, there's an interfering cause, like a later shortening before certain consonants, borrowing, or analogy. So here "rule" means something exceptionless across a whole sound system and across centuries, and it doesn't feel mental the way "daxed" does.
5. "dive": I say "dived", but "dove" sounds fully English to me, just not *my* form. I don't have a rule that produces "dove" from "dive" — it just sounds like an acceptable form out of nowhere. That seems different from both case 1 and case 3.
6. My niece said "goed" for a while. I'd say she'd induced the past-tense rule and was overapplying it, and "goed" being wrong shows the rule has to get beaten back by memorized exceptions. But in case 2 I said "went" isn't a violation of any rule for go... so which is it?

My half-baked principle is something like: a rule is whatever a speaker has to follow to be speaking the language, regardless of whether it's computed or stored; on that view "daxed", "went", and "sang" are all rule-governed in the same thin sense, and the only thing special about case 1 is my confidence in extending it to new words. But that collapses the difference I feel between case 1 and case 2, and I'd rather not loose that.

Is there a principle that gets all six verdicts, or nearly, and what does it say about the cases I keep going back and forth on: "sneak -> snuck" (which used to feel wrong to me and now feels fine), a kid who says "brang" for "brought", and whether a historical sound change like the Great Vowel Shift is even the same kind of thing as the mental rule that gives "daxed"? If no single principle gets them all, which of my verdicts should I drop?
