I am a second-year undergraduate reading history and philosophy of science, and alongside my studies I work as an editorial assistant for a university press that is preparing a series of lightly modernised student editions of early modern scientific texts. The series editor has just received an unfavourable review noting that earlier volumes modernised too freely: terms with no genuine contemporary equivalent were swapped for present-day ones, diagrams were redrawn using conventions the original authors would not have recognised, and some mathematical derivations were silently rewritten in modern notation. She has asked me and another assistant to draft a procedure that a general copy editor, with no background in the history of science, can apply in roughly twenty minutes per passage. It should tell them when a proposed substitution, gloss, or redrawing in a translation is a harmless aid to an undergraduate reader and when it is an anachronism that must be refused or flagged. It must not simply forbid all modernisation, because the series is meant to be readable by first-year undergraduates who may have no Latin and little mathematics.

Please write that procedure. It should set out the questions to ask in order and, for each question, state what in the text or the editorial apparatus would count as sufficient evidence to answer it. It should distinguish at least the following kinds of anachronism: terminological, conceptual, notational or diagrammatic, and anachronistic standards of proof or explanation. It should mark the points at which the decision stops being a mechanical test and becomes a judgment call, and state where the procedure must hand the decision to the series editor or an academic consultant rather than the copy editor. It should also say explicitly what the procedure cannot catch however carefully it is applied. Then apply the procedure to three worked cases: (i) a proposal to translate an early modern physiological author's talk of animal spirits acting in the nerves as nerve impulses; (ii) a proposal to replace an eighteenth-century chemical author's phlogiston with oxygen in a sentence about combustion; and (iii) a proposal to redraw a geometrical proof from a seventeenth-century mechanics text as an algebraic derivation in modern notation. Include at least one case in which the procedure produces a verdict the production editors will find frustrating, and explain why the procedure gives that verdict despite that frustration.
