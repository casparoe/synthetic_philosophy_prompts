At work we run a ton of A/B tests. We randomly split users into control and treatment, ship the feature to the treatment group, try to hold everything else as fixed as we can, and if the metric moves by a statistically significant amount we say the feature caused the move. I get that with observational data correlation isnt causation, and the experiment is supposed to be special because we do something rather than just passively watch, but I cant articulate what theory of cause is doing the work under that. A few things bug me about it. First, we treat the result as causal even when we have zero idea how the feature produced the lift; the mechanism is a black box and we are basically fine with that. Second, we act like a test tells us something that will keep holding after the test, and we discount effects that fade when we ramp to everyone or that look like a novelty effect. So we seem to assume causal relationships are the kind of thing that stay stable across some range of conditions, not just a one-off association. Third, we say the control and treatment groups have to be identical except for the treatment, but that sounds circular to me, since to know what counts as the treatment you already have to know which differences are causally relevant and which are just side effects or noise. So my question is: what view of causation does this practice presuppose? Is it basically the view that causes are things you can intervene on, and that a causal relationship is one that holds up under interventions? Does the circularity I'm smelling have a name, and is it a real problem or just a fact of life? And what would we have to change about how we run and interpret tests if we instead thought causation was just regular correlation, or if we thought you needed an actual mechanism before you could call something a cause?
