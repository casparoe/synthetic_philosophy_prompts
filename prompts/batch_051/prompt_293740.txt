Write a satirical ethnographic report of about 500 words in the voice of a sixteenth-century travelogue, but with the customary gaze reversed. The authors are visitors from a coastal Brazilian society of the kind described in Montaigne's essay Of Cannibals, and the country they have returned from is sixteenth-century France. Their report should describe French customs — public executions, judicial torture, wars pursued over fine points of doctrine, ragged beggars dying outside the houses of the rich, armed men obeying a child king — with the same fastidiously neutral tone European chroniclers used for the Americas. If the visitors' own practice of consuming slain enemies arises, treat it as Montaigne does: as a custom with an internal logic of honor and vengeance, never as cheap or lurid material. The satire should land Montaigne's inversion — that barbarism is largely a matter of where the observer stands — without ever stating it, and without winking. The visitors should be observant, serious, and genuinely puzzled rather than ironists, and the piece should read like a period document, not a modern op-ed.
