I serve as the student representative on my district's curriculum review board. We are considering a proposal to pilot a ninth-grade course called Formation Before Information, loosely based on John Locke's Some Thoughts Concerning Education. The course would replace one period of traditional academic instruction each day for a full year. In place of the usual content, students would spend that period on physical exercise, practical tasks such as gardening or carpentry, structured conversation about manners and honesty, and short readings drawn from history and biography that are meant to illustrate good judgment rather than to be tested. The course designers argue that schools have the order of priorities backwards: they treat academic content as the main business of education and character as a byproduct, whereas Locke held that the first aim of education is to form habits of virtue, wisdom, and good breeding, while book-learning is the least and last part. Opponents on the board say this is fine as a private tutorial for a seventeenth-century gentleman's son, but it is an irresponsible use of instructional time in a public high school, and it would delay skills students need for advanced coursework.

I need to bring a written decision memo to our next meeting. It should recommend either approval, rejection, or approval with specific conditions, and it should include the reasoning in a form the board can actually act on. It should address the strongest consideration on the losing side, not just restate the winning side. Please also engage a specific philosophical difficulty that came up in discussion: if Locke is right that the habits that govern adult conduct are laid down early through repeated practice before children can reason well, then a ninth-grade course may arrive too late to form character in the way he intends; but if older students are capable of reflecting on their own habits, then the course may be treating them too much like children. Tell me which parts of the Lockean model, if any, can survive being moved into a modern public high school, and which parts cannot, and make the memo's recommendation turn on that distinction.
