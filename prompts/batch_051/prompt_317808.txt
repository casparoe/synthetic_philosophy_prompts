I work in the ombuds office at a big public university, and we keep hearing from students that our academic-integrity appeal hearings have a credibility problem. The specific charge is that Black and Latino students, when they offer the same kinds of explanations that white students offer — computer crashed, uploaded the wrong file, roomate had a medical emergency — are simply believed less often, and that the right diagnosis is testimonial injustice: a speaker getting a credibility deficit because of identity prejudice, not because their story is actually weaker. The students pushing the complaint have read some philosophy, so the phrase gets thrown around a lot, but we can't just take the diagnosis on faith.

The provost says she'll fund an audit or re-training, but first she wants to know whether that's really what's happening on our campus, as opposed to some other kind of unfairness or just ordinary variation in how plausible different students sound. So here's what I'd like from you: take on the role of an epistemologist who also designs empirical studies, and think carefully about the question. Start by brainstorming two or three ways we could operationalize "being believed less" from our hearing records or from a small controlled pilot, then pick one and defend the choice.

Then spell out exactly what pattern of results would count as evidence for the testimonial-injustice diagnosis, and what pattern would push you toward a different explanation (like some students presenting genuinely weaker cases, or a process problem that affects everyone equally). Finally, be explicit about what no test can settle here — for instance, whether any individual hearing officer was prejudiced, or whether a particular student's appeal was wrongly decided. Don't just gesture at "audit study"; give me enough detail that I could hand your answer to our data people and IRB.
