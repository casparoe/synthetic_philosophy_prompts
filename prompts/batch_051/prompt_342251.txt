I have been trying to work out what I think about the moral equality of combatants — the thesis that soldiers on both sides of a war have the same permission to kill one another. My judgments about a series of cases are as follows, and I would like to know whether any principled account, or a small set of candidate accounts, can capture them.

1. A soldier in a just defensive war shoots and kills a uniformed enemy soldier who is advancing on his position. I regard this as more than excused: it is justified.

2. A volunteer soldier in a war of conquest shoots and kills a defender under the very same circumstances, complying with every law of armed conflict — the defender is in uniform, is not surrendering, and so on. I regard what he does as wrong, and I blame him for it, even though he violates no rule of war.

3. A conscript in that same aggressive war, forced into service on threat of imprisonment and deliberately misled by his government about the cause, performs the identical act. I again think the act is wrong, but I do not blame him, or blame him only marginally.

4. A defending soldier kills several enemy soldiers who have surrendered. I think this is plainly wrong, and I do not think the justice of her cause lessens it.

5. An invading soldier kills several enemy soldiers who have surrendered — the same kind of act as in the previous case. I think this is wrong, but I also think it is worse than the defender's identical act. I cannot say why the same deed should be worse when done by someone fighting on the unjust side.

6. A civilian engineer in the aggressor state designs targeting software for its military but never deploys and never fires a weapon. I think she bears some responsibility for the war, clearly less than the volunteer in (2), but I cannot say how much, or on what basis.

My provisional principle had been that a combatant's permission to kill derives entirely from the justice of his side's cause, so that the two sides are not moral equals. But that principle seems too blunt for (3), (5), and (6), and I do not see how to repair it without surrendering an intuition I also have: that the soldier in (2), once he surrenders, must be treated as a prisoner of war and should not be punished merely for having fought. I am not sure whether that intuition conflicts with (2), or merely shows that wrongdoing and punishability can come apart.

There is also a further case about which my judgment remains unstable: a soldier on the unjust side who sincerely but unreasonably believes his war is just — for instance, because he has ignored overwhelming evidence. Where does he fall between the coerced conscript in (3) and the knowing volunteer in (2)?

Please state the principle, or small set of candidate principles, that best captures cases (1)–(6), precisely enough to be tested against new cases. Say which of my verdicts it can accommodate, which it forces me to abandon or revise, and what it predicts for the case I cannot settle.
