So a friend of mine has this argument about deepfakes that I can't shake. She says once deepfakes are indistinguishable from real recordings, the video camera stops being what she calls the epistemic backstop, and that this means something much scarier than just don't trust everything you see online. Her steps go something like this. (1) A piece of evidence gives you reason to believe a claim only if that claim makes the evidence more likely than its competitors do. (2) If a video of an event could just as easily be a perfect deepfake as the real thing, then the hypothesis that this video is genuine and the hypothesis that this video is a deepfake predict exactly the same footage. (3) Soon, for basically any video you didn't shoot yourself, it could just as easily be a perfect deepfake as a genuine recording. (4) So soon, videos you didn't shoot yourself will give you no reason to believe what they show. (5) But video has been our epistemic backstop: it's the thing we could fall back on when witnesses, reporters, and officials all disagreed or lied, because the recording could correct them. (6) So once (4) is true, there's no backstop left for anything you didn't personally witness, and justified belief about most of the world collapses. That's clearly too strong, but I can't find the bad step. Which premise or step should I give up, and what does giving it up cost? I most want to hang onto (5) and (6).
