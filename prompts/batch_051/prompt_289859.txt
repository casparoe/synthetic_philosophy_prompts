I'm a novelist — midway through a book about a poker player (or maybe a futures trader, still deciding) who slowly destroys himself on bets that are each, individually, 'correct.' I went down a wormhole on what I think people call ergodicity economics, or the time-average-versus-ensemble-average thing. My understanding of the basic idea: a bet can be positive in expectation if you average over a thousand people each taking it once (the ensemble average), but negative for one person taking it over and over, because returns multiply and a bad streak compounds. So a policy can be right for the group and ruinous for the person actually living through it.

Here's what I actually want. Give me your actual credence — a number, 0 to 1 — that for one individual making a genuinely multiplicative decision (a business, leverage, repeated gambling, maybe certain creative-career choices), the correct way to evaluate it is by its time-average growth rate, something like maximizing geometric mean or log growth, rather than by raw expected value, whenever the two disagree. And I want the number decomposed. Specifically: (a) what's the probability that expected-value maximization and time-average maximization are actually just the same thing in disguise once you pick the right utility function, so there's no real dispute; (b) what's the probability that 'rational' has any determinate meaning here at all, rather than splitting into rational-for-a-group vs rational-for-a-life-lived-once; (c) what's the probability that the time-average criterion is normatively right even when the stakes aren't monetary or repeated — like one-shot career or artistic gambles; (d) whatever other terms you think the credence splits into. For each, give a rough probabilty and a line. Then tell me how you combined them, and tell me which term the final number bends around most, and what evidence — real-world or thought experiment — would move that one the most.

For calibration: my friend, an economist, says this whole 'ergodicity' thing is just expected utility with a log-utility function, old news, and the right rule is plain expected utility maximization all the way down; he's at about 0.1 that the time-average framing wins. I'm at about 0.65 that there's something genuinely different going on. Use the decomposition to tell me where our disagrement most likely lives. Don't give me 'it depends.' Give me a number and show me the weak joints.
