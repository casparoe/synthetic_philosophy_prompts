I host a small philosophy podcast, and next week I'm interviewing a philosopher who defends a broadly Humean standard of taste: she holds that aesthetic judgments are sentiments, not reports of objective properties of artworks, but that some verdicts are still better than others because a work's merit consists in its tendency to draw the joint verdict of ideal critics — people with delicacy of taste, practice, wide comparison, freedom from prejudice, and good sense — and that this convergence is what the test of time gradually reveals. She's heard all the stock questions (isn't beauty subjective? don't tastes just differ?) and has her replies down cold; her eyes glaze over faster then mine. I need eight to ten interview questions that get under the view rather than having her restate it.

At least one question each on: the circularity worry — ideal critics seem to be picked out by the very verdicts the standard is meant to explain, so applying the standard looks like it begs the question; the normativity gap — even if ideal critics did always converge, their agreement by itself makes their verdicts predictable, not correct, and it's unclear the view has the resources to close that gap; whether the cross-generational consensus she appeals to can be distinguished from canon-lock, where the judgments of past taste-makers simply get reproduced; and the slack she grants to variation she treats as blameless (differences of age, country, and individual temperament) — does the standard have anything left to say about the genuinely hard cases? For each of these, include a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, not just a more pointed version of the same question. And end with one question about what would actually count as evidence against her view.
