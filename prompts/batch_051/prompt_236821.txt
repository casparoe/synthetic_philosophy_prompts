I'm doing a social policy PhD on workfare and conditionality, and for a seminar I'm going through a regional benefits tribunal's anonymised rulings with my supervisor to see whether there sanction appeals follow any coherent principle—or whether the decisions are just vibes with legal formatting. Four rulings keep getting cited in class. (1) They overturned a sanction for a lone parent who missed a work-focused interview because her regular childcare fell through the night before; she phoned the office an hour before the interview to explain and gave her sister's number. (2) They upheld a sanction for a claimant with diagnosed anxiety who missed two appointments, didn't get in touch either time, and only handed in a GP note after the sanction had already been issued. (3) They overturned a sanction for a woman who left her job after months of being paid below the legal minimum, where she had kept texts showing she'd complained. (4) They upheld a fraud overpayment decision for a man who did small cash-in-hand cleaning jobs while on benefits and never declared them because he assumed such irregular small amounts didn't count. And in an uncomplicated case—a fit young claimant with no dependents who skipped a mandatory training course repeatedly because he found it pointless—they upheld the sanction as well.

Now our seminar has to write a mock judgment on a new appeal, and we're split. The claimant is a woman who looks after her elderly mother full time but has no formal carer's status. She missed an appointment because her mother wandered off during the night and she spent the morning finding her; she called the tribunal office the following afternoon, not before the appointment. One camp says the closest analogy is the childcare case and the tribunal should overturn the sanction; the other says the closest analogy is the anxiety case—there's a good story, but she didn't notify anyone promptly and the board seems to care about evidence and process, not just bad luck. I want to know what the tribunal should decide if it's actually trying to be consistent with itself, which precedent is the nearest analogue, what principle it would be adopting under each of the two options, and what further case would cleanly separate those two principles. Bonus if you can say which of the five earlier rulings now looks like the outlier under whichever principle wins.
