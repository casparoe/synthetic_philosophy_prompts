I've been reading Origen's On First Principles, and one passage near the start of Book IV keeps coming back to me. Origen is explaining how to interpret Scripture, and he says the divine Word planted certain snags and impossibilities in the text—like a creation sequence that has days before the sun and moon are made, or a mountain tall enough to see every kingdom of the world. His idea is that these are signals: when the surface meaning breaks down or becomes impossible, the reader is meant to stop resting in the literal story and look for a spiritual sense. He doesn't say the literal sense is always wrong, just that Scripture sometimes deliberately forecloses the option of staying at that level.

I want to use that as the lens for analyzing the early-twentieth-century fundamentalist-modernist controversy, particularly the 1925 Scopes trial and the way literal interpretation became a marker of orthodoxy. If Origen is right that the text itself contains those snags, what exactly was the fundamentalist defense of literal reading doing—defending the text, or overriding the text's own instruction about how to read it? Also, is Origen's idea of the literal sense even the same thing as what fundamentalists meant by literal interpretation? I suspect they often weren't talking about the same level of reading, but I want that pressed carefully rather than just asserted.

Then turn it the other way: what does the Scopes episode and the rise of inerrancy show about Origen's model? Does the fundamentalist worry—that allegorical reading is just a license for the interpreter to make the text mean whatever he wants—count as a genuine limit on Origen, or is it a self-fulfilling problem that only emerges once you've already rejected his picture of spiritual sense?
