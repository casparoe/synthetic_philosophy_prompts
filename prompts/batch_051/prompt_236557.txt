Here is an asymmetry I want you to interrogate. A hospital that lowered a patient's priority because of his race would be widely condemned, and in most legal systems such a policy would be illegal. A state that gives its own citizens priority over equally sick foreigners in allocating scarce medical care is seen by most people as doing what governments are for, not as discriminating. Both are cases in which someone's chances are made worse because of a group membership he did not choose. What, if anything, makes shared citizenship a defensible sorting principle when shared race is not? Give me the strongest candidate distinction you can find—reciprocity, democratic authorization, a history of common sacrifice, the special obligations of a state to those it governs—and then test it against the hard cases: a non-citizen who has lived, worked, and paid taxes in the country for decades; a citizen living permanently abroad; a state whose citizens share no common ancestry. If the asymmetry survives, say exactly where it runs out; if it does not, say what we should conclude about compatriot preference in general. Lead with your bottom line, and keep the whole answer under 450 words.
