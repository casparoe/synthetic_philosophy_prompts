You are a professional philosopher working in social ontology and the epistemology of stereotypes. I want you to work through this problem slowly and explicitly: before settling on anything, brainstorm the obvious candidate definitions of the key term, note where each one breaks down, and then build your final proposal from that.

The task is to produce a working definition of a contested notion and test it against hard cases. The notion is a national-character generalisation: a generalisation about a people's or a nation's character. The deeper question behind the exercise is whether national character is a coherent notion, and whether it is ever acceptable to generalise about peoples. I want the definition to force an answer to that deeper question rather than assume one.

Here are eight cases. Do not treat my tentative verdicts as authoritative; override them wherever your definition demands it. A bare "any generalisation about a group" definition would be too broad, and a definition requiring a mystical group essence would be too narrow; your definition should explain why the paradigm cases land where they do.

1. A diplomat says of a country in which she has served: "They are a proud and ancient people; you cannot negotiate with them unless you understand their pride." I regard this as a clear instance.

2. A large cross-national study reports that respondents in country A score, on average, half a standard deviation higher on the personality trait Conscientiousness than respondents in country B, using representative samples. I am genuinely unsure whether this counts as a claim about national character.

3. A guidebook warns tourists: "In this country queueing is taken very seriously; do not push in." I suspect this is a claim about norms rather than character, but I cannot say why the distinction should matter.

4. A scholar claims that there is a distinctive national character — a set of deep, enduring dispositions shared by members of the nation and transmitted across generations — and that this character explains the country's political institutions. I regard this as the paradigm case.

5. A politician says that people from a particular ethnic background are "naturally dishonest." This also seems to me a clear instance, though of course an objectionable one.

6. A reputable survey reports that the proportion of people who say they trust most other people is more than twice as high in one European country as in another. I am unsure whether simply reporting this is to generalise about national character.

7. A historian writes that a country's nineteenth-century commercial success owed something to a widely shared commercial character, inculcated by religion, law, and everyday practice, which made long-distance trade possible. I suspect this is an instance, but I cannot tell whether it is more respectable than case 5.

8. A person from a small nation, speaking of her compatriots, says: "We are a hospitable people; a guest is never turned away." I am inclined to count this as an instance, but it feels different from case 5, and I cannot articulate why.

(b) Run the definition or definitions through all eight cases, giving a clear verdict for each. (c) If you offered two definitions, say which verdicts change when you move between them. (d) Tell me where the resulting definition surprises you or where you think it gets a case wrong, and say whether you would revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict. (e) In a brief closing paragraph, state what the completed exercise implies about whether national character is coherent and when, if ever, generalising about peoples is acceptable.
