I'm a retired biologist (fish physiology, mostly) and I've been reading philosophy of biology the last couple years. There's a pattern I keep noticing that I don't have a clean word for. In my old lab, nobody hesitated to say the gill's function is gas exchange and ion regulation — meaning roughly what it does now that keeps the fish alive. But when people argue about whether a stretch of junk DNA is "functional," they slide into a different standard: it has to have been maintained by selection for that effect, not just happen to do something. A colleague called a piece of noncoding RNA that happens to bind a toxin "functional," another said no, that's just a side effect until selection has done something with it. Same thing comes up with a lab strain that has a mutation making it heat-tolerant: everyone sees that the mutation does something, but people split on whether heat tolerance is one of its functions, since the strain has never lived in the heat. The closest word I know is exaptation, where an old part gets co-opted for a new job, but that presuposes the part already had a function; I'm talking about effects that were never selected at all. What is this split called? Are there two rival views of "function" here, and if so what names do they go by? I don't just want the label — I want to know where the line actually falls, what a clean test case would be, and whether one side has to give up something or whether "function" just needs to be split into two concepts.
