I'm teaching a two-hour seminar for third-year undergrads in a course called History and Philosophy of Mathematics, and I've been asked to focus on one specific danger: anachronism when reading early modern mathematics. I want to build the entire session around a single passage from Galileo's Dialogues Concerning Two New Sciences (First Day). Here's the passage I'll be using:

Simplicio: Here a difficulty presents itself which appears to me insoluble. Since it is clear that we may have one line segment longer than another, each containing an infinite number of points, we are forced to admit that, within one and the same class, we may have something greater than infinity, because the infinity of points in the long line segment is greater than the infinity of points in the short line segment. This assigning to an infinite quantity a value greater than infinity is quite beyond my comprehension.

When I've taught this before, the modern students pounce immediately: they say Galileo is stumbling toward Cantor's result that a set can have the same cardinality as a proper subset, and they treat him as someone who saw one-to-one correspondence but couldn't stomach the conclusion. I don't want to dismiss that comparison, but I want them to see why it's a form of reading later mathematics into an earlier text, and why Galileo's specific problem about whether "equal," "greater" and "less" apply to infinite quantities is a different question from cardinality. Could you plan the teaching sequence rather than explain the passage to me? In particular: what must be in place before the passage can be read as a seventeenth-century argument and not as failed set theory; which single example should carry the hour (the squares argument from the same dialogue, or something else, and why); what anachronistic readings to expect and at which point in the sequence to defuse each one; what I should openly refuse to claim at this level; and one question I can ask at the very end to tell whether they've actually gotten the distinction rather than just learned to avoid the word "Cantor." I'd also be grateful if the written plan could be in British English, since it's going to be circulated to a tutorial group at a UK university.
