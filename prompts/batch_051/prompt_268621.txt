I am a doctoral candidate in history, preparing a dissertation that examines conflicts between a community's collective memory and the historiographical record. As part of my prospectus, I have had to state the positions I find myself defending across the project. They each seem right to me in isolation, but I am no longer confident they hang together, and I would rather discover the fault lines now than have them exposed later. I would be grateful for an audit of the set.

The positions are these:

1. History and collective memory are different practices. History is answerable to the best available evidence and is corrigible; it may overturn what a community has long believed. Collective memory exists to sustain a group's identity and self-understanding and is answerable to that function rather than to the record.
2. Selectivity, anachronism, and even factual error in a community's memory are therefore not, as such, defects. To criticize a community's memory for failing to meet historiographical standards is usually a category mistake; memory should be appraised by whether it sustains the community that carries it.
3. Nevertheless, when memory and the record conflict in public institutions—state schools, national museums, official commemorations, public broadcasting—the best available historical record should prevail. A public institution should not teach or present as fact what the record contradicts.
4. But when a community tells its story in its own spaces—its ceremonies, family histories, and communal rituals—neither the state nor the historical profession has authority to correct it. To intervene there in order to set the record straight is presumptuous and paternalistic, regardless of the inaccuracies involved.
5. Public recognition of a group's narrative can be warranted even where that narrative departs, in places, from the best available record. A state apology, a day of remembrance, or the display of a group's own account in a museum may be justified, because what is being acknowledged is standing, harm, and equal membership—not the literal truth of every element of the narrative.
6. There are limits, however. A community's memory-work should not be permitted to deny, minimize, or rehabilitate documented atrocities. The internal function of memory does not insulate such denial from correction, whether it occurs in a public institution or in the community's own ceremonies.

I would like you to go through these six commitments and tell me which pairs are in genuine tension and which only appear to be, explaining briefly why in each case. For the genuine tensions, rank them by severity rather than treating them all as equal, say what each side of the tension would cost to preserve, and identify the least costly revision in each case. Finally, tell me which single revision would do the most to make the set cohere. I have been too close to the material to see where the problems are, so I would prefer an audit of the structure of the commitments rather than an exegesis of them.
