I'm a philosophy professor working on collective personhood, and I keep setting my students the bit in Hobbes's Leviathan, Chapter XVI, where he says that a multitude becomes one person only when one man or one person represents them, with the consent of each, and that it is the unity of the representer, not the unity of the represented, that makes the person one. But my own verdicts on cases won't cohere with that, and I want a principle that does better.

Here's my gut. A chartered trading company whose governor signs a treaty: one corporate person, and the stockholders are bound by it, even if they complain. A village commons association that manages a grazing common by majority vote at a public meeting, with no single representative in sight: still one corporate person. A crowd of strangers outside a courthouse all shouting that the prisoner should be hanged: not a person, even though they're all doing the same thing at the same time. A trade union whose officers sign a contract the members then repudiate: the union is bound, but only if the officers were genuinely authorised. A state with a written constitution that splits power across three branches: legally one person, but I hesitate to say its unity comes from a single representer. A DAO running on smart contracts and token votes: I find myself denying it's a person, even though there is a mechanism that aggregates preferences.

You're an expert on early modern political philosophy and contemporary group agency. Please think carefully, start by brainstorming three or four candidate principles, then say which one best captures my verdicts, which of my verdicts it forces me to drop or revise, and what it predicts about the Hobbes passage. Also say what the best principle implies for a case I haven't listed: a university senate that delegates its degree-granting power to a committee, and the committee then acts beyond its remit. Please write your answer in British English.
