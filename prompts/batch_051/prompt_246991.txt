A colleague has been arguing that a country's choice to extract and burn its remaining cheap fossil fuels is, in fact, morally unproblematic. I want to know whether his argument goes through, or where exactly it breaks.

The choice he is considering is between two strategies. Under Burn, a country uses cheap carbon-intensive fuel, enjoys a near-term economic boom, and leaves the people who will live a century later a hotter, poorer, and less fertile environment. Under Shift, the country pays higher costs now to build clean infrastructure, and the people a century later will be materially better off. The two strategies would, however, alter employment, settlement, and family formation so profoundly that no particular person who comes to exist under Burn would have existed had Shift been chosen; the future populations are disjoint. Assume, too, that the effects on people currently alive are small and diffuse enough that neither strategy makes any currently existing person worse off; and that the people who will live under Burn will have lives that are difficult but still clearly worth living.

His argument runs as follows:

(1) An action is morally wrong only if it harms at least one person.
(2) To harm a person is to make that person worse off than that person would otherwise have been.
(3) For any person who exists under Burn and lives in the degraded environment, had Shift been chosen instead, that very person would never have existed.
(4) Since that person's life under Burn is worth living, existing in that degraded condition is not worse for that person than never existing at all.
(5) Therefore, Burn makes no future person worse off than that person would otherwise have been.
(6) Therefore, Burn harms no future person.
(7) Burn also harms no currently existing person, by our stipulation.
(8) Therefore, Burn harms no person at all.
(9) Therefore, Burn is not morally wrong.

I find (9) hard to accept, but the path from (5) through (8) to (9) looks valid, and the premises seem individually plausible. Please work through the argument step by step. Is it deductively valid? If it is not valid, identify the exact step where the inference fails, and say what the premises would have to mean for that step to fail. If it is valid, identify which premise or premises should be rejected, state the strongest case against each, and then say which single step is the best place to stop the argument. In doing so, please be explicit about what notion of harm and what notion of wrong each step needs for the argument to work, since I suspect the argument quietly shifts between senses of one of those terms.
