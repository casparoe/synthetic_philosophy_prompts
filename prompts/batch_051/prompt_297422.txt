I teach a philosophy of war seminar, 18 undergrads, one 80-minute slot. I want an activity rather than a lecture. The idea I want them to smack into is the moral equality of combatants: roughly, that a regular soldier on the unjust side and one on the just side are morally on a par in what they may do, as long as both follow the laws of war (no targeting civilians, no executing prisoners, that sort of thing). Most of my students start with the opposite intuition: if the war is unjust, the soldier fighting it is just a murderer in uniform.

Design me a runnable classroom game, role-play, or forced-choice exercise where that intuition gets put under real pressure before I name it. It has to work in a bare seminar room, no tech. I'm open to a fictional war, a tribunal, a blame-line ranking — whatever, but the structure has to make them run into the problem themselves: if they condemn the unjust-side soldier for an act, they should immediately have to face the identical act done by a just-side soldier or a civilian on the unjust side, and either produce the principle that seperates them or give up one of the judgments. Include the rules or roles, a timeline with phases, minimal materials, and a debrief that maps the moments of the exercise onto the machinery people use to defend moral equality (duress, ignorance of the cause, role obligations, liability to attack, the POW convention). Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be upfront about which feature of the view the activity can't actually create and I'll have to just assert. I want them to leave less sure that the unjust-side soldier is a murderer in uniform, and aware of what it costs to either keep or drop that judgment. Don't name the idea until after they've tripped over it.
