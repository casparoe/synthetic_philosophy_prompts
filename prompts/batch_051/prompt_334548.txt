Someone I know summarized supererogation in a way that's been rattling around my head, and I can't tell which parts are load-bearing. Their gloss went something like this: a supererogatory act is one that's morally good to do but not wrong to omit — heroism, huge self-sacrifice, donating a kidney to a stranger. The philosophical punchline, they said, is that this category is only stable if you think duty is a floor you can go above. In any view where the right act is just whatever produces the most good, every opportunity to produce more good becomes an obligation, so nothing can be genuinely optional or 'above and beyond.' And conversely, if you admit some good acts are optional, you've given up on having a complete set of duties that decides everything. So utilitarians have to deny supererogation outright, and what looks like going beyond duty is really just them re-labeling the best available act as required. They closed by saying that's why the concept lives naturally in deontological, Kant-flavored territory, where duty is strict and bounded, and why consequentialist-leaning people talk about it as a kind of useful fiction for praising heroes without condemning everyone else.

I'm inclined to accept the anti-utilitarian part, since I've seen consequentialists shrug off demandingness objections in comment-section arguments, but the claimed link between supererogation and a 'duty floor' feels suspiciously tidy. I suspect they're conflating 'not required' with 'no longer evaluable' or sneaking blame into the definition of obligation. I'd like an accuracy audit of this gloss: seperate what's right, what's a common simplification, and what a consequentialist or a defender of supererogation would object to. Also, if you fix the distortions, does the conclusion that utilitarians must deny the category survive? I don't need citations or reading suggestions—I'm trying to use this in a back-and-forth about demandingness and I want to not embarrass myself.
