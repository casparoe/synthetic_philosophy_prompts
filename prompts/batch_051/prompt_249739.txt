Last night a friend of mine who's a nurse and I got into a long argument about a consent case at her hospital, and it ended with her telling me my whole approach was "just vibes," which stung because it's a little true. I do the first-pass ethics review for a university hospital: I screen research consent forms and I get called in when there's a disagreement about whether a patient or study participant actually consented to something. I'm not a lawyer and I'm not a philosopher. I see five to ten of these cases a week, I get maybe 20 minutes per case, and the actual board just receives a one-paragraph reccomendation from me that they mostly rubber-stamp. The stakes are real: people end up enrolled in trials they didn't really agree to, or they get left out of something that would help them.

Write me a reusable procedure for deciding whether consent is valid. Don't write me an essay about the history of consent; I need the actual steps. Organize it around the three things that actually do the work when consent fails — deception, coercion, competence — but turn each one into concrete tests I can actually run: what to ask, what to look for in the room, and what counts as a hard stop versus a problem we can fix by re-consenting, giving someone more time, or having a neutral third party present. Put the steps in the order I should run them, and mark the points where the procedure is really just me making a judgment call so I don't pretend a checklist made the decision for me.

Then apply the procedure to these three cases so I can see it work:

1. A psychology study tells volunteers in advance that it's about memory and attention, and the consent form accurately describes every task and risk. In the debriefing afterward they learn the study used a cover story: the real question was about how people assign blame after a workplace accident. Nobody objects to the tasks themselves, only to having been lied to about the point.

2. A healthy 30-year-old who is between jobs and nearly out of money is offered $1,600 to enroll in a phase I drug trial. The consent form is accurate: the drug has no expected benefit for him, and he'll probably have several days of fever, nausea, and blood draws. He signs within two days. Is his consent valid, or has the payment made it coerced?

3. A 72-year-old woman with mild cognitive impairment is enrolled in a long-running observational study that her late husband originally signed her up for. She can't accurately paraphrase the risks, but she says she wants to continue because her husband thought it mattered and she would feel guilty quitting. The staff say she seems content and not distressed. Is her consent valid, and if not, what should happen next?

Make sure at least one of the three comes out in a way my gut would not like, and say explicitly what the procedure cannot do — the cases where no checklist saves me from having to decide.
