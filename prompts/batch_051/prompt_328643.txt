Last week at our philosophy of science reading group, a colleague ended a dispute about whether categories like weed and invasive species are real with: the sciences carve nature at its joints. Their argument was that physics and chemistry cut at the real joints, while ecology just sorts organisms by human interests, so a category like weed is a convenience rather than a kind. I'm sure that sentence is doing real work there, and I'm equally sure I can't say exactly what work, because it could be making several quite different claims. Please lay out the distinct precise claims that 'the sciences carve nature at its joints' might express — from readings that amount to little more than 'some classifications are more useful for prediction than others' to readings that assert a single mind-independent order of kinds that mature science progressively reveals. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or actually false. Then say which of those readings the colleague's dismissal of weed and invasive species would actually require, and whether the slogan, read that way, really delivers the dismissal. I want the readings as a menu with verdicts, not a single explanation of one preferred interpretation.
