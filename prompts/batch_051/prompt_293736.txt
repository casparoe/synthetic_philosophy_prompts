So I'm a journalist who covers philosophy and ideas, and I keep hearing moral philosophers use the phrase 'one thought too many' as if it settles something, but I genuinely can't tell what precisely it's suppose to show. The standard move is to bring up a case where a man's wife is drowning and he saves her, but if he also has to think that morality permits or requires saving her, there's something wrong not just with the action but with him. Then the philosopher says the extra moral thought is one thought too many and uses that to push back on impartial moral theories, especially utilitarianism and Kantianism. I want to know what actual claims that phrase could be making. Is it a psychological claim about what a loving person's deliberation actually looks like? A normative claim that you shouldn't need to think about permissibility in such cases? A claim about the content of the justification, as if citing a principle somehow changes the character of the rescue? Or something else entirely? Could you lay out the distinct readings, from the weak to the radical, and for each say whether it's true, whether it's just a platitude, and whether it actually licenses dismissing someone who after the fact offers a moral justification for saving his wife? And if someone replied that the man can save her and later reflect that he did the right thing, which reading would they be appealing to, and does that version survive the criticism?
