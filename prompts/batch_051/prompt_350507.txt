I'm a retired chemist who's recently gotten into philosophy, and I've been working through Locke's Essay Concerning Human Understanding. In Book II, Chapter xxvii, there's the passage near the start where he distinguishes an oak from a mass of matter: the mass is just those particular particles, while the oak is the same particles but organized into one living body, so it can shed and replace some bits and remain the same oak even though the mass changes. That passage has been sitting in my head alongside the statue-and-clay problem.

I've been calling something 'process closure' and I want a candid assessment of it. Here's the idea. A particular thing is an individual when its coming into existence, its persisting, and its going out of existence are all governed by the same sort of lawlike regime. A mere lump of clay has its boundaries and changes set from outside, by whatever force happens to act on it; its persistence is just the persistence of the matter. An oak has its changes organized from within, and its beginning, continuing, and ceasing form one unified process. A statue sits somewhere odd: its matter behaves like a lump, but the object's identity conditions seem tied to the sculptor's plan plus the material's stability. So the same bit of matter can be involved in two process closures at once, and that's why the statue and the lump are one thing in one respect and two in another.

My question: is this a genuine distinction or just Aristotle's formal cause wearing a lab coat? I know there's a modern statue-and-clay literature, incluing Wiggins's constitution-not-identity idea, but I can't tell whether my process closure adds anything or is just restating it. Give me the strongest version you can, name the nearest existing ideas, and then dont hold back: where does process closure break? In particular, what does it get wrong about persistence across change, and what kind of case would make it give the wrong answer?
