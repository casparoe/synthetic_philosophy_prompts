Next week we're reading Fearon on rationalist explanations for war plus a recent piece applying the same machinery to AI races. The basic claim is that an AI race is like war in the relevant respect: all parties would prefer, ex ante, a coordinated and safety-conscious build-out, but you can still end up with a full sprint that nobody wanted. I want to spend the session on a game rather than a lecture.

Could you design a classroom activity for about 16–20 people, one hour total including the debrief, that makes participants feel the force of Fearon's commitment problem as applied to an AI race? I'm happy to leave the private-information mechanism and indivisibility to one side — I want the game to isolate the commitment problem: even with full transparency about everyone's capabilities and intentions, a cooperative slowdown can unravel because each player's promise to stay slow is not credible once the others are slowing down, or because whoever pulls ahead cannot credibly commit not to exploit the lead. I'd like rules, materials (index cards and a whiteboard are fine), a payoff scheme that doesnt need real money, and a debrief that maps what happened back onto Fearon's categories — and onto ideas like time-inconsistent preferences, preventive-war logic, cheap talk, and the difference between a treaty and a payoff structure. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and which feature of the real AI-race situation the game cant reproduce and I'll have to supply myself in discussion.
