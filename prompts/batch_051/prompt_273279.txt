I am a retired chemist, and philosophy is a late-life project; I have been working through constructive mathematics and trying to pin down exactly why the classical law of excluded middle is said to fail. I built the following argument to test whether the constructivist restriction can really be as substantial as advertised, and the result now seems to prove far too much, though I cannot locate the step at which it goes wrong.

Take the predicate G(n): the decimal expansion of π contains a block of one hundred consecutive 7s beginning at the nth digit. For any fixed n this is decidable, since a program can compute the first n+100 digits of π and inspect them. Thus (1) for every natural number n, either G(n) or ¬G(n). Now imagine running that check for n = 0, 1, 2, ... in order. Each finite check returns a definite answer, so (2) the results constitute a well-defined infinite binary sequence, with 1 for "yes" and 0 for "no." (3) Any well-defined infinite binary sequence either contains a 1 at some position or consists entirely of 0s; these two cases are exhaustive. (4) If it contains a 1 at some position, then ∃nG(n). (5) If it consists entirely of 0s, then ∀n¬G(n). Hence (6) ∃nG(n) or ∀n¬G(n). And since the construction works for any decidable predicate of the naturals, it appears to establish that for every decidable predicate the unbounded existential is already settled one way or the other. But that is precisely the sort of disjunction constructivists say one may not assert without either a witness to the first disjunct or a proof of the second, and the argument supplies neither.

Where does this go wrong? My inclination is to keep (1) and (2), since they only record ordinary facts about algorithms; that seems to make (3) the suspect step. Yet (3) sounds like a mere truism about sequences—either a 1 occurs or none does. I would like an honest diagnosis: which step should be rejected, why did that step look so innocent, and what exactly does a constructivist give up in rejecting it? If (3) is somehow innocent after all, tell me which other step carries the cost, and what that cost amounts to.
