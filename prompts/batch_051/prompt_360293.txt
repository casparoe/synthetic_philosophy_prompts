For a semester-long independent study on how medical systems decide whom to treat when resources are scarce, I am analyzing the anonymized decision log of a hospital's scarce-resource committee during a flu season in which ventilators were briefly in short supply. The committee allocated one ventilator between two patients at a time and never wrote down an official allocation rule; the log records only the patients' characteristics and the decision. All patients were adults, and the probabilities are the committee's own estimates made on the day of decision. In class we have discussed quality-adjusted life years and other allocation rules, but the committee never states which, if any, it was following.

Here are four decisions from the log:

1. Two 40-year-old patients, both otherwise healthy and each expected to live about 40 more years if treated successfully. One had an 80% chance of surviving with ventilation and the other 50%. The committee gave the ventilator to the patient with the 80% chance.

2. A 30-year-old and a 65-year-old, both otherwise healthy and each with a 70% chance of surviving with ventilation. If treated successfully, the 30-year-old could expect about 50 more years and the 65-year-old about 18. The committee gave the ventilator to the 30-year-old.

3. Two 65-year-olds, each with a 70% chance of surviving with ventilation, but one could expect about 15 more years if treated and the other about 3 because of an underlying disease. The committee gave the ventilator to the patient with the 15-year outlook.

4. Two 35-year-olds, identical in every recorded respect—75% chance of survival and 45 expected remaining life-years if treated. The committee used a random lottery.

The committee now faces a new case. A 25-year-old has a 55% chance of surviving with ventilation and, if she survives, about 58 expected remaining life-years. A 55-year-old has a 70% chance and, if he survives, about 24 expected remaining life-years. Both are otherwise healthy, they arrive at the same time, and the ventilator cannot be split, moved, or shared.

If the committee wants the decision that is most consistent with cases 1–4, what should it do? I can see how the earlier cases could support more than one principle, and those principles point in opposite directions in the new case. Please identify the alternative principles that fit the earlier decisions, say clearly which patient each principle would give the ventilator to in the new case and what rule the committee would be committing itself to by choosing that patient, identify which earlier case is the closest precedent, and then commit to a decision: which principle is best supported, and what should the committee do? End by describing the single most useful further case for telling the leading principles apart. I want a definite conclusion with reasons, not just a survey of options.
