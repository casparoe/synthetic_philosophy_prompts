Consider the following proposed replacement for university tuition fees and student loans.

Under the scheme, public universities may not charge domestic undergraduates or taught postgraduates; they are instead paid a per-place sum set by a subject cost band, plus a supplement based on the median earnings of the institution's graduates five years after leaving. Every graduate then pays a levy of six per cent on earned income above a threshold indexed to median full-time earnings, for twenty-six years from the first April after graduation. The levy is flat rather than progressive, is owed regardless of subject, institution, or age at graduation, and continues if the graduate emigrates: the tax applies to worldwide income, and consular and passport services can be refused to those who do not file returns. From year eleven, a graduate may buy out the remaining obligation with an actuarially fair lump sum based on their previous three years' income. The levy base includes salaries, bonuses, rental profits, and dividends, but excludes capital gains and inheritances; it is collected through payroll for employees and through self-assessment for everyone else.

Treat the scheme as machinery, not as a moral claim about free education. What does it incentivise for students choosing courses and careers, for universities deciding what to teach and how hard to mark, for employers negotiating pay, and for high earners planning their affairs? How would each group game it, where do the second-order effects appear, which groups would end up paying more or less than intended, and which failure modes are curable by adjusting parameters rather than built into the design?

Where your analysis leans on evidence from existing fee, loan, and graduate-tax arrangements, separate what is reasonably settled from what is genuinely contested among people who study this, and do not present a contested view as if it were the consensus.
