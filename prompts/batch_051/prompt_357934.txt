I keep running into this argument that expected value is the wrong test for risky choices, and I cant locate the exact step that bothers me. It runs:

(1) If you repeatedly stake your whole wealth on a multiplicative gamble, your wealth grows at the time-average growth rate, which for a multiplicative gamble is set by the expected log return, not the expected arithmetic return.
(2) Take a gamble that, with 50/50 odds, either multiplies your wealth by 1.8 or by 0.5. Its expected arithmetic return is +15%, but its expected log return is about −5.3% per round.
(3) So someone who keeps taking this gamble and reinvesting everything will see their wealth collapse toward zero almost surely in the long run, even though the ensemble average of wealth over all possible histories grows without bound.
(4) The same point applies to a single play: the relevant question is what the decision does to the time-average growth of your wealth, not what happens on average across parallel universes.
(5) Therefore a rational person should reject this gamble even as a one-off, and expected-value reasoning gives the wrong verdict for one-off gambles.

(1) through (3) look watertight to me, and the conclusion feels too strong, so I suspect (4) is smuggling something in. Is the argument valid? If it isnt, say exactly which step fails and give the strongest repair that stops short of just assuming the conclusion.
