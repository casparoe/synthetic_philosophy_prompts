I am trying to figure out what account of causation, if any, my own case judgments commit me to. Below are six cases with my verdicts. I want you to extract the principle or account that best captures the pattern, say where it fits the cases and where it strains, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, or whether the principle needs an extra clause to keep all six. Then apply the account to a seventh case on which I have not made up my mind.

1. Two people each throw a rock at a bottle. The first rock shatters the bottle; the second would have shattered it an instant later had the first not been thrown. I judge that the first throw caused the shattering and the second did not.

2. Two marksmen fire at the same moment; either bullet alone would have been enough to kill; both strike the target at once. I judge that each shot caused the death.

3. A trained lifeguard on duty watches a swimmer drown and does nothing. I judge that the lifeguard's failure to act caused the drowning.

4. A tourist on the same beach also watches and does nothing, although a life ring is within the tourist's reach and, had the tourist thrown it, the swimmer would have been saved. I judge that the tourist's failure to act did not cause the drowning.

5. A passerby kicks a ball, deflecting a rock that was about to shatter a window; the window remains intact. I judge that the kick caused the window's remaining intact.

6. A doctor gives a standard dose of a drug that proves fatal only because of an undiagnosed, extremely rare allergy. I judge that the injection caused the patient's death; I also judge that the doctor was not at fault.

The seventh, unsettled case is this. A tree falls and comes to rest in the path of a boulder that would otherwise have rolled on and destroyed a house; the boulder is stopped and the house is undamaged. Does the tree's falling cause the house's remaining undamaged? I do not have a settled verdict, and I want the account to predict one.

The pattern that troubles me: in cases 1 and 2 the things I call causes are not necessary for the outcome, because a backup would have produced it, yet I treat the sniper case and the rock-throwing case differently. In cases 3 and 4 an absence either is or is not a cause, apparently tracking the person's role more than the bare counterfactual facts. In case 5 a positive event causes a mere absence. In case 6 causation and fault come apart.

My question is whether a single account of causation captures all of this. The three accounts I keep returning to are: that causation is a real relation in the world; that it reduces to something like counterfactual dependence or difference-making; and that it is partly a projection of our interests, roles, and norms, so that the concept might ultimately be replaced by a cleaner vocabulary. I do not want a survey of these positions; I want the position that best fits my verdicts, stated precisely enough to generate predictions for new cases.

Finally, I want the response to be self-critical. Tell me which single consideration or counterexample would most likely lead you to a different account, and where in the reasoning the greatest risk of failure lies — for instance, a part of the pattern that is pulling toward one account while the other verdicts cannot bear the weight.
