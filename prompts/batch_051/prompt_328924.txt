I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and last weekend a designer friend wouldn't stop going on about Hofstadter's 'Person Paper' — the old satire where the stock defenses of sexist language get restaged with race in place of sex, so the moves people use to keep saying 'chairman' and generic 'he' end up looking like moves nobody would tolerate. Her takeaway was stronger than 'it's funny': she thinks the piece shows that any argument for keeping the male-default setup in English has a structurally identical racist twin, and since all decent people reject the racist twin, the sexist defaults are rationally indefensible. I'm not sure satire works that way. It transplants the form of an argument into another domain and leans on a gut reaction, but a disanalogy between the two domains can break the infererence even when the structure matches. My own suspicion is that the real target is default assumptions — the unmarked human being coded male — and that's where the analogy either lives or dies. I want to work through this properly. Treat the Person Paper as an analogy between sex-based and race-based ways of marking and defaulting people in language, and audit it. Give me the correspondence table: what maps onto what — the generic term, the marked term, honorifics, insults, the 'it's just grammar' move, the 'tradition' move, and so on. Then say where the mapping breaks or overreaches, and which disanalogies actually matter for the conclusion she wants. Finally: does her strong conclusion survive — that no defense of these defaults can be principled — or is the satire carrying more than it can bear? And if it half-survives, what's the strongest version of the claim the Person Paper actually earns you?
