You are a moral philosopher with expertise in Augustine’s ethics and in theories of culpability. Begin by working through the possible principles one might invoke, then give a final reasoned verdict.

Consider this passage from Augustine’s *De mendacio* (*On Lying*):

“Wherefore, that man lies, who has one thing in his mind and utters another in words, or by signs of whatever kind. … For from the sense of his own mind, not from the verity or falsity of the things themselves, is he to be judged to lie or not to lie.”

Now consider an asymmetry in ordinary moral judgment. A person who tells a lie that is believed and causes real damage—say, a false accusation that destroys someone’s reputation—is ordinarily blamed much more severely than a person who tries exactly the same lie but is immediately seen through, or who says something false while trying to deceive but accidentally asserts the truth. Yet Augustine’s test seems to locate the wrong of lying in the speaker’s will to deceive, not in whether the utterance is false or whether it succeeds in misleading anyone. On that view, the successful lie and the failed lie are equally lies, and perhaps equally culpable, because the inner act is the same.

Is the ordinary outcome-sensitive asymmetry between successful and unsuccessful lies something that can be given a principled justification, or is it a moral illusion that a consistent Augustinian about lying should reject? Give the strongest available defence of the asymmetry, test it against the case where the two lies are identical except that one is believed and the other is not, and then state whether you would keep, revise, or abandon the asymmetry.
