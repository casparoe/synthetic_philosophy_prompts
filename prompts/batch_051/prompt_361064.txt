I keep going back and forth on the 'user illusion' picture of consciousness—roughly, the idea that the self and what it seems to be aware of is a simplified interface the brain generates, the way a desktop shows you folders rather than voltages. The stock objection is that this is self-defeating: an illusion needs someone to be fooled, so you can't call consciousness an illusion without presupposing a conscious subject. I want to test an argument that says that objection is too quick.

(1) A user interface like a desktop is not a false appearance occurring inside the computer; it's an appearance produced for the human outside the machine.
(2) So the 'user' in 'user illusion' isn't a homunculus inside the system being deceived—the illusion is the outer user's simplified grip on what the machine is doing.
(3) Applied to consciousness, the 'outer user' is just the whole person or organism, not some additional inner observer.
(4) So a user-illusion theory of consciousness isn't committed to a hidden inner subject that consciously experiences the illusion.
(5) Therefore the standard objection—that an illusion requires a conscious experiencer and so illusionism about consciousness is self-defeating—attacks the analogy rather than the theory.

Is this valid? If not, which step fails, and what's the strongest form of the objection that survives after you fix the step? I can't shake the feeling that step (3) is smuggling something in, maybe the idea that the person can play the 'user' role from outside their own head, but I can't make the wrorry precise.
