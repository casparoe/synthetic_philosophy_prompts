Act as an expert in moral philosophy and decision theory who specializes in strong longtermism and in converting abstract normative commitments into decision procedures that trained non-philosophers can operate under time pressure. Think carefully, and begin by briefly laying out the version or versions of strong longtermism you will operationalize, including what the thesis does and does not imply about near-term, high-evidence interventions.

I am the director of a mid-sized private foundation making about forty major grants per year, with individual grants ranging from $100,000 to $5 million. We currently fund global health and education. A bloc on the board wants us to adopt strong longtermism in grant selection: the idea that, because the number of future lives could be extraordinarily large, the expected value of a grant is dominated by its effects on the long-run trajectory of civilization, so a grant can be a better use of funds than a proven near-term intervention even when its long-run effects are small in probability or impossible to quantify precisely. I need more than the slogan.

Give me a reusable decision procedure my program officers could run on any proposal in under two hours, with no formal decision theory and no calculation of expected value that requires precise probabilities or utilities where none are available. The procedure should be a flowchart in words: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure exits with a "no" or with "this needs board-level judgment," and which steps are genuine judgments dressed up as procedure. You may introduce distinctions from the philosophical literature, such as persistence, lock-in, option value, and cluelessness, but every such term must be translated into an operational question a program officer can actually answer. The procedure must also handle at least these traps: (1) every proposal can be made to sound like a longtermist bet by telling a story; (2) the fact that no single grant can be proven to change the long-run future can be used to dismiss all longtermist work; (3) a proposal that feels existentially important may simply be a Pascal's mugging. End the procedure with a step for recording what evidence would make us change our mind, and a short statement of what the procedure cannot settle.

After presenting the procedure, run it on three hypothetical proposals: (a) a childhood vaccination campaign in a low-income country with strong randomized evidence of near-term benefit but noisy, indirect evidence about long-run economic effects; (b) a small independent technical research group working on safety standards for advanced AI systems, where the long-run benefits are plausibly large but highly speculative and some critics argue the work could accelerate the relevant technology; and (c) an effort to build and maintain distributed knowledge archives for agricultural and ecological knowledge, intended to make civilization more resilient. Make sure at least one of the three is a case where the procedure returns an answer your staff will not like, and say plainly where the procedure's judgment comes from a value choice rather than a factual claim.
