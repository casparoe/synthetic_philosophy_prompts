At a reading group I attend, two members — Lena and Ravi — got into an argument after a session on AI alignment, and I'm still not sure who was right. Lena claimed that saying alignment is "solvable in principle" is close to empty. Her reason was that the target of alignment — what humans would actually endorse on reflection — is not a stable object: it shifts over time, and any finite set of demonstrations, labels, or conversations is consistent with indefinitely many possible reward functions. That, she argued, makes the problem underdetermined in a strict mathematical sense, not merely difficult. Ravi pushed back that underdetermination by finite data describes all of ordinary learning: children, scientists, and engineers all converge on competent models of a target from finite information because the target exists and constrains them over time. He said there is a fact of the matter about what a particular human would endorse under specified conditions, and "in principle" requires only that some process could track that fact; it does not require us to write the fact down in advance.

Lena replied that on this reading, too many things count as solvable in principle for the phrase to do any work — the phrase has to be read relative to the constraints that actually define the problem, such as finite information and fallible, manipulable overseers, or else the claim becomes trivial. Ravi answered that this move builds the difficulty into the definition: the point of distinguishing "in principle" from "in practice" is to separate structural impossibilities from ordinary obstacles, and if every obstacle gets absorbed into the problem's definition, then no problem is ever solvable in principle, which is equally empty. Lena said the alignment target differs from a fixed object like a planet whose properties are independent of the measuring instrument, because the human endorsement a system would track is itself shaped by the very systems attempting to align with it, so there is no observer-independent fact for a solution to converge on. Ravi replied that a sufficiently deferential system could in principle track a moving target without thereby fixing it, and that a supervisor who is sometimes moved by the system is still a dynamic process with real state, not an impossibility proof. Lena countered that a supervisor whose authority survives that influence is itself part of what alignment must deliver, so Ravi's "in principle" solution presupposes the very capacity whose possibility was at issue.

I didn't take a side, and I'd like help sorting it out. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: identify which claims are sound, which are overstated, and where the argument turns on different senses of "in principle." Then say who, if either, has the stronger overall position, and what a better version of the disagreement would need to settle first. Keep the response under 650 words and write in American English.
