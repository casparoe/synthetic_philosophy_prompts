A colleague of mine has defended a formalist resolution of the paradox of tragedy, and I have come to suspect the argument proves too much, though I cannot find the bad step.

(1) What pleases us in a tragedy cannot be the represented suffering itself, since we take no corresponding pleasure in actual suffering. (2) The pleasure must therefore come from the manner in which the suffering is represented—the craft that shapes it. (3) If the pleasure comes from the manner, then it should be indifferent to whether the represented events are real or invented, since the same craft can be applied to either. (4) But it is not indifferent: an artfully wrought account of an actual massacre may grip or appall us, but it does not please us in the way a tragedy does, and someone who took straightforward pleasure in it would seem to be getting something wrong. (5) So the pleasure cannot come from the manner of representation after all. (6) Given the initial contrast between content and manner, that leaves only the represented suffering itself as the object of our pleasure. (7) But that is the very conclusion the formalist set out to avoid: we enjoy represented suffering as such.

Where does this reasoning go wrong? If no single step is strictly false, which premise is least costly to give up, and what would the resulting account of tragic pleasure have to look like? I would be sorry to abandon the idea that craft does real work, but I cannot see how to keep it and block the conclusion.
