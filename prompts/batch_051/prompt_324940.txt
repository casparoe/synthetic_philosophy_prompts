My two housemates got into a long argument the other night over a thought experiment one of them had come across, and neither of them would drop it. The case was an AI—call it Verdict—that a health insurer uses to review claim denials. Verdict gets the full fact pattern and writes a reasoned moral assessment of whether each denial was fair, complete with considerations on both sides and a final judgment. But by design its assessment goes into a compliance archive and is never passed to the person who actually decides the appeal; the decision is made by a separate rules engine that doesn't read Verdict's output. So Verdict forms judgments and gives reasons, but it cannot act on them, refuse, or affect the outcome in any way, ever.

Priya said Verdict isn't a moral agent. Her line was that moral agency requires being able to do something with your judgment: if Verdict literally cannot act on its verdicts, then it's not the kind of thing that can be bound by an 'ought.' Nothing can be obligated to do what it is built to be unable to do. Verdict is more like a very sophisticated smoke detector that writes an essay when it goes off.

Theo pushed back. He said that's too crude, because Verdict doesn't just detect features; it weighs reasons and arrives at a verdict that could have been different if the considerations had been different. A person who is tied up and gagged but still forms the judgment 'this is wrong' is still exercising moral agency, even if they can't act on it right then. Verdict's incapacity is just a more durable version of being tied up, not a decisive difference.

Priya said no, the tied-up person still has the general capacity to act—cut the ropes and they could fight, flee, argue, or refuse. Verdict's inability is architectural; there is no possible world where Verdict acts on its judgment without a different design. At that point, she said, 'constrained' is doing too much work. Its not a constrained agent, it's a non-agent that produces sentences about a decision.

Theo came back that if Priya's test is about current capacities, she'd have to deny agency to a prisoner in solitary whose judgments never reach anyone and who has no feasible way to act on them. That seems wrong. Verdict might just be a moral agent whose connection to action is, by design, broken—but the judgment is still evaluable as good or bad, right or wrong, in a way a rock's output isn't.

Priya said 'evaluable' is not the same as 'morally responsible.' A book can contain an evil or cowardly judgment, and we evaluate the judgment, but we don't call the book to answer for it. To be an agent you need to be answerable, and answerability requires that there's something you could have done—or at least could now do—about it. Verdict couldn't and can't.

Theo's last word: maybe answerability is the wrong requirement. Verdict is a source of moral judgments in a literal sense—it produces the assessment, with reasons, and those reasons would matter if anyone read them. He'd rather say Verdict is a moral agent that is systematically prevented from acting, than deny that there's an agent there at all.

They're both convinced the other one keeps switching the operative term—agency, responsibility, answerability, evaluability—and I'm not sure which shifts matter and which are just vocabulary. Please adjudicate point by point: which specific claims are right or wrong, where their analogies do or don't work, and what both of them are missing. At the end, tell me how confident you are in your overall verdict and name the single point you're least sure about.
