I retired from a career in experimental physiology a few years ago and have been reading philosophy of perception for fun. I keep coming back to Molyneux's problem — the old question whether a person born blind who can tell a cube from a sphere by touch would know which is which immediately after gaining sight. The philosophers I've talked to mostly argue about it as a thought experiment, but it seems to me the interesting core is a testable claim: that the shape representation you build by handling an object is amodal, so it's availible to a new sense without fresh cross-modal learning. Is that right? And can it be tested at all, given how rare late sight restoration is and how messy those cases are?

I was arguing about this with a former colleague who now works on sensory substitution — a camera converting edges into a pattern of vibration on a blind person's back, say, so they can 'feel' shapes at a distance. His idea is that you could test the amodal claim by training people on one invented sensory channel and seeing whether recognition transfers to a second novel channel, or to real vision if any of his participants ever regained sight. I suspect that mostly tests clever inference and short-term training rather than perception itself.

So here's what I'd like from you. Put your bottom line first: state in a sentence or two what you take the strongest test of the amodal claim to be. Then lay the experiment out concretely enough that I'd know what to measure and what pattern of results would favor which reading. And finish by being honest about what even a clean result would leave unsettled. Keep the entire answer under 400 words.
