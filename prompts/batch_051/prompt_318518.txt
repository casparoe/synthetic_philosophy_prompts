I’m a psych PhD student and I’ve been sitting in on the stats-methodology arguments that happen after basically every replication seminar. The frequentist folks in the room say the replication crisis isn’t a failure of frequentism; it’s a failure of not following the rules: p-hacking, selective reporting, tiny samples, post hoc hypothesizing. The Bayesian folks say the fact that those rules can be gamed so easily is itself the problem, and we should report Bayes factors or posteriors instead. And then the frequentists say priors are subjective, so a Bayesian analysis can be just as p-hacked. And around we go.

After two years, I’ve noticed nobody changes their mind even when shown simulations or new replication results. I’ve had alot of trouble getting either side to say what would actually change their mind. My working theory is that the disagreement is only partly about math. It’s also about what a statistical method is supposed to guarantee: long-run error control across repetitions, or rational updating from the data you actually have. And maybe also about what 'evidence' means in science. I want a diagnosis, not a verdict: is this a substantive disagreement about which method would actually reduce false positives if adopted, a verbal dispute about the word 'evidence', a disagreement about what counts as a scientific warrant, or two groups answering different questions under one heading? Given your diagnosis, what would actual progress look like—a simulation, a formal result, a distinction, or is it just never going to move?
