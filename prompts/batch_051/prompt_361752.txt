I've been arguing with my mates about drug policy for about two years now, and it recently got personal: my cousin was caught with a small amount of cannabis he uses for chronic pain, and now he has a conviction that stops him working in his current job. Since then I've been reading around, and the argument seems to have settled into three camps that simply don't budge. The prohibition people keep citing addiction rates, family breakdown, and the idea that the state should never signal that drug use is acceptable. The decriminalisation people point to Portugal's reforms in 2001 and say the drop in overdoses, HIV transmission, and drug-related crime proves that treating use as a health issue rather than a criminal one works. The legalisation people say half-measures keep the black market, criminal networks, and unregulated product alive, and that only a taxed, controlled market like with alcohol or tobacco solves the supply side.

What confuses me is that all sides seem to accept the same empirical facts—or at least they accept that Portugal's overdose deaths fell, and that cannabis legalisation in some US states changed arrest rates but had mixed effects on youth use—yet they infer completly different policies. It reminds me of Mill's discussion of poisons in On Liberty. He treats poison as a legitimate article of commerce that can also be an instrument of crime, so he thinks society may impose precautions such as recording the buyer's identity, but may not simply prohibit it. If I've got him right, he's distinguishing between preventive measures attached to a risky instrument and outright prohibition on what people may put into their bodies. That seems relevant, but I can't decide wether it clarifies the debate or just adds another layer.

I don't want a verdict on drug policy. I want a diagnosis of why this dispute is so stuck: is it a disagreement about facts, about the burden of proof (must legalisers prove no harm, or must prohibitionists prove harm?), about whose risks count, or about whether the state is allowed to paternalistically override autonomy? Could someone actually change another person's mind with a certain kind of evidence, and if not, what would count as progress? Answer in British English.
