Construct a decision tree that sorts a respondent into one of six positions on the question whether artifacts have politics, for use as a handout in an introductory philosophy of technology class. Build the tree from the logical space of the debate rather than from the standard labels: first choose the diagnostic questions and lay out the answer combinations they allow, then locate the positions in that space, and explicitly mark any combination with no well-known occupant, diagnosing it in one line as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Every branch node must be a concrete, self-contained case or a plain-language question that a novice can answer without knowing the positions. A node may offer two or three options. Use the following six cases as the core nodes, replacing at most one if you judge another case sharper:

(1) a low-clearance highway overpass that buses cannot pass under, on a road to a public beach;
(2) a handgun left in a drawer versus a kitchen knife left in the same drawer;
(3) a nuclear power station versus rooftop solar panels, considered with respect to how much centralized, hierarchical management each can safely operate with;
(4) a video platform whose recommendation system optimizes for watch time rather than honoring user-chosen subscriptions;
(5) a wheelchair ramp built into a building's main entrance staircase rather than added at a side door;
(6) a residential speed bump versus a shared-space street with no signs or markings.

Each node should ask about the case in the respondent's own terms, never in terms of a theory, and never by asking which label or philosopher the respondent already identifies with.

The tree must include these leaves, first in plain language and then with the usual label in parentheses:

- a hammer is a neutral tool; all politics comes from people's choices about use (instrumentalism);
- artifacts carry politics only insofar as designers or users put it there, and it can be read off from those intentions (intentionalism);
- artifacts are never neutral because their built-in affordances make some actions easier and others harder, but any determinate political valence arises only in a context of use (affordance view);
- most technologies are politically flexible, but a special class is inherently political because their normal technical operation demands a particular kind of organization (inherent-politics view);
- every artifact, including mundane ones, is political because it crystallizes relations of power and social order (critical-materialist view);
- no individual artifact has politics on its own; politics attaches only to the wider socio-technical system of practices, institutions, and meanings around artifacts (socio-technical systems view).

Give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment. At the end, flag any two or more leaves that the tree cannot separate on these cases alone, and state what further question would separate them.
