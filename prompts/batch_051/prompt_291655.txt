Two of my friends got into an argument over a thought experiment our tutor gave us, and I honestly can't tell who has the better case. The setup: a superhuman predictor with a flawless track record flips a fair coin. If it lands heads, it pays you $10,000, but only if it predicted in advance that you would have handed over $100 had it landed tails. If it lands tails, it asks you for the $100. You're told truthfully that the coin landed tails. The tutor said its called counterfactual mugging, because the mugging only bites in the branch you never actually see? something like that.

(1) Maya: Paying is just wrong. The coin has already landed tails; the heads world never happened. Handing over $100 now doesn't reach back in time and buy you ten grand, so you're simply out $100.

(2) Jonas: But the only reason the ten grand appears in any heads world is that the predictor believes you would pay in the tails worlds. Refuse now, and you reveal the policy "don't pay after seeing tails"—so in every heads world you get passed over. Paying now makes you the kind of agent who banks the $10,000 whenever heads comes up.

(3) Maya: That's like paying a mugger on the theory that if you were the sort of person who pays muggers, nobody would bother mugging you. Not paying strictly dominates paying in the branch you're actually in. A counterfactual twin collecting money somewhere else doesn't make the $100 real here.

(4) Jonas: You're ignoring the precommitment point. Before the coin flip, you'd have accepted an arrangement where tails costs you $100 and heads gets you the $10,000—if you could bind yourself to pay on tails. You'd sign that without hesitating. After tails comes up, refusing is just defecting on an arrangement you would have chosen before the uncertainty resolved. You can't run that as a general policy.

(5) Maya: There's a difference between ex ante and ex post. Before the flip, paying had positive expected value because both branches were live. After the flip, the heads branch is dead; the only consequence of paying now is losing $100. Rationality is about steering the actual world, not keeping promises to imaginary twins in branches that didn't happen.

(6) Jonas: But the $10,000 in the heads branches depends on the very policy you are choosing right now in the tails branch. You can't have a policy that pays on tails only when you feel like it, because the predictor reads the real policy—not the mood. So either you run the paying policy across branches, or you admit you'll never collect on heads.

(7) Maya: Then the theory is telling me to take a guaranteed $100 loss in order to subsidize a version of me I'll never be. At that point it has stopped tracking anything I care about. I'd rather be the kind of person who doesn't pay; if the heads version of me misses out, that isn't me.
