In my reading group we were on about whether you can always know your own mind, and two of the regulars, Priya and Tom, got into a genuine row. I'll reconstruct as best I remember.

Priya set it up like this. Suppose someone goes from definitely feeling cold to definitely feeling warm over an hour, in steps so small they couldnt tell any two adjacent moments apart. At the start they know they feel cold; fair enough. Now if knowing you feel cold means you couldnt easily have been wrong in a nearly identical case, then they must still feel cold at the next step, then the next, and by induction all the way to the end. But they dont feel cold at the end. So the idea that whenever you feel cold you can know it must be false—and the same argument, she said, is meant to sink the old KK idea that whenever you know something you're in a position to know you know it.

Tom said that's just the sorites paradox with 'knows' glued on. Whatever solution you like for heaps—sharp cutoff somewhere unknowable, degrees of truth, contextual shifts—you can rerun it here. So the argument doesn't reveal anything specific about self-knowledge or second-order knowledge.

Priya came back: there's a real disanalogy. With heaps you're classifying a vague word against a world that's already there. Here the adjacent cases are, for the subject, indiscriminable, and the whole point is that the subject's own powers of discrimination create the margin for error. That's not the heap problem.

Tom said that still leaves the key premise—if you know you feel cold, then you're in the cold state one step over—looking like a safety requirement on knowledge, and safety is exactly the controversial bit. You can't just assert it.

Priya said it's weaker than a whole safety theory: it's just a point about reliability, and any knowledge is incompatible with being that close to error.

Tom replied that reliability is normally statistical and tolerant of borderline misses. A thermometer can be reliable even if there are two temperatures a hundredth of a degree apart it occasionally mixes up.

As a retired physicist I found myself thinking about measurement error and instrument calibration, but I couldn't frame the transfer in philosophical terms, so I stayed out of it. Now they want a referee.
