So here's a practice I've never been able to square. Banks and apps sell commitment devices: locked savings accounts with early-withdrawal penalties, apps that fine you if you skip the gym, self-exclusion programs for gamblers. The whole selling point is that you, right now, dont trust your future self and want to bind them. But the same legal system that enforces these contracts also has cooling-off periods that let you undo a decision you made yesterday, and courts will scrap an unconscionable contract because your earlier self was desperate, misled, or under pressure. So sometimes the earlier self gets treated as the real you whose choices bind, and other times the later self gets protected from the earlier one. What theory of diachronic rationality does this patchwork presuppose? Is the implicit picture that you're a single agent with unstable preferences, a sequence of distinct selves, or something about exponential versus hyperbolic discounting? Reconstruct the view of agency-over-time that makes the most sense of the practice, say which parts of the practice strictly require what (and which parts are compatible with several theories), then tell me what courts and banks would have to do differently if they instead took the rival view seriously.
