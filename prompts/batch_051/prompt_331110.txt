I run a pricing desk at a small sportsbook, and every day we set decimal odds for upcoming events. I want a reusable audit procedure—a sequence of checks a junior trader could run in about fifteen minutes on a proposed set of odds before we post them—that catches any combination of our own quoted prices on which a customer could make a guaranteed profit. We use decimal odds and at most a pocket calculator; I sign off on any sheet where the procedure stops with a no or hands the decision up. Ground the procedure in what the Dutch book argument actually establishes: that a set of prices is coherent only if it satisfies the probability axioms, and that incoherent prices can be converted into a sure loss for whoever offers them. Do not just restate the theorem. Translate it into an operational checklist, and for each check say what counts as passing, what counts as a hard stop, and what counts as a judgment call that a person must make. Keep clearly distinct the audit of our internal fair odds for coherence—the philosophical Dutch book check—from the audit of the published odds with their margin for customer arbitrage, and explain exactly how those two checks are related and where one does not reduce to the other.

Run the procedure on three worked examples, showing the arithmetic and where each gate stops. First, a three-way soccer line at 2.20 home, 4.50 draw, 5.00 away, where the draw and away prices were deliberately lengthened to attract new accounts. Second, a two-outcome basketball moneyline that passes the simple check but sits next to a correlated winning-margin market; show both what the procedure can certify and where it must stop. Third, an incomplete partition, such as a twelve-outcome tournament winner market where only five outcomes have been priced. At least one example should produce an answer I will not like, namely that I must change a price I had commercial reasons for wanting. End by listing what the procedure cannot catch without a richer model of correlations and joint probabilities, and where residual judgment remains even after every gate is passed.
