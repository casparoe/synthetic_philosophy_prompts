I've become convinced of a view sometimes called free-floating rationales: the claim that there can be reasons why a structure or behavior has the shape it does — reasons that genuinely explain it, and in some sense even justify it — without any reasoner who entertains them. The eye is for seeing, a market price aggregates dispersed information, a trained model's policy can be rational relative to its training distribution; on this view none of that is merely a façon de parler or a projection of our own psychology. What would have to be true for this view to be false? Give me the strongest defeaters, sorted by how decisive they are: empirical findings from animal cognition or machine learning, arguments about what it takes for something to count as a reason at all, difficult cases the view cannot describe without stretching, and internal tensions about how to individuate reasons or distinguish genuinely free-floating rationales from ones that are merely imputed by an observer. Then tell me whether the view has been insulated against refutation — whether its defenders could absorb any purported counterexample as just another free-floating rationale — and whether you think that insulation is a defect for a metaphysical view of this kind or simply what a view like this should look like.
