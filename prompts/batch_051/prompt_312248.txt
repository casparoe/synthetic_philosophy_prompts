Act as a philosopher of mathematics with expertise in the historiography of early numeracy; think through the question step by step, and write your answer in British English.

I am a philosopher of mathematics, and in my seminar on the origins of mathematics we keep returning to a version of the old question: when does counting, measuring, or reckoning become mathematics? Our test case is Plimpton 322, the Old Babylonian clay tablet usually dated to around 1800 BCE and now held at Columbia University. Its surviving fragment contains a fifteen-row, four-column table in sexagesimal notation. On the standard reading, its rows encode Pythagorean triples, or the reciprocal-pair parameters from which such triples can be generated; the entries are arranged in a deliberate order, and the tablet contains errors as well as damage. Its purpose is disputed: proposals include a number-theoretic or geometrical investigation, an aid for surveying or architecture, a teacher's store of exercises, and something closer to an administrative table.

A colleague defends the following claim: the activity that produced Plimpton 322 was mathematics properly so called, not merely applied reckoning, bookkeeping, or pre-mathematical numeracy. I am not sure the claim is testable, because the word mathematics is carrying much of the weight and a single surviving artefact can be read in several ways.

Your task is to design the strongest empirical test the claim can be given: the observation, measurement, or pattern — realisable in principle on this tablet and the surrounding corpus of Old Babylonian administrative, school, and mathematical texts — whose outcome would most bear on whether my colleague is right. Begin by brainstorming at least five candidate ways of operationalising the difference between mathematics and sophisticated counting or recipe-following. Then select the one or two that can genuinely be brought to bear on the surviving record, and for each specify what evidence would count in favour, what evidence would count against, and how much a favourable result would actually establish. Pay particular attention to the hardest boundary: distinguishing a scribe who follows a fixed procedure from one who grasps a general relationship. Close by saying exactly what even the best feasible test leaves unsettled — which part of the claim no observation can reach, and why. Please write your answer in British English.
