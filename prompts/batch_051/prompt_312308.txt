I'm a journalist covering legal theory and the empirical study of institutions, and I'm preparing a feature on a dispute that has been running for years between two camps in the literature on judicial review. For shorthand, I'll call them the strong-review camp and the departmentalist/popular-constitutionalist camp. The first holds that the judiciary should be the authoritative interpreter of the constitution, its readings binding the other branches between elections unless overridden by amendment or by the court's own later doctrine. The second holds that constitutional meaning is developed through continuing contestation among the branches and the public, and that legislatures and executives may act on their own good-faith constitutional readings rather than treating the most recent court ruling as conclusive. Each side believes the other has engaged only a flattened version of its actual commitments.

I want to convene an adversarial collaboration between representatives of the two camps and publish the document they produce. Please draft that joint statement. It should include: (1) a numbered list of substantive claims about judicial fallibility, legislative incentives and institutional capacity, constitutional change, and public constitutional engagement that both camps can sign without hedging; (2) a precise statement of their remaining disagreements, phrased so that each side would accept the wording of its own view and the wording of the other's; (3) the cruxes, meaning the specific sub-questions such that a persuasive answer would move at least one side; and (4) for each crux, a concrete historical episode, institutional design choice, or body of empirical evidence that both sides would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. I suspect some apparent disagreements are verbal — for example, whether the phrase constitutional meaning refers to what a provision meant when adopted or to the best current understanding of its abstract commitments — and part of the task is to see which stated differences survive careful paraphrase.

My own hunch is that at least one crux will concern whether elected officials possess both the incentives and the institutional capacity for good-faith constitutional interpretation, and another will concern whether the departmentalist account is meant as a description of existing practice, a normative ideal, or both. But I would rather see these emerge from the drafting than have them imposed by my framing. I am less interested in a survey of the debate than in a document the two parties could actually co-sign, and please avoid footnotes, references, or reading lists; I'll add those separately if needed.
