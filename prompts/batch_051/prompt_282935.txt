I'm a third-year undergrad in a seminar on Kant's practical philosophy, and next week I present my term paper on the murderer-at-the-door essay. My reading goes agianst the grain of the class, so I want to rehearse the Q&A before I walk in.

My thesis: Kant's essay is usually read as the claim that you may never lie, even to save someone's life, because truthfulness is an unconditional duty that beats all consequences. I think that's wrong, or at least overstated. Kant is really making a narrower point about *right*: there can be no right to lie from philanthropy, because a maxim like "I may lie whenever it would prevent a harm" cannot be willed as a public law — if everyone could claim that permission, the practice of promising and testifying that the permission would have to ride on collapses. The murderer at the door is an instance of that point, not a blanket ban on misleading. So the standard "Kant would rat out Anne Frank" objection misfires: Kant isn't giving you a decision procedure for the door, he's denying you a certain kind of standing to claim a lie was permissible. On my reading he's silent, or at least not committed to the monstrous conclusion, about whether you may mislead the murderer in some other way.

The audience is mixed, and they'll come at me from different angles. The professor is a textualist Kantian who thinks the standard absolutist reading is right and will say I've read the conclusion away. There's a grad student who's a committed consequentalist and thinks all the right/standing stuff is so much Kantian face-saving: either lying is forbidden or it isn't, and my view has to say which. Another student works in philosophy of law and is going to press me on whether I mean "right" in the juridical sense (Recht) or the ethical sense, and whether my public-law test is doing moral work or just legal work. And there's a quiet one who keeps asking whether my reading lets you mislead without lying — "I don't know where he is" while pointing at the basement — and, if not, wether the reading buys me anything at all.

Give me a prep sheet, not a defense of my paper. I want the objections in roughly the order I'll actually get them, phrased the way those specific people will phrase them, with what I should concede, what I should hold, and a note on where each reply is weakest. Be honest about which objection is most damaging, and include the one I'm secretly hoping nobody asks — I think it's something about the murderer having forfeited his right to the truth, which looks like it could make my own public-law test actually license lying to him, and then I'm not defending Kant anymore, I'm defending a cleaned-up Kant.

Format: end with a last line beginning \answer{...} that gives a two- or three-word label for the objection I'm most likely to lose to.
