In Thomas Henry Huxley's "On the Hypothesis that Animals are Automata, and its History" (1874), he introduces the steam-whistle analogy: consciousness is produced by the working of the brain but has no power to modify that working, just as a locomotive's steam-whistle is produced by the engine but does not influence its machinery. Huxley deploys this analogy to license epiphenomenalism about conscious states. Audit the analogy. Give an explicit correspondence table—what in the mind–brain case corresponds to the whistle, the engine, the steam, the sound, and the direction of causal influence—and identify where the mapping breaks down. Which disanalogies matter for the conclusion that consciousness is causally inert, and which are incidental? In particular, address the paradox of phenomenal judgment: if the analogy were accurate, the judgment that one is in pain would not be caused by the pain itself but by underlying neural events. Does the epiphenomenalist conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying the argumentative weight all along?
