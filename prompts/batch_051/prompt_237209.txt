A friend keeps trotting out an argument he calls the 'periodization treadmill' and I can't figure out where it goes wrong. It runs like this:

(1) Historians mostly agree by now that 'the Scientific Revolution' is a later imposition: the term and the tidy story of a single sixteenth–seventeenth-century break were put onto the period afterwards, mostly by twentieth-century historians, and the actual decades were messier and more continuous than the label suggests.
(2) If a category is imposed on the past by later historians rather than recognized by the people living through it, it doesn't mark a real joint in the past.
(3) So the Scientific Revolution isn't a real break.
(4) The same test, he says, dispatches the Renaissance and the Middle Ages too.
(5) But the test seems to apply to every period label there is: the Cold War, the Industrial Revolution, the Bronze Age — all named and bounded after the fact, not by the people inside them.
(6) So if the test is sound, there are no real periods or breaks in history at all, just an undifferenciated stream we chop up however it suits us.
(7) And that seems absurd: the Bronze Age collapse and the Industrial Revolution are exactly the kind of thing history should be able to say happened.

He thinks the argument shows all period labels are projections, but I suspect it proves too much. Which step would you reject, and what do you give up by rejecting it? I'd rather keep (7) than (2), but I don't know whether I then have to throw (2) out entirely or just qualify it.
