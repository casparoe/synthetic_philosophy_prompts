You are an expert in philosophy of language and experimental psycholinguistics. Work through this problem carefully, beginning with some brainstorming about how a word's "connotations" or "default associations" could be operationalized, and then develop a concrete experimental design.

In his essay "A Person Paper on Purity in Language" (from *Metamagical Themas*, Basic Books, 1985), Douglas R. Hofstadter writes under the satirical persona "William Satire." The narrator, defending the race-neutral use of "white" for all people, says:

There is nothing denigrating to black people in being subsumed under the rubric "white"-no more than under the rubric "person." After all, white is a mixture of all the colors of the rainbow, including black. Used inclusively, the word "white" has no connotations whatsoever of race. Yet many people are hung up on this point.

The paragraph is an inverted parody of a familiar defense of generic masculine terms: that "man" and "mankind" are semantically neutral, that a competent speaker can use them inclusively without any mental coloring, and that the suggestion of male defaultness is supplied by the oversensitive listener rather than by the word itself. Suppose a colleague in my department defends this real-life analogue in essentially the narrator's terms, insisting that, for ordinary competent speakers, generic "man" has no masculine connotations whatsoever.

I want you to treat that colleague's claim as a testable hypothesis rather than an armchair semantic claim. Propose the strongest available experimental test of whether generic masculine terms carry masculine default associations despite a speaker's inclusive intentions. Be concrete: the design, the contrasting conditions, the outcome that would favor the neutrality-of-generic-"man" reading, and the outcome that would favor the masculine-default reading. Then say exactly what a positive result would and would not settle. In particular, address whether any such experiment could show that the terms should be abandoned, or only that they tend to have certain psychological effects; and whether the colleague could always dismiss a result by saying the test measured an incidental association rather than the meaning of the word. Finally, reflect briefly on whether Hofstadter's satirical inversion itself does any philosophical work in this dispute, or whether it merely makes an empirical hypothesis vivid while leaving the real question to an experiment.
