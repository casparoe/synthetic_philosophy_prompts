I teach an undergraduate historiography seminar, and next term I want to use Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day as a literary test case in the argument between history from below and microhistory, on the one side, and the older history of elites and states, on the other. Treat the two allied movements as distinct enough to matter: history from below is about whose lives count as the material of history and how much agency ordinary people have; microhistory is about scale and whether a closely read small case can force large questions. Now read the novel through this lens and give me a critical reading I can build a class around.

The central question the session should press: Stevens the butler appears to hold that a life acquires historical significance only through proximity to powerful men making consequential public decisions; his ideal of dignity is service in a great house where affairs of state are transacted, and the novel lets the great events of interwar diplomacy surface mostly through domestic routine, overheard fragments of meetings, and matters kept offstage. Does the novel endorse that criterion of significance, undermine it, or hold both in suspension without deciding? Please say what the novel shows or dramatizes about the relation between an ordinary life and the history of states, and where a history-from-below reading tends to overreach.

I especially want you to work through three things. First, one or two scenes or narrative habits in which ordinary life and domestic detail are given a historical weight the great-men narrative would deny them. Second, one place where the novel resists being turned into a vindication of history from below, for example because Stevens's self-erasure is self-deceiving rather than heroic, or because the household is complicit in Lord Darlington's politics, so that the vantage point of the servant is not put forward as innocent. Third, what the novel adds to the methodological debate that argument alone cannot, in particular whether the obscure life is best read as a window onto structures of power or as a way of disappearing from them.

Treat the novel and the historiographical debate as equally serious: do not reduce the novel to an illustration, and do not bend the debate to fit the novel. End by telling me which scenes you would assign for the session and which interpretive move the novel most resists. Also tell me how confident you are in each of your main interpretive claims and single out the point you are least sure of.
