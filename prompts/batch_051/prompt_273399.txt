I am a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I teach a graduate seminar on how the epistemology of expertise should inform science reporting. I am grading a take-home exam and would like a careful second read of one answer. The exam question was:

In your own words, explain the disagreement between conciliationists and steadfasters about how a person should respond when an epistemic peer disagrees with them. Define "epistemic peer," and then identify one way each position might bear on how a science journalist should report a case in which two equally qualified experts hold opposite conclusions.

The student wrote:

Peer disagreement occurs when two epistemic peers — people roughly equal in intelligence, training, and command of the same evidence — arrive at different conclusions about the same question. Conciliationists say that upon learning of such disagreement, each party should split the difference: give the peer's view equal weight with their own and lower their confidence accordingly. Steadfasters deny this. They hold that a careful reasoner may retain her original confidence after learning that a peer disagrees, because she has first-person access to her own reasoning but not to the peer's. For science reporting, conciliationism indicates that when credentialed experts line up on both sides of a question, the matter is genuinely open, so coverage should give the two camps roughly equal space and equal epistemic standing. Steadfastness indicates the reverse: a reporter who has examined the evidence may treat the expert whose reasoning she finds most transparent as authoritative and need not seek out a dissenting expert for balance. The two positions therefore supply competing heuristics for covering expert controversy.
