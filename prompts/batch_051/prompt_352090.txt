I am a novelist, writing a book set across Paris and Berlin in the years around Germany's final nuclear exit. The pivotal scene is a Berlin hearing: my French protagonist, a reactor safety engineer who has spent two decades with a large nuclear operator, appears before a newly formed cross-party commission asked to advise the next government on whether the phase-out should be softened, extended, or left alone. I want the question period to sound as if it were scripted by her opponents, not by someone who already agrees with her.

Prepare her Q&A sheet. Her stated position, which she has fifteen minutes to make:

1. The decision to shut down operating German reactors was a retrospective mistake — not because nuclear power is without risk, but because it accepted certain, cumulative climate damages in order to avoid a set of nuclear risks that could have been managed more narrowly.
2. France's fleet is not a model of cheap new build. Its advantage is that its reactors exist, are largely decarbonized, and are already paid for, while carrying their own vulnerabilities: aging components, heat waves and low river flows, maintenance backlogs, and debt. She concedes that recent new-build projects in France and elsewhere have been disastrous on cost and schedule.
3. The sensible policy is not to restart the closed plants, and not to restore the old nuclear-versus-renewables rivalry, but to run existing low-carbon plants until they can be replaced cleanly, and to treat firm low-carbon power rather than the abolition of a technology as the actual objective.

The audience is a Berlin energy-policy commission. Its members, with their own vocabularies, include: a former Green state secretary who treats the phase-out as a hard-won democratic settlement; an NGO campaign director whose worldview was formed by Chernobyl and Fukushima and who frames waste as intergenerational injustice; an energy-transition economist who believes recent nuclear build costs have made new reactors unreachable without subsidies; a Social Democratic industrial-union representative whose real concern is cheap, reliable power for manufacturing jobs; a physicist who accepts the climate consensus but distrusts centralized infrastructure on resilience grounds; and a policy analyst from a renewable-heavy state who suspects any nuclear revival is mainly a project of utility incumbents and the French state.

Draft me the prep sheet, not a defense. I want the eight or so objections this audience would actually raise, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are. For each, give me: the objection phrased the way that particular member would put it; where its real force lies; what, if anything, she should concede; a two-minute reply; and a note on where the reply is softest. Tell me which objections she should concede up front in her opening rather than wait to be challenged on, and which one reply has to land for her to keep the room. End with the question she is hoping nobody asks — the one that would most expose the weakness in her third point — and write the honest two-sentence answer she dreads giving.

The hearing is dated loosely; I need the standing fault lines of the debate, not the latest figures.
