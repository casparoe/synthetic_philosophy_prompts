You are a professional philosopher specializing in risk, uncertainty, and the ethics of precaution. Think carefully, and begin by brainstorming two or three ways one might defend or attack the rule below before settling on your considered judgment.

I have a standing rule, and I want to know whether it is rationally defensible or just a bias dressed up as a rule. When something new enters my life—a new food additive, a new building material, a new app that collects data—I refuse to use it or support its adoption until someone has positively shown that it will not cause irreversible harm. I know demanding proof of a negative is often impossible, and I accept that this rule will sometimes keep me from good things. I also notice that I do not hold the familiar things I already use to this same standard; I treat their risks as an acceptable baseline. My rationale is that with reversible risks you can learn by trying, but with irreversible risks the experiment runs only one way. Is my rule defensible? Is the asymmetry between new and familiar risks defensible? If not, what is the best nearby rule that keeps what is good in my caution without freezing me into the status quo?
