I keep going back and forth on Arendt because as a retired physicist I like things to line up. In The Origins of Totalitarianism, especially the last chapter, she treats the camps as revealing a kind of radical evil. As I understand her, she's not saying the camps were just a larger quantity of ordinary wrongdoing. She's saying they broke the old moral frame: the victims were rendered superfluous, and the crimes exceeded what punishment or forgiveness could reach, because those categories assume a self and a humanity that the camps were designed to erase. That's a pretty strong claim about evil being radical.

Then in Eichmann in Jerusalem, subtitled a report on the banality of evil, she looks at one of the main organizers and concludes he isn't a monster. He's a nobody with a head full of stock phrases, bad at thinking, mostly concerned with his career. The horror, she suggests, is that this enormous destruction was moved forward by a thoughtless bureaucrat, not by a deep or demonic will. Evil on that account is banal, not radical.

So my question is wether these are actually in conflict, or whether she's looking at two different objects: the totalitarian system versus the individual functionary. Could the system be radically evil while the people who work it are banal? Or is the later book a correction of the earlier one, maybe because she came to think calling evil radical gave it too much metaphysical weight? Which reconciliation is honest and which is just special pleading? And is there a third reading where the tension is real but productive rather than a mistake?
