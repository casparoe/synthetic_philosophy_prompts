In debates about health spending and triage, people routinely take it for granted that a government acts decently if it spends extraordinary sums to save a single identifiable person — a child who has fallen down a well, a miner trapped underground, a patient already on the ward — while it is merely prudent, not obligatory, to spend the same money on measures that would save a larger number of as-yet-unidentified people from dying. The same asymmetry appears inside the hospital: under fixed capacity, a triage officer will divert a ventilator to the deteriorating patient in front of her rather than to two unseen patients with better expected survival, and she is more likely to be praised for loyalty to the person before her than criticised for losing the two. Yet the QALY arithmetic says that the cheaper, more effective choice in both cases is the statistical or unseen one. I want to know whether the asymmetry between the identified and the statistical life expresses a defensible principle or only a vividness bias. Find the strongest justification you can — I suspect it will draw on something like the duty to rescue the person with whom one is already in contact, the difference between failing to save and letting die, the expressive value of a society that refuses to price the person in front of it, or the thought that identification is what turns a number into a specific claim — and test it honestly rather than merely dressing it up. Among the cases I want you to run it against are these: a health service that keeps a register of everyone the screening programme would have saved and refuses to open it, so that whether a beneficiary is named depends on a bureaucratic choice; a public official who makes the same trade-off in private with full knowledge of the numbers; and a triage officer who faces one visible patient and one not-yet-arrived patient whose expected benefit from the same bed is twice as great. If no principle survives those cases, say so plainly, and say what practice should replace the current one and what we would lose by giving it up. Write your answer as continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write an email or a letter.
