With no formal training in philosophy or Buddhist studies, I have been trying on my own to work out whether Buddhist ethics, especially in its Mahayana forms, is best read as a kind of consequentialism — the view that what ultimately makes an action right or wrong is the amount of suffering or happiness it brings about for sentient beings. I have collected what seem to me to be the standard considerations in favor of that reading. (1) The tradition's organizing problem is the diagnosis and cessation of dukkha, so its moral teaching presents itself as a set of means for eliminating suffering rather than as a table of independently binding rules. (2) Karmic evaluation turns on the intention behind an act and on the kind of result the act tends to produce, which suggests that acts are graded by the quality of what flows from them rather than by some intrinsic feature of the act itself. (3) The precepts are framed as rules of training rather than categorical commands, and the tradition openly discusses setting them aside when keeping them would serve the welfare of beings less well than breaking them, which gives the rules an instrumental, consequence-tracking role. (4) In the Mahayana stories used to illustrate skillful means, a bodhisattva who kills a person about to murder many others is portrayed as having done something meritorious because the killing prevents a much greater harm, which looks like consequentialist reasoning about when rules may be broken. (5) The ethic is organized around impartial compassion for all sentient beings, with no special weight given to oneself or one's kin, which is close to the impartial aggregation of welfare that defines consequentialist outlooks.

I would like you to give me a further argument for a consequentialist reading of Buddhist ethics that is genuinely different from these five — not a rewording, a special case, or a combination of them. I am not asking for references to particular authors or texts; I want the argument itself stated plainly. If such an argument exists, state it as clearly as you can and then assess it honestly: does it actually support the consequentialist reading, and could someone who resists that reading turn it aside cheaply? If you think no such argument exists because these five already exhaust the case, say so directly and explain why the remaining candidates you consider are only variants of the items above.
