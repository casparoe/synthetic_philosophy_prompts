I want my main characters — a public defender, a law professor, a muckraking journalist, a former cop — to each hold a genuinely different account of why blackmail is a crime, because a few big scenes are just them talking straight past each other.

Can you build me a decision tree (or taxonomy, whichever comes out cleaner) that sorts the actual positions on the paradox of blackmail — the puzzle that it's usually legal to reveal the information, and usually legal to accept money to stay quiet, but illegal to say 'pay me or I release it.' Build it from the diagnostic fault lines, not from whatever the named positions happen to be. Each branch node should be a question or micro-case I could put to a smart layperson without them knowing which camp it pushes them toward: whether blackmail should still be punished when the threatened disclosure is true, whether the blackmail victim is really the main person harmed, whether blackmailing someone with their own secret is different from blackmailing them with a third party's secret, whether it matters that the two halves are each legal on their own.

Leaves should carry the name of the underlying rationale — 'the coercion view,' 'the privacy-as-property view,' whatever fits — or 'unnamed' if no one really defends that cell. Give each leaf one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and flag any two leaves your tree can't actually pull apart. Also tell me which empty cells in the logical space are empty because they're incoherent, which because they're merely unattractive, and which because nobody's bothered to fill them. And please make my two scenarios — the affair and the pollution secret — land differently for some positions; I can hang character reversals on that.
