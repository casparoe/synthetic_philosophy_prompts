I am a professor of philosophy and I serve on my university's Faculty Senate Committee on Elections and Procedures. The committee must recommend, before the end of term, which voting procedure the full faculty should use for decisions with three or more alternatives — for example, selecting among several proposed interdisciplinary research themes, ranking nominees for a university-wide teaching award, or choosing among competing general-education curricular packages. For decades the faculty has used simple plurality: the alternative with the most first-preference votes wins. The committee keeps hearing the same complaints: a winner can be opposed by a majority of voters; two similar proposals can split their natural base and allow a more polarizing alternative to win; and committees can game the ballot by strategically deciding which alternatives even appear. We have narrowed the field to three options: keep plurality, adopt instant-runoff voting, or adopt the Borda count. At our last meeting, a colleague invoked Arrow's impossibility theorem and argued that since every non-dictatorial method for aggregating ordinal preferences over three or more alternatives violates at least one of Arrow's seemingly mandatory conditions — unrestricted domain, Pareto efficiency, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship — there is no principled fact of the matter about which method is better. His conclusion was that we should keep plurality, not because plurality is good, but because any attempt to choose among the alternatives would pretend to a rationality the theorem shows is unavailable.

Draft the committee's decision memo, around 900 words, recommending one of the three options, or recommending a conditional path with a clear default. The memo should lead with the actionable bottom line, specify criteria the committee can actually apply, assess the alternatives against those criteria, and give an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side, including the Arrow-based argument that the choice is arbitrary. Do not let Arrow's theorem simply shut down the decision; engage with what it does and does not imply for a single-winner faculty vote, while keeping the memo usable by non-specialist faculty members who will ultimately vote on the recommendation.

After the memo, append a brief structured summary as a JSON object with the following fields: recommendation (string: the option or conditional path the memo advocates); bottom_line (string: the one-sentence actionable conclusion); criteria (array of strings: the criteria used to evaluate the options); arrow_implications (object with keys what_it_shows and what_it_does_not_show, each an array of strings); losing_side (object with keys position and strongest_argument); and residual_risk (string: the main unresolved risk under the recommendation).
