I'm a journalist working on a long feature about panpsychism, and I keep running into the same move. A philosopher or physicist will admit that the combination problem sounds bad — how do all the tiny bits of subjectivity add up to one unified stream of experience — and then reach for an analogy to make the problem look like a non-issue. The one I hear most often is water: an individual H2O molecule isn't wet, but a puddle is, and nobody treats the combination problem of wetness as a deep mystery. So, the argument goes, the fact that the micro-experiences of my neurons (or quarks, depending on the source) don't feel like a full human mind is no reason to deny that a full human mind results from them. I've also heard a pixel version: no single pixel contains the picture, but the screen shows one anyway. I can tell these analogies are doing alot of work — they're the main reason my sources think the combination problem is overrated — but my hand starts waving when I try to write that section of the piece.

So: audit the analogies for me. Give me the full mapping — what plays the role of the molecule, the wetness, the pixels, the picture — and then the disanalogies. I especially want to know which disanalogies matter for the exact job the analogy is being made to do, namely making the combination problem look like ordinary emergence, and which are just irrelevant differences. Does the conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table? And if water and pixels both mislead, tell me what a better analogy would be, or whether the fact that no good analogy seems available tells us something about panpsychism itself.
