I've been designing a text typeface for the past year, and the part that surprises me isn't the drawing but the categorizing: what keeps a glyph a member of the letter category 'a' at all? A double-storey 'a' and a single-storey 'a' are put together differently, a blackletter 'a' and a geometric sans 'a' are built in nearly opposite ways, and yet all of them read as the same letter without strain. Meanwhile, take an unmistakable 'a'-shape, reassign it to a different sound in a made-up alphabet, and something about its letter-identity seems to shift even though the shape has not. I want a decision tree that sorts the main positions on what makes every 'a' an 'a' by how people answer concrete cases, not by which label they happen to like. Build it from the logical space: first the splitting questions and the combinations of answers they permit, then locate the named positions in that space — something like an ideal-form view, a prototype/category view, a feature-or-topology view, a contrastive/functional view, and a deflationary label-convention view. Make the tree take seriously the split between style as inessential local variation and style as partly constitutive of which letter a mark is, and mark any combinations of answers that no one occupies, with a one-sentence diagnosis of why. Four cases must appear among the diagnostic questions: single-storey versus double-storey 'a'; a heavily stylized brand glyph readers identify as 'a' only because of the surrounding letters; an ordinary 'a'-shape reassigned to a different sound in a private alphabet; and a transitional historical form halfway between two letterforms. The audiences are a type-design workshop and a reading group on concepts, so it should be precise enough for both. Make definite choices: which splitting questions are the deep ones, which positions survive as leaves, and what each leaf's most awkward commitment is — resist the 'it depends' reflex.
