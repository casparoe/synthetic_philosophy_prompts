I'm a PhD student working on transcendental arguments, and I've been trying to figure out exactly how much a transcendental argument can pull out of the bare fact that I have self-conscious experience. In a seminar we reconstructed what I think of as a "persisting subject" argument, and I can no longer tell whether it is a genuine proof, a disguised tautology, or a modal slide. The argument runs as follows.

(1) A mental state is genuinely mine only if I can, in principle, ascribe it to myself; to have a pain or a thought as one's own is already to represent it as belonging to a subject. (2) That subject is not identical with any one of the states so ascribed: when I move from ascribing the pain at one time to ascribing the thought at another, the first-person element is represented as a single common item rather than as a fresh item each time. (3) Representing one common item across temporally distinct ascriptions is representing it as persisting through the interval. (4) A representation that is indispensable to the possibility of self-conscious experience cannot be non-veridical, since any evidence against it would itself be something I must self-ascribe; so if self-conscious experience requires representing a persisting subject, there really is one. (5) Therefore, for any being with self-conscious experience, a persisting subject distinct from its experiences exists.

I find (1) through (3) hard to reject, but something about moving to existence in (4) makes me uneasy. A deflationary line I keep running into is that all this licenses is that there is thinking going on, not that there is a thinker: the unity belongs to the stream of representations, not to an item in the world. But I am not fully convinced, because a representation so basic that I cannot coherently abandon it while still thinking at all looks, on many views, like a perfectly good entitlement.
