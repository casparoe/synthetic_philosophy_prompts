I'm a novelist, right now deep in a book about a small-town preacher whose adult son walks away from the faith, and for background I've been reading around the old argument about whether Paul and James actually contradict each other on faith and works. A friend of mine—not a theologian, but he grew up soaked in this stuff—keeps telling me the whole conflict evaporates if you just use the picture he says both writers share: faith is like a living tree, and good works are like its fruit. The tree doesn't become alive by producing fruit; the fruit just shows, to other people and to yourself, that the tree is alive. So Paul is talking about what makes the tree alive in the first place, and James is talking about what the evidence of a living tree looks like. From that he concludes not only that Paul and James aren't really in tension, but also that worrying about whether you've produced enough good works is as backwards as an apple tree worrying whether it's fruited enough to count as an apple tree.

I'd like you to take that analogy seriously and audit it rather than just endorse it. Lay out explicitly what's supposed to map onto what—faith to the tree, works to the fruit, justification or salvation to the tree's being alive, and so on. Then push on the places where the mapping creaks: whether good works actually follow from genuine faith automatically the way fruit follows from a healthy tree; whether James, when he says faith that never shows up in action is dead on its own, is treating works as mere evidence or as partly what makes faith living; whether the law-works Paul says people are justified apart from are the same thing as the neighbor-love works James points to; and whether the tree image quietly imports a passivity that makes my friend's don't-worry-about-your-works conclusion look more natural than it is. Then say how much of his no-contradiction conclusion survives once those disanalogies are on the table, and whether the tree/fruit picture is salvageable with patches, or should be set aside for something better—or for no image at all.
