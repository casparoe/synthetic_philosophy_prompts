American law draws the line between culpable and non-culpable deception in two quite different places depending on the context. In the law of perjury, the offense requires a knowingly false statement under oath: a witness who gives a literally truthful but materially misleading answer, intending the questioner to infer what is false, has not committed perjury. In securities regulation, by contrast, a company can be liable for fraud for saying something literally true if it omits facts needed to make the statement not misleading, and the half-truth doctrine treats a partial, technically accurate disclosure as a basis for liability when the overall impression it creates is deceptive. What theory of the wrong of deception is presupposed by the coexistence of these two practices? Do they jointly commit the law to the claim that the distinctive wrong of lying is asserting a false proposition rather than inducing a false belief, or do they presuppose a more contextual view, on which the wrong depends on the role the speaker occupies, the addressee's reasonable expectations, or the interests the regime protects? Reconstruct the implicit theory or theories as precisely as the practices allow, distinguish what the practices strictly presuppose from what they are merely compatible with, and say whether they can be reconciled without attributing inconsistent commitments to the law.
