After spending most of last evening arguing with a friend about whether practical stakes can change what someone knows, I want to pin down the rule behind her judgments before we pick the conversation back up. She was confident and consistent about these earlier cases.

In the low-stakes bank case, a man has solid evidence that the bank is open on Saturday — he checked the posted hours and called the automated line — and his belief is true, but nothing practical turns on it. She says he knows the bank is open. In the high-stakes bank case, the same man has the same basis, but he has already written a check that will bounce, with fees and a collections call, if the bank is not open Saturday. She says he does not know the bank is open.

Her travel cases run parallel. A leisure traveler remembers from her itinerary that her flight stops in Chicago; the memory is correct, and being wrong would cost her only an hour's wait. She says the traveler knows the flight stops there. The same traveler with the same memory, but who will miss the connection to a surgery she has waited years for if the itinerary is wrong, does not know the flight stops there.

She was also explicit that the difference is in what the person knows, not merely in what they are willing to say or do. When I suggested that the man in the high-stakes case knows all along and is simply not in a position to act on it or to assert it, she rejected that suggestion.

Now I want to predict her verdicts on three new cases and, more importantly, to identify which rule she is actually running. The candidate rules I can see that fit her pattern so far are: an attributor-contextualist one, on which the standards for a knowledge attribution are set by the practical situation of the person making the attribution; a subject-sensitive invariantist one, on which knowledge itself depends on the practical interests of the person the attribution is about; and a classical invariantist one, on which stakes never change whether someone knows, combined with some explanation of why my friend describes high-stakes subjects as not knowing. There may be others I am missing.

The new cases:

1. High-stakes subject, low-stakes attributor. Carmen looked up her train's departure time and believes truly that it leaves at six, but she is in a high-stakes situation: if the train is not at six, she misses a life-changing appointment. I have nothing at all riding on whether Carmen knows the train is at six; I am merely wondering. Would my friend say that my statement "Carmen knows the train is at six" is true, or that it is false?

2. Low-stakes subject, high-stakes attributor. Dana is deciding whether to say "Carmen knows the train is at six." Carmen is in a low-stakes situation and her true belief rests on good evidence. Dana takes no train and faces no risk from the train itself, but Dana has a large and embarrassing bet riding on whether that sentence is true in Dana's own mouth. Would my friend say Dana should count the sentence as true?

3. Unaware high stakes. A traveler has the same correct memory of the Chicago stop and is in fact in a situation where getting it wrong would cost her a great deal, but she falsely believes that nothing turns on the itinerary. Assume my friend is told the traveler's actual stakes and has no stake of her own. Would my friend say the traveler knows?

What I want is less a yes-or-no list than the principle the pattern forces on us. Tell me which of the rules above — or which combination or fourth view — best fits her verdicts, and say which of the three new cases would genuinely separate the candidate rules. If none of them does, give me the further case that would. Finally, please separate what is actually settled in the epistemology of knowledge attributions from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and do not present one live position as if it were the consensus.
