I keep having the same argument with a friend about whether some system—a thermostat, a convnet that labels tumors, a transformer that writes passable code—really represents the world or is just responding in ways we can't help describing as meaningful. We always end up talking past eachother, and I've realized it's because we've never agreed on what a theory of representation would actually have to do. Before I read any more, I want the spec: what does an adequate theory of how a physical system comes to have content have to deliver? I've mostly been reading two naturalizing camps—one that says content is reliable causal covariance with the environment (smoke means fire, but then how does anything ever misrepresent?) and one that says content is what a state is biologically supposed to track (then a frog snapping at a passing pellet represents a fly, which sounds wrong). I want a requirements list, not a survey.

Lay out the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse (indicating versus representing, original versus derived content, being caused by something versus being about it), and the traps it has to avoid (making misrepresentation impossible, smuggling in an interpreter who already has content, requiring consciousness or language). For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it. Then flag any pair of requirements that look like they can't be jointly satisfied—my guess is that allowing error and starting from purely non-semantic pieces are going to pull against each other, but I'd like the actual map of tensions. Finally, say which single requirement does the most to eliminate the standard candidate theories, while keeping the emphasis on the criteria rather than on scoring the contenders.
