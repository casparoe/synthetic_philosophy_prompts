Last week I sat in on a reading group where two members, Sam and Mira, got properly stuck into a question I thought was settled ages ago, and I genuinely couldn't tell who had the better of it.

The trigger was a discussion of Darwin's On the Origin of Species, specifically the bit in the chapter on instinct where he treats the case of sterile worker insects. Darwin's point, as Mira summarised it, was that because the workers of an ant colony don't reproduce, natural selection cannot favour modifications in the individual worker; the modification must be selected for in the family or colony as a whole, since only the fertile members pass anything on. Mira said that this shows Darwin had effectively discovered the superorganism: he saw that the colony, not the ant, is the unit on which selection acts, and so the colony is the organism in the relevant sense.

Sam pushed back. He said Darwin was making an inheritance-theoretic point about where the variation gets transmitted, not an ontological claim about what counts as an organism. The fact that selection must be represented at the level of the community doesn't mean the community is an organism, any more than the fact that genes are a unit of selection makes genes organisms. A colony, he said, is still a population of ants, each with its own body, its own germ line, and its own life cycle; calling it an organism stretches the word past usefulness.

Mira replied that the same evasion could be aimed at our own bodies. A human body is a population of cells, many of which are non-reproductive, and some of which have different genomes, like immune cells after V(D)J recombination or mitochondrial copies. Yet we call the whole thing one organism. If functional integration, division of labour, and metabolism are what make a body an organism, ant colonies score high on all three: the queen and workers are like a body's reproductive and somatic cells.

Sam came back with a differnt angle. He said that once you go down Mira's route, the term organism starts to engulf every holobiont. Coral polyps with their zooxanthellae, plants with their rhizosphere microbes, even a human with a gut microbiome would each be an organism-and-a-half, and then we'd have no clear boundary between an organism and an ecosystem. He said the better move is to keep organism for the individual biological unit with its own developmental lifecycle, and treat colonies and holobionts as higher-order systems or communities, not organisms.

Mira's last word was that a developmental-lifecycle criterion smuggles in the individualist assumption from the outset: an ant colony has development too — it grows, differentiates castes, reproduces by producing new queens, and senesces. She said the boundary between organism and superorganism is a matter of degree, and Darwin's sterile-insect case marks the point where the degree tips.

I'd like you to go through this exchange and say who's right about which specific point, and what, if anything, both of them are missing. Don't just give a 'both sides have a point' answer; I want a point-by-point adjudication of the arguments as they were actually made.
