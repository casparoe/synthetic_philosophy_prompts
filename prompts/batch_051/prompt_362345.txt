I got into an argument on a forum about whether a magazine that ran a column saying a politician probably took bribes could be sued for defamation, and it sent me down a rabbit hole. The law seems to sort statements into facts, which can be true or false and so a harmful false statement about someone is actionable, and opinions, which are treated as if they can't be true or false and get protected. But then I realized forecasts and credit ratings don't sit cleanly on either side. A rating like "this bond is speculative" is something the agency calls an opinion, probably to get legal protection, but the only reason anyone pays for it is that it's supposed to track reality. A forecast is even stranger. If someone says 70% chance of rain and it rains, were they right? Not exactly. If it doesn't rain, they weren't exactly wrong either, since they left 30% for that. But if they say 70% a hundred times and it rains only 40 of those, we'd say their forecasts are factually bad, not just disagreeable.

So here's the parallel I can't shake: the legal fact/opinion cut looks like it maps onto a distinction between all-or-nothing claims that can be true or false, and graded credences that can't be judged one at a time but can be scored over a batch. Ratings and forecasts look like opinions under the law but function like measurable factual judgments in practice. Is that a deep structural sameness, or am I just pattern-matching on the word "opinion"? If it's real, should defamation law carve out a third category for scoreable forecasts and ratings, or would that import a weird "accurate across many cases" standard into a law that needs yes-or-no verdicts? If it's not real, where exactly does the mapping come apart?
