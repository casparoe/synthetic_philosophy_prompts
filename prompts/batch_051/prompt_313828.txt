Since retiring from a career in atmospheric research, I have been sitting in on a philosophy of religion discussion circle, and an argument between two of its members has stayed with me long after the meeting. The first, an engineer by training, maintained that petitionary prayer is quietly incoherent: if God is omniscient, the one praying cannot be conveying information; if God is immutable, the prayer cannot be moving the divine will; and if God is perfectly good, the prayer cannot be inducing God to do what God would not already do. Every petition, on his view, is therefore either redundant or a disguised attempt to change what cannot be changed. The second, a retired teacher of classical languages, replied that this is a picture of prayer as machinery and that the tradition means something different. Petition, she said, is ordered less toward altering God than toward conforming the petitioner, so that the one who asks is drawn into God's own willing rather than bargaining with it; moreover, an omniscient and immutable God can from all eternity will to give a good because it was asked, so the request enters the divine plan as a condition rather than as a cause that moves a fixed God. The engineer answered that if the real work is done on the petitioner, then the grammar of asking is dispensable and could be replaced by confession or self-examination without loss; and that once answered and unanswered prayers cannot be distinguished from plain fortune, the practice no longer claims to reach beyond the agent's psychology. The classicist said a request can be non-redundant under an already settled plan, just as a parent may have decided to grant something only if the child asks; to demand that petition show up in a statistical comparison, she continued, mistakes a relational act for a technology. The engineer's last move was to insist the analogy fails precisely where it is needed, since a parent remains changeable while God, ex hypothesi, cannot be. She granted the disanalogy but denied it matters: the analogy was meant only to show that being a condition on a fixed will need not be idle, and immutability, on the classical account, is an unchanging eternal act that already contains what appears to us in time as an answer.

I was the bystander for this exchange and have deliberately kept quiet, though I have begun to form a view. I would like you to adjudicate it point by point: which of the specific claims each of them made is right, where each argument overreaches, and what distinction or idea the two of them would have needed to make the debate advance rather than circle.
