For the past two years I have experienced intermittent episodes that resemble what I have since learned is called pain asymbolia. During an episode I can report accurately where a pain is, how strong it is, and whether it burns, stabs, or aches; but the sensation makes no claim on me. It does not feel bad, I do not guard the limb or reach for relief, and I can describe it with the same indifference as if I were describing a colour. What is missing seems to be the very thing that makes pain matter. I am not asking you to interpret my neurology; I offer these episodes only as the data that forced the question on me.

These episodes, following an earlier period of ordinary chronic pain, have made me rebuild my view of what the badness of pain consists in. I have arrived at a position I call the Pledge Theory, and I would like an honest assessment of it.

The theory is this. A pain is bad, when it is bad, not because of its sensory intensity and not because pain is intrinsically awful, but because it presents a demand whose authority the sufferer cannot cancel. There are two components. The first is felt urgency: the pain insists that something be done. The second is unanswerability: there is no response available that counts as either satisfying the demand or legitimately declining it. In pain asymbolia, on this account, the urgency survives — which is why the location and character of the pain can still be reported — but the authority is absent, so the sensation is not bad. In a stubbed toe, by contrast, the pain is bad even after I have pulled my foot back, because the demand has not been discharged: the toe keeps insisting, and I have no answer to give it. The degree of badness, I propose, is a function of the strength of the demand and its unanswerability together. A low-intensity chronic ache can be terrible because it is non-negotiable; a vivid pain under combat stress or deep concentration can be nearly indifferent because its authority has been suspended, not because the signal has disappeared.

I ask you to assess this as a piece of theory, charitably but candidly. Reconstruct it in its strongest form, and tell me whether it is genuinely distinct from the positions it plainly resembles — that pain is bad because of what it represents about bodily damage; that pain is bad because it is a kind of command; that pain is simply bad in itself; that pain is bad only because one wants it gone. Then press the hardest objections you can. At minimum, test the theory against these cases: pain suffered during sleep, when no demand is felt; the pain of grief; pain that is welcomed or sought, such as the burn of chilli or pain within consensual practices, where the sensation is vivid but the person does not want it removed; and pain in creatures or infants who cannot conceive of authority or answerability. I also want to know whether the theory wrongly predicts that any unrefusable demand — an itch, a full bladder, a bodily-felt social obligation — should count as painful in the same sense, and whether distraction or meditation, by suspending pain's authority, should genuinely dissolve its badness rather than merely cover it over.

Finally, please say what would change your verdict. Which empirical finding about asymbolia, chronic pain, or the effects of attention on pain, and which philosophical consideration, would most likely force you to revise your assessment? And where is your own reasoning least solid?
