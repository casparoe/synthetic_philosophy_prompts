I'm in a philosophy of mind class and we've been going around in circles about epiphenomenalism, specifically the paradox of phenomenal judgment: if qualia never cause anything, how is it that I'm sitting here correctly reporting on them? A friend of mine thinks the paradox dissolves once you drop the idea that justification requires causation. He laid his version out like this:

(1) On epiphenomenalism, the pain itself never causes my judgment that I'm in pain; a neural state N causes both the pain and the judgment.
(2) For a belief to be justified, it just needs to be formed by a process that reliably tracks the truth.
(3) The process running from N to my pain-belief is reliable: in nearby worlds where N occurs, pain occurs and I form the belief; in nearby worlds where N doesn't occur, I mostly don't.
(4) So my pain-belief is justified even though the pain never caused it.
(5) The paradox only looked like a paradox because it smuggled in a crude causal theory of justification, which nobody should accept anyway.

I've got a naging worry, though. If the pain itself is causally inert, what makes my belief be about the pain rather than merely about N? It feels like step (3) explains at best why I have a true-ish belief about something, not why its content is specifically phenomenal. My friend says content doesn't require causation either, and that I'm just re-running the same crude causal theory one level down, now about reference instead of justification. Something still feels off to me, but I can't locate the slip.

Is his argument valid? If not, which step fails? If it is valid, does it actually dissolve the original paradox, or does it just move the problem from justification to content? I'd rather not get a survey of positions: commit to the move you think is right, and tell me which premise of his, or which premise of mine, you'd reject or defend.
