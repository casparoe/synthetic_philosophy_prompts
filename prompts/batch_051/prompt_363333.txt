I'm preparing a session for an advanced seminar on Davidson's radical interpretation, and I want a thought experiment built to order. The case is meant to isolate a specific gap in the principle of charity: belief and meaning are fixed together, so an interpreter can spend charity either on making the speaker's beliefs come out true or on making her desires and values come out rational, and these two ways of spending charity can yield different truth conditions for one and the same sentence. Please construct a scenario in which two interpreters observe exactly the same behavior, share all the evidence Davidson allows a radical interpreter to have, and both apply the principle of charity scrupulously, yet converge on different interpretations. The divergence should fall out of this belief/desire-versus-meaning trade-off, not from one interpreter's laziness or from a stipulation that one reading is the correct one.

Constraints: the case should be ordinary and human — a workplace dispute, a family quarrel, a market transaction — with no exotic stimuli; it should center on a single utterance whose interpretation matters downstream; and neither reading may be declared right by fiat, since part of the exercise is to see what charity itself licenses. After the scenario, spell out each interpreter's assignment and the charity-based reasoning that licenses it; identify the single feature of the case that does the work and say what happens if that feature is removed; say whether Davidson's later triangulation idea, or a wider evidential base, would break the tie; and give the strongest attack a defender of the losing reading could make against the case.

One caution: if I have Davidson wrong — if, for instance, his principle of charity actually does determine a unique interpretation once the evidence is fixed, or if the belief/desire distinction I am leaning on is not one his holism allows — say so plainly rather than building a case on a confused premise. Format: exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, no introduction and no conclusion; the items should cover, in order, the scenario, interpreter A's assignment and reasoning, interpreter B's assignment and reasoning, the feature doing the work and what happens if it is removed, and the triangulation question together with the defender's attack.
