I chair the program committee of a mid-sized foundation devoted to improving the welfare of people living in extreme poverty. At our next meeting we must allocate an unallocated $25 million between two finalists. The first is a five-year grant to a coalition seeking to make agricultural and trade policy in several low-income countries more responsive to smallholder farmers, by building civil-society monitoring capacity, supporting legislative drafting, and funding policy research. Its theory of change is that durable rule changes would improve the terms on which millions of households earn their living; the causal chain is long, the probability of success is genuinely uncertain, and near-term measurable impact is likely negligible. The second is a portfolio of large-scale cash transfers to households in extreme poverty, delivered by an established partner with strong evidence of reliable, modest welfare gains per dollar; the gains are incremental, depend on continued funding, and may shrink as the most reachable households are covered.

The committee is divided. A majority-leaning faction argues that with a fixed marginal dollar, the foundation should fund the option with the highest credibly estimated marginal welfare gain, and that systems change is too often a license for unaccountable, high-variance spending. A minority argues that marginal-impact reasoning is structurally biased against interventions that could change the background conditions under which millions live, and that a foundation that never takes such bets forecloses its largest possible contribution.

Please draft a short decision memo for the committee. It should recommend a concrete allocation—the full sum to one proposal, a split, or a defined process such as a smaller pilot with milestone-based follow-on funding—and defend that recommendation. Engage the strongest consideration on the losing side seriously, and address explicitly whether the dispute is best resolved at the level of individual proposals or at the level of portfolio strategy. Justify the recommendation on its merits; do not include a bibliography or rely on named authorities.
