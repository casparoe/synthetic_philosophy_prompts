Before I bring this to our hospital's clinical informatics committee, I would like a careful reading of a governance rule. Our policy on predictive models states: "A model may not be used to inform a discharge decision unless the attending physician can state, for the patient in question, the specific factors in the record that most drove the model's output, and can say how the output would have changed had any one of those factors been different." The rule was written when explaining a model standardly meant reporting the coefficients of a logistic regression, and its authors assumed that a physician's ability to state the drivers of an output would amount to describing the model as it actually computes.

The committee is now evaluating a readmission-risk model built by an outside group. For each patient it prints a risk score and a single rationale sentence, such as: "Risk is elevated primarily because of three admissions in the past year, low mobility, and elevated BNP." A physician given only that output can state the named factors and can describe how the score would have changed had the BNP reading been normal. But an independent audit that systematically removed and reweighted inputs found that the rationale sentences are produced by a separate module trained after the fact to generate natural-language rationales, penalized only for gross mismatch with the score. The sentences track the score loosely: some of the factors they name barely move it, while two features the sentence never mentions — the day of the week on which the previous admission ended and the frequency of certain phrases in the free-text nursing notes — are among the strongest actual drivers of the model's output. At the same time, in a prospective test, physicians using only the rationale sentences reached override decisions that agreed, in the overwhelming majority of cases, with the decisions of physicians who had been shown the model's true top features.

Does this model satisfy the policy? I would like the clause read three ways — from its plain words, from its evident purpose, and from what its drafters would have said had this two-module architecture been described to them — and then a judgment as to which reading should control for the committee, and why. In particular, does it matter under any of these readings that the physician's understanding is partly surrogate rather than veridical, and could the observed clinical equivalence satisfy the policy even though the stated factors are not in fact the strongest drivers?
