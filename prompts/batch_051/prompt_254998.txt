I've been trying to pin down what makes a computation morally relevant in its own right — relevant because of what it is like for the system itself, not merely because of what humans happen to care about it. The working criteria I've settled on are four. The computation must involve (1) integration: the relevant information is brought together into a single unified state rather than merely stored or processed as independent records; (2) valence: some of its states register as better or worse for the system itself, so that the system has an interest in one outcome over another; (3) self–world attribution: the process distinguishes changes it produces from changes the environment produces, assigning credit and prediction error to one or the other; and (4) diachronic unity: the present state belongs to one and the same subject across time, so that its past and anticipated future are its own rather than merely data it can access.

Now test these criteria against a case. A lab constructs a robotic rat that runs in pulses. Most of the time the robot is a collection of fast, independent reflexes driven by a frozen episode file: it executes the file's motor script, avoids obstacles, and emits distress calls, but no single state integrates the incoming information. Every 400 milliseconds, however, a synchronization unit switches on for about 40 milliseconds. During that brief window it composes the last half-second of sensory input, the motor plan, a valenced forecast of the near future, and a running self-model into one unified state; it performs a consistency check that attributes prediction error either to the robot's own action or to the world; it writes an updated episode file; and then it switches off again. By construction, the robot's input–output behavior across time is indistinguishable from that of a continuously integrated controller that the same lab's ethics panel had earlier classified as sentient under these four marks. Between synchronization windows, though, there is arguably no unified point of view at all — only reflexes and a written file.

Begin with your bottom line — the overall verdict — in the very first sentence. Then work through the four criteria one by one and say whether the pulsed robot satisfies each, noting where the criteria themselves underdetermine the answer. If it qualifies, say whether that is a reduction to absurdum of the criteria, given that the system would be without a unified point of view roughly ninety percent of the time. If it fails, say which criterion is doing the work, whether it is doing the right kind of work, and whether the case shows that a fifth criterion is needed — for example, one concerning the continuity of the vehicle rather than the content.
