Could you act as a professional philosopher of science for a moment? I'm a retired physical chemist who has recently started reading philosophy of science, and there's a puzzle that keeps bugging me. Please think carefully, and don't just answer on my terms — if I'm presupposing something false, I'd rather you pointed that out first. Maybe start by sorting out the different things 'natural kind' could mean before you answer. (And if you could write in British English, I'd appreciate it.)

Here it is. I keep comming across philosophers who treat it as an open question whether the kinds that science discovers are real features of the world or just useful projections of ours — whether there are 'natural kinds' in anything more than a loose sense. I don't get it. Chemistry seems decisive to me. Water is H2O. Gold is the element with 79 protons. The periodic table groups the elements into natural families, and it even predicted the properties of missing elements before anyone had found them. If protons and electrons are real, and atomic number fixes the chemical behaviour, then the joints are just out there in nature waiting to be found.

I know the usual moves: jade turned out to be two different minerals, isotopes blur the edges of a single element, and biological categories like 'tree' or even 'species' are messy. But those seem like complications at the margins, or about folk categories rather than hard science. At best they show that not every category is a natural one. They don't seem to threaten the central case — the chemical elements. So what are philosophers actually doubting? Are they using 'natural kind' in some much stronger sense than I think? And is chemistry really as decisive as I'm assuming, or do the messy cases infect even the elements?

I'm not after a reading list or a historical survey. I want to know where my reasoning goes wrong, if it does, and what the live options actually are once the confusion is cleared up.
