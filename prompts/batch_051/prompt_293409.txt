In a meeting of my congregation's worship committee, someone proposed adding a brief instrumental prelude to the service. The proposal was dismissed with the line that the regulative principle settles the question, since in worship whatever is not commanded is forbidden. When I asked whether that reasoning would also forbid pews, a printed order of service, and meeting at ten o'clock, I was told that those are circumstances rather than elements of worship. By the end of the discussion I was convinced that one compact slogan was doing several different jobs at once, and I want to be able to say exactly what it does and does not commit us to before the committee meets again.

Please lay out, as a menu I could take to that meeting, the distinct precise principles that the slogan whatever is not commanded is forbidden might be expressing. For each reading, specify three things: what exactly it permits and rules out in corporate worship; whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, or plainly false; and whether it actually supports excluding the instrumental prelude while still allowing pews, a fixed order of service, and vernacular singing. The menu should separate at least the following dimensions, since I suspect they are what keeps getting conflated: the scope of the principle, as between corporate worship only, the whole of the Christian life, and the conscience of individual believers; the sense of commanded, as between expressly commanded, required by good and necessary consequence, established by approved biblical example, and merely needed as a circumstance for carrying out a commanded element; and the force of forbidden, as between sinful for anyone to do, improper for inclusion in a service, and merely not something the congregation may impose. Finally, identify which reading is doing the real argumentative work when someone uses the slogan to end a debate about a new practice, and state what would have to be true for that reading to be a serious theological claim rather than an all-purpose conversation-stopper.
