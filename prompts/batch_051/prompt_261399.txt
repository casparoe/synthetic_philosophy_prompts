I'm giving a department colloquium next month and I want a Q&A prep sheet — not another defense of the talk, I've got the talk. The thesis I'm defending is that Hempel's ravens paradox should be dissolved by keeping the Nicod criterion (a generalization is confirmed by its positive instances) and rejecting the equivalence condition, the principle that logically equivalent hypotheses are confirmed by exactly the same evidence. My line is that confirmation is hyperintensional: 'all ravens are black' and 'all non-black things are non-ravens' are truth-conditionally the same, but they organize the world into different instance-classes, and evidence bears on a hypothesis as it's framed, not on its bare truth conditions. So a white shoe really does confirm the contraposed hypothesis but not the original, and the paradox shows contraposition changes confirmation-relevant content.

The audience: a fairly analytic department, mostly epistemologists and philosophers of language. Two of them are hardcore Bayesians who think confirmation just is probability raising and will treat rejecting equivalence as close to apostasy. There's a senior person who works on Goodman's new riddle and thinks the ravens thing is just grue wearing a nicer coat; a logic/conditionals person who'll worry that hyperintensional confirmation smuggles psychology into epistemology; and a couple of people sympathetic to Hempel but unsure the Nicod criterion is worth saving.

Give me the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this group, not the objections I'd get from someone who'd read my dissertation. Order them by roughly how likely and how damaging they are, most damaging first when those conflict. Phrase each objection in the voice of the person who'd raise it — paraphrase their likely wording, dont put invented verbatim quotes from any real paper in quote marks — and for each give a two-minute reply plus an honest note on where that reply is soft. End with the question I'm hoping nobody asks.

Format: lead with a one-sentence bottom line up front, telling me what I should concede early and what I have to hold no matter what. Then do the objections as a table with columns for who's asking, the objection, the reply, and the weak spot. Keep the whole thing under 900 words; if you're over, cut the replies before you cut objections or weak spots.
