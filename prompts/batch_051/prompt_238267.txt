so i just went back and forth with a friend for like an hour about whether a legal system can amend its own amendment rules without falling into paradox, and he kept using Nomic as his trump card. in case you don't know it, Nomic is that game where changing the rules is itself a move governed by the rules, and the rules even let you change the rule that says how rules get changed, as long as you follow the current one while you do it. his analogy was: a legal system is basically Nomic scaled up — the constitution is the initial rulebook, the legislature is a player making a move, the courts are the table checking whether a move was legal, and the amendment clause is just a rule about rules. and since Nomic chugs along fine, he says self-amendment can't be the deep puzzle people make it out to be; a constitution can legitimately rewrite, or even replace, the very clause that authorizes rewriting, and nothing outside the system has to bless the change.

i left the arguement convinced the analogy is doing a lot of quiet work, but i couldn't make the disanalogies bite. can you audit it? i want the explicit map (a table of correspondences is fine) — what plays the role of the rulebook, the players, a move, a win condition, the table, and so on in the legal version — and then the places it breaks. i especially want to know which features of a game it smuggles into law without argument (players choosing to sit down? a win condition? no real-world stakes until we impose them? everyone accepting the rulebook?), and which disanalogies actually matter for his conclusion versus surface ones. the actual question is wether his conclusion survives the disanalogies: is self-amendment really no big deal, or was the game analogy carrying the whole argument? and if the game analogy is bad here, tell me what a better one would be. at the end, say how confident you are in the verdict and single out the point you're least sure about.
