The old argument I have in mind is the one behind inverse reinforcement learning and revealed-preference-style alignment work: if you watch an agent choose actions across enough different situations, you can infer a lot about its objective, because the observed behavior is the trace of an objective being optimized. The premise doing the heavy lifting is that the system really is an optimizer with a mostly stable utility function and some fixed-ish space of actions or plans it searches over.

The development that's been bugging me: current frontier systems (and plausibly the ones that matter later) are LLM-based agents. They weren't trained on a task-specific reward; they were pretrained to predict text, then prompted. The objective seems to get assembled on the fly out of the prompt and the training distribution, it can be incoherent, it can flip if you edit a few words of the prompt, and the same weights will happily roleplay agents with completely different goals. It looks less like one optimizer with a utility function and more like a simulator sampling text in which goal-directed behavior appears. The model even writes it's own new instructions mid-episode sometimes.
