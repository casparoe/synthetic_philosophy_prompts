So I just got back from a long argument with a friend about whether the American South could ever "do a Germany" about the Confederacy — the monuments, the school textbooks, the whole Lost Cause thing — and whether Japan and Turkey are the clearest cases of countries that never did. He kept contrasting Germany's Holocaust memorials and school trips with Japan's vague prime-minister apologies and Turkey flat-out refusing to call the Armenian genocide a genocide. Fair enough, but here's the thing I couldn't stop noticing, in his argument and in lots of these debates: the past gets treated like a debt you can settle. Once a country has done enough official memory work — the museum, the apology, the law against denial, the chancellor at the right memorial — people start talking about it like it's past has been paid off. The country moves into the "confronted its past" column, and from then on it's basically granted a license to be the moral authority. What it's doing right now stops being the thing people actually check. A country that skipped the ritual, meanwhile, gets treated like its past is still radioactive, and almost nothing it does in the present can make up for it. The argument always comes down to who did the homework, not what the homework was supposed to change. My friend literally said Germany had "earned the right" to lecture Japan, and when I asked what exactly that reckoning changed in Germany's present behavior, he looked at me like I was cheating.

Is there a name for that setup, where facing up to a historical atrocity becomes a kind of moral get-out-of-jail-free card that entitles a country to judge everyone else? Or am I lumping together two or three different things? If there's no term, what would you call it, and where's the line between that and the ordinary, decent idea that a country should honestly face what it did?
