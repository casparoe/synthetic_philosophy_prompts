So I've been reading about Abelard's ethics — the view that the whole moral weight of a thing is in consenting to it, not in the outward act, so that if you fully consent to a sin but the act never actually happens, you're as guilty as if it had. I've been trying to figure out whether I actually believe that by testing it against my gut on some cases, and my gut keeps coming out all over the place.

Take this: a man decides to kill his business partner for the insurance payout. He sneaks into the office and swaps the partner's heart medication for what he is absolutely certain is a lethal dose of poison. Unbeknownst to him, a coworker had already switched the bottle as a prank and it's just sugar pills. The partner is fine. My gut says the man is fully a murderer, even though nothing happened. No hesitation. But then: a driver who blows through a red light while texting and kills a pedestrian, versus another driver who does the exact same thing but gets lucky because no one was crossing. My gut wants to punish the first one way more, even though the only difference is luck about whether someone happened to be there. So now I've got one case where the act didn't happen but I still blame fully, and another where the act happening or not seems to change everything.

Then there's this: a husband has decided to cheat on his wife, books the hotel, drives over, and the women texts to cancel when he's already in the parking lot. I want to say he's already done something wrong, but is it really as bad as if they'd gone through with it? I flip-flop on that. And the case that really messes me up: a caregiver who, in a moment of rage, fully consents in her head to smothering her elderly patient, even imagines doing it, but then doesn't — not because she changes her mind, but because she's afraid of getting caught. Is she guilty of murder "in her soul" the way the first guy is? And what about the reverse: someone who causes terrible harm with zero prior consent or intent, like a surgeon who follows every protocol and the patient dies from a freak reaction. I don't want to blame the surgeon at all.

Is there a single principle about where the wrong lives — in the consent, in the act, in the harm, or in what you knowingly risk — that gets all of these, or at least tells me clearly which of my verdicts I should give up? And what would that principle say about the case I keep going back and forth on: someone who genuinely consents to a wrong while drunk, knowing from past experience that he'll agree to things he'd refuse sober, and then the act happens?
