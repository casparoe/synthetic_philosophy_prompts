In the course of a recent reading of Hegel, I found myself stuck on the exact role of labor in the master–slave dialectic. In J. B. Baillie's translation of The Phenomenology of Mind, Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel writes, in the section on lordship and bondage:

The repressed and subordinate type of consciousness, on the other hand, becomes, in the formative activity of work, an object to itself, in the sense that the form, given to the thing when shaped and moulded, is his object; he sees in the master, at the same time, self-existence as a real mode of consciousness.

I would like a precise explanation of the conceptual issue at stake here. Hegel seems to argue that the bondsman achieves an objective, durable self-consciousness through forming things in labor, whereas the master remains caught in an immediate relation to desire and consumption. Please explain how the argument is supposed to work. Then, drawing on your understanding of the recognition-theoretic framework developed earlier in the same section, assess whether the bondsman's self-formation through work is genuinely independent of the recognition relation, or whether it covertly presupposes the master's prior acknowledgment. I am a retired natural scientist reading philosophy slowly, and I would prefer an analysis of the argument's joints to a paraphrase of its conclusion.
