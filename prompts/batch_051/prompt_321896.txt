I have come to hold the view that James Madison's equality argument in the Memorial and Remonstrance against Religious Assessments (1785) gives the correct criterion in disputes about establishment: a state may not tax citizens for the support of religion, nor grant special legal standing to particular religious communities, because both violate citizens' equal religious freedom. The passage I have in mind runs as follows:

> Because the bill violates that equality which ought to be the basis of every law, and which is more indispensible, in proportion as the validity or expediency of any law is more liable to be impeached. ... Above all are they to be considered as retaining an "equal title to the free exercise of Religion according to the dictates of Conscience." Whilst we assert for ourselves a freedom to embrace, to profess, and to observe, the religion which we believe to be of divine origin, we cannot deny an equal freedom to them whose minds have not yet yielded to the evidence which has convinced us. ... As the bill violates equality by subjecting some to peculiar burdens, so it violates the same principle by granting to others peculiar exemptions.

I take this to be a fully general principle rather than a judgment about one bill: established churches and church taxes are, as such, incompatible with equal religious freedom, however benign the regime in practice. But England has an established church and is routinely counted a liberal regime for religious freedom; Germany collects the Kirchensteuer and ranks well on religious-freedom assessments; France's laïcité and America's disestablishment generate their own standing grievances. What would count against my view? I want the counterevidence and arguments mapped from fatal to merely awkward, with an honest account of which objections a committed Madisonian would concede as counting against the principle and which they would dismiss as local failures — and, at the end, a judgment of whether the principle can be qualified enough to survive England, Germany, France, and the United States without becoming vacuous, and whether that insulation from refutation is a problem for a principle of this kind.
