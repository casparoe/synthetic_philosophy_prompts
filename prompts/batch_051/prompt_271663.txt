I work for a small nonpartisan city charter comission staff. We get asked maybe three or four times a year by municipalities whether to switch from first-past-the-post to some form of proportional representation, or sometimes whether to oppose a ballot measure that would switch them. The decisions are high-stakes: once a city changes its electorial system it's very hard to undo, and both the city council and the voters have to sign off. We have no in-house philosopher. I need something reusable, not a one-off memo.

The argument I keep trying to make operational is Mill's in Considerations on Representative Government, chapter VII. His claim there is that a genuinely equal democracy would give every section of opinion a share of representatives proportionate to its numbers—so a minority of electors would have a minority of representatives—and that if that doesn't happen you don't have equal government, you have a government of inequality and privilege where one part of the people rules over the rest. I take that as the core argument for PR, but I know it's not the whole debate, and I don't want to just apply it mechanically.

You are an expert political philosopher and electoral systems consultant. Start by brainstorming the main values at stake and the ways Mill's principle can conflict with other values. Then think step by step.

Please write a reusable decision procedure—a checklist or flowchart in words—that our staff can run on a proposal to switch a given municipality from FPTP to PR, or to block such a switch. The procedure should have questions in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each step. It should say where the procedure stops with a no, where it says "this is a values call, not a procedural one," and where it should refer the decision to the city council rather than to us. It has to be usable by a policy analyst with no philosophy background, and it has to be runnable in about two weeks of desk research, no new data collection.

The procedure should not just assume Mill was right. It should explicitly handle the standard objections: the value of a single directly accountable local member; the risk that party-list systems give too much power to party machines; the worry that some PR systems generate permanent coalition haggling and instability; and the difference between a minority that is geographically concentrated and one that is dispersed. Also, say where Mill's argument is silent, or where his principle pushes one way but another value might push another.

Then run the procedure on three hypothetical cases. One: a city with one large dominant party and a 30% minority party that is evenly spread across wards. Two: a city with two parties and a long tradition of single-member district constituent service, where a ballot measure would replace that with closed-list PR. Three: a city with three large ethnic and linguistic communities, two geographically concentrated and one dispersed, considering open-list PR. At least one of the three should produce an answer the asker will not like, whatever the procedure says, and you should say so plainly.

Finally, please make the last line of your answer a one-sentence summary of the procedure's bottom line, prefixed exactly with "Summary:".
