Please role-play a committed defender of expected-value reasoning about everyday mortality risk. Your position is that a small chance of death should be priced and traded off against ordinary benefits exactly like any other cost — a micromort is a one-in-a-million chance of death — and that if an activity is worth its actuarial risk in micromorts, that settles whether to do it; if two options carry the same probability of the same outcomes, any preference between them is an inconsistency to be explained away, not respected. Stay in character until I say "stop." You may concede that people are poor at judging small probabilities, and that consent, control, and the social meaning of a risk can bear on whether it is just to impose risk on others; but do not concede the core claim that, for a person deciding what to do, one micromort counts the same whether it arrives through a familiar background activity or an unfamiliar explicit gamble. Be sharp and concrete; do not make me supply the good arguments. Keep each reply to one substantial paragraph.

Here is my opening. Stipulate that a three-hundred-mile drive you are considering carries exactly one micromort, and that the drive itself is pure cost — a tiring chore you accept only to reach an old friend's dinner. Set aside nonfatal injuries and every other difference: in both the drive and the machine I am about to describe, the only possible outcome is that you arrive and have the same dinner or die instantly and painlessly. Now imagine a well-tested public teleport machine that would bring you to your friend's door instantly; pressing its button kills you with probability one in a million and otherwise delivers you safely. The risk is stipulated, not estimated, and the machine is as trustworthy as the car. I suspect you would still feel a strong reluctance to press the button, and that you might take the drive instead. But the machine offers the same dinner and the same one-in-a-million chance of death while saving you the chore. By your own standard, refusing the button while taking the drive is an inconsistency, and pressing the button should be not merely permitted but preferred. So tell me: is the reluctance a bias your theory must condemn, so that a rational person should press the button and regard the drive as the worse option, or is there a difference between the drive and the button that your standard can count as a reason rather than a psychological cause? If you claim a difference, state it precisely — and note that appeals to familiarity, salience, dread, or a subjective feeling of control count only if you can say why they are reasons rather than descriptions of the reluctance itself. Then, without breaking character, end with one sentence naming the strongest objection to your reply, the consideration that would most likely force you to revise it. Write in American English.
