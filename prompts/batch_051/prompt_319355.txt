I've just been landed with my first batch of first-year criminal law scripts to mark, which has come at an odd time, becuase last month my brother was given a fixed penalty notice for busking in the town centre under a council bylaw that makes it an offence to behave in a way that annoys other people. He was just playing a ukelele near the fountain, someone complained, and the warden told him the whole thing was a matter of judgement. Since then I've been reading around the void-for-vagueness doctrine, so marking an exam answer on roughly that point feels oddly personal.

The question the student answered was: Dunton council has introduced a bylaw making it an offence, punishable by a fine of up to £1,000, to annoy other users of a public park or to behave in an inappropriate manner there. Mabel is charged after spending two hours photographing wildflowers beside the children's play area; no member of the public objected, but the park warden says he found her lingering annoying. Assess whether the bylaw is void for vagueness, explaining what the doctrine requires.

The student's full answer was:

Void for vagueness holds that a penal law is invalid if it fails to give fair warning of what it forbids or if it authorises arbitrary enforcement. The first limb asks whether an ordinary member of the public can understand from the text which actions are criminal. The second limb reflects the rule of law, because the law must not leave it to a police officer or judge to make up the offence as they go along. On these facts, the bylaw fails both limbs. Annoy is ambiguous, because different people are annoyed by different things, and where a penal provision is ambiguous it must be construed strictly in favour of the accused, so this ambiguity alone makes the bylaw void. The bylaw is also overbroad: it catches Mabel's quiet wildflower photography even though nobody actually complained, and a law that criminalises conduct it should not criminalise is precisely the defect the vagueness doctrine is meant to cure. Finally, the bylaw gives the warden unfettered discretion to decide what counts as inappropriate, since no objective standard guides him. The bylaw is therefore void for vagueness and the charge should be dismissed.
