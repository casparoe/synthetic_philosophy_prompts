I work as a software engineer on the safety training pipeline for a large language model, and I've been trying to get clear on what our current approach implicitly assumes about ethics. In practice, we have human annotators compare pairs of model outputs and mark which one is more helpful or less harmful; we train a reward model on those comparisons to emit a single scalar score; and then we fine-tune the language model to maximize that score, with a penalty term that keeps it from drifting too far from the original behavior. Some of our colleagues also maintain a fixed list of written principles and use model-generated feedback to score outputs against those rules, but even that gets collapsed into the same scalar reward signal.

Looking at this from outside philosophy, it seems to me that we are committing ourselves to the idea that moral quality is something you can learn as a real-valued function from aggregated human preferences, that helpfulness and harmlessness sit on a common scale and can be traded off, and that disagreement among annotators is essentially noise around some underlying value we're trying to estimate. Is that an unavoidable engineering simplification, or are we quietly building a particular ethical theory into the system? And if someone held that some moral requirements are absolute constraints that cannot be traded off against usefulness, or that ethical behavior is a matter of character and judgment rather than assigning scores to individual outputs, what would the training setup actually have to look like instead? I'm less interested in labels for views and more interested in knowing which parts of the pipeline strictly presuppose a controversial position, which parts are compatible with several positions, and what a redesign would force us to give up.
