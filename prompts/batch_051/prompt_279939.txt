For the last year I have been writing a novel about a foreigner living in a city whose small customs he misreads throughout: he jaywalks at the wrong crossings, fails to tip where tipping is expected, queues in the wrong direction or not at all. In working on the book, I developed something I call the second-order promise account of convention, and I would like an honest assessment of it.

The account begins from an observation about what happens when someone joins a queue. Joining a queue is not merely adopting a strategy for being served. It is placing oneself inside a practice that other people are actively maintaining, with expectations they have built around one's presence. I want to say that a convention has two layers. The first layer is the particular rule: queue here rather than there, tip fifteen percent or leave the change, wait for the light. The second layer is an undertaking one enters into simply by participating: not a promise to obey any specific rule, but a promise to treat as binding whichever rules the other participants are treating as binding while one is among them.

On this account, a tourist who has never heard of a local tipping custom is nonetheless obligated to comply with it once she sits down in a restaurant. She is obligated not because tipping is independently justified and not because she consented to that particular rule, but because entering the practice was itself a promissory act of a second-order kind. The same holds, less comfortably, for the local: once she is standing in the queue, she cannot quietly redefine its rules for herself, because her earlier act of joining promised deference to the practice as she found it.

Three features of the account seem to me to give it advantages. First, it explains why the obligation varies from place to place without collapsing into relativism: failing to tip where tipping is practiced has the structure of a broken promise, not the structure of a mistaken moral calculation. Second, it explains an asymmetry I think we actually feel: it is worse to jump a queue than to avoid the queue altogether, even when neither act much affects when others are served, because the person who joins has activated a second-order promise while the person who stays away has promised nothing. Third, it gives visitors a determinate though limited duty: learn the local conventions that others are actively relying on and follow them, while remaining free to ignore conventions whose breach would not turn anyone else's compliance into exploitation.

I am aware the idea sounds close to tacit consent and to the principle of fair play, and I suspect it may do its work only by smuggling in a moralized notion of participation. What I want to know is this: does the account already exist under another name, and if so, what distinguishes my version, if anything? What does it get right that those neighboring accounts miss? And what are the three strongest objections to it? In particular, I want to know whether any objection shows that the second-order promise is doing no explanatory work, or that the account binds people to conventions they should be free to reject.
