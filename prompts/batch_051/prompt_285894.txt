I know Rawls had a lot to say about religion and liberalism: he argued that coercive laws need to be justifiable in terms that reasonable citizens can accept, that reasonable religious citizens can participate in politics from their comprehensive doctrines, but that they should have some readiness to give public reasons too. There is also his proviso idea, that religious reasons can appear in public debate as long as they are eventually backed by properly public reasons. But he mostly thinks about citizens and society, not about firms.

So I can't figure out how he would come down on a claim by a business owner that serving a particular customer or including a particular item in a health plan would make them complicit in sin. Could you reason through what his likely position would be if he were alive today? Specifically:

(2) How would the proviso apply in a commercial setting, where the "debate" is not really about legislation but about whether a law can be enforcable against this owner?

(3) Would he think a liberal state may legimately require businesses to serve everyone on equal terms, even when that burdens some religious comprehensive doctrines?

I want you to seriously consider both directions: the reading on which Rawls would side with the religious owner, and the reading on which he would side with the law.
