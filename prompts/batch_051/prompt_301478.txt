I've taught philosophy of biology long enough to watch the adaptationism debate go through several cycles, and I'm struck by how static it has become. On one side, you have people working in the adaptationist programme: they treat natural selection as the main or default explanation for most traits, they model organisms as if each trait is a solution to a selective problem, and when they see a puzzling feature they start by asking what it's for. On the other side, since Gould and Lewontin's spandrels paper, the critics say this atomizes organisms into traits, ignores developmental constraints, allometry, pleiotropy, drift, and treats by-products or co-opted structures as if they were directly selected. Everyone now agrees that natural selection is real, that constraints exist, that not every trait is an adaptation, and yet the same arguments get recycled and nobody moves. My working hypothesis is that the real disagreement isn't empirical at all: it's about what you're allowed to treat as the null hypothesis when you encounter a trait. Adaptationists think the null is something like "no adaptive story yet found, so keep looking"; pluralists think the null is "no demonstration that selection produced this trait, so don't assume it did." But maybe I'm wrong and there's a hidden factual or formal question lurking underneath, or the two groups are really answering different questions about what an evolutionary explanation has to do. Diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is—verbal, methodological, about standards of evidence, about which cases count as data, or about starting intuitions that can't be argued for—and tell me what, if anything, would count as progress. I'm not asking for a survey of the literature, just a diagnosis of the structure of the impasse.
