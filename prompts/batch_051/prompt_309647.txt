I am a third-year undergraduate taking a formal epistemology course, and next week I have to lead a one-hour revision session for classmates who can apply Bayes' theorem but treat the claim that priors wash out as a slogan rather than as a result with conditions. I want the session to make the washing-out claim precise enough that students leave knowing both what it takes for it to happen and when it fails.

Plan the session rather than write it out, and commit to one definite order of presentation with reasons; I do not want a menu of possible approaches. In particular, please address the following.

First, the sequence of ideas. How should I build from a finite set of hypotheses and repeated, conditionally independent observations to the idea that the likelihood term compounds and gradually overwhelms the prior? At what point must I distinguish two different things: a single agent's posterior concentrating on the hypothesis that fits the data best, and two agents who begin with different priors coming to agree in their posterior probabilities? Where do the conditions that the true hypothesis is among those under consideration and that it starts with positive probability need to be introduced?

Second, the carrying example. I have in mind a biased coin with a small finite set of candidate biases, but I am open to alternatives. Whatever example I choose, what structural features must it have to do the work, and what features would confuse the point?

Third, the misconceptions. I expect the class to slide into claims such as convergence is guaranteed so priors do not matter, two rational agents must eventually agree no matter what, more data always reduces disagreement monotonically, and the prior washes out because the likelihood is the objective part of the update. Which of these should I head off explicitly, and at which point in the session should each be defused rather than saved for the end?

Fourth, what to omit. At this level I would rather keep the session finite and conceptual, avoiding continuous parameter spaces and the machinery of general convergence theorems. What else would you deliberately leave out, and does omitting infinite hypothesis spaces risk leaving students with a false picture?

I want a definite teaching sequence and a definite example, defended. End with the one check for understanding you would use to verify that students can state a condition under which priors wash out and describe a case in which they do not.
