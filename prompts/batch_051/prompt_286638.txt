In a graduate seminar on the concept of rights, I recently found I could not bring the room to agreement about what Bentham actually meant when he dismissed natural rights as "nonsense upon stilts." The line was introduced by a student arguing that a constitution which declares a right to housing but creates no remedy, no identifiable duty-bearer, and no mechanism of enforcement does not really declare a right at all; for that student, Bentham's slogan served to collapse the distinction between a right and a legally enforceable claim and to reclassify unenforced constitutional guarantees as rhetoric rather than law. The discussion then divided: some in the room took Bentham to be making a purely terminological point (a right is by definition a creature of positive law, so "natural right" is a category mistake), others took him to be pursuing a semantic-eliminativist point (the phrase fails to refer, and claims about natural rights are not false but meaningless), still others took the operative word to be "imprescriptible," reading the slogan as an attack not on rights-language generally but on the idea that some rights are placed beyond every future legal reconsideration even where utility would counsel overriding them, and one participant proposed a debunking reading, on which the epithet "natural" merely dresses a preference in the costume of an entitlement and "stilts" names that rhetorical elevation.

I would like you to disambiguate the slogan rather than defend one reading. Please set out the distinct precise theses that the sentence "natural rights are nonsense upon stilts" could be used to assert, with the readings above included wherever you find them genuine, and any further reading you detect. For each, state the claim sharply enough that one can evaluate it; say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or false; and identify which reading or readings would actually underwrite the student's argument that a constitutionally declared but unenforced right is no right at all. I would also like you to flag which readings are commonly run together but should be kept apart, and to distinguish, so far as your knowledge of Bentham permits, the reading Bentham's own text most naturally supports from the readings the slogan is nowadays conscripted to serve. You need not supply citations; I am interested in the analytical lay of the land.
