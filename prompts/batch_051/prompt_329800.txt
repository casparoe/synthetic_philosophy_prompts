I am taking an upper-level cognitive science course on analogy and concept formation, and we have been studying the proposal that analogical mapping is not one reasoning skill among others but the most basic mechanism by which concepts are formed, compared, and extended. A familiar practice has started to look theoretically loaded to me: the verbal analogy item used in standardized reasoning examinations.

The format is strikingly rigid. An item presents a pair such as hammer : nail and asks the examinee to select, from a fixed set of options, the pair that stands in the same relation; the answer key designates exactly one option as correct, the rest as wrong, and the resulting count of correct selections is reported as a measure of analogical ability. Yet for a pair like hammer : nail, relations multiply quickly: saw : board shares the relation tool-for-working-a-material, screwdriver : screw shares tool-for-driving-a-fastener, drill : wall shares tool-applied-to-a-surface, and so on. The test suppresses this fluidity by crediting a single relation as the right one.

What theory of analogy-making does this testing practice presuppose, as opposed to what it merely assumes for convenience? Specifically, does it strictly presuppose that an analogy has an objective, context-independent structure with a determinate best mapping, and that analogical competence decomposes into discrete items each with one correct answer? Or could the practice survive if analogy is instead fluid, context-sensitive, and open to multiple defensible construals? If the latter view is right, is the test an innocent idealization for scoring purposes, or does it quietly build in a false picture of cognition that its reported scores inherit? Where is the practice genuinely committed to a particular theory, and where is it underdetermined between rival accounts?
