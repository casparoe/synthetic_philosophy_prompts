I serve on a city commission charged with recommending what to do about a monument to a nineteenth-century local political figure that currently stands in front of our county courthouse. The options before us are to leave it in place as it is, to leave it but add interpretive material, or to move it to the historical society's museum. This stopped being an abstract issue for me at our first hearing, when several residents argued that moving or removing the monument would be erasing history. The phrase that seemed to settle the room was simply the one-liner "you can't erase history." I believe the speaker intended it as a complete argument for leaving the statue alone, and it was received that way.

I am not asking whether the conclusion is right. I am asking what the slogan is actually asserting. It seems to me it could mean very different things: that past facts cannot be retroactively changed; that a monument is itself a surviving trace whose loss would damage the historical record; that people will think less about the figure or the events if the statue is no longer in front of them; that removing it expresses or encourages a community's desire to avoid confronting its own past; or that a community's choices about what to honor are themselves part of its history. There may be others I have not managed to articulate.

I want a menu of the distinct precise claims this slogan could be making, each in its strongest form. For each reading, tell me whether it is true, false, or an empirical question; whether it is the sort of thing people who repeat the slogan usually mean; and, most important for my commission's work, whether it actually supports the conclusion that the monument should stay where it is. Please do not settle for explaining the reading you find most plausible and treating it as the answer; I need the full spread so I can show my fellow commissioners which claims are doing the work and which are only being gestured at.

Finally, please say how confident you are in what you claim and single out the point you are least sure of.
