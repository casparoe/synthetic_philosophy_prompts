Ok so I've been turning the inheritance tax debate over in my head for a while and I think both sides are mesuring the wrong thing. Everybody argues about weather inheritance should be taxed at all, or at what rate, but they always fixate on the dollar amount of the estate or the check the heir receives. My suspicion is that for equal opportunity the amount doesn't matter nearly as much as the gap. If every kid in a country got the same $200,000 from their parents, that would be a huge inheritance but it wouldn't create any unfair head start, because everyone's starting line moved by the same amount. But if everyone else gets nothing and one kid gets $50,000, that smaller amount does more damage to equal opportunity than the uniform $200,000. So here's the idea I've been calling the handicap principle: tax an inheritance according to how far it pushes the heir above the middle of the pack for their age group, not according to its raw size. At or below the middle: little or no tax. Just above it: modest tax. Way above it: very steep. A family farm could pass down basically untaxed as long as it doesn't actually put the kid ahead of most other kids. A huge trust fund that buys a giant lead gets taxed hard. It's like a golf handicap for dynastic wealth. I'm not an academic, and I honestly don't know whether someone already tried this under some name I've never heard of. So give it to me straight: is the principle coherent, or does it collapse once you try to define the middle, handle non-money inheritances, or compare people at different ages? Is there a name for this in the stuff people usually cite? And what is the strongest single objection, the kind that's not just a practical headache but something that might make the whole idea wrong even in principle? Tell me whether it's fatal or just uncomfortable.
