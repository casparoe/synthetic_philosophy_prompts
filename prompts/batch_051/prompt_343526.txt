I'm an applied statistician who works mostly with Bayesian model comparison and regularized prediction, and I've recently been put on a scientific review body that sometimes has to judge whether a proposed mechanism plus observational data is enough to justify acting. That has forced me to confront something I used to ignore: philosophers of science speak of inference to the best explanation as a distinctive form of nondeductive reasoning that licenses belief in a hypothesis because it would, if true, best explain the evidence. Some of that sounds like model selection under another name, and some of it doesn't. Please restate the central commitments and fault lines of inference to the best explanation — that explanatory virtues such as simplicity, unification, scope, consilience, and mechanistic plausibility carry evidential weight; that explanations are underdetermined by the data; that there is a debated gap between what makes an explanation good and what makes it likely to be true; and that such reasoning may be a basic nondeductive rule rather than a special case of probabilistic inference — in the vocabulary I actually use: likelihoods, priors, Bayes factors, predictive accuracy, information criteria, regularization, and the bias–variance tradeoff. For each commitment, say what translates cleanly, what translates only with distortion, and what has no expression in that vocabulary at all. Then tell me whether the residue is a defect of my statistical vocabulary or a sign that the philosophical claim was less precise than it looked. Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people working on inference to the best explanation, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
