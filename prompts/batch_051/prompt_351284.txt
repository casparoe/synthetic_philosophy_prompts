I am a high school student, and I have been trying to understand how nineteenth-century debates about industrial machinery anticipated present-day arguments about transformative AI. In that context I read Samuel Butler's 1863 letter "Darwin among the Machines," written to the editor of The Press in Christchurch, New Zealand. One sentence unsettles me because Butler insists he is not speaking figuratively:

“We have used the words ‘mechanical life,’ ‘the mechanical kingdom,’ ‘the mechanical world’ and so forth, and we have done so advisedly, for as the vegetable kingdom was slowly developed from the mineral, and as in like manner the animal supervened upon the vegetable, so now in these last few ages an entirely new kingdom has sprung up, of which we as yet have only seen what will one day be considered the antediluvian prototypes of the race.”

I tried to reconstruct Butler's argument in numbered form so I could check it step by step. Here is what I have:

(1) A new kingdom of life can emerge gradually from an older one: on Butler's picture, the vegetable kingdom was slowly developed from the mineral, and the animal kingdom in turn supervened upon the vegetable.

(2) What makes something a new kingdom in this sense is not what it is made of, but how it develops — namely, by accumulating small improvements that are preserved because they fit the conditions they face.

(3) The mechanical kingdom develops in exactly this way: from the lever, the wedge, the screw, and the pulley up through clocks to the machinery of Butler's day, machines have accumulated useful improvements, and subservience to human use has performed much the same selective office that natural selection performs among organisms.

(4) Therefore machines form a new kingdom in the same literal sense as the vegetable and animal kingdoms.

(5) And since this mechanical kingdom is developing far faster than the vegetable and animal kingdoms did, it is on track to become humanity's successor in the supremacy of the earth.

Is this reconstruction fair to Butler? Is the argument valid, and if not, which is the first step that fails, and exactly how does it fail? My suspicion is that "kingdom" and "developed from" may change meaning between Butler's organic examples and his mechanical one, but I do not want to dismiss the argument merely because its conclusion sounds strange, so I would like that suspicion checked as rigorously as every other step.
