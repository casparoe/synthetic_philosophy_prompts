I've been turning over a puzzle about hypocrisy and the standing to blame, and I find myself suspended between a thesis I find compelling and an intuition I can't bring myself to drop, with no clear sense of which one should be revised.

The thesis I'm drawn to treats blame as a kind of accusation whose aptness is settled by two things: whether the target actually committed the wrong, and whether the accusation describes that wrong accurately. On this picture, facts about the blamer—her own matching misdeeds, what she has covered up, how closely her history resembles her target's—are, strictly speaking, irrelevant to whether the blame itself is deserved. I'm attracted to it because it refuses to let a true charge be dismissed on biographical grounds, because it doesn't hand a veto over moral criticism to the morally spotless, and because it handles the corrupt official who exposes an even worse official without forcing us to call his exposure inappropriate.

But the intuition is stubborn. When a manager who has quietly protected several men accused of harassment takes it upon himself to publicly condemn a junior employee's single off-color remark, I don't merely find him insufferable or strategically clumsy. Something specifically moral seems wrong with his blame: I want to say he lacks standing. And it doesn't feel like a roundabout way of saying his accusation is false. The standard reply is that a true accusation can coexist with hypocrisy, because the latter concerns the speaker and the former concerns the target. That dissolves the problem only if my intuition was that the accusation is false—and it wasn't. If I accept the thesis above, I seem forced to file the case under "true statement, distasteful source" and move on, and filing it there feels like losing a real distinction rather than explaining it away.

So I'd like a negotiated verdict. Please present both sides at their strongest and then work through what would justify each move. To sharpen the task, these are the questions I actually need answered. First, is "lacking standing" a third normative status—distinct both from "the accusation is false" and from "the accusation is true but outweighed or impolitely delivered"—or does it reduce to one of those once we spell out what the speaker is doing? If it is a third status, where does it sit: is it a condition on the fittingness of the blame, on the right to express it, on the blamer's authority, or on something else, and do those come apart? Second, would adding such a condition be a genuine amendment of the thesis, or only a boundary the thesis was already committed to, since the thesis never said anything about who may voice the charge? Third, how should I think about the edge case where the hypocrite is both right about the target and better placed than anyone else to get the misconduct taken seriously—say, because he controls the investigation, or because a spotless critic would be ignored? I want a verdict that says to debunk the intuition, amend the thesis, or reconcile them, accompanied by an account of what would count as evidence for choosing that over the alternatives.

Please end with a final line beginning "Verdict:" followed by exactly one of "debunk-the-intuition", "amend-the-thesis", or "compatible"; if you choose compatible or amend, add at most one short clause identifying the reconciliation or the amendment.
