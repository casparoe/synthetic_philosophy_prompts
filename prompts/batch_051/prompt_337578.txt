Suppose a country replaces ordinary cost–benefit review with a "risk budget." Any new health, safety, or security regulation can only take effect if the proposing agency also repeals or relaxes an existing rule that, under the government's own standardized micromort-per-dollar accounting, is at least as cost-ineffective as the new rule is cost-effective. The offset has to come from the same broad domain — so you can't pay for laxer food inspection with better road barriers. An independent office certifies the numbers, and the public can challenge them.

Walk me through how this actually plays out. What kinds of rules get passed, and what kinds get quietly bargained away? How do agencies, industry lobbies, and politicians game the "same broad domain" constraint or the metric itself? Does it force real tradeoffs into the open, or does it just make everyone better at laundering bad math through a micromort ledger? Which failure modes are fixable by tweaking the design, and which are baked in?
