I've got a rules-interpretation problem that's turned into a bigger question about tolerance, and I'm not sure how the pieces fit. My debating society's constitution has a purpose clause, clause 1, which says the Society exists 'to provide a forum for the open discussion of all subjects, however controversial, among people of good faith.' Then clause 7 says: 'Every member shall have the right to express any opinion whatsoever in the Society's meetings and publications, and no member shall be disciplined for the content of any opinion they express.' That's the whole clause — there are no built-in exceptions.

The case: a faction has formed within the Society that isn't threatening anyone, isn't disruptive, just well organized. Their platform is that certain views — they name a set they say amount to bigotry — should no longer be permitted on the floor at all. They're running for committee positions, and their campaign literature says openly that once they have a majority they'll amend the constitution to ban those views from meetings and publications. Some members now want to discipline or at least bar them from office, on the ground that they're using the free-expression guarantee to destroy the thing it protects. Others say clause 7 is clear: it protects the content of their opinions, full stop, and their opinion happens to be that some other opinions shouldn't get a platform.

Does clause 7 apply to this? What does each interpretive approach give — the plain words, the purpose stated in clause 1, what the people who drafted it would have said if they'd been asked about exactly this situation, and what a reasonable current member reading it would take it to mean? Where do those diverge, and which should actually govern? I'm also curious wether this is just the paradox of tolerance in club-bylaw form, or whether writing the thing down as a rule with a stated purpose changes the analysis.
