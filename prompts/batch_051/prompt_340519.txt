Adam Smith's discussion of colonies in the Wealth of Nations is sometimes read as an early anti-imperialist statement. Smith condemns the British monopoly of colonial trade as a violation of the colonists' rights and a distortion of the natural distribution of capital; he also observes that the discovery of the Americas, however profitable for Europe, brought dreadful misfortune and injustice to the native inhabitants. He goes on to propose that Britain either grant the American colonies representation in a parliamentary union or voluntarily relinquish its authority over them. Other readers counter that Smith's target is the mercantile system rather than empire as such, and that his ultimate test is still whether the colonies are worth what they cost the mother country.

Write an essay on this interpretive controversy: was Adam Smith an opponent of empire, or only an opponent of mercantile restrictions on colonial trade? In addition to weighing the main arguments on both sides, distinguish three possible kinds of criticism Smith could be offering — that empire is economically disadvantageous to the metropole, that imperial policies violate natural liberty and justice, and that the original acquisition of colonies rested on historical wrongs — and assess how much anti-imperialist force each would carry on Smith's own principles. Do not end on a non-committal note; reach and justify a definite conclusion.
