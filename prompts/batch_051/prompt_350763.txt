I got into a long argument with a friend last night about whether you can wrong someone purely in your attitude, even when your actions are fine. His line was that actions are what count and that attitudes are just private mental states nobody can see; my line was that there are whole categories of wronging—condescension, being treated as invisible, having your judgment ignored—that don't reduce to any particular harmful act. He kept pushing me to name a case, and I kept reaching for novels instead of examples, which annoyed him but got me thinking: the house-elves in the Harry Potter books are basically a case study, because the wizards either treat them as furniture or are "kind" to them in the way you'd be kind to a pet, and the one person who wants to liberate them (Hermione) doesnt actually listen to what the elves themselves say they want. I'd like a careful reading of the house-elf storyline through the lens of respect and recognition—the idea that we owe each other something in attitude, not only in behavior.

Specifically: does the series show that a wrong can consist in an attitude (contempt, patronizing affection, refusal to see someone as a full agent) even when the accompanying action is harmless, or even beneficial? Where does it complicate the picture—for example, Dobby wants freedom and work, Winky is destroyed by being freed, Kreacher is awful but has a deep loyalty that Sirius refuses to honor, and Hermione's S.P.E.W. campaign seems to be about her own picture of what the elves should want rather than about them. Is the books' moral that what the elves are owed is recognition of their standing as agents with their own ends, and if so, what does that look like in practice, given that different elves want very different things? Be honest about where the narration undercuts the reading—the comedic tone of the kitchen scenes, the way Dobby's devotion to Harry is played as cute, the fact that the reader is clearly meant to find some of this funny.

After the reading, give me a brief structured summary in a table with these columns: "Scene/character" (the specific moment or elf), "Action" (what the character actually does), "Attitude" (what attitude the action conveys or fails to convey), "What the attitude/action distinction predicts" (whether the wrong is in the action, the attitude, or both), and "Where the lens strains" (a note on how the text complicates the philosophical point).
