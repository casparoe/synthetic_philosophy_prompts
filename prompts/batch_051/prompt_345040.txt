I'm drafting a short essay on Bertrand's paradox and the principle of indifference, and I'd be grateful for comments on the argument before I commit more time to it. The topic became personally relevant after I tried to implement a small Bayesian model for a side project and found myself repeatedly having to choose among formally equivalent but practically different prior distributions. The standard advice—use a uniform prior unless another distribution is forced by known constraints—turned out to be far less determinate than I expected, and I ended up down a rabbit hole about exactly what Bertrand's paradox does and does not show.

Here is the core of the essay as I currently conceive it. I argue that the familiar reading—Bertrand's paradox refutes the principle of indifference—is too quick. My claim is that the principle should be understood as licensing a uniform distribution only relative to a specified invariance group or set of symmetries, and that Bertrand's three ways of generating a random chord illustrate three distinct problems rather than one problem with three correct answers. In each variant, a different physical process picks out a different symmetry structure: one fixes an endpoint, one fixes a midpoint, one fixes an orientation. The apparent inconsistency arises because the bare wording of the problem does not specify which symmetry structure governs the experiment. So the moral is not that indifference is invalid, but that indifference is under-specified until the relevant invariances are made explicit. I also consider the objection that this simply relocates the arbitrariness from the prior to the choice of symmetry group, and I try to answer it by saying that physical generating procedures, when present, supply the symmetry group, while problems without such procedures may genuinely lack unique probabilities.

I would especially like comments on whether this is a fair reconstruction of the paradox rather than an anachronistic imposition of invariance ideas onto a nineteenth-century puzzle, whether my distinction between under-specification and arbitrariness carries its load, and whether the essay would be more interesting if I framed it as a contribution to the epistemology of probabilistic modeling rather than as another gloss on a familiar paradox. I'm not asking for a reading list; I want to know if the argument itself is internally sound, whether it says something worth saying, and where it would be most vulnerable to a sharp critic.
