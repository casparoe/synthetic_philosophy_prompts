I'm teaching a small seminar on idolatry and misplaced worship, and I want to spend two sessions reading Moby-Dick as a case study in what makes valuing something the wrong way, rather than just overvaluing something genuinely good. The book practically begs for this: Ahab treats the white whale as the face of all evil and not just an animal that bit off his leg, and the Pequod starts to look like a congregation with its own rites, symbols, and a kind of liturgy around the hunt. Write me a critical reading that takes that thought seriously but doesn't turn the novel into an allegory with a moral. I especially want you to press on the doubloon scene, where everyone reads a different meaning onto the same gold coin, and on whether the book ever lets us see Ahab as an idolater rather than just showing him that way. Where does the novel resist the obvious reading, and what does it add to the philosophical question that an essay on idolatry by itself wouldn't?
