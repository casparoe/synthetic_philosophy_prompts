I was reading about how the vocabulary of medicine and the sciences gets coined, and I noticed an asymmetry in our ordinary linguistic practice that I cannot quite justify. When a new condition, device, or substance is named, we tend to reach for Latin or Greek roots: a heart attack becomes a myocardial infarction, hair loss becomes alopecia, a fever reducer becomes an antipyretic. These words were invented by living people, usually in the last few hundred years, from languages no community speaks as a first language, and yet they carry an immediate air of being technical, precise, and neutral. By contrast, when a term is formed from the living language around us — heart attack, lockjaw, hair loss — or when a transparent native compound is proposed for something new, it tends to sound everyday, commercial, or faintly unserious, as though it belongs to conversation rather than to science. Both kinds of term are deliberate inventions, both have to be learned, and neither is more natural as a name for the thing. So the asymmetry is this: assembling a new word from a dead language confers scientific authority and apparent neutrality, while assembling an equally new word from a living language confers informality, even though for most speakers the dead-language term is the more opaque of the two.

I want to know whether this asymmetry is defensible or merely a habit we have inherited. Please work through the strongest candidate justifications on its behalf — the stability of dead languages against semantic drift, international uniformity across vernaculars, the idea that opacity can be a feature by keeping everyday associations out of technical language, and the historical fact that Latin and Greek were the languages of learned elites — and test each against nearby cases, such as scientific traditions that coin from living languages, or attempts to replace Greco-Latin terms with native compounds. In particular, consider whether the asymmetry tracks anything about the words themselves or only about the prestige of who coined them and why. End by saying whether you would keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or give it up, and on what grounds. Please write your answer in continuous prose only, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write a letter.
