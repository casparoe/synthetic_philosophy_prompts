I've been going back and forth on this after an argument at work. I do software for autonomous warehouse robots, and we had a bug where a bot would try to dock at a charging station, fail because a pallet had shifted, reverse, try again, and keep at it until it's battery died. A firmware engineer on my team called it sphexish, after the wasp that drags a cricket to its burrow, leaves it at the entrance, checks inside, and if someone moves the cricket a few inches it just starts the whole sequence over from the beginning, indefinitely, as long as you keep messing with it. I ended up going down a rabbit hole and found that some philosophers use the wasp to mark a supposedly important difference between mechanical rigidity and human free will. The position I currently hold, more or less, is that the wasp, the robot, and me are all doing the same kind of thing; humans just have a lot more layers of feedback, better internal models, and enough prefrontal machinery to simulate options before acting. So there's no extra ingredient that makes one of our decisions free in a categorical sense, and the difference is engineering, not metaphysics.

What I'd like you to do is take that position seriously and tell me what would actually count against it, not just what critics already say. If the difference between sphexish rigidity and human flexibility is more than a matter of degree and processing power, what would have to be true in the world or in the concept for that to show up? Try to separate evidence that would be decisive from evidence that would just be awkward for the deflationary view. I'm interested in whether people who hold this sort of view have commitments they would have to abandon if specific empirical or conceptual results came in, or whether they've set things up so the view can absorb anything. I'd personally find something like a clean demonstration that a person can step outside their own learned routines in a way no possible control system could, maybe in cases where the story and the behavior come apart, pretty persuasive; but I'm aware I might be overweighting that because I build control systems all day. Also, if the honest conclusion is that the position is well insulated from evidence and that's a problem, or that it's unfalsifiable but still better then the alternatives, just say so.

One thing though: I know this area has a lot of genuine disagreement. Please keep straight what's settled from what's contested among people who actually work on this, and don't present one live position as if it's the consensus. And write it as continuous prose, no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables, like an email.
