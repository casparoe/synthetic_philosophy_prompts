I am preparing a presentation I must give next month to the education and youth committee of the city council in the French city where I now live and work, and I expect real hostility. I am defending a modest municipal proposal: that school canteens provide genuinely workable alternatives for the main religious dietary restrictions, and that the city's after-school and youth programmes permit children to wear modest religious symbols while using those services. My argument will be that this is compatible with laïcité properly understood, because laïcité binds the state and its agents in the exercise of their functions; it does not require children and families using public services to leave their religious identities at the door. A state genuinely neutral between religions should not make equal access to public schooling and youth services depend on a family's willingness to set aside its dietary and dress obligations.

The committee is the audience I need to prepare for. Its members are mostly long-serving municipal officials and school principals formed in a strict separationist reading of French laïcité, together with two academics who work on republican integration. They care above all about the unity and neutrality of the public sphere, equality understood as a relation between individuals rather than groups, and preventing any slide into communautarisme. Several of them treat the 2004 ban on conspicuous religious symbols in public schools as the settled baseline and will hear my proposal as an attempt to soften it.

Please give me a question-and-answer prep sheet, not a defence of the proposal: the objections in roughly the order I am likely to hear them, phrased the way this particular audience would phrase them rather than the way Anglophone multiculturalists would, with what I should concede up front, what I should hold, where each reply is weakest, and the one question I should most hope nobody asks.
