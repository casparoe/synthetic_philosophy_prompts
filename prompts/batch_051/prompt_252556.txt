I'm giving a department works-in-progress talk next month and want a prep sheet for the Q&A, not a defense of the paper. The thesis I'm defending is this: in pluralistic liberal societies we should lean harder on shame than on guilt in moral education and public moral discourse — but only one kind of shame, what I call "standing shame," the felt loss of standing in a community whose standards you actually endorse, not the stigmatizing, humiliating kind. My line is that guilt is too act-focused: it lets a person say "I did a bad thing" while leaving their self untouched, which sounds admirable but has a moral-licensing downside and under-motivates repair because it doesn't touch who the agent is. Standing shame, by contrast, is self-focused but reparative: it says "I've fallen short of the person I take myself to be," and that's what actually moves people to restore relationships. I also argue this isn't just a preference: cultures can partly choose their moral-emotion repertoire through institutional design — restorative-justice procedures, apology norms, who gets publicly praised or blamed — so we should choose shame-with-repair over guilt.

The audience is a mixed workshop: a virtue ethicist who thinks shame is an honor-culture emotion that belongs to the ethics of esteem, not obligation; a Kantian who will say guilt respects autonomy because it targets the act while shame attacks the person; a pscyhologist/x-phi type who is going to bring the standard data that shame-proneness predicts aggression and depression while guilt-proneness predicts repair; and a critical-theory person who will say institutionalized shame is just normalization and social control, and that its weight falls hardest on already-marginalized bodies. Give me the objections roughly in the order they'll actually come, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way those people would phrase them, with what I should concede, what I should hold, and the weakest point in my own position that I need to defend rather than hope they miss. Flag the objection I'm seceretly hoping nobody asks. Then tell me which single objection would most change my mind about the thesis, and where my whole argument is most likely to fail — not where I'd nuance it, but where I'd actually have to give it up.

Format: write the prep sheet as ordinary prose, but then give me a JSON summary with this exact schema. An "objections" array, each element with: "objection" (the objection phrased in the audience member's voice), "likely_from" (which audience member), "order" (integer, 1 = first raised), "severity" (integer 1–5, 5 = fatal if unanswered), "concede" (what to grant), "hold" (what not to grant), "reply" (short response), and "weak_point" (where that reply is fragile).
