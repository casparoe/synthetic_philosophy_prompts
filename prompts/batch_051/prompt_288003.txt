I'm writing a term paper on Lewis's triviality results for indicative conditionals, and I've ben going in circles trying to find a response that isn't just one of the usual escape routes. The proof I keep coming back to is the first one from David Lewis's Probabilities of Conditionals and Conditional Probabilities (1976): assume the probability of If A then C equals the conditional probability of C given A whenever P(A) is positive, and assume that same equation keeps holding after you condition on an ordinary event like C or not-C. Then any nontrivial probability function collapses into independence. The families of reply I can classify so far are:

1. Reject the equation outright: the probability of a conditional is not its conditional probability, full stop.
2. Deny that conditionals express propositions at all, so there isn't a proposition whose probability the equation is even about.
3. Restrict the equation to simple, unembedded conditionals, so it does not apply to the conditional after conditioning on C or not-C.
4. Deny that the class of admissible probability functions is closed under ordinary conditionalization, so the proof cannot move from the prior to the conditionalized probabilities.
5. Reject enough classical Boolean logic for conditionals that the total-probability or expansion-by-cases step loses its force.

I want a sixth route. It has to be genuinely different from these five: not a restatement, not a special case, not a combination of two of them. It should block the first proof, or at least seriously soften its conclusion, and I'd like a short verdict on whether it is just a technical dodge or whether it changes how we should think about if-sentences in ordinary reasoning.
