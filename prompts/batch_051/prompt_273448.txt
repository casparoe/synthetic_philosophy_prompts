I'm a novelist, and a few weeks ago a copyeditor flagged a line in my manuscript where a character says, "I ain't never met a man who could outwork him." She changed it to "I never met a man who could outwork him," with a note that the double negative would read as an error. At dinner I told two writer friends, Sam and Dana, about it, and they immediately took opposite sides. I mostly listened, and I honestly couldn't decide who was right; now they've both asked me to settle it, and I'd rather hand the whole thing to someone else.

Sam said the copyeditor was right: lots of readers will process that sentence, even inside quotation marks, as the author's mistake rather than the character's voice, and an editor's job is to keep you from being read that way. Dana said that has it backwards. Negative concord is rule-governed in plenty of English dialects, so calling the sentence an error is just false — it's grammatical in the character's dialect, and "fixing" it is like correcting a Scottish character's "wee," which no editor would do; so it isn't really about grammar, its about which dialects get taken seriously. Sam answered that grammar rules aren't only prejudice: standard written English exists precisely so strangers can parse one another, and a published novel is a public text, not a transcript. Dana said the parsing point won't work — "two negatives make a positive" is itself a made-up Latinate rule, and the double negative doesn't make the line harder to understand — and if Sam's principle were real, it would force an editor to standardize "y'all" in Southern dialogue, but she wouldn't, which shows the rule is applied selectively against low-prestige forms. Sam said fine, standard English is a prestige dialect, but it's the prestige dialect of the market that publishes me; and there's nothing inconsistent about enforcing some dialect markers but not others, because conventions about which forms are "print-legible" are real social norms, and the editor's job is fidelity to those norms, not to descriptive facts. Dana said that concedes her point: if the copyeditor is just enforcing a market convention, then it's a marketing decision dressed up as a grammar correction, and dressing it up is itself the problem — a novelist's job is supposed to be rendering a voice faithfully, not laundering prestige bias.

Please go through their exchange point by point and say who is right about what at each step, and who is more right overall. Also, I want the part that would change your mind: which fact or consideration, if it turned out true, would flip your verdict, and where is your reasoning most likely to fail?
