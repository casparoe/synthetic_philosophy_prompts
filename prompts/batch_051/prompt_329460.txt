I'm grading a take-home essay for an intro New Testament course and I'd like a second opinion on one student's paper before I write comments. The exam question was:

"In what sense, if any, do Paul and James disagree about justification by faith? Reconstruct the apparent conflict, explain how each writer uses the key terms, and assess whether the conflict is real or merely verbal."

One student wrote the following:

"On the surface Paul and James contradict each other. Paul says a person is justified by faith apart from works of the law, while James says a person is justified by works and not by faith alone. But the contradiction disappears once you see that the two writers mean different things by the key words. Paul's works of the law refers to the boundary markers of Jewish identity—circumcision, food rules, Sabbath observance—which certain Jewish Christians wanted to impose on Gentile converts. His point is that these markers do not get you into the covenant people; trusting allegiance to Christ does. James, by works, means the ordinary good works of mercy and obedience, and by faith he means bare intellectual assent. So when he says faith without works is dead, he is correcting a misreading of Paul that had turned faith into a purely mental posture. Justification is different too: for Paul it is the initial act by which God declares a sinner righteous, whereas for James it is the visible demonstration that one's faith is genuine. Since the two are not using faith, works, or justification in the same way, there is no real disagreement between them. This harmonizing reading is now broadly accepted among historians."
