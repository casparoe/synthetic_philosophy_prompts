I am a retired evolutionary biologist, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of biology. I have come to hold a view that I would like you to test as rigorously as you can. The view is that cultural variants are literal replicators: that talk of memes is not a metaphor but a claim about a second substrate on which Darwinian evolution runs.

By a replicator I mean an entity of which copies are made, where three conditions hold. First, copying is accurate enough that offspring resemble their parents more than they resemble randomly chosen members of the population. Second, copying errors are themselves copied, so variation is heritable. Third, differences among variants affect how many further copies get made. A gene satisfies all three. My claim is that many cultural items — a story, a recipe, a boat-building technique, a musical phrase, a turn-taking norm — satisfy them as well, and that cumulative cultural change is best explained as the differential copying of these variants. I do not claim that cultural copying has the fidelity of DNA replication; I claim only that its fidelity is sufficient, relative to the strength of selection, for selection to operate. I do not require cultural variants to be particulate in the strict Mendelian sense, and I concede that cultural items merge, blend, and recombine more readily than alleles do. My position is that these are differences of degree within a common replicator framework, not differences in kind.

The rival view I keep encountering says that this is exactly where the analogy breaks down: that cultural transmission is not the copying of a variant at all, but reconstruction by a learner working from incomplete cues against a shared cognitive background — the way a child does not copy sentences but builds a grammar. On that view, what recurs is not a lineage of copied variants but a recurring attractor, a stable end-state produced independently on each occasion by similar minds in similar environments. Selection, to the extent the word applies, acts on such attractors only indirectly, and many standard results about cumulative adaptation do not carry over.

I want an honest map of what would count against my view, sorted by how decisive each item would be.

First, which empirical findings about how people actually transmit techniques, conventions, or representations would be effectively incompatible with the replicator claim, and which would be merely awkward? I have in mind findings such as evidence that imitation is systematically biased or transformative rather than faithful, that cultural items are routinely reinvented rather than inherited, or that transmission chains converge on a canonical form regardless of the starting variant.

Second, which of the three conditions I listed — resemblance, heritable error, fitness differences — is my view most exposed on, and what would the world have to look like for that condition to fail? I want failures that are live possibilities, not merely logically possible ones.

Third, have I already insulated the view into unfalsifiability by making fidelity relative to the strength of selection, by permitting blending, and by retreating to the claim that selection can act on fuzzy lineages? Are those qualifications principled, or do they leave the position at risk of nothing? I would like this answered directly, including the possibility that being insulated in this way is a serious problem for the view.

Fourth, what does the replicator view predict that the reconstruction view does not, and what observation or experiment would most cleanly separate them? If no clean observation separates them, I want to hear that said plainly, along with what follows for the claim that memes are more than a metaphor.

For my own part, I would find evidence from iterated transmission most persuasive — chains of learners copying and re-copying a variant — provided the chains are designed so that lineage copying and convergent reconstruction would actually show up differently. But I want the decisive and the merely awkward clearly separated, and I want an honest judgment about whether the position is even capable of being refuted.
