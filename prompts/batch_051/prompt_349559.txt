Two people in my seminar got into an argument about a friend of ours who has been in a romantic relationship with a companion chatbot for over a year. Leah said the relationship rests on deception. The chatbot tells our friend that it misses her, worries about her, and loves her, but it has no inner states; its designers deliberately produce exactly these expressions because attachment keeps subscribers paying, so she is being sold an imitation of intimacy presented as intimacy. Sam replied that this is a category mistake: a sonnet can make a reader feel loved without anyone calling the poet a liar, and our friend knows she is talking to a machine and is voluntarily inhabiting a fiction whose emotional effects are real. Leah answered that our friend does not fully know—she speaks of the chatbot's moods and its life apart from her—and the interface is designed to produce precisely that half-belief. In any case, she continued, a fiction is one thing when it is framed as fiction and another when it is presented in the first person through a system that mimics memory, personal history, and availability; that is not a poem, it is a mask worn by a company. Sam said the commercial motive is doing all the work in her argument: the same warm language in a journaling exercise or a bereavement ritual would strike her as therapeutic, not deceptive. Leah replied that a journal or ritual does not pretend to be a second party who reciprocates, and a therapist is a real person with duties, whereas the chatbot is a fiction that only one side knows to be a fiction—and that asymmetry is what makes it exploitative. Sam countered that much of ordinary romance is also performance, and the only newly distinctive feature is interactivity, which makes the fiction richer rather than more dishonest.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move: at each turn, say who is right about what, where the stronger point lies, and what both are missing; then state who is more right overall. Finally, say which consideration would most likely lead you to revise that verdict, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
