In my working life I did nonlinear dynamics and statistical physics, and since retiring I have been drifting into philosophy; by way of philosophy of history I began reading the historiography of the Cold War, and one thing keeps snagging my attention. The field's central old dispute — who started it? — seems to be fought in a causal vocabulary that my own training never used. I think in terms of coupled systems, feedback amplification, basins of attraction, critical slowing down, structural instability, and the difference between what a single trajectory does and what the nearby counterfactual trajectories would have done. The historians, by contrast, argue over the intentional actions of identifiable agents: the orthodox case that Soviet expansionism and ideology drove the rupture, the revisionist case that American economic and atomic diplomacy did, the post-revisionist move toward structural and mutual-security-dilemma explanations, and the later view that the question is itself misframed.

I would like you to translate the main positions in this "who started it" debate into the vocabulary I actually think in — attractors, phase transitions, feedback, perturbations, and ensembles of counterfactual trajectories — as faithfully as you can, without smuggling dynamical-systems assumptions in and calling them translation. Then give me the residue report. Which of the historians' claims map cleanly onto that vocabulary, which map only with distortion, and which — agency, blame, moral responsibility, the idea that someone could have chosen otherwise at a decisive moment — have no counterpart at all? Finally, tell me what the residue means: is it a defect of my vocabulary, or evidence that "who started it" is the wrong question, or something in between? I have a working hypothesis that "who started it" asks which variable deserves credit for a phase transition, and I want to know whether that is illuminating or reductive.
