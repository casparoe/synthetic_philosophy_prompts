In my ethics reading group we've been arguing about supererogation sense October and have gotten nowhere. The two camps make the same moves every week. One side (the friends of supererogation) says once you allow that some acts are morally good without being required, you've allowed supererogation, and that a morality without it can't make sense of people who give away far more than decency requires or take serious risks for strangers. The other side (the anti-supererogationists) says this is a contradiction in the making: if the act you skipped really was morally better than the one you did, and it was open to you, then the only honest verdict is that you ought to have done the better one; so-called supererogation is just duty plus a story we tell ourselves so we can feel fine about not doing it.

What's frustrating is how much they agree on. Both accept that standard deontic logic, with a single 'ought' operator, can at least capture the formal shell of supererogation — permitted but not required — even if it says nothing about the act's being especially good. Both agree the disagreement isn't about which acts actually are supererogatory. And both agree that a morality which made the single best available act obligatory in every choice would be absurdly demanding — the anti-supererogationists just conclude that our ordinary talk of "going beyond duty" is a face-saving myth rather than a real deontic category.

I have a hypothesis about what's going on. It seems to me the anti-supererogation side is committed to a closure principle: if you ought to do A, and B is morally better than A, and B is open to you, then you ought to do B. They never state it, but it's doing all the work when they claim that recognizing the better act just is recognizing the obligation. The pro-supererogation side denies that principle and instead treats duty as a floor or threshold: acts below it are wrong, acts above it are optional-but-good. So maybe the whole deadlock is really a disagreement about the logic of 'ought' — whether obligation is closed upward under moral betterness — and not a substantive ethical disagreement at all. Is my hypothesis right? If it's wrong, what kind of disagreement is this: a semantic one, a disagreement about how demanding a moral theory may be before we reject it on independent grounds, a difference in starting intuitions about cases, or something else? And what would count as progress once we know what kind of disagreement it is?
