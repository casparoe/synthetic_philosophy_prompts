In a psychology lesson on decision-making under uncertainty, my teacher presented the Ellsberg paradox. In the version we worked through, one urn contains 50 red balls and 50 black balls, while a second urn contains 100 balls in an unknown proportion of red to black. People tend to prefer betting on red from the first urn rather than the second, and they also tend to prefer betting on black from the first urn rather than the second—a pattern that seems inconsistent with assigning the second urn any single probability of drawing red. A classmate dismissed the puzzle with the proverb "better the devil you know than the devil you don't," as though that settled whether such choices are rational. I suspect that one sentence is actually hiding several distinct claims, not all of which would support his dismissal. Could you lay out five precise readings of the proverb in this context, ranging from a bare description of what people in fact prefer to a strong normative principle about what it is rational to choose? For each reading, say whether it is trivially true, a substantive and defensible claim, or false, and say whether it would actually establish that the Ellsberg pattern needs no further explanation. Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction or conclusion, and for each item end with the specific consideration or argument that would make you revise that verdict.
