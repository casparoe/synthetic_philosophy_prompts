I retired from a research career in the natural sciences and have recently been working through early twentieth-century philosophy of time. I am currently reading John Ellis McTaggart's "The Unreality of Time" (Mind, 1908), and a short passage has become the subject of a disagreement with a physicist friend. I would like you to audit his reading of it.

After introducing the A series and the B series, McTaggart writes:

"It is possible, however, that this is merely subjective. It may be the case that the distinction introduced among positions in time by the A series -- the distinction of past, present and future -- is simply a constant illusion of our minds, and that the real nature of time only contains the distinction of the B series --the distinction of earlier and later. In that case we could not perceive time as it really is, but we might be able to think of it as it really is."

My friend, who is more at home in this literature than I am, summarizes the passage roughly as follows. McTaggart is distinguishing two ways of ordering the same events: the A-series, which is the moving, perspective-dependent division into past, present, and future, and the B-series, which is the fixed, objective ordering of earlier and later. According to my friend, McTaggart is claiming that the A-series may be no more than a constant illusion our minds project onto the world, and that the real nature of time may consist only of B-series relations. If that is right, my friend continues, presentism—the thesis that only what is present exists—loses its foothold, since there is no mind-independent "now" for presentism to be about. The sober, scientific picture would then be eternalism: all events stand in permanent earlier-than/later-than relations. He adds that this is essentially the position modern B-theorists defend, and that McTaggart's paper deserves credit for forcing that position into prominence.

My own suspicion is that the passage is doing something more conditional than my friend allows, but I have not worked through the rest of the argument carefully enough to pin it down. Please separate what the gloss gets right from where it slides away from what McTaggart actually says. Does McTaggart assert the B-series-plus-illusion position here, or is he entertaining a possibility he goes on to reject? Does the inference from this passage to a refutation of presentism and an endorsement of eternalism survive a closer reading? Where the gloss is wrong, is the error a harmless compression or a distortion that reverses the direction of McTaggart's argument? Please answer in American English.
