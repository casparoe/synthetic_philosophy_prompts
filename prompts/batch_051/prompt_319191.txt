I'm a software engineer, not a philosopher, but over the past few years my work has pushed me into constructive logic: through type systems, a proof assistant I learned on the side, and too many bugs caused by functions that claimed to return an answer without actually being able to produce one. I've become genuinely attached to the propositions-as-types picture. A proof of a disjunction carries a tag telling you which disjunct was proved; a proof of an existence claim carries a witness; and the gap between "I know this should hold" and "this term constructs an answer" stays visible. That discipline earns its keep. I'm now close to convinced that, for this kind of reasoning, excluded middle should not be built in. For arbitrary predicates, an axiom that gives me `Either p (Not p)` would let me branch on questions for which I have no decision procedure; for halting predicates it would simply be a halting oracle, and I know no such oracle can be implemented. So the constructive restriction looks principled rather than merely conservative.

But one intuition won't sit still. Take a definite program and a definite input — say, a particular seed in a deterministic fuzzing run. That execution will either reach a termination state or it never will; it will either surface a counterexample or it never will. The run isn't unsettled just because no uniform procedure settles the halting question for every program–input pair. For this one fixed pair, the disjunction "it terminates or it does not" seems not merely assertable but forced by what the machine actually does, whether or not anyone is in a position to furnish the relevant proof. The absence of a general algorithm does not touch the particular case. So I'm left with a theory I admire telling me that a disjunction is licensed only when one side can be furnished, and an intuition I can't abandon telling me that for a single, definite execution the disjunction is true without anyone's being able to furnish either side.

I've been told these can be reconciled: constructive logic still proves excluded middle for decidable predicates, and my intuition is only a pre-formal picture of a determinate run, while the logic governs what I can legitimately extract from a program. That sounds plausible, but I can't tell whether it's a genuine reconciliation or just a relabeling of the difficulty. What I want is a negotiated verdict. What would justify giving up the intuition as a pretheoretical habit that persists only because I keep smuggling in a classical reading of "or"? What would instead justify amending or rejecting the constructive account — for example, by allowing classical reasoning at some layers of a system while reserving constructive logic for the layers where computational content matters? Or is there a precise interpretation under which both survive without either being reinterpreted into triviality? I want to know what would justify each move, not just be told which side wins.
