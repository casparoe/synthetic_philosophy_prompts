Me and a couple of friends got into an argument about whether Letterboxd and Goodreads have made the professional critic pointless, and its sent me back to Hume's essay on the standard of taste. As I understand him, the move is roughly: beauty is a matter of sentiment rather than reasoning, so taste is subjective in one clear sense; yet we constantly treat some judgements as better than others, and to make sense of that we need a standard, which Hume locates in the converging verdicts of what he calls true judges — people with a delicate palate, long practice, wide comparison, freedom from prejudice, and good sense.

The trouble is that this picture seems to presuppose a particular cultural environment, one where a person works through a canon, compares across genres and periods, and arrives at their tastes in something like independence. My friends' point is that the actual environment of taste is now algorithmic: we discover things through personalised feeds, we read and watch what the platform predicts will hold us, and the nearest thing to a shared verdict is an aggregate score from millions of mostly untrained users. I'm trying to work out whether Hume's argument survives this change, needs repairing, or is effectively dead. Which specific premise takes the main hit — the descriptive story about how true judges are formed, the claim that practice and comparison improve taste, or the assumption that a standard must be a convergence of expert judges rather than a statistical aggregate? Is there a weaker repaired version that keeps the core thought but replaces the true-judge device with something like aggregated comparison — or would that repair keep the word standard while quietly dropping the part that made it worth having? I don't want a survey of the literature; work through the argument. And please tell me how confident you are in the answer and the one point you're least sure of.
