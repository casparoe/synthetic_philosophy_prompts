I teach a third-year undergraduate seminar on philosophy of mind, and we're about to spend two sessions on Dennett's critique of the Cartesian theater and his multiple drafts alternative: the view that there is no single place in the brain and no single moment at which a perception, decision, or memory is presented to an audience of the self, but rather many parallel, partial drafts of content in continual competition, with the draft that comes to dominate being the one we later report as what we consciously experienced. I want the first session to be an activity rather than a lecture. The class has about 18 students and we have 75 minutes. The learning goal is for students to catch themselves, in the course of the activity itself, assuming that somewhere in their own minds there must be a finished mental item that gets displayed to an internal audience at a definite time — and then to watch that assumption fail under the pressure of the game, before I have explained anything.

Please design a runnable in-class game or staged exercise with rules, roles, materials available in a classroom (index cards, a projector, a stopwatch, paper slips, maybe a simple web form on their phones), and small non-monetary stakes. The activity should be built so the phenomenon emerges from the mechanics before it is named: for instance, participants might produce competing drafts of what just happened under different cueing or delay conditions and then discover that there is no canonical version of the event that their reports are imperfect copies of, only the version that happened to win in later reporting. Include a roughly 20-minute debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto specific parts of the idea: multiple parallel drafts, the absence of a central audience, the retrospective settling of what gets called conscious, and the role of probing or reporting in making one draft dominate. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — especially the risk that students walk away thinking there is no fact about what they experienced or that all interpretations are equally valid — and how to correct that without quietly reintroducing the Cartesian theater. Be explicit about which part of the multiple drafts idea the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply by argument rather than experience. Finally, if you think any exercise of this kind necessarily misleads at some point — for example, by depending on the very audience metaphor it is meant to dissolve — say so plainly and redesign it, rather than giving me something polished but wrongheaded.
