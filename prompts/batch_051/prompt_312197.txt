An economist in my institute defends a version of indirect normativity — the view that, rather than building systems to act on people's stated preferences, we should build them to act on the preferences people would have if they were better informed and more rational. She supports it with an analogy: idealizing a person's preferences, she says, is like using a stronger telescope to read a sign that was already there. The telescope does not write the sign; it only lets you read it more accurately. So the method merely reveals preferences that are already ours, makes no normative choices of its own, and that is supposed to be what makes it legitimate.

Audit this analogy. Set out the correspondence table: what plays the role of the sign, the telescope, the observer, the distorting atmosphere, and the improved instrument? Then identify where the mapping breaks, and say which of the disanalogies matter for her intended conclusion and which do not. In particular, what features of reading a distant sign does the analogy quietly import into preference idealization? Does her conclusion — that idealization is normatively neutral and merely revelatory — survive once the disanalogies are on the table? If not, say what weaker version, if any, the analogy can actually support, and whether a better analogy is available.

Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on idealized preference and practical reason, and do not present a contested view as the consensus.
