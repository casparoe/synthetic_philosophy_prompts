I work as a software engineer, and my team has been arguing about an idealized runtime model in which an unbounded sequence of callbacks fires at times 1/2, 3/4, 7/8, and so on — at 1 - 2^{-n} — so that infinitely many callbacks complete before a deadline at t = 1. We then ask what state the system is in at the deadline itself. One senior engineer has a set of verdicts on these cases that strike me as consistent, and I want to formalize the rule behind them well enough to predict her answer on a new case.

Her track record, as best I can reconstruct it: (1) A cursor moves continuously along a progress bar, and at each callback time its position is constrained only to be 1 - 2^{-n}; she says that at the deadline the cursor is at position 1, the end of the bar. (2) A boolean feature flag is flipped at each callback time from false to true and back; she says that at the deadline the flag has no determinate value — not because a real machine couldn't perform the task, but because the specification simply doesn't pick out false or true. When asked for a general principle, she refuses to state one and says only that the first case has an obvious limiting value and the second doesn't.

The new case: a digital instrument panel is programmed so that at the nth callback it displays the numeral 1/n. Its readout gets arbitrarily close to 0 as the deadline approaches, but at no finite stage does it actually display 0. What would she say the panel reads at the deadline? I can make out at least two principles that fit her two earlier verdicts and that give different answers here. What are the candidate principles, what does each predict about the panel, which is she most plausibly running given her track record, which earlier case is the closest analog, and what further case would settle which principle she holds?
