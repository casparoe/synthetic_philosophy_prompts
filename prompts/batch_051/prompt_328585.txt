I'm a third-year undergraduate in a seminar on moral and legal responsibility, and the question that has been bothering me for weeks is complicity: when is someone properly answerable for a wrong in which they took part without causing it? I've been recording my own verdicts on concrete cases, and the only rule I started with — that you are complicit only if your action makes a causal difference — keeps failing. Here are the cases as I've written them down.

1. A man waits in the car while two acquaintances rob a shop; he strongly suspects the plan but does not ask. A third person was parked nearby as a backup driver, so the robbery would have gone ahead without him. I judge him clearly complicit, and the causal irrelevance of his driving does not move me.

2. A woman signs her name as a witness to a will she knows has been forged by her employer. The will is later set aside on unrelated technical grounds, so nobody loses anything, and a notary who was also present would have witnessed it if she had refused. I judge her complicit.

3. A nurse administers a sedative to a patient on a physician's orders, knowing the physician uses such orders only to keep patients quiet on understaffed shifts rather than for their medical benefit. The patient merely sleeps through the afternoon, and the physician would have administered the drug himself if she had refused, so the outcome would have been the same. I judge her complicit, though somehow less than the first two.

4. A software engineer maintains a generic payment-processing system that she knows her company also uses for contracts with a government widely condemned for arbitrary detention. She has never worked on that account, and her code is not customized for it. I do not judge her complicit in that government's wrongs.

5. A bartender works at a club he knows the owners use to launder money. He serves drinks and has no role in the books; the legitimate bar business alone covers his wages. I do not judge him complicit in the laundering, or at most only trivially.

These verdicts make me uneasy because they pull apart cases that look causally alike. The driver, the witness, and the nurse made no difference to what happened, yet I hold them responsible; the engineer and the bartender knowingly stood in some relation to wrongdoing, yet I let them off. The distinction I keep reaching for is that the first three were woven into the particular wrong — they lent their will, their name, or their skill to that act — while the latter two merely provided indiscriminate background resources. But I cannot tell whether this is a real principle or a post hoc rationalization of my intuitions.

The case I keep going back and forth on is this: a printer runs off innocuous leaflets for a group with a reputation for incitement, announcing the time and place of a rally that later turns violent. The leaflets make no causal difference to the violence, because the rally was also publicized online and by phone, and she would have printed the same leaflets for any client. Is she complicit in the violence?

I'd like you to help me find the best principle, or a small set of candidate principles, that captures as many of these verdicts as possible — stating each clause and which case motivates it, saying what the view predicts about the printer and about any additional cases you think put pressure on it, and being explicit about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and which, if any, I should overturn. In particular, please tell me whether the idea of being part of the particular wrong rather than providing mere background resources can be made precise, whether an intention-based account does better, or whether the truth lies somewhere else.
