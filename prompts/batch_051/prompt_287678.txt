I've been reading a well-regarded popular cognitive science book, and there is a passage on the modularity of mind that I keep turning over in my head. The book's version runs something like this. Fodor showed that the mind is a collection of modules: specialized mental organs, each built for one job and each triggered automatically by its own kind of input. A module is, in essence, a reflex with a narrowed job description: it processes only its proprietary kind of information — faces, speech sounds, grammar — and it runs fast and mandatorily no matter what you believe or want. Because modules explain perception and language so convincingly, evolutionary psychologists drew what the book calls the obvious next step, sometimes called massive modularity. The whole mind is a Swiss army knife: deliberation, judgment, and general problem-solving are just the joint output of many domain-specific circuits that natural selection shaped for recurring ancestral problems, and the idea of a general-purpose central processor is a folk myth. Fodor's unwillingness to accept this extension, the book suggests, was mainly a matter of temperament — loyalty to an old-fashioned picture of a unified, rational mind — and the real fight is between an evolutionary view of cognition and a pre-Darwinian one.

I find the early part about perception plausible, but the jump from perceptual modules to a mind that is modules all the way down strikes me as much less straightforward than the book admits, and explaining Fodor's objection as temperament feels like a dodge. I would like to know, from someone who knows the actual debate: which claims in this presentation are accurate, which are fair but lossy simplifications, and which are subtly wrong? In particular, the book mentions in passing that modules are informationally encapsulated but then leans almost entirely on speed and specialization — is encapsulation doing the real work in Fodor's notion of a module, and does the argument for massive modularity survive once it is taken seriously? And what would Fodor, or a defender of his position, say in response to the claim that his only objection is an outdated attachment to a unified mind?
