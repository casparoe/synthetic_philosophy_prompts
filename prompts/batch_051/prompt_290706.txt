I am a doctoral student in philosophy working on Avicenna's psychology, and I have been trying to reconstruct his flying man thought experiment as a formally perspicuous argument, because the usual discussions of it seem to move too quickly from an epistemic observation to a metaphysical conclusion. The scenario, as I understand it, is this: suppose a human being is created all at once, as a fully developed adult, suspended in empty space, with eyes veiled, limbs separated so that they touch neither one another nor anything else, and with no sound, smell, or tactile resistance reaching him. Avicenna's claim is that such a person would nonetheless affirm that he himself exists. I would like to know whether the following reconstruction is valid, and if not, exactly where it goes wrong.

(1) In the scenario just described, the subject would be aware, with certainty, that he himself exists.

(2) The subject would have no awareness of his body, or of any limb, organ, or other bodily part; for awareness of the body and its parts requires sensation, and the scenario is constructed so as to remove all sensation.

(3) Nevertheless, by step (1), the subject is aware of something—namely, himself, the thing whose existence he affirms.

(4) If the self of which he is thus aware were identical with the body, or with any bodily part or collection of parts, then, in being aware of that self, he would already be aware of the body or part with which it is identical.

(5) Therefore the self of which he is aware is not identical with the body or with any bodily part; it is known otherwise than through the senses.

(6) Hence self-awareness reveals an entity numerically distinct from the body—the soul.

My own suspicion is that the trouble lies around step (4), or in the passage from (2) through (4) to (5): the argument seems to treat awareness as if it were a transparent relation, so that identity could be substituted inside the object of awareness, whereas the thought experiment only tells us something about what appears to the subject—about the content or mode of his self-awareness. But I cannot tell whether the first genuine fault is there, whether step (2) is stronger than the scenario actually warrants, or whether the argument is in fact valid and only step (6) overstates what follows. Could you assess the argument step by step, identify the first step or inference that fails, state the principle it tacitly relies on, and say whether some weaker conclusion—for example, that self-awareness in this scenario is not mediated by the external senses—does survive the example?
