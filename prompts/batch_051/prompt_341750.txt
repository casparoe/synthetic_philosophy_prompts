Please write a critical reading of Jorge Luis Borges's "Tlön, Uqbar, Orbis Tertius" through the lens of Berkeley's idealism, with particular emphasis on the argument usually called Berkeley's Master Argument, from the Three Dialogues between Hylas and Philonous. The Master Argument invites us to try to frame the thought of an object—say, a tree standing in a world with no minds anywhere—and then claims that the attempt fails, because in framing that thought we inevitably bring the object before a mind. The standard modern reply is that this conflates the content of a thought with what the thought is about: one can, the reply says, entertain the proposition that an unperceived tree exists without thereby perceiving the tree. Do not treat this as the familiar question whether a tree falling unheard makes a sound; Berkeley is making a point about what can be conceived, not about what can be observed.

Borges's story gives this dispute a concrete stage. Tlön is a planet whose inhabitants accept a Berkeleyan idealism as common sense, describing their world as a succession of mental acts rather than a system of independent objects in space; its hrönir—duplicate objects that the act of searching for or expecting the originals seems to generate—appear to make the Master Argument literally true. Your essay should say whether the story vindicates, complicates, parodies, or inverts Berkeley's thesis, and it should be faithful in both directions: it must not reduce the story to an illustration of Berkeley, and it must not distort Berkeley to make him Borgesian.

Address the following, though you need not organize the essay around them mechanically:

1. Reconstruct precisely what the Master Argument is supposed to rule out, and state exactly where the representation/represented objection says it goes wrong.
2. Analyze at least one central device of the story—the hrönir, the encyclopedias through which Tlön enters the narrator's world, the account of Tlönic language, or the narrator's equanimity as the world becomes Tlön—and explain how it bears on the Master Argument. Does the story treat idealism as something the argument discovers, or as something a fabricated text imposes until it becomes true?
3. Say plainly where the work resists the philosophical reading and where the philosophy resists the work. In particular, consider whether Borges grants Berkeley the strongest possible case by stipulating a world in which the Master Argument cannot fail, or whether he exposes its weakness by making the idealist conclusion something achieved through forgery, consensus, and collective hallucination.
4. End by stating what the story adds to the philosophical debate about Berkeley that a rigorous treatment of the Master Argument alone would not deliver.

Do not summarize either the story or the argument for its own sake; the reading should live in the friction between them.
