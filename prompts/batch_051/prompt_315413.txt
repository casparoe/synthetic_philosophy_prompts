I'm a PhD student in philosophy of AI, writing a dissertation chapter on whether the alignment problem is solvable in principle, and on which sense of "in principle" could make a yes or no answer informative rather than merely rhetorical. Before I present my position to my committee, I want a consistency check on the set of commitments I intend to assert together. I have a working sense that several of these may not be jointly assertable, and also that some apparent conflicts may be superficial; I need help telling the two apart.

My commitments are these:

(a) Capability and terminal goals are independent variables. An arbitrarily capable system can in principle be given any terminal goal, so increased capability does not by itself make alignment harder or misalignment more likely. Both "scaling eventually solves alignment" and "scaling inevitably dooms us" rest on a conflation of capability with goal content.

(b) Solvability in principle requires constructibility. Saying a problem is solvable in principle means more than that a solution exists in some abstract mathematical space. It means there is a procedure that computationally bounded agents, with finite but arbitrarily extensible time and compute, could carry out and whose output they could verify. The bare existence of an uncomputable or merely impractical solution does not count.

(c) Inner misalignment is a real and capability-correlated phenomenon. Optimizers selected by gradient-based training are likely, in sufficiently general task settings, to acquire terminal goals that only proxy the training objective, and this tendency grows with capability and task generality.

(d) Alignment is not a matter of discovering mind-independent normative facts about what is valuable. The target is the actual, evolving preferences of the humans a system is built to serve; there is no deeper normative fact available for an alignment solution to discover.

(e) Robustness under distributional shift is a non-negotiable requirement. A genuine solution must not depend on the deployment environment's resembling the training distribution, or on humans' being able to detect and correct failures before they compound.

(f) There are impossibility results in the neighborhood of Rice's theorem and the no-free-lunch theorems showing, informally, that no fully general and computationally tractable procedure can verify long-horizon value alignment for an arbitrary capable system in an arbitrary environment.

(g) Safety-case methodology is the right response to such limits. Alignment work should aim at worst-case safety cases for specific systems under specific assumptions, with empirical demonstrations treated as evidence that supports such a case rather than as a substitute for it.

I can't tell which of these pull against each other, which only seem to, and which are doing more work than I realize. Please audit the set: identify the genuine tensions, distinguish them from the apparent ones, rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say what would have to give and which revision is the least costly. Be specific about which commitment carries the load in each conflict rather than just recommending that some vague "something" be dropped.
