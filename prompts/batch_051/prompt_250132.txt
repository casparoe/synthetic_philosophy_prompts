Last night I had a long argument with a friend about whether a citizens' assembly can ever properly be described as giving voice to 'the will of the people.' The argument kept slipping because each of us appealed to different items in her own track record of judgments, and we could not agree on which principle she has really been running. I want to submit her track record to you and ask what it commits her to.

Over the years she has defended these judgments:

1. A compulsory national referendum, held after months of public argument, in which 61 percent voted for a clearly drafted constitutional amendment: she called this a genuine expression of the people's will.
2. A high-quality opinion poll showing 64 percent support for a policy that was never submitted to any institutional process: she refused to call this a popular will; it registered attitudes, not a will.
3. A massive and costly protest movement that forced the resignation of a government but never held a vote: she judged it a genuine popular will, formed outside formal channels.
4. A citizens' assembly, randomly selected by lot and commissioned by parliament, whose 160 members sat through six weeks of expert hearings and converged on a 78 percent internal consensus; parliament later adopted the recommendation. She called the assembly's convergence close to a textbook case of a formed popular will.
5. A government's unilateral decree on an issue absent from its election platform, justified by its parliamentary majority: she denied that it expressed any popular will; an electoral mandate cannot be stretched that far.
6. An optional referendum with 38 percent turnout, in which 52 percent supported a measure that had drawn almost no public attention: she denied that this was a popular will; it was too thin and unrepresentative.

Now consider this new case. A citizens' assembly is organized by a civic campaign rather than commissioned by parliament. It uses the same random selection by lot and the same two-month expert-hearing format as in 4, and its members converge on an 80 percent internal consensus in favor of a controversial electoral reform. During the same weeks, a methodologically careful poll of the national electorate finds 58 percent opposed to that reform. The assembly's recommendation is not adopted by any public authority.

How should I expect her to judge this new case if she is trying to be consistent with herself? Would she call the assembly's recommendation an expression of 'the will of the people,' or not? Identify the principle or principles she is implicitly running—for example, an aggregative view on which the will is the sum of individual preferences expressed through inclusive authorization, or a deliberative view on which the will is constituted through structured reflection, or some third principle—and say which earlier case is the closest analog and which case pulls hardest in the other direction. If her record underdetermines the new case, say so, but still lead with your best-guess verdict and state what principle she would be adopting under each possible answer. Then propose one further case, not already on the list, that would cleanly settle which principle really governs her judgments.

Finally, tell me which step of your prediction is most likely to fail, and what new information about her earlier reasoning—or which changed feature of the new case—would most change your answer. Keep the entire response under 400 words.
