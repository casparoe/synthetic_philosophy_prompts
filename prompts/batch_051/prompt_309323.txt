I am a functional analysis PhD student who reads and referees papers in which existence results are proved with Zorn's lemma—bases for arbitrary vector spaces, maximal ideals, Hahn–Banach-type extensions, maximal orthonormal sets. I know the statement of the lemma, but I have mostly applied it by pattern-matching, and I want something more reliable. Assume a standard ZFC setting throughout; I am not asking about the lemma's equivalence with Choice.

Please write me a decision procedure, runnable in about twenty minutes and without a set theorist, for deciding whether a proposed application of Zorn's lemma—in something I am reading or trying to write—is valid, invalid, or incomplete. It should be a sequence of questions in a fixed order. For each question, say what counts as a sufficient answer, and which answers stop the procedure with 'invalid' or 'the argument is incomplete'. Make the procedure require at least the following: stating the set and the partial order explicitly; checking that the set is non-empty; checking that every chain—not merely every finite or countable chain—has an upper bound lying in that same set; and checking that the element Zorn's lemma supplies is a maximal element, not a maximum, and that its maximality is actually converted into the claimed property rather than assumed. Flag the steps that are genuine judgement and cannot be mechanised.

Then run the procedure on the following three proposed applications, reporting the verdict at each step:
1. The standard proof that every vector space has a basis, using the set of linearly independent subsets of the space, ordered by inclusion.
2. A fallacious proof that every vector space has a maximal finite linearly independent set, by applying Zorn's lemma to the set of finite linearly independent subsets, ordered by inclusion.
3. A proof that there is a maximal countable ordinal, by applying Zorn's lemma to the set of all countable ordinals, ordered by the usual ordering of ordinals.

One of the three should force the procedure to give an answer the proof's author would not like, and one should expose the difference between requiring every chain to have an upper bound and requiring only every countable chain to have one. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do: which failures it cannot catch, and which decisions it will still leave to mathematical judgement.

Please lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the procedure and the reasoning; no preamble and no restating of the question.
