One influential family of attempts to naturalize mental content holds that the content of a representational state is fixed by its biological function: very roughly, a state represents whatever it is the state's function to indicate, where functions are understood in selectionist or adaptationist terms. A persistent conceptual difficulty for such accounts is the problem of novel or uninstantiated contents, that is, contents involving things that were never encountered in the evolutionary history of the organism, such as a person's thought about a newly invented kind of mechanism or an animal's representation of a toxin that exists only because it was synthesized in a laboratory. Explain precisely why such contents pose a problem for a selection-based account of content, rather than a merely empirical complication about the reach of the relevant selection pressures. Then lay out the main strategies a teleosemanticist might use to accommodate such contents, and state what each strategy must assume or establish about the relation between biological function and content in order to succeed.
