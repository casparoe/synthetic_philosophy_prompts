A committed Popperian falsificationist and a committed Lakatosian defender of a Malthusian research program have just completed a good-faith adversarial collaboration on Malthus's population-resource theory. Write the joint statement they now issue. The falsificationist holds that the theory, construed as a set of long-run empirical predictions, was refuted by the demographic transition and by twentieth-century agricultural productivity, and that later defenses of it are largely ad hoc rescues. The Lakatosian holds that the Malthusian core — population pressure operating on finite resources, with checks that can postpone but not permanently abolish that pressure — identifies a real mechanism that was right in kind but asserted prematurely, and that the successive modifications were largely progressive rather than ad hoc.

The statement should consist of a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table.

In the agreed-claims section, state what both sides can sign without capitulation. Include a neutral reconstruction of what Malthus actually claimed, carefully distinguishing the tendency claim from any dated prediction about when a subsistence crisis would occur; the historical developments both sides agree are relevant, including at least the transformation of agriculture from the nineteenth century onward, the demographic transition in industrializing countries, and the persistence of famine and land pressure in some regions; and the points about the special difficulty of testing long-run predictions on which their methods converge.

In the disagreements table, give each residual dispute its own row. For each row, state: the dispute; each side's position in wording that side would accept as its own rather than a caricature by the other; the crux, understood as the sub-question whose settlement would move at least one side; the case, argument, or empirical development both sides accept as bearing on that crux; and each side's prediction recorded in advance, including what pattern of evidence would cause that side to revise its position. The rows should cover at least these disputes: whether the original theory was falsified by observed history or merely left underdetermined by it; whether the modifications that kept Malthusian reasoning alive after the 1798 essay were progressive, degenerating, or an evaluable mix of the two; what it takes for a modified theory to count as the same theory rather than a different theory inheriting the name; whether the claim that the mechanism was real but offset is testable or tautological when the offsetting conditions are not specified in advance; and who bears the burden of proof in the assertion that population-resource pressure will reassert itself in the twenty-first century.

The statement should be rigorous enough that a working group in the philosophy of science could use it to design a pre-registered test of this disagreement. Where the two sides discover that a past disagreement was verbal, say so explicitly; where it is substantive, do not dissolve it into ambiguous compromise language. The document should be self-contained: include no bibliography, reading list, or citations. Write the answer in American English.
