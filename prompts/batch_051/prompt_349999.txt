Right, I need a hand drafting a memo. I'm the token philosopher on my university's academic conduct committee, and we're revising how we handle first-offence plagiarism by undergraduates. The committee is split. One camp wants a largely private process: formal finding, then the student writes a structured reflection on why what they did was wrong and what they'll change, possibly with a private apology to the marker. The other camp wants a restorative meeting where the student has to sit with the instructor and a couple of affected classmates and hear how the cheating damaged the course and let people down. The first lot say the second approach is just a shaming ritual that leaves students exposed and resentful. The second lot say the first is cosy and lets a student rationalise the whole thing without ever feeling the social cost of it. A third group says a university shouldn't be trying to engineer students' moral emotions at all, though the rest of us keep answering that not choosing is itself a choice. I suspect the real disagreement is over which moral emotion we want the process to inculcate, and whether a university can choose it through policy wording. Your an expert on the guilt/shame distinction — can you think carefully, start with a bit of brainstorming on each side, then write me a short memo for the committee with a definite recommendation (including a form of words for the new policy and the criteria reviewers should apply), while giving a proper run to the strongest argument on the side you don't pick?
