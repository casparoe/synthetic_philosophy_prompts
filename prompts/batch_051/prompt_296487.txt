You are an analytic philosopher who specializes in the metaphysics of causation. I want a working definition of when a failure to act — an omission — is a cause of an outcome. By a working definition I mean a criterion I could actually apply to new cases: it should say something specific about the relationship between the omission, the outcome, and the background situation, not just gesture at what would have happened otherwise. Build the view from first principles rather than reaching for citations or named authorities.

Start by briefly stating two or three candidate analyses you are weighing — for example, a counterfactual dependence test, a mechanism or causal-chain view that rejects absences as causes, or a view that makes the relevant expectations, roles, or norms part of the causal facts. Then commit to your preferred definition and run it through each case below. For each case, say what your definition yields, whether that matches ordinary causal talk, and where you would revise the definition or instead defend a surprising verdict. End by saying which case your definition handles least well.

The cases:

1. Signalman. A signalman is trained and paid to divert an approaching train onto a safe track. He falls asleep and does nothing; the train crashes.
2. Gardener. A hired gardener is paid to water a house's plants while the owners are away. She skips one week; the plants die.
3. Lifeguard and bystander. On a beach, a lifeguard on duty and a holidaymaker both watch a swimmer struggle and neither acts; the swimmer drowns.
4. Withholding and withdrawing. A physician withholds a ventilator that would have postponed a patient's death; in another scenario, a physician withdraws the same ventilator, and death follows just as quickly.
5. Goalkeeper. A goalkeeper makes no attempt to save a penalty kick; the ball goes in.
6. Missing cotter pin. A steering mechanism is missing a cotter pin because of an assembly-line mistake; the steering fails on the highway and the car crashes.
7. Distant non-donor. A person in Toronto does not contribute to a famine-relief fund; a particular man in South Sudan dies of starvation a month later.

My tentative intuitions are that 1 and 2 are omissions that cause their outcomes, that 5 and 7 are not causes, and that 3, 4, and 6 are the genuinely hard ones — but do not defer to these. In particular, I want to know whether the difference between the clear causes and the clear non-causes is a difference in the world, or only a difference in which roles and norms we happen to notice.
