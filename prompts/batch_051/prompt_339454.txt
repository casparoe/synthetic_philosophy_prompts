As a journalist who covers science and ideas, I have long operated with a tidy theory of what it is to understand something: a good explanation is a compact description from which the phenomenon can be recovered, so explanation is, in effect, a form of compression. The theory has real virtues—it explains why a handful of laws can cover a universe of cases, why a model with dozens of free parameters feels like curve-fitting rather than insight, and why prediction and understanding usually travel together. But I have a firm intuition I cannot shake: compression and understanding come apart in both directions. Some deeply compressible results are opaque to us, and, more troubling, there are systems in which the phenomenon Wolfram calls computational irreducibility seems to hold, so that no account shorter than running the system will reveal the outcome—yet we plainly do understand such systems at least partially. Climate scientists understand the dynamics of a warming planet, evolutionary biologists understand mass extinctions, and ecologists understand regime shifts, not by squashing those histories into a few equations but by running many simulations, comparing whole families of scenarios, and identifying which mechanisms are possible and which are ruled out. I do not want to be told which side wins. I want to know what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the compression theory of explanation to be amended or abandoned, and whether there is a reading of 'explanation' or 'compression'—perhaps one indexed to the question being asked rather than to the full dataset—on which both survive.
