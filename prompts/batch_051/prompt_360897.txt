I recently joined my town’s public arts committee, and we have been debating whether to fund a permanent municipal theater. The most persuasive argument from my fellow members has been that theater “forms taste and refines judgment,” an idea I wanted to trace back to its original form. In the preface to his *Letter to M. d’Alembert on the Theatre*, Rousseau reprints the passage from d’Alembert’s Encyclopédie article “Genève” in which that argument is made, including this sentence: “les représentations théatrales formeroient le goût des Citoyens, & leur donneroient une finesse de tact, une délicatesse de sentiment qu’il est très-difficile d’acquérir sans ce secours.” In substance, d’Alembert is claiming that theatrical performances would form citizens’ taste and give them a delicacy of feeling that is hard to acquire otherwise.

Reading this pushed me to articulate something I have been calling **spectator’s credit**. The idea is that a represented moral action is not only a release or purgation of emotion, as Rousseau is sometimes read as saying, but a kind of transaction. By imaginatively participating in someone else’s virtue on stage, a spectator acquires the feeling that the moral debt has already been discharged. Later, when a real situation requires something from that spectator, the demand feels muffled, not because the emotion was used up but because it was, so to speak, prepaid on credit. On this view, the theater does not merely give us the inward movements of virtue while sparing us its outward acts; it also rewrites our sense of what we have already done.

I want an honest assessment of whether this is a real extension of Rousseau’s position or just a relabeling of his claim about the sterility of the spectator’s pity. Does the anti-theatrical tradition already work with something like this under another name? And where does the concept fail? For instance, documentary theater and certain performances seem to leave audiences newly motivated to act rather than feeling exonerated; are those counterexamples to the concept, or are they cases where the spectator stops being a spectator in the relevant sense?
