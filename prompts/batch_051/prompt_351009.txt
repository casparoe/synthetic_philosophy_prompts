I write about science and ideas for a general readership, and I am working on a long essay about the strange career of Malthusian prediction. The organising question of the essay is whether a theory can be 'right but early'. What troubles me is that the very same move — the predicted event has not arrived, yet the underlying mechanism is still held to be intact — can look like patient truth-telling in one retelling and like unfalsifiable apologetics in another. I need a principled way to tell those apart, and I would rather have the criteria in hand before I shape the narrative.

Before I outline the piece, write the desiderata: the requirements that any long-run predictive theory in this family must meet before it deserves to be treated as still live, rather than rescued ad hoc or quietly abandoned. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that motivates it — the demographic transition, the repeated postponement of subsistence crises by technological change, the localised famines that did occur, the contrast between getting a mechanism wrong and getting its timing wrong, or whatever else you think genuinely separates the readings. I am especially interested in which pairs of desiderata may be jointly unsatisfiable — for example, a horizon long enough to absorb repeated postponement combined with a requirement that the theory be falsifiable within a decision-relevant window — and in which single requirement rules out the most candidates. Mark which requirements you regard as non-negotiable and which are merely preferences of a particular camp. A short scorecard against the core Malthusian claim and one or two variants is welcome, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves. My readership is intelligent but not academic, so state the desiderata in ordinary language and unpack any technical machinery as you go. Please write the answer in British English.
