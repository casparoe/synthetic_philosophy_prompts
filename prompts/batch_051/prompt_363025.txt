I've been trying to figure out whether sexual exclusivity has any moral weight of its own, or whether it's just a convention couples opt into. Here are some cases and how I react to them.

1. A married couple explicitly agreed to be monogamous. The husband sleeps with a coworker once on a business trip and never tells his wife. I think this is a serious betrayal—worse than breaking a minor promise, something on the level of lying to someone about the terms of their life together.

2. Another married couple explicitly agrees to an open relationship, with rules about using protection and not bringing other people home. The wife has a casual thing with a friend, follows the rules, and tells her husband. I don't think she's done anything wrong. The sex itself seems totally fine to me.

3. A couple has been together for years and never actually discussed exclusivity, but they've both acted as if they were exclusive: they live together, call each other boyfriend and girlfriend, and so on. The man gets drunk on a trip and sleeps with an ex. I think he did something wrong even though there was no explicit agreement being broken.

4. A couple agrees to a monogamish deal: hookups are allowed only when one of them is traveling. The wife, on a random Tuesday when both are in town, hooks up with an old flame. Strictly she violated the agreement. I do think it's wrong, but it feels more like breaking a rule than like betrayal; I wouldn't put it in the same category as case 1.

5. A woman's husband has advanced dementia and no longer recognizes her. She visits him daily and remains married, but she has a quiet long-term lover. She doesn't tell him (he couldn't understand anyway) and doesn't bring the lover around. I honestly can't bring myself to say this is wrong, or at least it's much less wrong than case 1, even though she's having sex outside the marriage without her spouse's agreement.

6. A couple agrees to monogamy. The husband does not sleep with anyone else, but he has a months-long emotionally intimate relationship with a coworker—sharing private details about the marriage, daily texting, calling her when he's upset. He hides it from his wife. I think this is wrong, maybe nearly as wrong as case 1, despite there being no sex.

Maybe my underlying principle is something like: sex or intimacy outside the relationship is wrong exactly when it breaks an explicit or implicit agreement, and its weight depends on how central that agreement is. But that feels like it reduces everything to promise-keeping, and case 1 feels like more than a broken promise while case 3 feels wrong despite there being no promise.

Is there a single principle of sexual exclusivity that captures all of these verdicts? If not, which of these verdicts should I overturn, and what would the cleanest defensible principle say about cases I haven't listed—say, a couple that both enthusiastically want polyamory, or one partner unilaterally announcing that the relationship is now open but staying? I want the principle stated precisely, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it, an honest statement of which of my verdicts it can't accommodate, and its verdicts on the new cases.
