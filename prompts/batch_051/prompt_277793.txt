Right — here's the thing. I'm a novelist, and I keep circling a question I can't shake: when I write an AI as a character, the difference between the thing literally having a false belief and it just spitting out a pattern that looks like one — is that a real difference, or just two ways of talking about the same data? I don't need a survey, and I don't want any citations or reading lists; I want a case built to order, one that forces the hinge to move.

Please construct a compact thought experiment — a tiny piece of fiction is fine, given my trade — that satisfies all of the following.

1. It features an unremarkable text-generating AI, no fancier than a current small language model, fine-tuned on misleading data from some ordinary domain, producing outputs in ordinary situations.
2. Someone using the intentional stance — treating the system as a rational agent with beliefs and desires — would naturally attribute to it one specific false belief, and that attribution would be the tidiest way to make sense of what it does.
3. Someone using a strict information-processing or statistical account — on which the system stores regularities but has no contentful beliefs at all — would predict exactly the same overt behaviour, including in one mildly novel probe situation built into the story. In other words, the two camps differ not on what the thing will do, but on what it is right to say its states are about.
4. The feature that opens the gap sits in the system's history or environment, not in a stipulation about whether it has a mind. Don't settle it by making the system conscious, by giving it hidden private states, or by making its internals opaque in a way that does the philosophical work.
5. The scenario should fit in a story exerpt of roughly 300 words.

The point of the case is to help me see whether a phrase like 'the AI believes P' earns its keep when the behaviour it rationalises can also be predicted from the training distribution — so the false-belief attribution must fall out of the design. A case that simply stipulates the verdict would be cheating.

Then, after the scenario, give me: the step-by-step reasoning each camp would give in this specific case, and why each arrives at the verdict it does; the single structural feature of the case that forces the two readings apart; what happens to the case if that feature is removed or toned down; and an honest note on how a defender of the statistical account would attack the intentional-stance attribution — the strongest case for saying the false belief is a projection, not a fact about the system.

And one last thing: please separate what's settled about the intentional stance from what's genuinely contested among people who actually work on this stuff, and don't present a contested view as though it were the consensus.
