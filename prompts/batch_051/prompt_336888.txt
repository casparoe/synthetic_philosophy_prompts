I've spent the past few months trying to decide whether the anti-adaptationist complaint about evolutionary psychology — that its explanations are unfalsifiable just-so stories — should be treated as a decisive critique or as a useful caution that has sometimes been overextended, and I find myself genuinely split. On the side of the critique, I am persuaded by its systematic virtues: it hands me one clean methodological standard, namely that no trait should be called an adaptation unless I have independent evidence about selection, function, heritability, and ancestral conditions, and it lets me diagnose a whole family of weak arguments under a single rule. On the other side, I cannot shake a firm substantive conviction: at least some adaptationist claims must be true. The near-universal sexual aversion between people raised together as siblings, across otherwise very different cultures, looks to me like a real inbreeding-avoidance mechanism rather than a local norm, and the near-universal disgust at feces and rotting meat looks like a genuine pathogen-avoidance adaptation; these are not things I merely find plausible, they are things I would be surprised to learn are culturally arbitrary. So I am caught between a methodological standard I respect and a conviction I cannot talk myself out of. I do not want to be told which side wins the literature. I want a negotiated verdict: what would it take for my firm intuition to be the thing that goes, deservedly debunked as exactly the pattern the critique says an overeager adaptationist mistakes for evidence? What would it take for the critique to be amended instead — kept as a warning against weak storytelling but not treated as a wholesale disqualifier of adaptation claims? And is there a reading of just-so storytelling on which the critique and my conviction are not really in conflict, but only look that way?
