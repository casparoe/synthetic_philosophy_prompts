A weird asymmetry in how we treat global misery: someone who is jailed and beaten because he protested his government gets refugee status almost automaticly; someone whose farm has been swallowed by drought and whose kids are now starving gets classified as an economic migrant and sent back. Both fled their homes, both face death or serious harm if returned, and neither one can fix the situation that made him leave. But most people, and most asylum systems, treat only the first as having a genuine claim to protection. I want to know whether that distinction can actually be justified. Is there a principle that makes being targeted by a persecutor the thing that matters, rather than the brute fact that returning would kill you? Give me the strongest version of that principle you can, then test it against nearby cases—for instance, a woman fleeing a government that won't stop a campaign of femicide it didn't itself order, or a region deliberately starved by a state that wants the land for something else. If the principle survives, say what it implies about who has to take these people in. If it doesn't, say whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up.
