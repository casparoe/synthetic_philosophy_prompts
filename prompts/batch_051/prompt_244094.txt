I am a PhD student in aesthetics working on depiction and the philosophy of perception, and a recent seminar discussion has left me unable to locate the flaw in an argument. We were examining the transparency thesis in the philosophy of photography: the claim that when we look at a photograph, we literally see the photographed object through the photograph, in roughly the way one sees an object in a mirror or through a telescope, whereas when we look at a handmade picture we see only the marked surface and do not see the depicted object itself. The standard motivation for this asymmetry is that a photograph's visible features are counterfactually dependent on the photographed scene through a mechanical process that does not require the photographer's beliefs about the scene to be true, while a drawing's features are mediated by the artist's beliefs and intentions in a way that blocks the relevant relation between object and viewer.

A visiting speaker pressed the following objection, which he said shows that the thesis, so motivated, proves too much.

(1) If photographs are transparent in virtue of some feature F, then F must be a feature that photographs have and handmade pictures lack, and F must be tied to the rationale that seeing through something is a matter of the object's being made visually available through a reliable, belief-independent channel.

(2) The only feature that satisfies this rationale is that the image's visible features counterfactually track the visible features of the source through a mechanical process that does not require the producer's beliefs about the source to be true.

(3) Photographs have this feature, which is why the transparency thesis is attractive in the first place.

(4) But a drawing made with a camera lucida, a careful tracing, a plaster death mask, and a brass rubbing have this feature as well: in each case the visible features of the artifact track the visible features of the source mechanically, and the tracking would survive even if the maker's beliefs about the source were false.

(5) So, by parity of reasoning, we literally see the object through a camera lucida drawing and the dead person's face through a death mask, in the same sense in which we see through photographs.

(6) If the transparency theorist refuses to accept (5), she must identify some further feature G that photographs possess and these other artifacts lack, where G is not ad hoc but is connected to the underlying rationale about visual availability.

(7) But no such G appears to be available: any candidate that excludes camera lucida drawings and casts — for example, that the image is formed directly by light rather than by a hand — either also excludes heavily processed or composite photographs whose relation to the scene is no longer the relevant one, or it draws a line that no longer does the philosophical work of explaining why photographs are special.

(8) Therefore, the transparency thesis either overgeneralizes to a wide class of mechanically produced representations, or it rests on an ad hoc distinction.

I want to keep two commitments if I can: that there is a difference in kind between looking at a photograph and looking at even a very accurate handmade drawing, and that I do not literally see a person through a death mask. But I cannot see which step to reject without giving up part of what made the transparency thesis attractive in the first place. Where does the argument go wrong? If the right response is to reject a step, say which one, explain why it initially looked acceptable, and spell out what a transparency theorist must accept or revise once it is rejected. If the right response is instead to accept the conclusion, explain how the transparency theorist can live with the overgeneralization and whether my two commitments can be salvaged in any form.
