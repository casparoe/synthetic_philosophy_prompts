I'm in a reading group going through Confessions Book XI, and one member keeps repeating that Augustine already showed the present has no duration, as if that settles it. She deployed it last week to shut down a discussion about whether the "now" in perception could be a short extended window rather than a dimensionless point, and basically told us cognitive scientists have been wasting their time on the specious present. I'm not convinced, but I also can't pin down what shes actually claiming.

The passage she leans on is in book 11, where Augustine says that the present would be eternity if it stayed present, but because it goes over into the past, it exists only by ceasing to exist. He then says the present has no space at all—something like that. (My Latin's not good enough to tell whether that's a claim about a mathematical point, a claim about what we can measure, or something about the mind.) He also says past and future are somehow there in the mind as memory and expectation, and that time is a kind of stretching-out of the mind.

Could you separate the precise claims hiding in "the present has no duration"? For instance, does it say the present is an instant? That any chunk of experience called "now" has parts? That the present cannot be measured? That only attention or memory gives it width? I want a menu: which readings are trivial, which are substantive and defensible, which are false, and which one has to be true if the slogan is going to do the work she wants it to do against an extended specious present. Also I'd like to know if any of the readings would be compatible with what cognitive scientists actually claim about the specious present, as opposed to the strawman she seems to be attacking.
