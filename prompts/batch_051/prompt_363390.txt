So I'm running a little reading group on the philosophy of emotion and two members have been arguing for months, and we finally convinced them to do an adversarial collaboration instead of just relitigating the same cases. One of them—call her the abolisher—thinks grief and deep sadness are pure costs: they hurt, they make you less functional, and if there were a safe intervention that kept your memories and your love intact but removed the suffering, you'd be irrational not to take it, other things equal. The other—call him the valuer—thinks grief isn't a price you pay for love but part of what it is to love someone who's gone, and that a grief-free life would be shallower in a way that matters, maybe not a better life at all. They've agreed I should draft the document they'd both have to sign.

I need the actual joint statement, not a summary of their argument. Please give me a numbered list of claims both can sign—about what grief is, what it costs, what a grief-removing intervention would have to preserve before either of them would even care, and which empirical facts both agree would matter. Then a disagreements table where each residual disagreement is worded so precisely that each side would nod at there own formulation. Then the cruxes: the sub-questions where settling one would actually move at least one of them. For each crux, give a concrete case or bit of evidence they'd both accept as bearing on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance. I suspect some of their fight is verbal—they keep sliding between causal and constitutive readings of 'grief is part of love', and they probably disagree about what it even means for a post-intervention person to be the same person—so part of the job is sorting out which disagreements are real and which are just about words.
