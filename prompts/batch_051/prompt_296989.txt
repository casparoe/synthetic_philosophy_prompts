I am working on a paper about whether everyday virtues and duty-like commitments—truth-telling, promise-keeping, mercy, special obligations to friends and family—are best understood as purely derivative from act-consequentialist value-maximizing, as heuristics or summary indicators of expected value, or whether they carry a more independent normative weight of the kind that rule-consequentialism and neo-Aristotelian virtue ethics each try to accommodate in their own way. In arguing about this, I have found that the cases I reach for tend to produce predictable alignments: either act-consequentialism stands alone against virtue ethics and rule-consequentialism, or rule-consequentialism and virtue ethics stand together against act-consequentialism. I would like a case that breaks that pattern, and I am asking you to construct it for me.

The specifications are these. Construct a single, ordinary-life scenario—no life-or-death stakes, no ticking bombs, no Nazi at the door, no perfect predictors, no demons or science-fictional devices, and no legal or professional code of conduct binding the agent—in which a neo-Aristotelian virtue ethicist and a sophisticated act-consequentialist agree on the right action, while a rule-consequentialist of the ideal-code variety prescribes a different action. The rule-consequentialist's action should be the more ruthless one: the action a morally serious but compassionate observer would wince at. The contested action should involve a duty-like commitment or a truth-telling choice in which a virtue such as mercy, loyalty, or tact pulls against strict general compliance. Do not simply stipulate the verdicts: the act-consequentialist's expected-value reasoning must favor the gentler action on plausible quotidian magnitudes, the virtue ethicist's verdict must issue from practical wisdom and specific virtues rather than from a general imperative to be nice, and the rule-consequentialist must not be reasoning like a deontologist—the operative rule has to earn its place because its general acceptance or internalization would produce the best expected consequences, not because it is fair or respectful in itself. Keep the scenario itself to one paragraph.

Then do the demonstration. State the contested action; give the virtue ethicist's verdict and the specific virtues and reasoning behind it; give the act-consequentialist verdict and lay out the expected-value calculation, including long-term effects on relationships, character, and future dispositions; and give the rule-consequentialist verdict, stating the ideal-code provision you take to be operative and explaining why its general value would be great enough that the ideal code would not build in an exception covering the case. Finish with the honest notes: identify the single feature of the case that is doing the real work, say what happens to the verdicts if that feature is removed, and explain how a defender of rule-consequentialism would attack the case—for instance, by rejecting your identification of the ideal code or by arguing that a full ideal code would already contain an exception clause that collapses the disagreement. Please write your answer in American English.
