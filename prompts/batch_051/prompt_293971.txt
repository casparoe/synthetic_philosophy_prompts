I'm trying to settle where I stand between democracy and epistocracy—the idea that political power should track knowledge rather than give everyone one equal vote. I don't trust myself to argue about this in the abstract, so I've been collecting my gut reactions to concrete cases. Here they are.

A. A six-year-old asks to cast a ballot in the national election, and her parents are willing to let her. I think she should not get a vote.

B. A sixteen-year-old's school board is about to vote on closing her school and sending her across town. I think she should have a real vote in that election, because it is her daily life being decided.

C. A citizen cannot name the current president or either candidate and marks the box because one slogan sounded friendlier. I think the law should still let him vote, though it honestly bothers me.

D. A non-citizen has lived in my town for twenty years, works, pays taxes, and has children in the local schools. I think she should be allowed to vote in municipal elections.

E. A relative fills out the ballot for a family member with advanced dementia and literally guides the person's hand. I think that vote should not count.

F. A proposed law would require everyone to pass a short civics quiz before voting. I am against the law, even though part of me is attracted to having better-informed voters.

G. My city's non-elected traffic engineers lower the speed limit on a dangerous road to 25 mph, and the mayor cannot override them. Most residents oppose it, but I think this is exactly the kind of decision that should be left to the engineers rather than a citywide vote.

Cases B and G feel as if they pull in different directions, and C and F do too, but I can't yet say exactly why. Is there one principle that gets all of these, or nearly all of them? If the only principle that saves every one of my verdicts is very complicated, I want to know that, and I want to know which verdicts a simpler principle would force me to abandon. Please also tell me what the principle says about a case I keep going back and forth on: a proposal to give each voter up to three votes on a ballot measure—one for simply being an affected citizen, and extra votes for correctly answering basic factual questions about the measure printed on the ballot.
