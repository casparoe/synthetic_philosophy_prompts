After retiring from a career in physical oceanography, I have been attending a reading group on the philosophy of time. At last week's meeting, two regular members got into a long argument about whether special relativity essentially decides the old dispute between the "block universe" and the view that time genuinely passes. One is a former civil engineer, the other an art historian; both had clearly done the reading, and I found myself unable to tell who was getting the better of it. I would like a careful adjudication of their exchange.

The argument went roughly as follows.

(1) The engineer opened by laying out the Rietdijk–Putnam argument. Pick an event that is in our future but spacelike separated from us—say a ceremony taking place next year, as reckoned in our frame, on a planet some four light-years away. No signal from that ceremony could reach us for several years. Yet for an observer in a suitably moving rocket passing Earth right now and heading toward that planet, the ceremony is happening now, simultaneously with the thought I am having at this moment. Since special relativity says no inertial frame is physically privileged, and since we ordinarily take what is simultaneous with us to be real, the ceremony must already be real. A large swath of the future therefore already exists, and the idea of a uniquely advancing "now" loses its physical basis.

(2) The art historian replied that the argument is smuggling in a philosophical premise. Relativity tells us how coordinates, clock readings, and simultaneity relations transform between frames; it says nothing about what exists. The step from "simultaneous in some frame" to "real" is not a theorem of the theory but a piece of presentist-sounding metaphysics, and it is precisely the premise at issue. At most the argument shows that if you already think reality is whatever is simultaneous with you, then relativity forces you to count many different frames' "nows" as real—which may just show that the premise was bad, not that the block universe is true.

(3) The engineer pushed back that he was not assuming any single frame's now is reality. His premise was only that no inertial frame may be assigned a unique metaphysical privilege. If a frame in which the ceremony is present is as valid as ours, then nature itself contains that ceremony as present. To deny this, he said, you must reintroduce something like absolute simultaneity—a privileged foliation of spacetime—and that is exactly what special relativity took away. So the burden is on the defender of passage to specify a non-ad-hoc structure.

(4) The art historian responded that rejecting the argument does not immediately require a privileged frame, because one can deny that frame-relative simultaneity has any ontological import. A presentist or growing-block theorist will, of course, eventually need some account of what "the present" is—perhaps a preferred foliation added as an extra lawlike structure—but that is a further cost to be weighed, not something this argument has already decided against. More pointedly, she added, the argument cannot even reach the events people usually care about: events in one's future light cone, such as one's own future decisions, because no Lorentz frame can make a timelike-separated event simultaneous with the here-and-now. The argument at most concerns the spacelike-separated "elsewhere" region, and the inference from that region to a full block universe is exactly the kind of philosophical extrapolation that needs independent support.

(5) The engineer dismissed the light-cone point as a technicality. The elsewhere region is already full of events that common sense regards as not yet real; if those events are real, then the natural ontology is the whole four-dimensional manifold, since nothing in the physics marks a boundary between the elsewhere and the future interior that would let reality stop at one side and not the other. He also objected that the historian's line still seemed to treat "the present" as a frame-independent fact, which special relativity appears to rule out. Without a specific proposal about what exists and why, he said, she was merely gesturing at positions that manage to avoid the conclusion.

(6) The art historian replied that her point was not that any particular alternative is correct, but that the physics underdetermines the metaphysics. Special relativity rules out a simple, frame-independent Newtonian present, but it does not force the interpretation that all events are equally real. Several live positions accept the relativity of simultaneity while denying the block, and for that reason the Rietdijk–Putnam argument is best seen as raising the cost of certain views rather than as a theorem whose conclusion every informed person must accept.

Please adjudicate the exchange point by point. For each numbered move, rate it from clearly mistaken to clearly right and briefly justify the rating. Then say who is right about which specific claims, and what, if anything, both parties are missing. Finally, I would be grateful if you would separate what is settled—by physics or by broad philosophical consensus—from what remains genuinely contested among people who work on this material, and be careful not to present a contested interpretation as the consensus.
