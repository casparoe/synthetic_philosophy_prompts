I'm teaching a small grad seminar on formal methods this fall, and the Arrow unit is in two weeks. Before I make anyone read the proof, I want a handout that just does the criteria: what should you demand of any rule that turns individual ordinal rankings over some set of options into a single group ranking or a chosen winner? I want it built around one concrete setting so the students don't float off into welfare-economics abstraction: a five-person faculty hiring committee that has to rank, say, five candidates. The members submit ordinal rankings only — they're not allowed to report utilities, even in principle — and the committee has to finish with either a full ranking or at least a hire.

Write it as a list of desiderata, with the consideration or motivating case behind each. For each one, mark whether it's non-negotiable (a rule that misses it isn't a serious contender), a modeling convention that got smuggled in as a moral requirement, or the preference of a particular camp — and name whose camp. I want the usual suspects (unrestricted domain, non-dictatorship, weak Pareto, independence of irrelevant alternatives, transitivity and completeness of the social ordering) but also the ones textbooks bury: anonymity, neutrality, resoluteness, strategy-proofness. Where two or more can't jointly hold, flag it and say which one is doing the excluding — I already know the fairy-tale version, that Arrow's list is jointly unsatisfiable, so the actual value is the reasoning about weather each one deserves to be in the list at all rather than merely being in the theorem.

On IIA specifically, give me both the strongest defense and the strongest complaint: the defense that it's really a no-agenda-manipulation or path-independence condition, and the complaint that it forces the pairwise comparison of x and y to ignore everything about where z sits relative to each, which is exactly the stuff ordinal rankings might still be allowed to carry. That's the one I expect half the seminar to want to throw overboard, and I want them ready to see what they lose. Also: the theorem assumes a transitive social ordering, but a hiring committee only needs a winner, not a full transitive ranking. Tell me whether acyclicity would be enough for that purpose, and make plain the cost of weakening transitivity — I have a dim memory it lands you somewhere oligarchic rather than democratic, but I don't want that just asserted. Finally, for each negotiable condition, say what relaxing it would actually buy: e.g., if we restrict the domain to single-peaked preferences, does majority rule start behaving well, and what must the committee assume about how candidates are arranged — one dimension? a shared sense of left-to-right? — to get that? Skip the scorecard of Borda versus pairwise majority versus approval voting; I'll run that as discussion afterward.
