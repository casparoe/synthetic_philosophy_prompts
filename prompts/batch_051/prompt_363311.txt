I'm putting together a closing session for a reading group on idealized-preference accounts of value, and I want to end the semester with a poem read aloud — the kind of piece that makes the problem feel lived rather than merely argued. The topic is coherent extrapolated volition, the proposal that you uncover what a person (or all of humanity) really wants not by taking their present preferences at face value but by extrapolating: what would they want if they had far more accurate information, thought faster and more clearly, were more the kind of person they already wish they were, and had their conflicting desires brought into coherence? The worry I keep circling back to is that the procedure is recursive in a suspicious way. The standards for what counts as "more information," what it is to "think clearly," which traits make up the person "they wish they were," and even what "coherence" amounts to are all supplied by the present, unidealized self. The extrapolation is supposed to correct the present viewpoint, but it keeps borrowing from the very viewpoint it is meant to supersede, so it is hard to tell whether the procedure is refining the starting point or laundering it.

Please write a pantoum about this recursion problem. For the form: a pantoum is a sequence of four-line stanzas in which the second and fourth lines of one stanza reappear as the first and third lines of the next, and the final stanza reaches back to the opening lines so that the poem ends where it began; aim for five or six stanzas. I want the recurring lines to do the argumentative work, not to be mere repetition: each return should make the reader feel how the supposedly idealized endpoint keeps getting rebuilt out of the non-ideal point of departure, and the final stanza's return to the opening lines should pose the question — not settle it — of whether extrapolation can genuinely exceed its starting point or only travels in a circle within it. The speaker should be someone attempting the extrapolation in earnest, uncomfortable with the circularity but not yet convinced it is fatal. Avoid both easy exits: neither conclude that coherent extrapolated volition is empty reframing, nor conclude that the circularity is benign. The poem should be accurate enough that someone who knows the coherent extrapolated volition literature would recognize the bootstrap worry, and good enough as verse that a reader who has never heard of the concept could still feel the structure of the problem. Write it in American English.
