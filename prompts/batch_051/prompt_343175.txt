I keep noticing a structural parallel between two claims from very different fields and I want a verdict on whether it is deep or superficial.

On one side there is Gödel's second incompleteness theorem. As I understand it: any consistent, effectively axiomatized formal system rich enough to represent elementary arithmetic cannot prove its own consistency. The system's own machinery cannot fully vouch for its own soundness from within; whatever assurance of consistency we have must come from outside the system, for example from a stronger system or from informal mathematical reasoning. On the other side there is the familiar jurisprudential problem of the ultimate rule in a legal system. Norms derive their legal validity in chains: a regulation is valid because a statute authorizes it, a statute because a constitution authorizes it. But the chain must terminate; the highest rule, whether one calls it the rule of recognition or a Grundnorm, cannot receive its own legal validity from a further positive rule inside the same system. Its authority rests on something outside the chain, such as the convergent practice of officials or the system's actual effectiveness.

Both cases seem to exhibit the same shape: a system cannot fully certify itself from within, so something outside it must do the certifying. My question is whether this is a genuine identity of structure, a loose analogy, or a confusion. I would like a committed answer, not a survey of options. Please spell out the proposed mapping (what is supposed to correspond to a formal system, a theorem, consistency, and proof); say what would have to be true of legal validity for anything Gödelian to transfer to law; and name at least one thing the parallel predicts that turns out false of actual legal systems. Where exactly do the two cases come apart, and is anything left of the analogy once they do? Also flag any imprecision in my rendering of the theorem itself.
