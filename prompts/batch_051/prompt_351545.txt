I've been building a little generative music box for my dad, who has early dementia. The box is finite and deterministic—it just walks through a giant table of thirty-second ambient pieces that all differ from each other in exact pitch, timing, and envelope, and it won't repeat within any realistic human lifespan. Dad hits the botton, a soft wash of slowly shifting chords comes out, and he says "that's nice" whether it's the third piece or the five-hundredth. His memory for the pieces themselves evaporates after a couple minutes; all he keeps is a vague sense that the box "does its thing." I've been going back and forth on whether he is actually having new experiences or just having the same experience—"hearing a fresh-sounding ambient piece"—over and over.

Suppose we say an experience is genuinely new for a subject just in case (a) its qualitative character is different from the qualitative character of every experience the subject has actually had before, where the difference can't just be that it happens later or has a new label; (b) the subject experiences that difference rather than merely believing it or inferring it afterward; and (c) the novelty is not an external fact hidden from the subject's point of view. Take, say, the two-hundredth piece. Does it qualify as a genuinely new experience for my dad? Go criterion by criterion; where the answer is no, tell me whether that's because the criteria are to strict or because of his situation, and whether the criteria themselves need revising. I want you to actually commit to a verdict and defend it, not lay out the alternatives and hand them back.
