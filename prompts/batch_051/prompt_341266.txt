I keep being struck by what looks like the same distinction in two places, and I want to know wether it's deep or just a coincidence of vocabulary. In programming, nominal typing says two types are the same only if they were declared the same way: in Java, two classes with identical fields and methods are nonetheless different types, because identity isn't settled by properties. Structural typing says the opposite: in TypeScript, two interfaces with the same fields are the same type, period. That's nearly the bundle theory vs substratum theory distinction: a bundle theorist says an object just is its properties, a substratum theorist says there is a bare particular underneath that secures identity independently of them. The analogy gets more interesting once you add mutation. A Java object can keep its identity — the same reference — while all of its fields change, which is the endurantist picture of persistence: a thing remains the same thing through change without being reducible to a time-slice. A structural record can't do that: once you update a field you just have a different record, and the idea of the same record changed is something you have to reconstruct by adding ids, version numbers, or paths, which sounds a lot like perdurantism (or like having to build the fourth dimension in by hand). So: is this a genuine identity of structure, or am I pattern-matching? What would have to be true of both sides for results to transfer — for instance, should the bare particular objection (that a featureless substratum does no explanatory work) predict that nominal identity in a type system is an unearned primitive, and should the bundle-theoretic problem of duplicate objects predict that structural systems can't really track sameness across updates without smuggling identity back in? And name at least one place where the parallel breaks in a way that matters, not just terminologically.
