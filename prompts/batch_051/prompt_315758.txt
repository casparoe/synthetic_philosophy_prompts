I've spent the last few years on the science-and-ideas beat mostly covering online polarization, and I keep running into the same stalled argument. One camp says the core problem is that people don't encounter the other side: it's an exposure problem, and the fix is feed-diversification, fact-checks, less deplatforming. The other camp says no, exposure is beside the point, because in a real echo chamber outside voices aren't just absent, they're already marked as untrustworthy before they arrive, so showing people more outgroup content just gets absorbed as more evidence of manipulation. These two groups cite the same debunking studies, the same platform scandals, the same country case studies, and nobody moves. I've got a working theory that its not really an empirical disagreement about interventions at all. It's that one side is trying to explain why people hold false beliefs, and the other side is trying to explain how distrust becomes self-sealing, and those are two different projects sharing the same vocabulary — 'echo chamber,' 'bubble,' 'polarization.' Is that the right diagnosis? Or is there a real empirical or formal question hiding underneath, something like whether trust repair has to come before belief change, or whether the information gap and the trust gap are the same mechanism at different stages, that could actually be settled? And if I'm right that it's mostly a conceptual tangle, what would count as progress: a sharper distinction, a different question, or honestly just letting the two projects split and stop arguing?
