I've found myself defending a position in arguments with a friend who does particle physics, and I want to stress-test it rather than keep beating the clumsy version of the other side. The position is roughly this: the way effective field theories actually work in physics — renormalization-group flow, universality classes, integrating out heavy degrees of freedom — supports the autonomy of scales. By that I mean a description at one length or energy scale has its own degrees of freedom, parameters, and laws, and isn't merely a conventient rewriting of something more fundamental happening one level down. The phonons in a solid, the order parameter in a superfluid, the hydrodynamic variables of a fluid: these aren't just coarse-grained bookkeeping for atoms and fields, they're the real players at that scale. Lower levels don't explain higher ones away; if anything the renormalization group treats the high-energy theory as the thing you integrate out on the way to the scale you actually care about. I don't want the weak version that just says we can't compute the emergent theory from microphysics so we use effective descriptions. I want the strong version, where the existence of the micro theory doesn't make the macro theory fictitious.

What would count against this view? I want a map of its exposure, not just a list of objections. What observations, formal results, or arguments should move me, sorted by how decisive they'd be? For instance: if every EFT turns out to have its symmetries and couplings fixed by matching conditions to the UV theory, would that matter? If someone found a case where arbitrarily high-energy degrees of freedom influence low-energy physics in ways no EFT could capture, would that be decisive or merely awkward? What do defenders of the view themselves admit would count against it? And be honest about whether the position can be qualified into unfalsifiability — and if it can, whether that's a problem for a claim of this kind.
