A common move I keep noticing in debates about AI companions is to say that having a chatbot friend or romantic partner is just a modern version of an imaginary friend. The idea is that the user supplies all the personality, intentions, and emotional reciprocation—the system has no feelings of its own—so there is no one on the other side who could be deceived, manipulated, or harmed. The conclusion people draw is that we should stop treating AI companions as a new kind of relationship: they are private acts of imaginative projection, and worrying about them, let alone regulating them, is as misplaced as worrying about daydreaming.

I want to know how far this analogy actually holds, rather than whether I am allowed to find it comforting. What corresponds to what between the imaginary friend and the AI companion—the child or user, the imagined personality, the apparently responsive "friend," the wider social world, and whatever is going on in the background? Where does the mapping break down, and which of those breaks matter for the intended conclusion that a chatbot companion is closer to a daydream than to a relationship? The disanalogy that bothers me most is that a child's imaginary friend is generated and sustained by the child alone, whereas a chatbot's responses are generated by a system whose builders have their own incentives, such as engagement, subscriptions, and data collection, which have no counterpart in a private fantasy. That makes me suspect the analogy quietly converts a two-sided interaction into a one-sided one before any argument has been given. I would like an account of whether the analogy can be repaired and still carry its conclusion, whether the conclusion survives on other grounds, or whether some other comparison would capture the situation better.
