I'm a retired physical chemist whose gotten into philosophy of action over the last few years, mostly through a reading group. Lately we've been stuck on the old problem of weakness of will, and the other night someone tried to dissolve it with the kind of time-discounting you see in behavioral economics. I half-buy the argument but can't locate exactly what bothers me. It runs like this. (1) A person is weak-willed only if, at the moment she acts, she judges that some alternative open to her is better all things considered. (2) In the standard present-bias or hyperbolic-discounting picture, a reward's value spikes as it becomes imminent, so a person can prefer a larger-later reward in advance and a smaller-sooner reward now. (3) So what we loosely call her judgment that the larger-later reward is better is really two time-indexed rankings, not one standing judgment. (4) Hence there is no single time at which she both judges the larger-later reward better and chooses the smaller-sooner one. (5) Therefore hyperbolic discounting doesn't explain weakness of will; it dissolves it, by re-describing the weak-willed choice as a rational (though time-inconsistent) choice by a sequence of time-slices. My suspicion is that step (1) is too strong, or that step (3) slides between ranking something higher and judging it better, but I'd like to know whether the argument is valid and, if not, exactly which step fails.

When you respond, please separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who know this material — for example, the shape of the discounting model is one thing, while whether present-bias is a form of weakness of will or a way of explaining it away, and whether the person at different times is the same agent in the relevant sense, are another. Dont present a contested view as if it were the consensus. Format it as exactly five numbered items, one or two sentences each, with no introduction or conclusion: one item for each step, and make the fifth item also give your overall verdict on whether the argument goes through.
