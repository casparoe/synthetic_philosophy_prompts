Me and my flatmate keep going round and round about whether the standard evolution-of-cooperation story still works now that most interactions are one-shot and online. As I understand the story: in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, defecting strictly dominates cooperating, so rational self-interested players defect. But once the game is repeated with the same partner and nobody knows when it ends, conditional strategies like tit-for-tat can make cooperation pay, because if I cheat today you'll punish me tomorrow. The future hanging over the present is what gives cooperation its foothold.

The development is that most of the interactions that matter now are not repeated with the same person. I buy from a seller I'll never see again, leave a review on a platform, and a stranger lets me into traffic, and none of us will meet twice. What stands in for repetition is the reputation system: ratings, scores, bans, histories that follow people around. The thing hanging over the present is now a database, not a person with a memory of what you did.

So here's the question. Does the standard argument survive this shift, or did it only work because it assumed the same two players meet again and remember each other? I can see it going two ways. One: reputation systems just make one-shot interactions functionally iterated, and the argument survives with the community, rather than a partner, doing the punishing. Two: indirect reciprocity is a genuinely different mechanism with different failure modes (fake reviews, fresh accounts, platforms that dont share records), and the original demonstration that conditional cooperation can win doesn't simply carry over. Which is right? Say exactly what the orginal argument needs to be true, and whether it's true when the future is a database rather than a relationship. Spend most of your answer on what the argument needs, not on policy patches, though if that's where it leads that's fine.
