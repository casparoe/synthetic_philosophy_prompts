I'm teaching a course this fall on how moral norms actually change—not how they ought to change—and I want to pair the theoretical material with a novel, and Mark Twain's Huckleberry Finn is the one I keep coming back to. The framework I'm giving students goes roughly like this: an older story says practices like slavery end when they stop paying out, and all the famous humanitarian arguments were mostly window dressing over shifting material interests (the Eric Williams line). A newer story says the real engine is honor: a practice ends when it stops being prestigious and starts being shameful, not when it gets refuted the way a bad argument does (the Appiah-style view). I want to use the novel to stress-test the honor-versus-interests-versus-arguments picture.

The obvious scene is the one where Huck, fully convinced that helping a runaway slave is a sin, writes the letter turning Jim in and then decides he'd rather be damned than send it. To me that reads like a moral revolution in miniature: nobody argues him out of anything, the respectable path is exactly the one his world would honor, and he just stops wanting that kind of respect. But the book also pushes back—he's one boy, not a society; the settled world on shore doesnt move an inch; and the ending hands Jim his freedom through Miss Watson's will, which Jim never even knew about, while Tom turns Jim's captivity into a game. So read the novel through that lens for me: what does it actually show about whether norms change through arguments, interests, or honor, which scenes would you have me close-read, where does the book resist the honor story, and what does it add to the debate that a careful philosophy paper can't?
