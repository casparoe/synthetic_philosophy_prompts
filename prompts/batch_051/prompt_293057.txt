I want to test a criticism of Avicenna's flying man argument by playing the objection while you play Avicenna. Stay in character throughout, write in the first person as Avicenna, and answer using the conceptual resources available in the De Anima section of your Shifa plus whatever I tell you about recent experimental findings. Do not concede the central point: that the flying man establishes the soul's existence as something distinct from the body and known independently of it. Keep each reply to one paragraph.

Here is my opening move. Your thought experiment, as I understand it, asks us to suppose a person created all at once and fully mature, with eyelids closed and limbs separated so that no part of his body touches another, suspended in empty air so that he feels no contact, and unable to see. You say he would nevertheless affirm that he exists, while denying that he has any limb, organ, or body. I put to you a modern finding: people placed in conditions that block ordinary touch and vision often continue to report a vague but persistent sense of bodily volume, boundedness, and orientation, a felt here with a horizon. Some researchers attribute this to interoceptive and vestibular signals that remain active even when external contact is absent. If that is right, your imagined man may still be relying on an effect of the body rather than on the soul’s bare knowledge of itself. How do you distinguish the two?
