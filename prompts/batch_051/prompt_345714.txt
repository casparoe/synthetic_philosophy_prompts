As a novelist, I am currently writing a character who is a perceptual psychologist with a deep, carefully considered skepticism about the idea that mental images are pictures in the head, and I would like his objections to go beyond the usual gestures. I have been reading the mental imagery debate, and I already understand the following standard objections to the depictive, Kosslyn-style position well enough that I do not need them restated:

- The regress, or homunculus, objection: a picture requires a viewer, so treating a mental image as an inner picture seems to postpone the problem of interpretation rather than solve it.
- The tacit-knowledge objection: the linear increases in reaction time found in scanning and rotation experiments can be reproduced by a propositional system using the subject’s knowledge of the task, so those results do not uniquely support a depictive format.
- The cognitive-penetrability objection: imagery is shaped by beliefs, instructions, and prior knowledge in ways that suggest heavily concept-driven processing rather than the operation of a fixed analogue medium.
- The indeterminacy, or grain, objection: unlike a picture, a mental image seems to leave indefinitely many details unspecified until the subject is prompted to fill them in, which is more naturally modeled by descriptions than by a depictive array.
- The imaging-data underdetermination objection: activation of early visual cortex during imagery does not decide the debate, because top-down activation of those regions is compatible with both a depictive and a propositional account.

I expect this list is close to the standard repertoire, and a character armed only with it will still sound conventional. Could you provide one further objection to the depictive view that is not on this list, not a rewording of any item on it, and not merely a special case or combination of several items? Once you have given it, tell me honestly how much force you think it actually has.
