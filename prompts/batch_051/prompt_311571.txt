Since my partner and I began living in different cities, we have adopted a small ritual: each of us watches the same film separately, then we describe it to one another and advise each other on whether to bother. The ritual has forced me to notice a division in my own habits of trust that I cannot yet justify. When my partner tells me that a film runs 160 minutes, that it was shot in black and white, or that two plot threads are left unresolved in the final scene, I take her word without hesitation; I would repeat those claims to a colleague the next day. But when she tells me that the film is achingly beautiful, structurally dishonest, or the most overrated thing she has seen all year, I do something odd. I am happy to let her verdict steer my decisions—I may skip the film, adjust my expectations, or warn a friend whose tastes I think resemble hers. Yet I refuse to adopt the verdict as a belief of my own. I would never say, "The film is overrated—I haven't seen it, but my partner says so." If pressed, I would insist I cannot really believe that until I have sat through it myself.

This is not ordinary distrust of a particular informant. I feel exactly the same way after reading a critic I have followed for a decade, even a critic whose taste has matched mine with uncanny accuracy. Many people I have described the ritual to recognize the pattern in themselves. It seems to be a structural asymmetry between descriptive testimony and aesthetic testimony: the former is treated as a source of belief, the latter only as a source of behavioral guidance. One thought is that there is a principled reason for this—that aesthetic judgments answer to how a work strikes a particular sensibility, so no report, however expert and however sincere, can stand in for the experience. A rival thought is that the asymmetry is a piece of epistemic snobbery: descriptions like "shot in Lisbon" sit only a short inferential step from evaluations like "visually unremarkable," and the line I draw simply marks the spot where I want to claim the judgment as my own. I want to know whether the asymmetry can be vindicated, and if so by what principle. Please give it the strongest defense available, then test that defense against the hard cases: a friend whose taste tracks mine perfectly; a reviewer whose judgment I trust more than my own first impression; and the fact that I cheerfully use aesthetic testimony to decide what to watch while refusing to let it settle what I believe about what I have not watched. If the right conclusion is that the practice should be revised rather than vindicated, explain what the revised practice is and what we would lose by adopting it.
