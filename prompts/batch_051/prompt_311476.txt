Write a decision tree for a joint reading group of interpretability researchers and philosophers of science, aimed at researchers who have trained or used sparse autoencoders (SAEs), linear probes, and activation patching but have not read the philosophy of mental representation. The tree should take a participant from first-person judgments about concrete cases to a position on whether trained neural networks literally have representations. It must sort participants by what they say about the cases, not by which label they prefer; no branch may ask a question like "are you a realist about features?"

The deliverable is the decision tree, not an essay. Begin with a short preamble glossing sparse autoencoders, linear probes, and activation patching in one or two sentences each, so the instrument is usable by the philosophers in the room. Then build the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of names. First give five to seven branch nodes, each a yes/no question about a concrete scenario a researcher could answer without already knowing the positions. Second, state which combinations of answers the branches leave open. Third, place the following positions among the leaves:

- mechanistic realism: a trained network's features and circuits are real constituents of the system and are causally responsible for its behavior;
- distributed realism: networks genuinely represent, but representational content need not be carried by a sparse set of independently meaningful, local directions;
- instrumentalism: feature and circuit talk is a useful modeling language for predicting and controlling networks, not a report of a pre-existing internal structure;
- measurement-relative realism: what features a network has is partly constituted by the choice of dictionary, probe, or other measurement scheme, so representational claims are true only relative to such a scheme;
- deflationism: the question is misplaced, because talk of representation adds nothing to a complete description of weights, activations, and input–output dispositions.

You may add at most one further leaf if the logical space demands it. For any combination of branch answers that ends up unoccupied, add a one-sentence diagnosis: is the view incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored?

The tree must respect three fault lines. Phrase each branch so it lands on one of them, but do not name the fault line in the question text. (a) Locality: whether a representation must be a legible, localizable constituent, such as a single direction or a small circuit, or may be distributed or relational. (b) Causal use: whether it is enough that a feature is decodable from activations, or whether the network must in fact use it in producing downstream behavior. (c) Lens-dependence: whether two equally good decomposition schemes that disagree about features can both be correct relative to their own measurement conventions, or whether at most one of them carves the network at its joints.

The branch questions should be answerable from ordinary experimental experience; use as material cases such as the following, which you may adopt or replace with better ones: two SAE dictionaries with equal reconstruction fidelity that disagree about whether a given direction is one feature or two; a linear probe that decodes a feature but fails to change behavior when used for activation patching; a circuit whose ablation reliably changes the output; and a feature that appears at one sparsity penalty and disappears at another. Finally, flag any two positions your tree cannot cleanly separate given the branch questions alone, and specify what further case or experiment would be needed to separate them.
