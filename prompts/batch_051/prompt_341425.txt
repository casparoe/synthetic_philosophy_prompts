I'm a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I've been turning over a question about complicity after some uncomfortable conversations at work. We build infrastructure: libraries, routing systems, ranking models, checkout flows. Most of what we ship has ordinary uses, but occasionally we learn that something we built helps someone do something wrong, even though our component doesn't cause the wrong act and we never direct anyone to do it. A colleague optimized a delivery-routing module for a logistics platform that mostly moves legal freight; we later learned the platform is also used to ship goods in violation of export controls. His module didn't select any shipment; it just made the whole system faster. Is he complicit in those illegal shipments? I have gut reactions to particular cases but no general principle that survives contact with them.

Here are the cases I keep going back and forth on: (a) A developer writes a generic data-aggregation library with no particular misuse in mind, and a payday lender later embeds it in a loan-application flow that traps borrowers in cycles of renewal; the developer never learns about this use. (b) An engineer at an ad-tech company knowingly tunes a ranking model to place high-interest payday-loan ads in front of users whose profiles the model classifies as "financially stressed"; the engineer doesn't design the loan product, set its rates, or touch any payment system. (c) A QA engineer tests a checkout flow, written by others, that hides the cancel-subscription option until after a free trial converts; the engineer knows the flow is meant to mislead but writes no copy and makes no design decisions. (d) A logistics engineer optimizes delivery routes for a firm that mostly ships legal goods but, as the engineer knows, also ships some goods in violation of export controls; the engineer has no influence over which goods are shipped. (e) A cafeteria worker at a company that is committing large-scale accounting fraud; the worker's meals keep employees at their desks and otherwise have no connection to the fraud. (f) A person buys shares in a company after reading credible reports that the company uses forced labor in its supply chain; the investment is passive and the shareholder has no governance role. (g) A security researcher publishes an open-source encryption tool knowing it will be used both by human-rights advocates and by criminal networks.

I think (a) and (e) are clearly not complicity and (b) clearly is, but I don't know how to handle (c), (d), (f), and (g). I'd like a working definition of complicity—ordinary moral complicity, not the technical requirements of any particular legal system—that runs through all seven cases and either classifies them or explains why some of them resist classification. If your definition gives a verdict I initially resisted, defend it. Please respond as exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion: (1) your definition, (2) the clear cases and how they fall out, (3) the hard cases and how they fall out, (4) which case most pressures the definition and how you would revise it, if at all, and (5) the verdict you are least sure about.
