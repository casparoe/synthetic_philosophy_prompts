You are a professional philosopher specializing in action theory, practical rationality, and medieval philosophy. You are writing for a seminar of advanced undergraduates who already know the rough shape of the Buridan's ass puzzle but have not studied formal decision theory in depth. Think carefully, and do not merely summarize the puzzle.

Here are two positions, closely paraphrased from the broader tradition of philosophical reflection on choice. I have deliberately paraphrased them rather than quoting, so evaluate them as claims rather than as textual interpretations.

First position. In the puzzle traditionally known as Buridan's ass, an agent is placed exactly halfway between two alternatives that are equal in every property relevant to choice. If an agent can choose only when some reason, preference, or apparent good favors one alternative over the other, then under such perfect equipollence there is no ground on which to choose. The agent is stuck: the ass in the stock example starves between two equally attractive bales of hay, even though there is an overwhelming reason to choose either one rather than neither.

Second position. In contemporary decision theory, a choice is rational when it selects among the options that maximize expected utility, and if two options tie for first, selecting either member of the tied set is itself fully rational and requires no further asymmetric explanation. On the stronger revealed-preference reading, someone who selects the left bale rather than the right has thereby revealed a weak preference for the left, or at least an indifference consistent with having chosen it. On this picture, a tie is a condition under which choice proceeds normally rather than a condition that blocks choice.

These two positions appear to conflict: the tradition that produces the ass treats equipollence as paralysis, while decision theory treats it as a standard case in which a tie-breaking selection is licensed. Can the two be reconciled without making one of them a caricature of the other?

First, produce a Brainstorming section that distinguishes at least three candidate reconciliations. To get you started, consider some of these distinctions, but do not limit yourself to them: a reason to take one of the available options versus a reason to take this particular option; preference as a comparative ranking versus preference as a commitment or policy acquired through choice; ex ante determination versus ex post rationalization; choice as an event that is caused versus choice as the exercise of a power to settle an open matter; and a requirement of the intellect versus a requirement of the will.

Then, in an Evaluation section, state the strongest version of each position and give your considered verdict: do they contradict each other, or do they speak past one another? For each candidate reconciliation, say whether it is an honest reading of both positions or a piece of special pleading that bends one position to save the other. In particular, say whether the idea of a revealed preference genuinely answers the worry that a choice without a prior asymmetry is arbitrary, or whether it merely renames the mystery. Avoid appeals to authority; argue from the structure of the positions themselves.
