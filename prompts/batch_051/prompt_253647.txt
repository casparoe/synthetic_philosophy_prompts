I'm a PhD candidate in ethics and social choice, and I've been trying to figure out which philosophical questions about pure time preference are actually load-bearing for policy and which are largely intramural. The working distinction I lean on is this: pure time preference in the strict sense is discounting a future benefit solely because it occurs later, as distinct from discounting that arises because future people are expected to be richer (and so have lower marginal utility) or because there is some probability that no one will be around to receive the benefit. A private foundation I assist is willing to fund roughly five person-years of conceptual work on the ethics of discounting, aimed at improving how a treasury or intergovernmental panel chooses the social discount rate for very long-horizon projects such as climate adaptation, nuclear waste, and biodiversity. They do not want another survey of familiar empirical disputes; they want a defensible agenda for genuinely unresolved normative questions.

What I would like from you is a ranking of the three unresolved conceptual questions about pure time preference and the discounting of future welfare that you think it is most important to settle, along with your reasoning. I have been turning over candidates such as: whether the welfare-relevant unit should be time, generations, or persons once population size changes; whether non-constant or declining discount rates can have a principled normative foundation rather than emerging only from uncertainty over future rates; and whether a planner's impatience with respect to calendar time can be cleanly separated from diminishing marginal utility once one drops expected-utility assumptions about risk. You need not stay within that list, and I would rather you not pad the list with variants of the same underlying problem. For each question you rank, please say why it is upstream of other downstream conclusions, how tractable it is for a philosopher with formal training rather than an empirical economist, and what sort of result over roughly three years would most change the applied debate. I also want candor about which widely discussed questions you would deprioritize despite their prominence, and whether their practical payoff would be small even if they were resolved.
