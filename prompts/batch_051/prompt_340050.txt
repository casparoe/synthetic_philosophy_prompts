I've been putting together an application for some behavioural work on animals, and something about the ethics paperwork is bugging me. If I want to run the study on mice or zebrafish, I need commitee approval, a justification of the sample sizes, a protocol for minimising pain, the works. But if I run essentially the same experiment on fruit flies, crickets or nematode worms, the committee doesn't want to know. The same line shows up outside the lab: kicking a dog would get me in real trouble, but slug pellets and wasp spray aren't things anyone loses sleep over. In practice we seem to draw the line somewhere around 'vertebrates, plus the occasional bright invertebrate like an octopus', but nobody can actually tell me where consciousness starts or why the line goes exactly there.

Here's the task. What theory about the distribution of consciousness across animals does this practice presuppose? I'm not asking what the correct theory is; I'm asking you to reconstruct the implicit view the practice is committed to. Does it presuppose that consciousness is basically a vertebrate affair, that small nervous systems can't support it, that invertebrates are probably not conscious, or just that we would rather not think about it? Distinguish what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, and say where it's philosophically underdetermined, such that several rival theories could license the same line. Then tell me what would practically have to change if we adopted a precautionary view on insect consciousness, or a functionalist view on which anything that can learn and care about outcomes counts. If the practice turns out to presuppose two inconsistent things at once, say which commitment is doing the real work.
