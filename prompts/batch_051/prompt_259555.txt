A colleague in my department teaches a graduate seminar on rescue cases and on whether the number of people at stake should settle what a rescuer does; she describes herself, broadly, as an anti-aggregationist. I have been keeping a note of her verdicts, and I now want to know how much her record actually constrains the one case she has not discussed.

Her verdicts are these. Choosing between one stranger and another, she would flip a coin; she says the two claims are equal and that simply picking one side would be arbitrary. She handles two-versus-two and five-versus-five the same way: equal numbers cancel, and some fair randomizing procedure is appropriate. In a case where the rescuer's own sister is the single person on one side and five strangers are on the other, she says the rescuer may go straight to the sister and need not randomize. In a case with three stable strangers on one side and three others who will die within the hour if not reached, she says the urgent three must be saved; she adds that this turns on urgency rather than on any question about aggregation. Finally, in a one-versus-one case in which one of the two had started the fire that now traps them both, she says still flip a coin: causal fault bears on what he owes afterward, not on his claim to be rescued.

The case she has somehow never been asked, though it seems the obvious one, is one stranger versus five strangers: all innocent, all equally urgent, none related to the rescuer in any way. I want to predict what she would say. Would she treat saving the five as required, as merely permitted, or as something that must be left to a chance procedure—and if chance comes in, what chances? If a lottery, should it be a coin between the one and the five, a weighted lottery that gives each of the six persons an equal one-in-six chance, or something else?

I am less interested in the bare prediction than in learning which principle, or which family of principles, her recorded verdicts genuinely support. If the record underdetermines the prediction—for example, if an equal-chance-to-each-side rule and an equal-chance-per-person rule both fit everything she has said—then say so, explain what principle she would be committing herself to under each answer, and tell me which further case, or which rereading of an earlier one, would best distinguish them. And if any part of my question rests on a confused premise, such as the idea that a person's case-by-case verdicts must obey a single principle at all, I would rather you say that plainly than produce a tidy prediction.
