For a paper on risk and consent, I have reconstructed an argument that keeps unsettling me because it proves too much, and I want it diagnosed rather than merely rejected. Here is the argument:

(1) If an activity imposes a risk of serious harm on a person, and that person has not consented to bear the risk, then the activity wrongs that person.
(2) Consent to bearing a risk must be given by the person who bears it; it cannot be supplied by a majority vote, an expert agency, or an implicit arrangement among strangers.
(3) In dispersed-risk activities — motorized transport, industrial emissions, ordinary social contact during an outbreak of infectious disease — the exposed group is anonymous and shifting, so at least one exposed person has not consented and could not have been asked.
(4) Therefore every dispersed-risk activity wrongs at least one person.
(5) If an activity wrongs even one person, the activity is impermissible.
(6) But some dispersed-risk activities are plainly permissible: a municipal bus service, a vaccination campaign, a wastewater treatment plant.
(7) So at least one step must be false.

I would be least willing to give up (2): it is the premise that keeps risk-bearers from being outvoted by those who benefit. I would also be very reluctant to deny (6), because I do not think the right conclusion is that we are forbidden to run buses or treat sewage. I want a diagnosis, not a set of options: trace the reasoning until it breaks, name the step or premise you reject, explain why it looked so secure, and price the rejection — what do I have to give up once I part with it, and is there a replacement principle that does the work (1) used to do? After you have chosen, tell me what would change your mind about the diagnosis, or where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
