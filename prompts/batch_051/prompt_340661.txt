I'm teaching Antigone in a philosophy of law-ish unit and I want to use it to get at sovereign immunity, but in the conceptual sense rather than the Black's Law Dictionary sense: can the state itself be a wrongdoer, or does wrongdoing always have to locate somewhere lower—in the officials or the ruler—while the state as such stays clean? The play gives us Creon issuing an edict, refusing to bend, and Thebes going along until things fall apart. Read the play through that lense for me, and be specific where you can. Does Sophocles let Thebes be wrong, or is all the wrongness concentrated in Creon the man? Is the play endorsing the idea that the sovereign's command is law simply because it's his, or is it setting that idea up to fail? Does the end restore some clean version of the state by expelling the bad king, or does it leave the state itself implicated? And does Antigone's appeal to the gods' unwritten laws amount to saying the state can be answerable to something above it even when no human court can hold it to account? I'd like a reading that does justice to the play's actual movement and is willing to say where the sovereign-immunity lens fits badly, not one that turns Creon into a legal theorist.
