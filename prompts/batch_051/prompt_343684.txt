I serve on my university's Academic Integrity and Recognition Committee, and we are now considering a case that I argued about with a friend well into last night; the argument has left me less sure that our usual reasoning will survive contact with the facts. A second-year student, Priya N., discovered that a teaching assistant had been changing students' grades in the course management system. She preserved screenshots, forwarded them to the department chair, and the tampering was undone: several grades were restored and the assistant was dismissed. The evidence we have seen, though, suggests that she acted from personal grievance rather than concern for fairness. In messages to a friend she said the assistant had humiliated her in section and that she wanted him to pay for it, and she appears to have held the evidence for weeks before sending it, finally doing so shortly after a dispute over a grade she regarded as unfair. We must decide whether she should receive the university's annual citation for exceptional integrity, which our policy describes as honoring students whose conduct shows outstanding commitment to academic honesty.

My friend's view, pressed on me over dinner, is that the committee is overcomplicating a practical matter: the university should reward the reporting of serious misconduct because that is what protects the grading system, and the costs of inspecting motives are high. On his account, whether Priya acted from righteous indignation or resentful spite makes no difference to what the institution should do, because what matters is the information she brought forward and the example her recognition would set. I find this unsatisfying. An award for integrity seems to say something about the agent and not only about the outcome of her actions, and the fact that she did the right thing for what look like the wrong reasons seems to matter a great deal. My friend replied that I was smuggling a philosopher's distinction into an administrative decision: something like acting in accordance with duty versus acting from duty, which he thinks gives no guidance to an employer, university, or other institution that must function through incentives.

Please draft the short written opinion I will circulate before our meeting next Thursday. It should recommend a concrete decision about Priya's award, with a time frame for any follow-up, and it should engage seriously with the strongest consideration on the losing side (I expect that to be the institutional-incentives argument rather than any defense of Priya's motives). It should also give the committee a usable criterion for future cases, one that separates the value of what a student has done from the student's deservingness of an integrity citation.
