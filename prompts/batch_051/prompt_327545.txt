After a career in the natural sciences, I have spent my retirement reading philosophy and history of science, and next month I am presenting to a joint seminar whose membership is split between historians and philosophers of science on one side and working physicists and mathematicians on the other. My thesis is that the standard prohibition on anachronism in the interpretation of past science conflates two distinguishable activities, and that separating them dissolves a dispute in which I currently talk past both camps.

The first activity I call reconstruction: determining what a past investigator meant, believed, aimed at, and counted as a demonstration, using only the concepts and standards available to that investigator. The second I call appraisal: determining whether what the investigator did succeeded by our own current standards. I want to defend the view that the ban on anachronism governs reconstruction but not appraisal, that reconstruction therefore must proceed without importing successor concepts such as the modern derivative or oxidation, and that retrospective appraisal is not a methodological sin but a necessary second pass that gives the history its intellectual stakes. In the talk I will use Newton's fluxional arguments and the transition from phlogiston to oxygen chemistry as my running examples.

The audience is not neutral. The historians and philosophers regard grading past science by present standards as the cardinal error and suspect that a retired scientist cannot keep modern categories out of reconstruction. The physicists and mathematicians regard that contextualism as a refusal to say plainly that past science was wrong, and they will expect the appraisal pass to carry most of the weight. I can already see that each camp will accept only half of my position and attack the other half.

What I want from you is a preparation sheet for the question period. Give me the objections I will actually receive from this audience, in roughly the order they will come, phrased as each group would actually phrase them; for each, give a two-minute reply, state what I should concede and what I should hold, and note where the reply is weakest. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks. And please commit: do not hand me a balanced survey of possible positions. Take a definite view of where my thesis is genuinely vulnerable, which objections I should concede more than I would like, and which part of my position I should prepare to defend rather than hope nobody notices.
