In the Grand Inquisitor's indictment as Ivan relates it in The Brothers Karamazov, the case for taking freedom from ordinary people depends on a particular kind of ruler: someone who has faced freedom, judged it unbearable for most, and deliberately assumed its burden out of love, supplying the bread, mystery, and settled authority that spare the many from the anguish of choosing. Suppose the arrangement changes: the burden of choosing is now relieved not by a small circle of knowing custodians but by impersonal systems — defaults, curated options, risk scores, bureaucratic routines — in which even the operators defer to metrics and no identifiable person has weighed the cost of freedom and chosen to bear it. Does the Grand Inquisitor's argument survive this development, need repair, or collapse? Identify the specific premise under pressure, and say whether a weaker but still serious version of the case remains.
