I keep having the same argument with a friend who trades for a living, and it always bottoms out in the same place: he thinks the price on a liquid prediction market is the single best estimate of the truth we have — better than any expert, better than a poll, better than the sharpest individual trader. I'm not sure he's wrong, but I'm suspicious. The no-trade line of thinking appeals to me: if a bunch of rational people with a common prior and private information genuinely disagree, the disagreement should wash out; roughly, they wouldn't trade purely on information, so a trade reveals something non-epistemic going on underneath (different risk appetites, liquidity needs, fees, noise). But real markets have noise traders, herding, correlated mistakes, and people who can't short. So the claim I want to pin down is this: take a reasonably liquid prediction market on something concrete, like "will the US enter a recession in the next twelve months." What is your actual credence that the market's price is, in expectation, closer to the truth than the subjective probability of the single best individual forecaster — where "best" means the forecaster we'd identify in hindsight?

Give me a number, and decompose it properly: how much rides on whether real markets approximate the no-trade ideal, how much on whether the best individual forecaster is even identifiable in advance, how much on correlated errors and herding, how much on the reference-class problem for "closer to the truth" when the event happens exactly once. I don't want a lecture on why it depends; give me your actual number. Put rougly a probability on each sub-question and show how they combine. Then tell me which term you're least certain about and what evidence would move it the most.
