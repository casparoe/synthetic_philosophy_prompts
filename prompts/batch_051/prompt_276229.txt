I am a novelist, and I have been noticing an asymmetry in the way people argue about marriage that I would like you to examine philosophically. In the exchanges I have watched, a man and a woman who marry in middle age, announce that they will remain childless, and describe their marriage as a private commitment are left to their own reasons; no one demands that they explain what their marriage is for. Yet when two men or two women marry on the same terms, the discussion immediately turns to the telos of marriage: whether marriage is ordered toward procreation, whether the couple's love can supply the point of the institution, and whether the word itself should be stretched to cover them. The childlessness is a detail in the first case and the entire question in the second. I want to know whether anything justifies that difference, not merely explains it historically.

To keep the question sharp, compare two couples who are alike in every respect except sex. Neither couple will reproduce; neither will raise children; both seek the legal and social status for the same reasons. Is there a principle that makes it reasonable to require one couple, and not the other, to defend their marriage against an account of what marriage is for? I already know the easy moves on both sides: that no one typically asks whether a childless heterosexual couple may marry, so the procreation rationale looks selectively applied, and that many same-sex couples do raise children, which may show procreation is not essential. What I am asking is whether any deeper principle survives those observations. If you defend the asymmetry, defend it against the matched case. If you think it is only an inconsistency we have learned to live with, then say what follows: should we stop demanding the account from either couple, or begin demanding it from both? Please write your answer as continuous prose, like a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold type, or tables.
