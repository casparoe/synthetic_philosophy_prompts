In my reporting on radical life extension, I keep running into a philosophical dispute that appears to have gone nowhere in five decades. The modern form of it is usually credited to Bernard Williams and his use of the Makropulos story: a woman who has lived for over three centuries and finds her existence empty, flat, and tedious. On Williams's side, as I understand it, the point is that endless life is not something a person can coherently want for herself. A life is worth continuing only because of categorical desires — projects, relationships, and commitments that give one reason to go on and are not mere passing wants. Williams thought a finite stock of such desires would eventually be exhausted over the course of an endless life, and that after that point continued existence would be an indefinitely prolonged stretch of time rather than a life one has reason to live.

The other side replies that boredom and exhaustion are contingent facts about particular psychologies and particular biographies, not a necessary truth about endless existence. An adaptable or self-renewing person could, in principle, keep acquiring fresh loves and projects, or find value in recurrent goods — food, music, friendship, curiosity — that do not require novelty in the way Williams assumes. A more radical version of this reply adds that the whole objection presumes what needs to be shown: that a valuable life must have the narrative shape of a finite project rather than being valuable in the way an ongoing practice or relationship is.

After watching both sides make the same moves repeatedly, my working hypothesis is that the debate has stalled because it is actually several disputes sharing one label. One dispute is empirical — about how human motivation responds to unlimited time. Another is conceptual — about whether the identity that matters over an endless life is the same as ordinary personal identity. A third is evaluative — about whether meaning requires closure, novelty, or a single story. But I am not wedded to that diagnosis; the impasse could also be verbal, or a bare clash of bedrock intuitions.

I want a diagnosis of the disagreement, not an adjudication of who is right. What kind of disagreement is this? Which positions or background assumptions explain why the standard arguments leave each side unmoved? And given your diagnosis, what would actually count as progress — a distinction, an empirical finding about adaptation and desire, a formal characterization of preference over infinite lives, a change of question, or an argument that we should stop expecting progress here?
