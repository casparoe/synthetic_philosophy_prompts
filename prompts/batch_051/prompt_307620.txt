I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I recently encountered a debate in the philosophy of action that I suspect I am misreading because I keep translating it into engineering terms. I would like you to do that translation deliberately, and then tell me what it fails to preserve.

The debate concerns whether a person can assign probabilities to their own choices while they are still making them. One position, sometimes called the crowding-out view, holds that from the standpoint of someone deliberating about what to do, there is something defective about simultaneously treating a prospective action as an object to be predicted and as a live option still to be settled. The rough argument is this. Assigning a probability to your own future action means taking an evidential stance toward yourself: you are treating the action as a fact about the world that can be estimated. Deliberation, by contrast, treats the action as still open, in the sense that there is no fact about what you will do fixed in advance of your deciding it. Assigning probability 1 would not be prediction at all, but a completed decision. Assigning a definite intermediate probability, such as 0.6, also seems to smuggle in a finished picture of yourself as an agent who is already 60 percent likely to do the thing, when the point of deliberating is precisely that you are the one who settles which kind of case you are in. On a strong reading, the view says self-assigned probabilities of this sort are incoherent, or at least that they cannot be made without abandoning the deliberative standpoint. On a weaker reading, the probabilities are possible, but a rational agent cannot feed them back into the very deliberation they describe without a kind of self-reference that ordinary decision-theoretic reasoning is poorly equipped to handle.

I want you to restate this position in the vocabulary I think in fluently, and I would like the translation to be technical rather than watered down. The vocabulary is: branch prediction and speculative execution; the distinction between a predictor that models a running process and the process itself that executes; programs that can inspect their own source, read their own past outputs, or call functions that model their own future behavior; feedback loops between a component and a model of that component; stale models versus live state; and the difference between computing a value and predicting what value a computation will produce.

Then give me a residue report. Specifically: which claims of the philosophical position map cleanly onto software phenomena; which claims you had to bend or fudge to get them to fit; and which claims have no counterpart in the software vocabulary at all. I want an explicit judgment on whether a software engineer's natural intuition about this debate is adequate. My working intuition is that self-prediction is just a fixed-point or feedback problem, and that the philosophers have mistaken an engineering difficulty for a deep impossibility, but I can feel that the translation may be losing exactly the part that matters. Tell me whether the interesting claim is the one that survives the translation or the one that does not.
