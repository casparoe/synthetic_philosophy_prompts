About a year ago I moved to a small town and joined a congregation in the Reformed tradition whose public worship is ordered by the regulative principle: in the church's gathered worship, whatever Scripture does not command is forbidden. The elders may arrange the circumstances of worship—time, place, seating, lighting, amplification—but may not introduce uncommanded elements. Since the congregation has no settled minister, its officers have begun supplying the pulpit by playing video recordings of sermons preached several years earlier by an ordained minister in another city, and I have recently been asked to serve on a small committee charged with deciding whether the practice may continue.

Two distinctions govern our discussions. The first is the threefold test for whether a practice is commanded: it must be warranted by an explicit scriptural command, by an approved apostolic example, or by a consequence drawn from Scripture by good and necessary inference. The second is the distinction between elements and circumstances of worship: the elements are the commanded acts themselves—the reading and preaching of the Word, prayer, the sacraments, and the singing of praise—while circumstances are the changeable conditions under which those acts are performed.

I want you to work through this case rather than merely pronounce on it. Apply the threefold test and the element/circumstance distinction one by one and say whether a pre-recorded sermon played to a gathered congregation counts as the commanded element of preaching, as an uncommanded addition to worship, or as a circumstance of preaching. Identify the exact point at which the criteria underdetermine the verdict—most importantly, whether a minister's living, simultaneous presence is part of the essence of preaching or only a circumstance of it. Finally, say whether this case reveals that the element/circumstance distinction needs to be revised or supplemented, or whether the regulative principle can handle a novel case like this without falling back on an ad hoc judgment of the kind the principle was designed to prevent.
