I've went back and forth on the dogmatism paradox and decided to lay out what I take to be the underlying argument, to see where exactly it goes wrong. Take an ordinary bit of knowledge: on Tuesday I know that my friend spent all of Monday in Berlin. (1) If I know that p, then p is true. (2) So, if I know that p, any evidence I later get that suggests not-p is misleading: p is true, and evidence that suggests not-p points away from the truth, which is just what it is for evidence to be misleading. (3) Therefore, on Tuesday I can already know that a particular piece of future counterevidence, like a dated photo of my friend in Madrid on Monday turning up on Wednesday, will be misleading. (4) If I know that a piece of evidence is misleading, then when it actually arrives I can rationally disregard it, and it shouldn't make me any less justified in believing p. (5) So when the photo turns up on Wednesday, I can shrug it off and keep believing p with just the same justification as before. (6) Nothing was special about this case, so in general: for anything I know, I can rationally ignore counterevidence to it.

This conclusion seems like it can't be right, since people do lose knowledge when new evidence comes in. But I can't find the bad step. I have a vague feeling the problem is in (4) or (5), maybe something about my evidence situation being different on Wednesday than it was on Tuesday, but I can't make the objection precise. Is the argument valid? If not, exactly which step fails, and under what reading? If it is valid, do I just have to accept that known propositions can never be rationally threatened by new evidence?
