I'm a science journalist writing a long essay about what the search for extraterrestrial intelligence actually assumes, before a single confirmed signal is in hand. The practice has a striking asymmetry worth unpacking. On one side, SETI listens: it scans the sky for narrowband radio signals or brief optical pulses, on the reasoning that these would be signatures of technology rather than ordinary astrophysical processes. It also maintains protocols for what to do after a candidate detection — verify with independent instruments, keep the result confidential during verification, and consult before making any public announcement. On the other side, it transmits almost nothing. Deliberate messages to the stars are rare, small, and contested; the very project of sending them, sometimes called METI, is the subject of a live argument about whether advertising our existence is wise or reckless.

What theory of intelligence, communication, and value does this patterned difference presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory that makes the practice sensible. It seems to assume that an alien mind would show up as a technological civilization, that it would use electromagnetic channels we can monitor, that it would either leak or intentionally emit something we could recognize as a signal, and that receiving such a signal would be worth the effort and the institutional caution. Distinguish which of these assumptions the practice strictly needs, which are merely pragmatic bets, and which are philosophically underdetermined. Then turn to the alternatives: intelligence that does not communicate, minds that are post-biological or embodied in forms we would not recognize, signals indistinguishable from physical processes. Say what the practice's implicit commitments would have to look like under each alternative, and whether SETI's way of searching quietly commits it to a particular view of what a mind is.
