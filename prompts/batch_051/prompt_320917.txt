I co-host a podcast on the governance of advanced AI, and we are about to record an episode with a political scientist whose recent work applies Fearon's rationalist framework for war to AI races among leading labs and states. Her central claim is that the three mechanisms Fearon uses to explain why rational states sometimes end up in costly and inefficient wars — private information together with incentives to misrepresent, credible-commitment problems, and issue indivisibility — also explain why parties who would all prefer to avoid a race nonetheless get pulled into one, and that policy should be organized around this diagnosis rather than around looser arms-race or coordination metaphors. She is a formal-theoretic type, not a polemicist, and her signature move is to absorb objections by saying that the framework was built to explain bargaining breakdowns in general, so questions that merely point out that a development race is not literally a war will not land. Our audience already knows the shape of Fearon's argument, so the interview should not spend time restating it.

Please draft eight to ten questions that push her where the application is least comfortable. Include at least one question on each of the three mechanisms, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. On private information, press whether the opacity of the other side's capabilities and intentions is really strategic misrepresentation, or instead structural uncertainty of a kind the actors themselves share, and ask what happens to the rationalist diagnosis if it is the latter. On commitment problems, press whether a first-mover advantage in AI is durable enough to do the preventive-war work the mechanism requires, given how quickly personnel, techniques, and even trained-model knowledge leak and how globally distributed compute has become, and whether the binding commitment problem might concern the gap between capability and safety rather than the technology itself. On indivisibility, press what exactly is supposed to be indivisible — a decisive strategic advantage, the fact of being first, or the crossing of some capability threshold — and why the usual rationalist remedy of side payments, such as shared model access, compute allocations, or civilian carve-outs, cannot divide the stakes. Also include questions on where the fit is weakest: the unitary-actor assumption in a field fragmented among private labs, state security bureaucracies, and individual researchers; the possibility that non-rationalist drivers such as status, hype, market pressure, and ideological competition do the actual causal work; and the awkward fact that the framework's policy implication flips depending on which mechanism is doing the work, since transparency can relieve an information problem while aggravating a commitment problem. Close with a question about what evidence would count against the framework as applied to AI, for example a race averted by unilateral restraint or dampened by mutual vulnerability rather than by any bargain. Order the questions as a cumulative line of pressure rather than a list of independent prompts, so that early answers set up later difficulties.
