In Bernard Williams's discussion of the Makropulos case, Elina Makropulos, after more than three centuries of unnaturally prolonged life, lets herself die because her existence has become unbearable tedium. The reading of the essay I was given says Williams takes this to be the inescapable fate of any endless life. The argument, as it was explained to me, runs like this: a person's reason to go on living is supplied by categorical desires, the projects and commitments that are not merely conditional on being alive; these desires are finite and exhaustible; once they have all been fulfilled or abandoned, nothing is left except conditional desires, which cannot give a reason to continue. New categorical desires cannot save the situation, because taking on a substantially new set of them would make the resulting person too different for it to count as the same life going on. On this reading, Williams has not merely shown that one kind of temperament would find immortality tedious; he has given the materials for a proof that eternal bliss is incoherent. However heaven is arranged, after enough time the saved would exhaust every categorical desire available to them, and their endless life would cease to be desirable, which is a contradiction in terms. I want to know the most defensible response for someone who regards Williams's paper as basically sound but still wants to hold that heaven is coherent. Do not survey the options; tell me which premise or step in the reconstructed argument you would reject or qualify, and give a definite answer to whether the doctrine of eternal bliss survives once that correction is made. At the very end, add a single line of the form \answer{...} with a one-phrase statement of your position.
