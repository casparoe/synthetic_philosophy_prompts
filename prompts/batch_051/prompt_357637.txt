I keep coming back to an idea I can't shake, and I want someone to tell me if its actually any good. I've noticed that most of the thinking advice I've ever gotten comes in two forms: either stories about famous thinkers getting things wrong, or lists of biases and fallacies. Neither one changes what I do when I'm tired, under time pressure, and actually have to make a call. So I've been toying with what I call decision katas. In martial arts, a kata is a fixed sequence you practice until the movement happens without having to think about it, then you test it in sparring, then, supposedly, it's there in a real fight. I think good judgment might be trainable the same way, except the sequence isn't a physical movement but a small thinking move: pull apart the base rate from the specific story you were told, or imagine a project has already failed and work backwards to what probably killed it. You drill one move at a time with made-up cases, ten times a day, thirty seconds each, slowly at first and then with time pressure and noise. Then you do low-stake sparring: real decisions where the cost of being wrong is small, and you force yourself to use just that one move. Big decisions come last, and only after the move has survived some cheap contact.

Here's the part I think might be new, and also the part I'm least sure about. The drill isn't for the reasoning itself, since most people can do the reasoning once it's pointed out. The drill is for the noticing. In a real conversation, the hard thing is recognizing that this is a moment to pull back and use the outside view, or that the vivid example someone just gave you is doing far more work than it deserves. So I set up the katas to train the trigger, not the conclusion. But I keep getting stuck on transfer. In martial arts, lots of people think katas are nearly useless unless the sparring is realistic, and I can't tell whether the decision-making version of sparring even exists. Maybe low-stakes decisions are just too different from the ones that actually matter for any of it to carry over. So here's what I'm asking. Give me the strongest three or four objections to this whole idea, and tell me honestly whether any of them is fatal rather than just annoying. Is this just deliberate practice or transfer of learning wearing a cheap costume, or is there actually something of its own in it? And give me the clearest example of a real decision where training someone this way would either backfire or just not move the needle.
