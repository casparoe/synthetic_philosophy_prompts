Last winter I had to decide whether to let a failing honeybee colony die slowly in the cold or to kill it quickly, and I realized I did not know what I was killing. I kept moving between two ways of thinking: the welfare of the individual bees and the welfare of the hive. The second may be pure metaphor, but I want to know whether it refers to anything. I already treat an individual bee as a partial moral patient and a thermostat as none; the hive is the case I cannot place on that gradient.

Here are the criteria I have been using for what counts as a moral patient, meaning a thing that can be harmed rather than merely damaged:

(i) a unified point of view: there is a single perspective to which its states belong;
(ii) valence: some of its states are better or worse for it;
(iii) flexible, goal-directed adjustment: it changes behavior to avoid worse states and reach better ones, not by fixed reflex alone;
(iv) a damage/harm gap: when it is damaged, the damage matters to it, shown in compensation, avoidance learning, trade-offs, or distress.

The borderline case is the colony itself. A honeybee hive holds its brood nest near 35°C by collectively fanning when hot and clustering when cold. When attacked, guard bees release alarm pheromone and the whole nest shifts into defense. If the queen dies, workers begin rearing replacements within hours and the colony's collective behavior changes until a new queen is laying. When the hive grows crowded, it arrives at a collective decision to swarm through a distributed quorum of waggle-dancing scouts; no individual bee makes the decision. None of this runs through a central nervous system. It is distributed across thousands of small brains, chemical signals, and local interactions.

Does the colony, as a colony, qualify as a moral patient by criteria (i)–(iv)? Work through each criterion honestly. Where it fails, tell me whether the colony fails the criterion or the criterion is malformed; in particular, I suspect (i) may rule out distributed subjects by definition, and I want to know whether that is a discovery or a stipulation. Does this case force me to revise the criteria themselves, perhaps by treating sentience and moral status as graded properties of processes rather than as something a unified subject has? And since a thermostat also maintains a set point and reacts to cold, say what exactly separates the hive from the thermostat.

End by telling me which consideration would be most likely to change your verdict, whether a fact about colony organization, a counterexample to one of the criteria, or an objection to the whole patient-versus-machine framing, and where your reasoning is least confident.
