I'm a novelist finishing a book set in London in the months the South Sea Company stock ran up and then collapsed in 1720. The machinery of that year carries the plot, and I've been reading George Berkeley's The Querist, the book of numbered questions he published in three parts in the 1730s, alongside his shorter essay from the crash's aftermath, An Essay towards Preventing the Ruin of Great Britain. I find his way of thinking about money strange and attractive in equal measure: he asks, in effect, whether money in its true idea is only a ticket or counter for reckoning and transferring the industry of a people, whether a nation's wealth consists in the industry and consumption of its inhabitants rather than in its hoard of gold and silver, and whether credit honestly managed might accomplish everything coin does, and more cleanly. I want to test that idea against the Bubble itself, not merely borrow its vocabulary.

Analyze the South Sea Bubble as a case for Berkeley's account of money and credit. Walk me through it in both directions. By the lights of The Querist, what would Berkeley say the actual actors faced and should have done — the Company directors, the subscribers, the Treasury, the Bank of England, ordinary buyers of stock, even the holders of the national debt who were persuaded to convert into shares? Where does the theory genuinely illuminate the episode, and where does it pass over or misdescribe what was happening — for instance the difference between credit circulating among productive trades and credit concentrated in a speculative stock, or the way the scheme depended on converting government debt into company equity rather than on new industry? Then turn the lens around: does the Bubble confirm Berkeley's diagnosis that England had mistaken metal for wealth and misdirected its credit, or does it show that his confidence in paper as such was part of the danger? I want the two-directional mapping to be honest and disciplined, because I intend to put these tensions into the minds of my characters rather than into a lecture.
