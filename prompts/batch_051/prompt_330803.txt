I'm a philosophy professor, and I'm teaching a grad seminar on decision theory and formal epistemology this semester. The students keep bringing me pop summaries of Ellsberg, and one of my student's pasted me this paragraph from an economics explainer site:

"In the early 1960s Daniel Ellsberg ran a simple experiment showing that people are irrational about uncertainty. He offered subjects a bet on drawing a red ball from an urn with 50 red and 50 black balls, or from an urn with 100 red and black balls in unknown proportions, and people overwhelmingly chose the known urn. Then he offered the same subjects a bet on drawing a black ball from the same two urns, and again they chose the known urn. Ellsberg called this the Ellsberg paradox and claimed it disproved expected utility theory: since people preferred the fifty-fifty urn both times, they were effectively assigning less than one-half probability to red and to black in the unknown urn, which is impossible. The upshot is that ambiguity aversion is just a cognitive bias, like loss aversion, and rational people should force themselves to treat unknown probabilities as if they were fifty-fifty."

I know enough to know this is off somewhere — I'm inclined to say the last line about treating unknown probabilities as fifty-fifty is overblown — but I can't put my finger on exactly where the slide happens. I'd like an actual sentence-by-sentence audit: what would a decision theorist accept, what would make her wince, and what's just wrong. And is the final claim, that ambiguity aversion is a mere bias and rational agents should use 50/50 priors, something that follows from anything correct in the summary? Also, is this two-urn version a faithful stand-in for what Ellsberg actually set up, or does the simplification already smuggle something in? No need for citations; I want the conceptual cleanup.
