Me and my coworker keep having the same fight. Every time I bring up algorithms that try to learn cause-and-effect from observational data -- like those Bayesian network / causal graph things -- he says "correlation is not causation" and acts like the discussion is over. I get the obvious cases: ice cream sales and drownings both go up in summer, but buying ice cream doesn't cause drowning. Fine.

But the more I read about Bayesian networks, the less sure I am that the slogan does the work he thinks it does. The way I understand it, you take a bunch of variables, map out every conditional independence that shows up in the data, and then look for graphs that imply exactly those independences. Two assumptions keep coming up. One is faithfulness: the pattern of independences is not a fluke where two paths cancel out and make things look independent when they aren't. The other is minimality: out of the graphs that fit, you keep the one with the fewest extra edges. Under those assumptions it seems like the data pins down a whole lot -- some edges get forced, some arrow directions get forced -- even though nobody ran an experiment. Doesn't that mean you can squeeze causal information out of purely statistical data, providing you assume the world isn't conspiring to hide the causal structure from you?

So what I want is a teardown of the slogan. What are the different precise claims someone could be making with "correlation is not causation," from the trivially true to the genuinely false? Which of those claims would have to be true for it to sink the Bayesian network approach, and which are compatible with it? And for each precise claim, does it survive once faithfulness and minimality are on the table? Lay it out like a menu, so I can show him which version he keeps invoking and which version I was never denying.
