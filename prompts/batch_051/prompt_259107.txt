I'm teaching a philosophy of language unit that ends by considering how Austinian speech act theory handles institutionalized speech, and I want to use Arthur Miller's The Crucible as the literary test case. I'd like you to write a critical reading of the play through the lens of Austin's theory of performative utterances — the distinction among locutionary, illocutionary, and perlocutionary acts, and the conditions under which a performative succeeds, such as an established procedure, the right persons and circumstances, correct execution, sincerity, and uptake. The play already seems to me like a kind of laboratory of speech acts: the girls' accusations, the court's questioning, the confessions and the refusals to confess all turn on who has the standing to speak, what the community is prepared to do with the words, and how the court's procedure fixes what has thereby been done. I'm especially interested in two places where the fit with Austin might strain. First, Austin tends to treat uptake as the audience's recognition of the act being performed; in Salem, uptake looks more like mass belief or social enforcement, and the play may suggest that once uptake is organized by fear, the truth or sincerity of a speech act stops determining what the words actually do in that community. Second, Austin's framework is built mainly around individual speakers performing conventional acts like promising or betting; the play may show that the decisive performative power belongs to collectives — the girls as a group, the court as an institution, the town as audience — in a way the individualistic examples do not capture.

I'd like a reading that is faithful in both directions: use the theory to illuminate the accusations, the signing and tearing of confessions, and the court's procedures, but also say where the play resists the theory, where the analogy between a Salem confession and an ordinary performative is strained, and what Miller's dramatization contributes to the philosophy that abstract cases cannot. Please avoid quoting from the play; paraphrase the scenes you discuss. At the end, say how confident you are in the reading as a whole and single out the point you are least sure about, whether it is an interpretive claim about the play or a philosophical claim about speech acts.
