I'm a PhD student in moral philosophy, working on the ethics of historical judgment. I've been trying to formalize a colleague's man-of-his-time defence of Jefferson, Churchill, and Columbus, and the result seems to prove far more than he intended. I would be grateful for a diagnosis.

Here is the argument:

(1) If a person's action conformed to the moral norms prevailing in their society at the time, then the person is not blameworthy for that action.
(2) Jefferson's slaveholding, Churchill's racial hierarchy, and Columbus's conquest all conformed to prevailing norms of their respective times and places.
(3) Therefore, Jefferson, Churchill, and Columbus are not blameworthy for those actions.
(4) If the participants in a practice are not blameworthy, then the practice itself cannot rightly be judged wrong in a way that licenses moral criticism of them.
(5) By parity, whenever a practice is widely accepted in its own time, no one is blameworthy for it, and the practice cannot rightly be judged wrong.
(6) But slavery and colonial dispossession were wrong, and later generations rightly judge them as wrong.
(7) So the man-of-his-time defence proves too much: consistently applied, it makes trans-historical moral judgment impossible, since judging a past practice wrong collapses into condemning people for failing to transcend their time.

I want to reject (7), because I cannot give up (6), and I also find (1) antecedently plausible. Which step should be rejected, and what exactly does rejecting it commit me to? If the argument is valid, tell me which premise is least costly to give up.
