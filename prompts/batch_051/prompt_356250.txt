I've been going back and forth with a friend about Camus and capital punishment. My friend is convinced that if Camus were alive today he'd be one of the loudest voices in the innocence movement: the DNA exonerations, the people who spent decades on death row and then walked free, all that. And maybe. But I've got a nagging feeling it isn't that simple, because as I read him Camus's case against the guillotine in the 1950s wasnt really built on "the system gets the wrong guy sometimes." He seemed to think the state's execution was a premeditated, cold-blooded killing even when the condemned person was guilty, and that the routine, mechanical way the guillotine did its work was itself part of what made the death penalty obscene. It was an argument against the thing in principle, not just against its errors.

So when I imagine dropping him into, say, 2005 or 2015, when innocence cases are making headlines and lethal injection is marketed as the humane upgrade, I genuinely don't know what he'd do with it. Could he think the focus on wrongful convictions quietly concedes that killing the right person cleanly would be acceptable? And would he see lethal injection as progress — less pain, less spectacle — or as something worse: a medicalized, almost calm killing that lets the public stop flinching without changing what is actually being done? So here's the question. What do you think Camus would have made of these two developments, the wave of DNA exonerations since the 1990s and the turn toward lethal injection? Would he have treated them as weapons for abolition, as traps for abolitionism, or one of each? Reconstruct what the most Camusian position would be, and be explicit about which parts of his actual views on premeditated state killing and the guillotine pull in which direction, and where you're least confident in the reconstruction.
