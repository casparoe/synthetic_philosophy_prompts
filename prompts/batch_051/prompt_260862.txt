Last night I spent two hours arguing with my friend Dana, who's a firmly consequentalist, about what I've seen called the paradox of deontology. Her line was: if I refuse to break some constraint (kill the one, falsify the record, break the promise) because breaching it would wrong someone, then when my refusal means four other people get wronged in the exact same way, I'm apparently committed to there being more of the very thing I said was intolerable. I kept saying there's a difference between what I do and what I merely allow, but out loud it kept sounding like I just didn't want to get my own hands dirty, and that's not the reason I beleive. I'd like my students to feel the pull of both sides, not just hear me caricature it afterwards.

I teach an upper-level undergraduate ethics seminar, around 15 students, and I have one 80-minute session set aside. Can you design a classroom activity — a game, role-play, or structured deliberation with rules and maybe token stakes, no real money — that makes them run into the paradox themselves before anyone names it? The setup should get them to a point where, in the role, most of them refuse to violate a rule themselves, and then the debrief shows them that their collective refusal produced a world where the same rule got violated more often — and then ask whether they'd really endorse the world with more violations. I want rules, roles, a timeline, the materials (or what to write on index cards), and a debrief that maps what happened to the tension between first-person constraints and the third-person "count the violations" standpoint. Please also say what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be honest about which feature of the real paradox the activity can't reproduce and the facilitator has to supply. It's fine to use pocket-ethics versions of the dilemma (lying, promises, breaking a group norm) as long as the stakes still feel real in the room.

Keep the writeup in American English. At the end, tell me how confident you are in the design and single out the point you're least sure about, whether that's a part of the activity that might flop or a place where the mapping to the paradox is looser than I'd want.
