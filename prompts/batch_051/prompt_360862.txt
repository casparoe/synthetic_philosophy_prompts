In my European history course we have just finished the French Revolution, and as my teacher moved from the Terror to Thermidor she insisted that the whole sequence was best captured by the old line that "revolutions devour their children." She was not just being rhetorical: she used the phrase to imply that the fall of Robespierre and the turn of the Revolution against so many of its earlier leaders was close to inevitable, something built into revolution itself rather than the result of war, currency collapse, factional infighting, or specific decisions. The line seems to be doing real explanatory work, but I cannot tell what exactly it asserts.

I would like you to disassemble that one-liner. What distinct precise claims could someone be making when they say "revolutions devour their children"? For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible empirical or historical claim, or likely false, and what kind of evidence would have to support it. Then focus on the reading my teacher needed: which precise version of the slogan, if true, would actually support the claim that Thermidor was the predictable product of revolutionary dynamics rather than a contingent outcome? Finally, connect the readings to the standard interpretive camps on the French Revolution—a class-struggle interpretation, an ideology-and-political-culture interpretation, and a contingency-centered interpretation—and say which camp is most entitled to use the slogan, which camp should reject it as misleading, and why.
