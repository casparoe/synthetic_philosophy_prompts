In my psychology class, we discussed the WEIRD problem: most of the experimental evidence about human moral psychology comes from participants who are Western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic, and this may have made psychologists mistake culturally peculiar patterns for universal ones. I have been trying to work out how much that matters for moral objectivity, so I wrote myself a test for deciding when a cross-cultural difference in moral judgment is deep enough to put pressure on objectivity, rather than superficial. My criteria are these. (1) Same target: the two groups are evaluating the same act in the same circumstances, with no relevant factual disagreement between them. (2) Persistence: the disagreement survives each side understanding the other side's rationale; it does not dissolve on reflection. (3) Independence: the difference is not explained by a non-moral disagreement, such as one side believing the act has supernatural consequences. (4) Not mere application: the two groups are not simply applying a single shared principle to different local conditions or social structures. When a difference meets all four, I have been treating it as a deep moral difference.

I want to test this test on a case I invented. Two small farming communities face each other across a river. The upstream community is integrated into a market economy and its members regularly cooperate with strangers under contract. The downstream community is organized around kinship and fixed gift-exchange partnerships between families, and contacts with outsiders are rare and carefully managed. In each community, a person walking home at dusk sees a child from the other community running toward a damaged footbridge. The person knows the bridge is unsafe, knows that a short shout would stop the child, and says nothing; the child is injured. In the upstream community, the passerby is blamed for failing to warn. In the downstream community, the passerby is not blamed. Both communities agree on the facts. In the upstream community, people say the passerby had a duty to warn anyone in danger. In the downstream community, people say a warning would have been a gift, that gifts are owed only within one's exchange partnerships, and that the child's family should have kept the child away from a stranger's bridge. Everyone agrees that a passerby who belonged to the child's own community would have been required to warn, and that the shout would have cost the passerby nothing.

Is this a deep moral difference by my four criteria, or does it fail one or more of them? Go through the criteria one by one, and where the verdict is unclear, explain why. Finally, tell me whether the case reveals that any of my criteria is too vague or doing too much work, and how I should revise the test.
