I'm a novelist, and I'm writing a book set in a city-state that runs its public archive by a rule my characters call the "living voice." I keep going back and forth on whether the institution actually makes sence, and I'd like you to stress-test it with me before I comit to it in the plot.

The rule works like this. The Civic Archive accepts almost any document anyone brings in — letters, deeds, diaries, account books, gossip sheets, songs written on scraps — and it never deliberately destroys anything. But a document only stays in the "bright room," where it can be read and cited, as long as at least one living person formally claims it. Claiming means showing up in person at the registry once a year, on the anniversary of the day the document was deposited, and saying its number and title aloud in front of a clerk. There's no ownership or literacy requirement: anyone can claim any document, one person can claim as many documents as they like, and the register shows every document with its current claimants, so claims are public. If a document has no claimant for a full year, it's lowered into the "dumb cellar" and never comes back out; nothing down there can be read, but a list of what's buried is posted on the door. Archivists and registry clerks can't claim documents while they work there. And — the part that matters most for my plot — the city's courts and guilds only count bright documents as valid evidence of a debt, a title, a marriage, or a pedigree.

I'm less interested in whether this is a good or bad idea in theory, and more in how it would actually behave over three generations. Act as an institutional historian who also thinks like a philosopher about records and power. Start by brainstorming the moving parts out loud, then walk me through it carefully: who gets recorded and who goes silent; which documents get claimed and which fall into the cellar first; how rich families, guilds, clerks, and anyone with a grudge would game it; what people stop writing down or write differently because of the rule; what it does to the city's legal and economic life; and where the first catastrophic failure happens. End by telling me the smallest patch that fixes the worst failure without gutting whatever point about memory the city thinks it's making.
