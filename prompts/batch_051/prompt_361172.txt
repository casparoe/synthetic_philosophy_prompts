Just got back from a long argument with a friend about low-impact AI, and Ive got a parallel stuck in my head that I cant decide is deep or just me pattern-matching. Heres the setup as we argued it. The usual move for getting an agent to do a task without wrecking everything else is to add an impact penalty: pursue the goal, but keep the world close to an inaction baseline -- the world as it would have been if the agent had never acted. Trying to make that precise immediately hits two problems: you need a counterfactual baseline (which of the many "would have happened anyway" futures is the reference?) and you need a distance metric between worlds, and both look like they smuggle in a whole account of what matters. My friend thinks that kills the project: you cant define side effects without already having the full value specification, so the penalty is either empty or its just ordinary utility maximization with extra steps.

Then I started thinking about tort law. Compensatory damages are supposed to put the injured person back where they would have been had the wrong never happened. That is also a counterfactual baseline with the bad act removed, and its supposed to work without a general theory of what a good life is. And tort law has hit versions of both problems. The but-for test chokes on overdetermination: two negligently started fires merge and destroy a house, and each defendant says the house would have burned anyway without him. The baseline is underdetermined too: maybe the plaintiffs business would have failed next month regardless, so which baseline world are you restoring? Courts handled this by fudging the counterfactual in rule-like ways, most clearly the lost-chance doctrine in medical negligence, where some jurisdictions award a fraction of the full damages equal to the chance the negligence destroyed, even though there is no single possible world in which the patient would probably have recovered. Thats replacing a point-valued baseline with a distribution over possible baselines and then picking a rule for aggregating them.

So heres the parallel: impact penalties and tort damages both treat a bad intervention as distance from an unobserved counterfactual world, both need a similarity metric doing evaluative work, and both get patched by moving from a single baseline to a distribution. Am I looking at one underlying problem, or two problems that happen to share some words? Give me the mapping first, then stress-test it. What would have to be true on both sides for tort laws fixes to transfer to impact measures, and what is one thing the analogy predicts that turns out false if you tried to actually build the penalty? My friend would say the analogy dies at the last step because a court has a human judge with background norms about which harms matter, and thats exactly what the penalty was trying to replace -- is he right, or does that just show the penalty needs something court-like? And since I keep flipping between the two readings, tell me what would change your verdict: what argument or case would push you from deep to superficial or the other way, and where is the reasoning most likely to fail?
