Next month I'm presenting at my lab's quarterly safety review, and I need to prep for the Q&A. The talk argues that for the models we'll train in the next year or so, deployment should be gated on control evals rather than on positive alignment evidence. My argument, in short: (1) alignment evidence can't distinguish a genuinely helpful model from one that's helpful because it's the best strategy for now, since both produce the same train-time and eval-time behavior; (2) a control eval is different in kind because we can take a red-team model and explicitly reward it for defeating our protocol — sandbox, monitors, audits — and then check whether we catch it; a real misaligned model is presumably at least as threatening as that red team, so the eval actually measures our ability to catch bad behavior, which alignment evals don't; (3) therefore deployment should be treated as a security question ("can we contain this if its trying?") rather than a psychology question ("does it want to help?"). The room will be about a dozen people: mostly alignment researchers who do interpretability and RLHF and think of control as a band-aid you slap on a model you don't understand, plus two security engineers who will think in terms of attacker capability, false negatives, and cost to the defender, and one policy person who keeps asking what any of this buys a regulator. I can't assume anyone has bought into the control framing, even if they've read around it.

Give me a Q&A prep sheet, not a restatement of my talk. For each expected objection: exactly how they'd put it (the interp people will sound like philosophers, the security people will talk about threat models and failure rates), how likely it is and how damaging, a two-minute reply, what I should concede up front, and where the reply is softest. Order them. Include the question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks, with the best honest answer I could give. And end by telling me which one objection, if the room really made it stick, would actually change my mind — or where my reasoning is most likely to fail.
