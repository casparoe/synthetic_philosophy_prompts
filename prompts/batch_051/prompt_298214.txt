I'm writing the final exam for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on model interpretability and evaluation, and I'd like one essay question on the faithfulness of chain-of-thought reasoning. I've followed the interpretability debates on LessWrong and adjacent forums closely, so I'd like the question to sit in the weeds of the conceptual issue rather than request the standard summary. The class has covered the standard terrain: chain-of-thought prompting improving task accuracy, the difference between a correct final answer and a trustworthy account of how that answer was reached, and the worry that a model's stated reasoning may be a plausible reconstruction rather than a report of the actual determinants of its output. I want a question that cannot be answered by reciting that distinction.

The question should force students to engage with what I take to be the crux: a tempting argument that because each chain-of-thought token is part of the context the model conditions on when producing later tokens, the trace is genuinely causally efficacious in generating the final answer, and therefore the practically relevant sense of faithful reasoning has already been established. The question should make students distinguish this causal-efficacy sense of faithfulness from a stronger reporting-correspondence sense, and explain why accuracy gains and causal involvement do not jointly establish which determinants of the answer the trace actually describes. Ideally the question presents the strongest version of that causal argument for students to assess, so a good answer has to steelman it rather than dismiss it.

For the deliverable I'd like three things: (1) the essay question, pitched at roughly 800 words of writing in a 90-minute final; (2) a model answer of about 800 words that makes the specific conceptual moves rather than summarizing the topic; and (3) a marking scheme out of 20 marks, split into five must-hit points with the marks each is worth and a one-line note on how candidates typically get that point wrong, plus a short paragraph separating an A answer from a B answer.

A few constraints. The question must not require citations, bibliographic recall, or knowledge of any named paper or dataset; it should be fully answerable by a student who has understood the conceptual issues from the assigned reading. The difficulty should come from the conflation between causal efficacy and faithful reporting, and from the problem of what ground truth about actual reasoning an empirical test would even need. I specifically do not want the essay that ends in the vague claim that chain-of-thought might not be faithful. A B answer and an A answer should be separated by whether the student can say precisely what would count as evidence that the trace reports the real basis of the answer, and what a feasible experimental design could and could not show.
