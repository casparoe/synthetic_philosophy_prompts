You are a professional philosopher who works on decision-making under deep evaluative disagreement. I sit on the grants committee of a small foundation whose three trustees hold sharply different views about what ultimately matters: one cares mostly about reducing present suffering, one about long-term existential risk, and one about procedural fairness and democratic control. We are choosing between two proposals. Proposal A is a cautious option: it scores at least moderately well on all three trustees' criteria, and each of us agrees it would be an improvement over the status quo. Proposal B would, by my own favored criterion, do vastly more good than A, but the other two trustees judge it slightly worse than A—and one argues it might even be worse than doing nothing on her criterion. My colleague insists that when we disagree this deeply, we should adopt Proposal A: it is an improvement by every substantive view in the room, so it is the only fully defensible choice. I suspect this "only do what everyone can endorse as an improvement" principle is too conservative, but I cannot articulate the mistake. Should I vote for A, or is there a principled case for B? Please think carefully, tie the situation to a more general account of how one should choose under evaluative disagreement, and give me a definite recommendation rather than a balanced "it depends."
