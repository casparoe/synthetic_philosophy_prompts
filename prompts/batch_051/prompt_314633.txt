I have been going back and forth with a friend about whether the concept of supererogation — an act that is morally good and praiseworthy but not required — can survive scrutiny, or whether it is a kind of conceptual illusion. My friend is convinced it is an illusion, and when I pushed him to make the objection precise, he gave the following reconstruction. Each step strikes me as plausible on its own, but the conclusion seems too strong, and I suspect the argument quietly imports a controversial claim about how moral goodness relates to obligation. I want to know whether that suspicion is right and, if so, exactly where the argument goes wrong.

The argument runs as follows.

(1) If one available action is morally better than another, then the agent ought to choose the better one.

(2) A merely dutiful act is one that satisfies all of the agent's obligations and does nothing beyond them.

(3) A supererogatory act is, by definition, morally better than the agent's merely dutiful alternative; that is what it means to go beyond duty for moral reasons.

(4) Consider a simplified choice: I have already met all of my obligations, and I can either retain a financial windfall for my own projects — a merely dutiful option — or donate the entire sum to an effective charity — a supererogatory option. Assume these are the only two options available.

(5) By (3), donating is morally better than retaining.

(6) By (1), I ought to donate rather than retain.

(7) But because donating is supererogatory, it is beyond duty: I am not required to donate, and not donating would not be wrong.

(8) Therefore the same act is both required and not required. If the argument is sound, the category of supererogatory acts is empty; every putatively supererogatory act either collapses into ordinary duty or is not really better after all.

Please work through the argument step by step and say whether it is valid, and if it is not, identify the exact step at which it fails and what the correct version of that step would be. I would especially like to know whether the whole argument turns on reading the ought in premise (1) as the ought of obligation, and whether what it really shows is only that a certain maximizing style of moral theory cannot countenance supererogation. Be specific about the senses of ought and better at each stage, and do not end on a merely noncommittal answer.
