I'm a software engineer who works on causal inference at a tech company, estimating which product changes actually cause changes in user behavior rather than merely predicting them. I have no formal philosophy background, so I want plain, careful reasoning rather than a literature survey; I'm not asking for authors or citations.

Over several years I've settled into a working view I can't fully justify. It's an interventionist view of causation. The core claim is that a genuinely causal or explanatory relationship between two variables is one that remains invariant under interventions on the supposed cause: if you could reach in and change that variable surgically, without changing the effect through some other route, the effect would change in the way the relationship says it does, and this would keep holding across different ways of intervening and across different background conditions. A merely predictive relationship can be stable under passive observation but come apart when you actually manipulate one side. My work keeps pushing me toward this view, because the practical gold standard for whether one thing caused another is roughly a designed intervention or something that mimics one.

I'm not asking you to defend this view. I'm asking for an honest map of its exposure: what would have to be true for it to be false, or at least badly limited. Please distinguish evidence that would be decisive from evidence that would merely be awkward, and say what defenders of the view would themselves concede counts against it, not just what outsiders say. I've seen the standard barometer-and-storm kind of case, and I'm satisfied the view handles it by denying that the correlation is invariant under an intervention on the barometer. I'm more worried about the harder cases.

Three things bother me most. First, the circularity worry: intervention, surgical, other route, and holding fixed all seem to smuggle in causal notions, so the account looks like it explains causation with causation. What would show that this circularity is fatal rather than tolerable, or vice versa? Second, I want concrete cases where the invariance-under-intervention test and genuine causation come apart in either direction: cases that pass the test but that we should refuse to call causal, and cases that fail it but that we should still call causal. If there are clean cases of this sort, walk me through the strongest ones, not the easy targets. Third, what does this view predict that rival accounts don't, and have any of those predictions failed or become difficult? The kind of evidence I personally would find most persuasive is a realistic case in which a relationship remains invariant under intervention and yet we would clearly not call it causal, or the reverse, one that cannot be handled simply by choosing finer or coarser variables. Finally, tell me honestly whether the view is flexible enough to be patched around any counterexample, and whether that patchability should make me trust it more or less.

Please write your answer in American English.
