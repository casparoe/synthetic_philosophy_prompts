In a philosophy discussion group at school, a friend gave an argument which, he says, shows that Nāgārjuna's tetralemma proves too much, and I cannot find the flaw or decide which premise to abandon. His reconstruction runs like this. (1) The tetralemma says that for any proposition there are exactly four possible statuses: true, false, both true and false, or neither true nor false. (2) Nāgārjuna and the Madhyamaka tradition use this to deflate every metaphysical thesis, since each such thesis can be shown to get stuck on one of the four horns and to be undercut by its rivals. (3) Now consider the proposition T, that every proposition has exactly one of those four statuses. T is itself a proposition, so by the tetralemma T must have one of the four statuses. (4) If T is simply true, then at least one proposition is simply true, and the deflationary project of showing that no thesis can finally stand as true is abandoned. (5) If T is false, then the method is simply incorrect. (6) If T is both true and false, then the method brings a contradiction into its own foundations, and if T is neither true nor false, then the method cannot be affirmed at all. (7) So either the tetralemma reinstates the very sort of privileged truth it was meant to dissolve, or it cannot be stated as a correct account of propositions. A defender will likely say that the tetralemma is meant to apply to itself, that it is only a method and not a further thesis, or that emptiness is itself empty. But that sounds to me like admitting the method cannot be called correct. Which step of the argument should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep (1), but I cannot see how to block (3). Please answer in continuous prose, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write a letter.
