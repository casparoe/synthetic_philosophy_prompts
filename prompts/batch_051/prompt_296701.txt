An influential strand of the moral-saints debate treats it as an empirical-sounding fact about persons that someone who is maximally morally good would tend to be deficient in non-moral respects — humor, charm, wit, aesthetic responsiveness, ease in personal life — and takes this to show that pure moral sainthood is not an unambiguously desirable ideal. Treat that empirical-sounding claim as testable. Design a concrete study, observational or experimental, that distinguishes three interpretations of it: (a) extreme moral commitment genuinely crowds out or suppresses non-moral excellences; (b) it leaves them intact but re-expresses them in forms ordinary observers do not recognize; (c) ordinary observers systematically underrate the non-moral traits that saints in fact possess. For each interpretation, specify the pattern of results that would favor it; then say what even the cleanest result would and would not establish about the normative question whether maximal moral goodness is desirable.
