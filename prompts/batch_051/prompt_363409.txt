I keep circling the old Ulysses-and-the-sirens case that people use for advance directives. In the original, he tells the crew to tie him to the mast and ignore any later order he gives, because when the song hits he won't be in his right mind; the later guy's pleas are just the spell talking, so the earlier self wins. The story gets all its force from the later self obviously not being competent.

But here's the version I can't shake. Suppose it isn't all-or-nothing. During the six hours they're passing the island, each hour there's an independent one-in-four chance that Ulysses is fully lucid: he remembers the plan, understands what he's doing, and in that hour he genuinely revokes the directive and asks to be untied, having thought it through and changed his mind. The other three hours out of four he's enchanted and asks to be untied for the usual siren reasons. He looks and sounds the same either way, calm and articulate, and the crew cant tell the two states apart.

Work out what the two usual positions actually say now. Position one: the earlier self was the only one who was definitely competent, so his instruction still settles it, and the fact that a current request might be lucid doesn't count unless the crew can verify it's lucid. Position two: the person in front of you now has authority over what happens to his own body, so once there's a real chance this is a lucid revocation, the crew can't just fall back on the old directive. Does position two force them to untie him the first time he asks, even though it's probably the spell? Or does it force a threshold rule—untie him only once the chance that he's lucid is past some number—and if so, what's that number and how should they update it hour by hour? Does position one still have a clean bottom line when the later self is exactly one-in-four likely to be lucid, and at what probability of lucidity would it have to flip?

Then say which part of the original case is doing the real work: the later self's incompetence, or the fact that the story ends with Ulysses thanking the crew for ignoring him, so a later-later self is around to ratify the earlier self's call? And if we lower the lucidity chance slowly toward zero, do the two positions slide together smoothly, or is there a sharp line where the earlier self's authority snaps back?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate the question.
