Over the past year I have been one of four editors of a small publication that puts out a weekly briefing containing factual and interpretive claims on tight deadlines. I have settled into a personal rule, and I want to know whether it is defensible or a bias dressed up as a rule. The rule is this: once the four of us have genuinely deliberated and reached a decision, I adopt that decision as my own. I do not merely stay quiet or go along in public; in my own later reasoning I treat the group's conclusion as provisional truth until the group itself reopens the question, and I stop treating my own dissenting reasons as live. I will also defend the group's conclusion in the first-person plural, even when I privately think we have made the wrong call. Last month I was the only one of the four who voted that we should issue a factual correction. The group voted not to correct, and I was the one who drafted the statement saying that we stood by the original piece. I still privately think the correction should have been made.

Part of me thinks there is a real idea underneath this rule: when people decide a question together in the right way, they form something more than an aggregate of individual judgments — a joint commitment or a plural subject, a first-person-plural subject with a view of its own — and that changes what it is reasonable for the members to believe afterward. But I worry that this is social metaphysics being used to launder conformity. I would like you to take the role of a professional philosopher of collective intentionality, joint action, and social epistemology, and to treat the policy on its merits. Under what conditions would a small deliberating group's conclusion actually be more trustworthy than an individual member's private assessment, such that adopting it wholesale would be epistemically rational? Under what conditions is this instead groupthink, group polarization, or an informational cascade? What would need to be true about how we deliberated, how we aggregated our judgments, how correlated our errors were, and what we were jointly committed to, in order for my rule to keep its benefits without its costs? And is there a corrected version of the rule that lets me remain a genuine member of a joint deliberator without silencing my private dissent?

Think carefully before answering. Before giving a final verdict, lay out the strongest case both for and against the rule, and say explicitly what you would need to know about my committee and its procedures to decide whether the rule is justified.
