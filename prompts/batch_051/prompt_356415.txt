I am a PhD student in legal philosophy, working on the question of whether sophisticated AI systems could intelligibly be recognized as legal persons. The view I have found myself defending, and which I want to stress-test rather than have confirmed, is that the strongest case for AI legal personhood runs through the analogy with corporate personality. A corporation is not a human being, yet developed legal systems routinely treat it as a person for many purposes: it can own property, enter into contracts, sue and be sued, and bear liabilities that are not simply the liabilities of its directors or shareholders. Legal personhood, on this picture, is not an all-or-nothing metaphysical status but a bundle of capacities and liabilities that a legal order creates for non-natural entities when doing so helps it allocate rights and losses cleanly. The corporate analogy therefore suggests that the central question about an AI system is not whether it is really like us, but whether bestowing a bounded form of legal personhood on it would better serve the law's purposes than treating it as a mere tool or as an extension of its owner or operator. I hold the stronger thesis that this analogy is not just one rhetorical move among others but the best available framework, because both corporations and AI systems are non-natural entities whose capacities are fixed, to a significant degree, by design rather than by a natural psyche, and because both face legal systems with the same problem: how to attach rights and liabilities to an entity that does not have the natural attributes of a human person.

I want to know what would count against this view, in the most candid terms. I am not looking for the standard objections in broad strokes — that legal personhood for AI would insulate developers, or that AI lacks the features of natural persons — but for an ordered account of the defeaters. Which disanalogies between corporations and AI systems are genuinely damaging to the analogy, and which are merely superficial? What would the law, the technology, or the surrounding economy have to look like for the corporate analogy to be not just incomplete but actively misleading? And which of these considerations would a defender of the analogy have to concede count against it, rather than dismiss? Finally, I would like you to be explicit about what would change your mind: which argument or development would lead you to a different verdict, and where you think your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
