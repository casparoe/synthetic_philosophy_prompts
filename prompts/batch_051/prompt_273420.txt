ive been going back and forth on whether Hans Jonas's heuristic of fear is right. In The Imperative of Responsibility, Jonas says that when technological power creates the possibility of irreversible ruin, predictions of possible disaster should be given much greater weight in practical decisions than predictions of benefits, because the stakes are so high that we should deliberately treat the worst-case scenario as if it were more probable than our usual methods would say. For resolution, let 'right' mean: as a rule for regulating emerging technologies, the heuristic would actually outperform ordinary cost-benefit analysis over the next century in reducing expected catastrophic harm, counting both false alarms and missed catastrophes as costs. What's your actual credence? I don't want 'it depends on the precautionary principle.' I want a number, and then the decomposition: how much of the credence comes from thinking catastrophic forecasts are more reliable or more informative than optimistic ones, how much from thinking asymmetric stakes justify asymmetric error costs even if the forecasts are equally unreliable, and how much from believing the rule can be made precise enough to avoid being used by people who fear all change? Which sub-question would shift your number most if you got strong new evidence on it?
