In the years after Franco's death in 1975, Spain's mainstream political forces and much of the public converged on what is often called the pact of forgetting: the Civil War of 1936–1939 and the repression of the Franco dictatorship were set aside as matters for public or legal reckoning, an arrangement given legal form by the 1977 amnesty law. Roughly three decades later this consensus broke down. In 2007, Spain's parliament passed the Historical Memory Law, which publicly repudiated the Francoist regime, funded the opening of mass graves from the war and its aftermath, and mandated the removal of Francoist symbols from public spaces; in 2019 Franco's remains were exhumed and removed from the Valley of the Fallen, a monument built in part with the labor of political prisoners.

Analyze this sequence through the lens of the theory that a community's memory is not a passive archive of what happened but an active, present-oriented reconstruction that serves the identity and cohesion of the group, and that such memory can therefore diverge from, override, or suppress the historical record. Be disciplined about the mapping: identify the relevant actors, what counted as the memory and what counted as the record in this case, what options the theory says the actors faced, and what the theory implies they should have done.

(2) Explain which features of the episode the theory captures well, and why.

(3) Explain which features it captures poorly or misdescribes, and where the theory is silent. Consider, for instance, the durability of the pact across several decades, the role of the unexhumed dead as a material fact rather than merely a representation, generational turnover, party competition, and international human-rights norms.

(4) Run the analysis in the other direction: what does this case show about the theory? Is it a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the theory never anticipated?
