Heres a thing I've never understood about how courts read statutes. In most common-law systems judges insist the text is the law and they won't rewrite a statute just because its policy seems dumb. But the same judges also run something called the absurdity doctrine: when the literal reading would produce a result so absurd that the legislature couldnt have meant it, the court sets the plain meaning aside. The stock example is a rule against shedding blood in the street, which nobody thinks should criminalize a doctor who opens a vein to save a collapsed pedestrian. So the court reads the provision as though it said shedding blood unlawfully, or words to that effect, even though those words aren't there.

Fine. But here's what bugs me. The same courts are extremely reluctant to fix the mirror-image problem. If a statute's obvious point covers a case but the text, read literally, doesn't reach it — a gap — the court says that's for the legislature to fill. If a statute's evident spirit extends beyond its words, the court says stretching it is not the judiciary's job. Yet the court will happily fix an obvious drafting slip, like reading a clause as saying now where it printed not, when the literal reading would be gibberish. So the practice seems to treat the legislature as a competent speaker — it couldn't have meant this incoherent thing — but not as a competent policy-maker — it may well have left a dumb gap, and that's the legislature's problem.

What view of statutory meaning, or of what a legislature is doing when it enacts a text, does this whole package presuppose? I want you to separate what the doctrine strictly requires from what is merely compatible with several rival theories (textualism, intentionalism, purposivism — use whatever labels fit). Is the line between absurdity and mere gap coherent, or is it just a vibe? And what would the practice have to look like if the presupposed view were replaced by full-bore purposivism, where the statute just is the policy the legislature was trying to enact? Bonus: courts keep disagreeing about whether absurd means logically incoherent or merely extremely unreasonable; which reading actually explains the surgeon-style cases, and do the two readings presuppose different things?
