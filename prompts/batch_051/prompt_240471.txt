After retiring from a career in the natural sciences, I have been working through the philosophical literature on punishment, and I keep coming back to the tension among retribution, deterrence, and the risk of executing the innocent. Consider a mechanism that I have not seen discussed. A jurisdiction adopts a rule that no death sentence may be carried out until twenty-five years after conviction. During that interval, an independent review office must reopen the case every five years; its only task is to look for evidence of innocence, and any credible exculpatory evidence automatically commutes the sentence to life without parole. The stated rationale is that this preserves the retributive and deterrent functions of capital punishment while making wrongful execution effectively impossible. Analyze how this institution would actually behave: what it incentivizes for prosecutors, defense counsel, juries, victims' families, prisoners, and the review office itself; what second-order effects it has on plea bargaining, the rate of capital sentences, and the kinds of cases that survive to execution; and where it fails. Conclude by saying which failure modes, if any, could be fixed without gutting the mechanism.
