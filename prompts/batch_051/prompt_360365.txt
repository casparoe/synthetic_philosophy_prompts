Last year I moved into a cooperative house, and not long afterward we agreed to take in four ex-battery hens from a local rescue. None of us would buy eggs or chicken, and the hens were not acquired to produce food, but they lay almost daily, and throwing the eggs away has begun to feel perverse. At a dinner a few nights ago, two friends in the house got into a long argument about whether we should eat them, and I have been unable to settle who was right.

Dana opened by saying the answer is obviously yes. The hens exist and are already being cared for; no one is going to breed or cage another hen because we cooked these eggs; the egg industry does its harm through the demand it sends to producers, and eating an egg that will otherwise be composted sends no such signal. Refusing to eat them, she said, is purity theater—a sacrifice that benefits no chicken anywhere. Eli disagreed: the problem was never only the market signal. Egg farming is wrong because it treats a hen's body and reproductive output as a resource for us, and eating the eggs, even rescue eggs, keeps that stance in place. And when you serve guests "rescue eggs," he added, you teach them that eggs can be a clean product, which undercuts the very norm we are trying to live by.

Dana replied that this overgeneralizes. Eating a found bike does not normalize bike theft, and eating roadkill need not normalize deer hunting; norms routinely distinguish producing from scavenging. If other people cannot keep that distinction, that is a problem with their reasoning, not a wrong in the act. Eli came back that the distinction fails on its own terms: Dana's principle would appear to allow the meat of a cow who died at a sanctuary, milk from a rescued goat, or the untouched meat left over from a catered event, since no demand is created in any of those cases. If she rejects those, her "no-demand" principle is not doing the real work; if she accepts them, she is reconnecting the very categories—animal products as food—that an anti-exploitation practice was meant to keep apart.

Dana conceded something: she does not think she would eat the cow's body, because that would be to relate to the cow herself as meat, but an egg is an unfertilized reproductive cell, not the animal, and no one is killed or confined for it. Eli said that is a line without an underlying rationale: if suffering is the test, then some products of exploited animals can be consumed once the suffering is over, yet Dana herself would still refuse most of them. The argument ended there. Please write as a moral philosopher working in applied ethics, reason carefully, and reach a definite verdict.
