I recently read a popular article on Bayesian epistemology that attempted to summarize van Fraassen's Reflection principle for a nonspecialist audience. The author's gloss went roughly as follows. Since you know that your future self will have strictly more evidence than you do now, rationality requires you to adopt whatever degree of confidence your future self will eventually have in any given proposition. If you now assign probability p to some claim, and you are certain that tomorrow you will assign probability q to that same claim, then you should immediately revise your current probability to q. Any reluctance to do so is just a failure of coherence: you would be treating your own future self as less reliable than yourself, which the author calls epistemic arrogance. The article then says that Reflection is therefore a straightforward consequence of the rule of conditionalization, and that it would be violated only by someone who expects to become irrational or to forget things, in which case the principle would still say you should defer to your future self because coherence demands it.

I felt uneasy about the last move, but I don't have enough technical background to articulate exactly what is accurate, what is a common simplification, and what is an overstatement. Which parts of this summary would van Fraassen or a careful formal epistemologist accept? Which parts are conflations or overstatements? In particular, does the claimed link to conditionalization follow from the accurate core of the summary, and does Reflection really still require deference when you expect your future self to be irrational or to have lost information? I'm looking for an accuracy audit of the summary rather than a defense or attack on the principle itself.
