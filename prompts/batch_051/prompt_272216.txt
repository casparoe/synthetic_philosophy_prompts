Okay so I've been arguing with my brother about this for weeks and I want someone to help me figure out what principle I actually believe. The question is which "useless" subjects a university should keep around. I have opinions on a bunch of specific cases but I cant tell if they're consistent.

Here are my verdicts:

1. A classics department that mostly teaches ancient Greek and Latin poetry to maybe a dozen students a year. Almost none of those students use it in their jobs. I think you keep it. It's kept a whole tradition alive and a few students come out genuinely changed by it.

2. A professor who spent fifteen years piecing together the grammar of an extinct language from about two hundred short inscriptions. Maybe twenty people in the world can read his work. I think this is beautiful and the university shouldn't cut it, but I'd be lying if I said I could explain why it deserves fifteen years of a salary.

3. A funded project on the history of buttons in nineteenth-century Lithuania. I think this one is silly and shouldn't be funded. But when I try to say why it's different from the extinct language one, I get stuck. Both are tiny, both are about reconstructing something nobody needs.

4. A philosophy seminar on whether numbers are real objects or just a useful invention. No practical payoff at all, but I think it's worth it because it makes people think carefully in a way that spills over into everything else.

5. A small research center that builds Rube Goldberg machines, those chain reaction contraptions that do something simple in the most roundabout way. They claim it teaches mechanical creativity. I sort of think keep it small, because it's half art and half engineering, and I've seen students who did it get good at improvising when things break.

6. A PhD thesis on the aerodynamic properties of dragons as described in fantasy novels. I think this one is a no. If you want to study aerodynamics, study real animals or planes. But then I worry, because the extinct language is also about something that isn't coming back, and I gave that one a pass.

So what's my principle? I want you to try to build one that gets all six, or tell me which of my verdicts I should give up and why. Try to show me which case each part of the rule is doing work for, so it's not just a slogan. Also tell me what your principle says about a case I can't decide on: a center for the history of board games, with one curator and occasional small exhibitions. And be honest if the principle ends up being more of a gut feeling than an actual rule.
