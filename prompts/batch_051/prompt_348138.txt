I'm a novelist, which mostly means I can't let a weird argument go unexamined, especially one that's been running for years between two people I like. The couple in question — call them M and R — are deciding whether to have a child. They've moved past the usual money-and-career stuff. M doesn't want one, and her real reason goes like this: if we bring a child into the world and that child suffers badly, that suffering is something we caused, full stop. But the happiness the child might have isn't something anyone is owed; there's no potential person hovering offstage who gets cheated out of a good life if we don't have them. So the possible bad stuff counts against having a kid in a way the possible good stuff just doesn't count in favor. R thinks this is perverse: if that were true, you'd have to think it's at best pointless and at worst wrong for anyone to have children, because the ledger could never come out positive. M shrugs and says maybe that's less crazy than it sounds.

I cant tell wether M has latched onto a real difference in how those two kinds of considerations weigh, or whether she's just framed nonexistence in a way that flatters a preference she already has. I'm not asking for relationship advice, and I don't want the usual dodge about how you can't reason about a child who doesn't exist yet. I want to know if there's a defensible principle here about how to count harms and goods for a person who would only exist if we create them. If there is, what makes a possible child's suffering weightier than its joy, given that neither is felt by anyone until after the fact? And does accepting that principle really commit you to saying having children is wrong, or is there a way to hold it without ending up there? Keep it grounded — I'm going to put whatever you say into the mouth of a character, so it has to sound like something a smart person would actually say at a kitchen table.
