I'm a retired geochemist, and I've recently got myself into a tangle about whether bad people can make good art. I keep hearing the line to seperate the art from the artist, but my own judgments don't obey it, and I'd like to know what principle, if any, they do obey. I think better in cases than in abstractions, so here are my verdicts, as honest as I can make them.

1. A painter from the 1600s who, by all accounts, beat his wife and was vicious to his children, but whose paintings are quiet, tender domestic scenes — a woman pouring milk, a child asleep, folded linen. There is no cruelty anywhere in the work, and he has been dead for centuries. Verdict: the paintings are good art, and I say it without flinching.

2. A living singer-songwriter, hugely successful, credibly accused of assaulting a former partner. The songs are love songs; there is no trace of the accusations in them. But every stream and every ticket puts money and influence in his pocket, and he has used that influence to attack the accuser. Verdict: the songs are good songs as songs, but I won't listen and won't recommend them. I can't tell whether that is an aesthetic judgment or just a practical boycott.

3. A dead novelist, a master of prose, wrote a novel in which the narrator's whole sensibility is anti-Semitic — not just the characters', but the book's own way of seeing. It is brilliantly written. Verdict: I can't call it good art. The moral ugliness is inside the thing I'm being asked to admire, not a fact outside it.

4. A film directed by a living man who served time for a serious violent crime, committed years before he made the film. The film itself is a gentle comedy, and it is the work of hundreds of people — actors, crew, editors — who had nothing to do with the crime. Watching it on a streaming service sends the director a bit of money. Verdict: the film is good, and I'll watch it. Refusing to call it good seems to punish the innocent majority.

5. A photographer, now dead, made a series of portraits of children that are formally stunning — the light, the composition. It later came out that he molested the children during the sessions, and the photographs are in some sense the record of the grooming. Verdict: these are not good art. I can't even look at them. The making and the thing made are the same.

6. An ancient epic, no single author, a genuine masterpiece of structure and language, whose climax celebrates the massacre of a city and the enslavement of its surviving people. Verdict: it is good art — great, even — and also morally horrifying. I don't think one cancels the other.

7. A living abstract painter whose work has no subject matter that could express his politics; he gives interviews full of genuine, unapologetic bigotry. He is already wealthy, and looking at a reproduction online doesn't benefit him. Verdict: I think the paintings are good, but I feel noticeably queezy saying it, more than I do in case 1.

The pair that nags at me most is 3 and 6: in both, the badness is in the work, but I keep the epic and shut the novel. I don't know what to make of that.

What I'm tempted to say is this: an artwork is good art if it repays the kind of attention you give art, and the artist's life only counts when (a) the badness is actually in the work, or (b) engaging with the work does ongoing harm. But that principle makes case 4 messy — I said the film is good even though the director does get paid. Case 6 is messy because the badness is in the work, yet I called the epic good. And case 2 has me saying the songs are good while refusing to engage with them. So maybe the principle has to distinguish evaluating the work, evaluating the act of engaging, and evaluating the person.

What I want from you is the best principle, or one or two candidate principles, that captures as many of my verdicts as possible, stated precisely. Then go through the seven cases and say, for each, whether the principle delivers my verdict or forces me to change it. Be honest about which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate — I'd rather overturn a gut feeling than keep a Rube Goldberg principle. Also test your principle on three new cases of your own invention, adjacent to these, and tell me what it predicts. Please answer in these sections, in this order: The principle (~200 words), Case by case (~350 words), Verdicts I'd have to give up (~150 words), Three new cases (~250 words), and What the principle still can't handle (~100 words).
