I help maintain a small proprietary audio editor, and I have been arguing with a friend who is a strict free-software person about whether my program counts as a derivative work of a GPL-licensed command-line audio processor that it invokes. I am not asking for a legal prediction or for references; I want the conceptual anatomy of the boundary. The working criteria I was taught for calling something a derivative work in this setting are roughly these: first, the second work must incorporate or adapt protected expression from the first work, through copying, translation, or recasting of its code or structure, not merely through use of its ideas, interfaces, or unoriginal function; second, the borrowing must be more than trivial; third, the two works must form a single work as distributed and run, rather than being an aggregation of independent programs that happen to cooperate; fourth, the relationship must be one of building on the original, not merely one program interoperating with another.

Now here is the case we keep going in circles about. Suppose the editor contains no GPL code. When the user hits its Enhance command, the editor checks whether an external, GPL-licensed command-line processor is installed, launches it in a separate process, sends it a generated job file in the processor's own command syntax, reads the output, and renders it. The editor's marketing and manual describe the processor as the engine behind the product, and with the processor absent almost every feature is greyed out. Yet the editor cannot perform the processing itself. The processor is a general-purpose tool written for no particular client; any conforming reimplementation could be substituted without changing the editor. The two programs never share an address space, and nothing from the processor is copied into the editor's source or distribution.

Apply the four criteria to this pair and say whether the editor counts as a derivative work of the processor. Be precise about the points at which the stated facts stop deciding the matter. Then say whether the case shows that one or more of the four criteria should be revised, added to, or dropped, and whether the real boundary should track code-level combination, the user's experience, the author's intent, or functional dependence.

Please answer in American English and organize the response as follows:

1. Criterion-by-criterion application (about 250 words): take each criterion in turn; say which facts push which way and mark where the criterion stops deciding.
2. Where the underdetermination lies (about 150 words): identify the judgment call that does the real work and explain why the plain facts leave it open.
3. Revised criteria (about 150 words): state which of the four criteria you would amend, add, or discard, and how you would redraw the boundary after considering the case.
4. Verdict (about 100 words): give your overall answer and your confidence, and identify the single additional fact about the case that would most change your verdict.
