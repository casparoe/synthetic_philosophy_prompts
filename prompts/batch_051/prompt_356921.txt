I retired from geochemistry a few years back and have been working my way through a philosophy reading group with two old colleagues, mostly because I spent a career treating things as measurable and now I want to know what "measurable" can mean when the thing being measured is a state of mind. We read Bentham's An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation, and this passage from Chapter IV ("Value of a Lot of Pleasure or Pain, how to be Measured") has stuck with me more than I expected:

"To a number of persons, with reference to each of whom to the value of a pleasure or a pain is considered, it will be greater or less, according to seven circumstances: to wit, the six preceding ones; viz.
- Its intensity.
- Its duration.
- Its certainty or uncertainty.
- Its propinquity or remoteness.
- Its fecundity.
- Its purity.
And one other; to wit:
7. Its extent; that is, the number of persons to whom it extends; or (in other words) who are affected by it."

Bentham really does talk like a surveyor taking angles on a pleasure. Then I looked at what the World Happiness Report actually does: one Cantril ladder question — imagine a ladder, worst possible life at 0, best possible life at 10, where do you stand right now — and the country rankings are the average answer to that single question, with the report explaining the national scores using GDP, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make choices, generosity, and perceived corruption. It doesnt ask about intensity or duration or purity at all. I cant shake the thought that a country could climb the rankings by raising the rung people report while doing nothing about how pleasure and pain actually arrive, linger, repeat, and spread. I want a poem that holds that gap up without lecturing.

Could you write me a sonnet about this? Id like the octave to be Bentham's seven circumstances as a kind of field notebook or ledger, and the volta to be the moment the reader realizes the famous ranking is a single self-placed rung on a ladder rather than the seven-column calculus. The closing couplet should leave open whether a country should try to top such a ranking — it shouldnt resolve it. It needs to be accurate enough that someone who knows Bentham's chapter and the report's actual method nods instead of wincing.
