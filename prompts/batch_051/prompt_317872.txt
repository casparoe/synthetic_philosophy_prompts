In a decision-theory reading group, someone ended the argument by summing up Good's theorem with the slogan "information can't hurt": since a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer can always ignore costless information, a rational agent should never refuse it, so any reluctance to learn is irrational. I suspect that slogan is smuggling several different claims under one sentence. Lay out the distinct precise principles "information can't hurt" could be expressing — for example, an ex ante claim about expected utility versus an ex post claim about realized outcomes; a single-agent claim versus a claim about strategic, market, or social settings where others observe what you learn; an instrumental claim about better decisions versus an intrinsic claim about the value of true belief; and a claim about one signal's being more informative than another. For each, say whether it is true, whether Good's theorem actually establishes it, and whether it licenses the conclusion that refusing free information is always irrational.
