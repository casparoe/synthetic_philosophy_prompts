I have been trying to get my commitments in virtue ethics and moral psychology into a single stable position, and I would like an audit of whether they actually hang together. I will list the commitments as plainly as I can, and I would prefer the diagnosis to rank tensions by severity rather than treat every point of friction as equally serious.

The commitments:

1. I accept a broadly neo-Aristotelian analysis of right action: an act is right just in case it is what a virtuous agent would characteristically do in the circumstances, where a virtue is a stable, cross-situationally consistent disposition of perception, affect, deliberation, and action.

2. I accept the central empirical claim of the situationist critique: morally relevant behavior is heavily influenced by minor situational features, and the evidence gives us little reason to believe that ordinary people possess the broad, cross-situationally consistent character traits that traditional virtue language attributes to them.

3. I nevertheless think the situationist critique leaves virtue ethics intact, because the theory can be rebuilt on local or situation-indexed traits. Rather than requiring a person to be honest across the board, one can attribute honesty-in-professional-contexts, honesty-under-scrutiny, fairness-in-deliberative-settings, and the like, and a virtue-based ethics can content itself with local dispositions of that kind.

4. At the same time, I want to insist that the localizing move should not quietly replace the traditional virtues. Honesty, courage, and compassion, understood as unified cross-situational excellences, remain the correct regulative ideals of character development. They are what moral education should aim at, and a patchwork of situation-indexed dispositions is a deficient substitute measured against that ideal, not a satisfying reconstruction of what virtue ethics was about.

5. Finally, I hold that ethical theory is answerable to moral psychology in this specific sense: if a theory's central prescriptions require ordinary agents to have motivational or cognitive structures that are not realistically attainable for beings like us, that counts significantly against the theory.

What I want to know is whether all five can be true together. Some of the apparent conflicts may be harmless, and some may run deeper than I think, but I do not trust my own sense of where the real damage is. Please audit the set commitment by commitment: identify the genuine tensions, rank them by severity, say which apparent tensions are only apparent and why, and state the least costly revision that would restore consistency, along with what that revision would give up.
