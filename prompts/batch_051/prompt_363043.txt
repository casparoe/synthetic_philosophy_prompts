In a discussion group on transhumanism we got stuck on what counts as eliminating suffering. I was taught that suffering has at least three components: (1) a negatively valenced feeling, the raw badness of the state; (2) a motivational component, you want it to stop; (3) an evaluative component, something registers as wrong or threatening. And pain is not the same as suffering: you can have nociception without pain, and maybe pain without suffering.

Now imagine a biotech treatment that targets the affective dimension of pain. It leaves the sensory-discriminative side intact — you still feel the sharp pulsing in a tooth and know exactly where it is and how intense it is — but removes the negative valence, so the sensation is no longer aversive. Call it valence stripping. Some people with pain asymbolia seem to be in something like this state naturally: they report feeling the sharpness and location of a pinprick but don't mind it at all.

After the treatment, a patient describes her post-surgical pain the same way: the throbbing is there, loud and impossible to ignore, and she can tell its intense, but there is no unpleasantness and no urge to escape it. It still grabs her attention constantly and keeps her from sleeping.

Here's the question. By the three criteria, has her suffering been abolished or not? If the pain still hijacks her attention and ruins her sleep, is that itself a form of suffering, or just a burden that isn't suffering? And does the hedonistic imperative's goal of abolishing suffering require eliminating such states too, or can it settle for eliminating the negatively valenced core? Work criterion by criterion, and then tell me whether the case shows the criteria need a fourth component, something like attentional capture counting independently.
