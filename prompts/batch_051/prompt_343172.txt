I keep running into the same architectural analogy in discussions about epistemic justification, and I would like it audited rather than just accepted or dismissed. A friend argues that foundationalism is forced on us: a building cannot stand unless each storey rests on something that does not itself rest on the building, so, he says, a chain of justifying beliefs must terminate in basic beliefs that are justified without being justified by further beliefs. Otherwise, he claims, you get either an infinite regress, with no ground ever reached, or a circle, with the structure supporting itself only by leaning on itself, and neither is a way for a structure to stand. People on the other side reach for different images: the raft at sea, kept afloat while its planks are replaced one at a time, or a web whose knots hold one another in place. Infinitists suggest that a chain can be ungrounded and still supportive if it goes on forever.

I want a careful audit of how far the architectural analogy really carries the foundationalist conclusion. Please give the correspondence table: what in the epistemic case plays the role of the storeys, the load, the foundations, the ground, and the builder? Then identify the disanalogies. For instance, the ground under a building is not part of the building and is not another storey, whereas basic beliefs seem to remain parts of the structure unless the foundationalist tells a further story about their source; a lower storey fails when a foundation cracks, but a belief can survive the loss of what once justified it; and real buildings cannot be mutually supported, whereas web-like and raft-like support may be possible in ways the building image rules out by fiat. For each disanalogy, say whether it matters for the intended use of the analogy or is merely decorative. Then assess whether the conclusion it was used to support — that justification must terminate in basic beliefs and that coherentism and infinitism are non-starters — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and say what a better analogy would be, if there is one. Finally, I would like you to flag where your own verdict is most likely to fail: which consideration, if it could be made to work, would most likely change your mind, and where a coherentist or infinitist would find the weakest point in your reasoning.
