I teach a second-year undergraduate seminar on the moral force of everyday rules, with sixteen students, and I have seventy-five minutes to spend on the following question: when locals treat a convention—tipping, queueing, walking on one side of the pavement, jaywalking at an empty crossing—as something a person ought to follow, is there genuinely a moral obligation in play, or only a social rule whose breach carries social costs? And what, if anything, changes for a visitor who follows a different local practice or simply finds the convention pointless?

Design a single runnable activity for this session rather than a lecture or a discussion of prepared cases. The participants should first converge on an arbitrary but useful convention through a game with real but harmless stakes, and then find themselves enforcing it against first a fellow insider and then a newcomer who could not have known the rule or arrives with a different local rule of their own. The setup should make them feel the pull of both judgments: that the rule has become a real obligation that licenses blame, and that it remains a merely arbitrary solution to a problem they happened to have. I want at least one moment in which a rule that began as purely practical gets treated as though violating it were morally wrong, and at least one moment in which the group must decide whether the visitor's ignorance or foreign practice makes a difference to that judgment.

Give me the complete design: rules, materials requiring no money and no technology beyond what is in the room, a payoff scheme in points or some other cheaply realized currency, a timeline for the seventy-five minutes, and a debrief. The key philosophical vocabulary—convention, obligation, blame—should not be used by me until the debrief. The debrief should map particular moments of the activity onto the distinctions the session cares about: convention versus moral requirement, the difference between resenting the consequences of a violation and resenting the violation itself, the asymmetry between insiders and visitors, and the difference between a coordination convention such as queueing and a normatively charged convention such as tipping. The students should leave seeing that a convention's arbitrary origin does not by itself settle whether it binds, and that the visitor case forces a choice between treating local rules as genuine obligations and treating them as mere manners. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each failure. Finally, be frank about what the activity cannot reproduce—for instance, the historical embeddedness of real conventions and the genuine dependence of strangers on one another's predictable behavior—so I know what I must supply myself in the debrief.
