I'm a PhD student in political theory, mostly working on eighteenth-century republicanism, and I keep coming back to the Geneva theatre controversy because it's one of the few places where a famously abstract argument about art and politics actually collided with a concrete political decision. The facts as I understand them: Geneva was a Calvinist republic with a long-standing ban on public theatre; in the 1750s d'Alembert's Encyclopédie article on the city suggested it should allow one, on the standard Enlightenment line that theatre would polish manners; Voltaire was staging plays at his house just outside the city and generally stirring the pot; and Rousseau responded with the Letter to d'Alembert (1758), predicting that a theatre would corrupt republican virtue, turn citizens into passive spectators, import French luxury and loose morals, and be worse than useless even when the plays were morally decent. The ban held for a long time after that. I know the broad story, so spare me the plot summary.

What I want is a careful, two-directional case analysis. First, spell out the mechanisms in Rousseau's account: what exactly did he think a theatre would do to a community, psychologically, economically, morally, politically—including the stranger parts like the effect on the standing of women and the difference he draws between tragedy and comedy—and which of those mechanisms could even in principle have been observed if the theatre had opened? Second, map the real actors and interests in Geneva: the pastors, the ruling councils, the ordinary citizens, the French residents, and say for each whether Rousseau's model captures them or turns them into cartoons. Third, run the episode against the theory: where does it confirm Rousseau, where is it an anomaly, and where is the theory silent or untestable—particularly, does the success of the ban tell us anything about whether his analysis was right, or only about who had power? Finally, say what the case does to the theory: is Geneva a vindication, a case of being right for the wrong reasons, or a situation his account was never really built to handle?

One meta-request, becuase I keep seeing smart people do this: separate what is settled among scholars of Rousseau from what is genuinely contested—there's a reading of the Letter that gets taught as if it were the consensus and it isn't—and when you're reconstructing facts about Geneva, flag the places where you're inferring rather than reporting.
