Most discussion of process-based versus outcome-based supervision of AI systems runs together several different questions: whether intermediate feedback improves performance, whether it makes a deployed system's behaviour trustworthy, whether it changes what the system is actually optimising, and whether the extra annotation and compute are worth it. I want these questions separated before I form a view, and I want the separation done by specifying what any adequate theory of the choice would have to get right.

By process-based supervision I mean training or reward signals that evaluate the intermediate steps, sub-actions, or working traces leading to a final output; by outcome-based supervision I mean signals that evaluate only the final output or terminal state, including sparse and delayed outcomes.

Write the full list of requirements such a theory must meet. For each requirement, give the case or consideration that forces it, and label it either non-negotiable or a camp-specific preference. The list should respect at least the following distinctions, and should add any you regard as missing:

- between a step's being locally checkable, as in a line of arithmetic or a proof step, and a step's being merely plausible to an evaluator, as in prose or dialogue;
- between process supervision delivered by a learned model and process supervision delivered by a structurally guaranteed check, such as a compiler, a proof checker, or unit tests;
- between supervising the correctness of steps and supervising the legibility or honesty of the chain, that is, whether the steps expose the real causes of the answer;
- between training-time process feedback and deployment-time process oversight;
- between process supervision as reward shaping, as improved credit assignment, as a form of regularisation, and as preference data; and
- between the claim that step-level feedback lowers the variance of credit assignment and the claim that it changes the objective being optimised.

It must also handle the standard cases: step-verifiable tasks; open-ended generation in which no intermediate step is determinably correct; temporally extended agentic tasks with tool calls and subgoals; and tasks whose final outcome is sparse but easy to score. Say why each case is a requirement rather than an application detail. The theory should likewise predict or rule out the characteristic failure modes: the system producing steps that look correct to the supervisor while being causally irrelevant to the answer; process feedback suppressing useful exploration because unfamiliar intermediate steps score poorly; step-level annotation becoming the bottleneck; and outcome-only supervision inviting reward hacking or myopic terminal behaviour.

Then state which single requirement most decisively rules out the two naïve positions, roughly that process supervision should always be preferred when steps exist and that outcomes are always enough. If you think some subset of the requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, identify it and explain why, because trade-offs between requirements interest me more than a flat list. Do not spend more than a short closing paragraph on how specific named positions or algorithms fare against the list; the criteria are the deliverable.

Open with the requirement that does the most work, then give the full list; a table for the list is acceptable. Hard limit: 1,000 words, including any table. Use British English spelling and conventions throughout.
