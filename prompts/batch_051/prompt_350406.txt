A line of thought about the Trinity has been stuck in my head, and I want a diagnosis of where it goes wrong rather than a verdict on the doctrine itself. (1) The Father is God. (2) The Son is God. (3) Things identical with the same thing are identical with each other. (4) Therefore the Father is the Son. (5) But the Father is not the Son; they are distinct persons. (6) So at least one of (1), (2), (3), or (5) has to be rejected. The obvious response is that the "is" in (1) and (2) is predication rather than identity: each person has the divine nature. But then I cannot see what "there is one God" amounts to beyond "there is one divine nature shared by three persons," and three human persons can share one human nature without being one human, so the oneness seems to have gone missing. The only other move I can construct is to abandon absolute identity and say that sameness is always relative to a kind of thing: the Father and the Son can be the same God while remaining different persons, which blocks the step to (4) read as an absolute identity claim. That preserves every sentence on the list, but it makes ordinary identity claims relative in a way I find hard to accept. Please walk me through which step is doing the real work, what each escape route costs, and which premise you would give up. I am most reluctant to give up (5) and the oneness of God, but I suspect that combination is precisely what forces the choice, and I want to be told if that is so.
