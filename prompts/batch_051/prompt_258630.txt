I am a novelist, and over the years I have watched the same argument recur among editors, reviewers, and fellow writers without either side shifting position. One side insists that a construction is bad grammar if it violates the rules set down in reputable handbooks and style guides; the other replies that a construction used consistently, and without causing misunderstanding, by a community of competent speakers is grammatical by definition. Both sides seem to accept the same facts about how people actually write and speak, and yet the argument never moves. What I want is not a ruling on which side is right, but an account of what kind of disagreement this is.

Acting as a professional philosopher of language, please diagnose the impasse. I suspect, though only tentatively, that it is not really an empirical dispute about language but a collision between two distinct enterprises: describing how the language in fact works, and policing a particular prestige variety. Begin by briefly restating each position in its strongest form, then decide whether the disagreement is verbal, factual, normative, a difference about what counts as evidence, or something else, and finally tell me what, if anything, could count as genuine progress. Take your time and reason through the diagnosis step by step.
