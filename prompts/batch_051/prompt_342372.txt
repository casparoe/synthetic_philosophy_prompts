Draft the joint statement that two camps in the Pascal's mugging debate would produce after a year of good-faith adversarial collaboration. Treat the two camps as follows.

The unbounded expected-utility maximizers hold that rational choice is expected-utility maximization, that a rational agent's utility function may in principle be unbounded, and that Pascal's mugging is not a decisive counterexample: in some parameterizations the rational agent is simply required to pay, and any theory that says otherwise must justify its restriction with an independent principle rather than with unease about the conclusion. They regard bounded-utility functions and complexity-sensitive priors as the main rival escape routes, and they suspect those escape routes smuggle epistemic shortcuts or practical heuristics into the decision-theoretic norm.

The complexity-prior decision theorists hold that a rational agent's prior already penalizes hypotheses by complexity, understood as the length of their shortest description. A mugger who announces a specific astronomically large payoff without a comparably compressible mechanism is assigned so little credence that the expected gain never outweighs a finite loss. They take the mugging to show that a decision problem with unbounded utilities and no complexity-sensitive prior is ill-posed, and they regard the conclusion that a rational agent must pay as a symptom of that ill-posedness rather than a requirement on the agent.

The document must have three parts.

1. Numbered agreed claims. These must be substantive but must not smuggle in either side's controversial commitments. They should include at least: a shared characterization of the canonical mugging (a fixed cost c in utils, and a sequence of offers whose expected value grows without bound, e.g. probability p_n = 2^-n of payoff U_n = 3^n, so p_n U_n = (3/2)^n); an exact statement of what expected-utility theory says conditional on taking those probabilities and utilities at face value; and a shared account of what makes ordinary beneficial gambles acceptable without thereby settling the mugging. Avoid wording either side would refuse to sign.

2. A disagreements table. Use columns: topic (in neutral wording); the unbounded maximizer's formulation (phrased to be acceptable to the unbounded maximizer); the complexity-priorist's formulation (phrased to be acceptable to the complexity-priorist); and a note stating what observation or argument would cause each side to revise. Push past formulas like "utility may or may not be capped." Separate disagreements about the utility function from disagreements about the prior and disagreements about what it takes for a decision problem to be well-posed.

3. Cruxes and pre-registered tests. I suspect the two main cruxes are as follows, and each needs a concrete case both sides agree in advance bears on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

- Whether complexity sensitivity is a rational requirement on the prior or merely a practical-epistemic aid. The agreed test is the two-mechanism mugger: one mugger offers an astronomical payoff generated by a short, compressible mechanism (for example, an artifact that doubles some resource each hour); the other offers the same expected value through a mechanism no shorter to describe than a long arbitrary table. Both sides should state whether and why the two credences may differ, and what the agent does in each case.

- Whether empirical evidence can overcome the prior. The agreed test is the calibrated escalation: a sequence of mugging offers, each with a probability and payoff that can be checked after the fact, where each new offer builds on a clean track record from the previous ones. Both sides should specify in advance how the agent's willingness should change after a run of verified payoffs, and where, if anywhere, the unbounded maximizer and the complexity-priorist finally diverge.

Add one further cross-cutting test: the duplicate-muggers case, in which m independent muggers each demand c for similarly structured offers with astronomically large expected values. Both camps should state whether rational evaluation here is per-offer or global, and what principle yields each verdict.

The final statement should read as a document both parties could actually sign: no slanted adjectives in the agreed claims, no caricature in the disagreement table, and each camp's own formulation carrying the full force that camp would insist on.
