I've been trying to get clear about belief revision under uncertain evidence, and I keep going back and forth. Jeffrey conditionalization looks to me like the right generalization of plain conditionalization: when an observation does not make any proposition certain but just redistributes my confidence over a partition of possibilities, it says my new credence in any proposition should be the weighted average of my old conditional credences in that proposition given each member of the partition, weighted by my new credences in those members. That is a complete, mechanical procedure from a very small input: it says exactly one thing, and it keeps everything else—all the conditional probabilities—fixed while shifting only the partition. I find that conservation genuinely attractive, because it seems to mean that we revise only what the evidence touched and leave the rest of our belief structure alone.

But I have an intuition I can't shake. Suppose that on the basis of a blurry glimpse through a dirty window I move from being 0.5 confident that the bird outside is a female cardinal to 0.8 confident; or suppose a friend whose reliability about birds I have never settled tells me it is probably a female cardinal and I end up at the same 0.8. Jeffrey conditionalization gives the same revision for every proposition outside the partition {female cardinal, not female cardinal} in the two cases, because it uses only the new distribution over the partition and my old conditional probabilities. But it seems to me the two routes to 0.8 are not equivalent: the glimpse also teaches me something about how much to trust my own glances in that kind of light, and the friend's report teaches me something about how much to trust her, so my other beliefs should move differently in the two cases. If the rule is applied to a partition too coarse to include those reliability hypotheses, it seems to miss this. The defender of Jeffrey conditionalization can reply: then make the partition finer, include the reliability hypotheses, and give their new probabilities as part of the input; the rule only applies once you have specified a rich enough partition. But once the input is allowed to be my full posterior over an arbitrarily fine partition, I worry the theory has stopped doing the substantive work the coarse version promised.

I want a negotiated verdict, not a side. Which should yield: my attraction to Jeffrey conditionalization as a complete, conservative rule, or my intuition that the same movement in a coarse partition can be produced by evidence that also bears on the links between that partition and everything else? Is the intuition just a confusion about under-described inputs, or does it point to a real limitation in how Jeffrey conditionalization represents uncertain evidence? What exactly would justify each option—amending the rule, re-describing the input, or dismissing the intuition—and is there a reading on which the two are compatible? Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
