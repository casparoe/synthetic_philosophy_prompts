I chair a methodology review board at a research institute that publishes long-range forecasts of resource, environmental, and demographic trends, typically with 20-to-50-year horizons. Every quarter we receive a few teams defending a forecast that missed one of its checkpoints with the claim that the underlying theory is right but early: the mechanism is real, the prediction will still come true, but on a longer clock. We need a reusable decision procedure for deciding when to accept that defense, when to mark the forecast failed, and when to send it back with conditions attached. The procedure will be run by a three-person board with generalist training (one economist, one ecologist, one policy analyst), none of whom is necessarily a specialist in the forecast's subject matter. Each review can take at most a half-day of meetings, and the outcome determines whether a line of the institute's published work is quietly withdrawn, assigned a revised horizon, or allowed to keep standing. I sign the final ruling, but I want the rule written so that I am supervising a procedure rather than substituting my own priors each time.

Please write me that procedure, grounded in the actual structure of long-run predictions and in what falsification can and cannot do when the failure is not yet rather than never. The procedure should be a checklist in the order we should run it, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where it stops with a no, and where it must hand the decision to a person because the question is one of values or institutional risk rather than method. In particular, it should force teams to separate the core claim from its auxiliary assumptions (technology, prices, substitution, policy change, measurement conventions), to say whether early was defined before the checkpoint failed or only after, and to name a future observation that would falsify the claim even if the full catastrophe has still not arrived. It should also distinguish delaying a deadline from weakening a claim, and distinguish a rescue that makes the theory more testable from a rescue that merely moves the target. Be explicit about what the procedure cannot do: the cases where no written rule can tell us whether a theory is right but early or simply unfalsifiable.

Then run the procedure on three cases: (1) the Malthusian claim that population tends to outrun the food supply, treated as a standing long-run structural claim rather than a dated prediction; (2) a synthetic case our own institute published, in which a finite mineral resource was projected to be economically exhausted by 2020 and was then defended as right but early after the checkpoint passed; and (3) a case of your own construction in which the right-but-early defense should be accepted, with the conditions that must attach. For each case, show which steps do the work and flag where the procedure gives an answer the board will find uncomfortable.
