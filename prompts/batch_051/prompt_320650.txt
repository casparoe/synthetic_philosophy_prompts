I've been running a jurisprudence reading group and we've spent months on the Hart–Fuller debate and its aftermath, and I'm starting to suspect the whole thing is stuck in a way that's not just "the question is hard." Hart says the Nazi informer statutes were laws — evil ones, but laws — because validity depends on the system's rule of recognition, not on morality, and that calling them "not law" just obscures the real choice to disobey. Fuller replies that a system whose rules are secret, retroactive, and ignored by its own officials has stopped being a legal system in any useful sense, and that law has an inner morality of generality, publicity, non-retroactivity, and congruence between declared rule and official action. Both sides seem to have the same historical facts, and I don't think either side is confused about what the other is saying. Yet the debate just restarts every time at the same intuitions about whether the Nazi-era statutes count as law.

My working hypothesis is that this is at bottom a disagreement about what the word "law" is for: Hart wants a descriptive concept we can use to criticize wicked law from outside, while Fuller wants a concept that tracks when a purported legal system is actually governing well enough to deserve the name. But that might be too neat. Is this a verbal dispute about "law," a substantive conceptual dispute, a disagreement about which cases count as data (the Nazi case as central vs. an outlier), or two different projects — descriptive jurisprudence vs. a normative theory of legality — happening under one name? Diagnose it using what each side and their successors actually say, and tell me whether anything could realistically move it: a distinction, a reformulated question, or just accepting the two projects and stopping the argument.
