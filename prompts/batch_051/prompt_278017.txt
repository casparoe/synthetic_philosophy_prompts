Next week I'm leading the final session of a small reading group on Hofstadter, and the assignment is the Typogenetics chapter: a toy formal system where strands over A, C, G, T encode enzymes via triplets, the enzymes bind by base-pairing and do dumb operations—cut, insert, delete, copy, move—on strands, until one strand codes for enzymes that rebuild the strand itself, and Hofstadter says this is the same move as Gödel numbering: DNA's "proteins can act on the DNA that coded them" is isomorphic to Gödel's "numbers can talk about the system that numbers them." The group is a programmer, a plant biologist, a philosopher of mind, and a retired civil engineer who's read everything but only for the puzzles. They all skimmed the mechanics and jumped to the punchline. I have ninety minutes, and I want them to walk out with the trick and the isomorphism, not just my enthusiasm.

Don't explain Typogenetics to me—give me a teaching plan.

I want: the order the pieces have to arrive in for the self-replication to land (what depends on what: binding rules before the operations? the strand-as-data versus strand-as-program distinction before self-reference can even be stated?), the one stripped-down carrying example I should build the whole session around and what it must have and what it must not have, the misconceptions they'll form along the way and exactly where in the sequence to head each one off—I'd guess 'the enzymes know what their doing,' 'Gödel is just the liar paradox with extra notation,' and 'so DNA is just a program, like software compiling itself' are the big three—plus what the isomorphism actually gets them that neither the DNA story alone nor the Gödel story alone would, and why that has to come last, what to leave out at this level and say so, and one final question I can ask to check they've got the loop rather than just been impressed by it.
