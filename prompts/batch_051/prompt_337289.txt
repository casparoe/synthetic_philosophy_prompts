I run a small pest-control company in the Midwest—something like forty or fifty jobs a week across three techs—and I'm trying to write a reusable protocol for deciding how aggressively we should kill insects on a job. We already use integrated pest management (inspect, identify, set action thresholds, least-toxic control), so I'm not asking for IPM from scratch. What I want is an ethical decision procedure to layer on top of it: when do we escalate from exclusion, vacuuming, and monitors to a targeted bait or a spray, and when do we just tell the customer to live with the bugs? I've been reading about insect welfare and the question of whether insects can feel something like pain, and it's moved me enough that I don't want "it's a bug, kill it" as the default. But I can't hand my techs a seminar's worth of philosophy. They need something they can run in ten minutes, often by phone with a freaked-out customer, and it has to be something they can actually explain to the customer (who signs off on the treatment) and that won't get us in trouble with state regulators. The pesticide label is legally binding, so the procedure has to respect that too.

The principle I keep coming back to is roughly this: the more evidence there is that a pest is sentient, or the more uncertainty about it, and the weaker the human interest (mere annoyance or aesthetics vs. health and safety), the harder the proceedure should push toward prevention and the least-lethal option, and the more we should avoid broad-spectrum residuals that kill a lot of insects besides the target. But that's not a procedure, it's a vibe. Turn it into something concrete—a flowchart in words or a checklist with steps in order. Work through at least two or three hard cases: say, a wasp nest over a back door where a kid with an allergy plays, German cockroaches in a kitchen, and carpet beetles that are doing no real damage but the customer hates them. Include at least one case where the procedure tells me to do something I won't like, like walking away from a job or using a more expensive treatment. Flag the steps where judgment can't be eliminated, and be honest about what the procedure can't do—where the ethics runs out and it's just a business or legal call.
