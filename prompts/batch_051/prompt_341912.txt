After forty years in experimental physics, I retired and began reading philosophy of mathematics seriously. I recently worked through Bertrand Russell's *Introduction to Mathematical Philosophy*, and the passage about the multiplicative axiom has been bothering me. As I understand it, Russell contrasts a collection of pairs of boots with a collection of pairs of socks: for boots, a selection can be specified by a rule, such as always choosing the left boot, whereas for socks, which come without distinguishing features, no such rule exists, so the axiom is needed to secure a choice set. I found the passage unsettling because it describes something I have done unthinkingly for decades.

My working policy has been to treat the axiom of choice as harmless whenever I am doing ordinary mathematical modeling, while becoming suspicious only when the result seems physically impossible or when the objects involved are highly nonconstructive. In practice, that meant I would accept a proof that every vector space has a basis, but I would get uneasy about nonmeasurable sets and would look for a different route if one existed. Now I wonder whether this is defensible. Is it legitimate to accept choice for routine structural existence proofs while excluding the wilder applications? Or does Russell's boots-and-socks distinction show that the real line is not between harmless and pathological applications, but between cases where a selection rule can be given and cases where it cannot? Is my policy principled, or is it just a bias toward consequences I can visualize? Please assess the habit and, if it is not defensible, say what a defensible policy would look like for a non-specialist who needs to use set theory but cannot follow every foundational argument. The answer should be in American English.
