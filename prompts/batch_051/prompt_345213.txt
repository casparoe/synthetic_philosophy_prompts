I am a high school senior and will be giving a twenty-minute presentation at my school’s Philosophy and Psychology Club. The talk defends a version of Wilfrid Sellars’s attack on what he called the myth of the given. My thesis is that sensory experience cannot, by itself, justify any belief, because whatever justifies a belief must be able to stand in logical relations to that belief, such as supporting it, contradicting it, or counting as evidence for it, and raw, unconceptualized experience cannot do this. I plan to end by arguing that the demand for a non-inferential foundation in pure observation is a mistake, and that justification always depends at least partly on other beliefs and on conceptual capacities we bring to experience, not on a direct encounter with what is simply “given.”

The audience makes the question period difficult because it is split. Most members are psychology students who think good science means starting from observation; two are strong debaters who have read some online philosophy and will demand precise arguments rather than slogans; the faculty advisor teaches biology and treats direct observation as close to a professional principle; and one senior has been admitted to a philosophy program, has heard of Sellars, and will catch any slide in reasoning. I know the shape of the argument I want to present, but I do not know how to handle the questions afterward. Please prepare me: give me the objections to expect, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way this particular audience would actually put it, with a roughly two-minute reply for each, a note on where that reply is weakest, what I should concede up front versus hold firm on, and the one question I should most hope nobody asks.
