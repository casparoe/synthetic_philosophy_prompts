I've been stuck moderating a year-long argument between two capable grad students in my ethics seminar, and I'd rather have them produce something than keep trading the same intuitions. One of them holds a Kantian line about moral worth: an action has full moral worth only if it's done from the thought that duty requires it, so the motive must track duty as such. The other holds that moral worth is a matter of acting for the right-making reasons themselves, and those reasons need not be present in deliberation under the description my duty, or because it's right; the agent can simply be responsive to the concrete moral shape of the situation. Neither is allowed to settle the question by appeal to what Kant meant.

Draft the joint statement they should be able to sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Use Kant's discussion of the prudent shopkeeper and the sympathetic benefactor in the first section of the Groundwork of the Metaphysic of Morals (Fundamental Principles of the Metaphysic of Morals, Abbott translation) as their shared test case, but require them to paraphrase the relevant passage rather than quote it, since both keep misremembering fine wording. I want: a numbered list of claims both can accept about the case and about moral worth generally; a disagreements table giving each side's own precise claim about what's missing in the other account, phrased so that side would accept the wording; and a list of two or three cruxes — sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one side. For each crux, include a concrete further case or piece of evidence both agree bears on it, and what each predicts about it. If part of their dispute is merely verbal, say which part and why. The point is not to prove one side right; it's to make the residual disagreement exact.
