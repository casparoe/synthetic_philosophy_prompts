I keep going in circles on the old rule that not knowing a law doesn't excuse you from it, and I've started using a term I made up to pin down what bothers me: "epistemic duress." Normal duress gets someone off the hook (or at least softens the blame) because they were forced against their will by a threat. My thought is that being bound by a rule you had no realistic way of knowing about is also a kind of forcing — not a threat aimed at you, but a trap laid where you couldn't see it. The state's command never reached you in any form you could have received, yet you still pay for violating it. So I want to say: liability under unknowable rules is duress in the epistemic register, and if we take ordinary duress seriously as a defense, we should take epistemic duress seriously too, or at least treat it as mitigation. I think it's different from a ordinary mistake of law, because its not about what you happened to believe; it's about whether the rule was knowable at all from where you stood — like an ambiguous agency rule buried in the federal register, contradicted by published guidance, that even a specialist couldn't have sorted out beforehand.

I'm not after a reading list or citations; I just want a candid read on the idea itself. Is the analogy to duress doing real conceptual work, or is it just a vivid metaphor that collapses when you press it? Has this already been worked out under a name I'd recognize? And where's the line between "unknowable in principle" and "I didn't bother to look"? Keep it to one tight paragraph, 200 words max, and lead with the bottom line: is epistemic duress a real category or not.
