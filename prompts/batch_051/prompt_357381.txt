I keep returning to the regularity view of causation and finding it compelling on broadly empiricist grounds. Everything we can actually observe, it seems, is constant conjunction: events of type A are followed by events of type B, and that is all. The supposed necessary connection between cause and effect is never itself an object of experience; on this view it is something the mind contributes through custom and habit after repeated exposure, an expectation we project onto the world rather than a relation we discover in it. The view is simple, remains within the bounds of experience, and avoids postulating unobservable powers. But I also cannot shake the conviction that when this match is struck, the striking really produces the flame — that there is something more in the world, not just in my mental habits, which distinguishes a genuine causal sequence from a mere coincidence. On the regularity view, after all, the same pair of events may occur in both cases, and the difference is located in what has happened before or in how my mind is disposed. I have come across the New Hume reading, on which Hume himself accepted the existence of real but unknowable necessary connections in the world while remaining skeptical about our access to them; that seems to promise a reconciliation, but I worry it merely relocates the mystery behind an epistemic veil. Give me the strongest case for each of the three resolutions — that the intuition should be debunked and given up, that the theory should be amended or abandoned, and that the skeptical-realist reading genuinely reconciles them — and then offer a considered judgment about which should yield, making clear what each option costs. Write your answer in American English.
