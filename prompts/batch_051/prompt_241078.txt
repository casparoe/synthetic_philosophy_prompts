After retiring from a career in the natural sciences, I have been reading moral philosophy and keep returning to Benjamin Franklin's project of moral self-improvement. As I understand him, Franklin proposed to make himself virtuous the way an investigator would attack a hard empirical problem: list the virtues, practice one at a time while keeping a daily ledger of failures, and thereby build each virtue as a durable habit. The project seems to me to rest on at least three empirical assumptions: that the virtues are stable, general traits of persons; that practice in one context transfers to the contexts that matter; and that sustained self-monitoring tends to produce the trait rather than merely measuring it or changing one's attention.

The development that worries me is the situationist strand in experimental moral psychology, together with more recent mixed findings about habit formation and self-monitoring. Taken charitably, these literatures suggest that behavior is often controlled by situations rather than by the broad dispositions Franklin's ledger is meant to build, and that monitored practice may not yield the durable cross-situational change the project requires. I would like to know whether Franklin's case for his method survives this development, taken in its strongest defensible form. Which premise is actually under the most pressure, is the damage fatal or only limiting, and what exactly would a repaired version of the program claim if it gave that premise up? Also say which consideration, if it turned out otherwise, would be most likely to make you reverse your verdict, and where your reasoning is least secure.

I am not asking for a survey of the literature or for citations, and please write in American English. End with exactly one machine-readable verdict of the form \answer{survives unchanged}, \answer{survives only in repaired form}, or \answer{does not survive}.
