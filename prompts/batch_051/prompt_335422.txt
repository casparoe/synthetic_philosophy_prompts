I sit on the Academic Discipline Review Board of a British university. At our next meeting we must decide whether to adopt a proposed "supported recovery pathway" for first-time plagiarism and collusion. The proposal would replace the present penalty — a zero on the assessment and a note on the student's file — with a twelve-month scheme in which the student signs a learning agreement, attends fortnightly meetings with an academic conduct officer, keeps a reflective journal examining why they cheated, and consents to having subsequent work checked by similarity software whose results are discussed in those meetings; successful completion expunges the record. The chair has asked each member to bring a short written opinion making a concrete recommendation, with a time frame, that the board could act on.

Last night I argued about the proposal with a friend, who pressed the line that this is Foucauldian discipline dressed as care: a bounded, visible penalty is replaced by longer and more intimate observation; the journal and the meetings solicit self-narration and turn the student into a case to be managed; and the monitoring presents itself as neutral knowledge while feeding administrative power. I am genuinely torn, because the present system is crude, and the pathway does seem to re-engage students rather than simply branding them. Please draft the opinion I will circulate. It should recommend adopting the proposal, rejecting it, or adopting it with specific limits; take seriously both the developmental case for the scheme and the Foucauldian concern about normalising surveillance rather than merely restating either; and state plainly where the losing side's strongest consideration still has force. The board writes in a formal register, and the opinion should be in British English.
