During the pandemic, lots of governments did stuff that sat oddly next to each other: they locked people in their homes, shut businesses, and closed borders, in some places making it extremely hard for their own citizens to leave or come back. Some of it was done under emergency statutes, but often the legal cover was somewhere between thin and nonexistent. Courts later mostly let it stand by saying the situation was unprecedented or that the measures were necessary — and even when a court said a measure was unlawful, it often refused any remedy because the state had acted for the public good. I don't need case law; treat that pattern as a practice worth reading for its implicit theory.

What does this practice presuppose about the relationship between legality and legitimacy? For example, does it presuppose that an emergency can make an act legitimate even when it wasn't legal — legitimacy floating free of legality? Or does it presuppose instead that the legal order already contains a standing permission to break it's own rules in extremis, so the acts were legal after all? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, and separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with. Also tell me whether the practice is coherent: could someone who thinks law is exhausted by the enacted rules and someone who thinks law also includes moral standards both sign off on it, but for different reasons? And what would have to change in the practice if we swapped in a clean picture where legality just is a matter of whether the enacted rules were followed?
