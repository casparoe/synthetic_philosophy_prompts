Before I commit either way on the regulative principle — the claim that whatever is not commanded in corporate worship is forbidden, as against the normative principle that whatever is not forbidden is permitted — I want the full specification of what an adequate theory of worship permissibility has to deliver. Write the requirements list: the cases it must rule on without ad hoc manoeuvring (instrumental music, hymnody of human composition, the liturgical calendar, images, and borderline cases like meeting times, amplification, and seating); the distinctions it cannot collapse (element versus circumstance, commanded versus merely permitted, corporate practice versus individual conscience, and the indifferent-in-itself versus the indifferent-in-worship); the objections it must survive (the principle itself seems not to be commanded in so many words, so its warrant must be an inference from Scripture, and the proponent must explain why that inferential route does not license the very innovations it is meant to forbid; and the fact that the New Testament does not reissue the ritual detail of the Old Testament law); and the pastoral phenomena it cannot contradict (scripturally serious believers divide over hymnody and holy days, and a congregation should be able to meet without a building, or in a borrowed one, without absurd rulings). For each requirement give the case or consideration behind it, mark which are non-negotiable and which are one camp's preference, flag any pair that cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which single requirement does most to exclude the main contenders. The scorecard can be brief or omitted.
