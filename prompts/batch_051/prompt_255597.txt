This has been eating at me for a week, partly because I'd just done the same thing to my partner—told them their jealousy about a coworker was "wrong"—and then couldn't say what I meant. On Thursday I was at dinner with my sister and her boyfriend, and they got into a version of the same fight about a friend of ours, Dana. Dana's older brother got married in a tiny ceremony three months ago and didn't invite her—or so she thought. What actually happened was the invitation went to an old email address, he assumed she'd ghosted him, and she spent the whole three months furious at him over being excluded. She found out the truth last week and is still angry, which is what started it.

Here's roughly how the argument went.

(1) My sister said Dana's anger was wrong, full stop. Anger like that is about being deliberately left out, and the leaving-out never happened, so the emotion misrepresents the world. She said if a belief with that content would be false, then the emotion with that content is incorrect in exactly the same way.

(2) Her boyfriend said that's a category mistake. Emotions aren't the kind of thing that can be true or false—a headache can't be wrong. What was false was the seperate belief; the emotion itself just is whatever it is.

(3) My sister came back and said that can't be right, because it would make it impossible to criticize any emotional response as wrong. If someone is terrified of their own shadow, or is delighted by another person's suffering, his view has no vocabulary except "false belief" to say what's bad about those responses. That seems absurd.

(4) Her boyfriend said there's a middle vocabulary: fitting, reasonable, proportionate. We evaluate emotions for how well they fit the situation as the person had reason to see it—not for truth. Dana's anger was fitting on her evidence; a phobic fear is unfitting even if the person believes it. So we can criticize emotions without calling them true or false. He said my sister keeps smuggling correctness conditions back in because she can't let go of the idea that an emotion is a kind of assertion.

They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't. Please adjudicate it move by move: rate each move from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a sentence or two of justification, then say who is more right overall and where exactly the line between "right about the emotion" and "right about the belief" should fall. I want you to commit—don't tell me each side is a bit of both and leave it there.
