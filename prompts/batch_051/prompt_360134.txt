I run a small film-and-philosophy discussion group — none of us has any academic training in philosophy, though a few of us have been reading around on our own — and we have reached a disagreement we cannot settle. After watching Arrival, the 2016 film based on Ted Chiang's novella Story of Your Life, the group split over what the film is actually claiming about the relationship between language and thought. The film appears to assert that learning the heptapods' written language — the circular logograms in which a whole statement is inscribed at once, with no beginning or end — changes Louise's mind so profoundly that she begins to experience time as the heptapods do, not as a sequence but as a whole. That looks like a strong version of the claim that the language one learns reshapes one's thinking.

But several of us have, independently, been reading about the language of thought hypothesis: the view that thinking happens in an internal, pre-linguistic system of mental representations — sometimes called Mentalese — that is already in place before any natural language is learned; that learning a spoken language is really a matter of translating between this private code and a public one; and that a first language could not be learned at all if one had to think in that language in order to learn it, since the learner would already need the concepts the language is supposed to teach. On that view, Louise could acquire Heptapod B only if she already possessed the underlying concepts — for instance, a tenseless or simultaneous way of taking in events — and the alien script could at most give her a new notation, a new habit of attention, or a new public code for thoughts she was already capable of having. Yet the film treats the language as doing genuine cognitive work, not merely relabeling old thoughts.

I would like you to write a reading of Arrival through the lens of the language of thought hypothesis, for the group. Where does the film genuinely conflict with the hypothesis, where could it be read as compatible with it, and where does the hypothesis have to stretch to accommodate what the film shows? Take the film and the philosophy equally seriously; do not reduce the film to an illustration of a theory. Point to particular scenes — the decoding of the first logogram, the exchange in which Louise reinterprets the heptapods' weapon as their language, the scenes with Louise's daughter, and the ending — and describe them rather than quoting dialogue from memory. It would help if you considered a middle reading: that an external symbol system such as a script or a notation can reorganize pre-existing conceptual capacities the way learning algebra or musical notation does, and whether that weaker claim is still a problem for the language of thought hypothesis. End by saying what the film dramatizes that the philosophical debate misses, or what it gets wrong that the philosophy gets right. Around 900 words.
