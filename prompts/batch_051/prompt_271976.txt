I have an argument from a friend that seems to prove too much, and I want a careful diagnosis rather than a vote on which horn to take. The argument is stylized so that nothing turns on disputed historical details; treat the descriptions in the premises as stipulated. If it helps, picture an East India Company–type body and the state that chartered it.

(1) The Company is a joint-stock corporation created by royal charter. Its charter authorizes it to raise troops, make treaties, coin money, levy revenue, administer justice, and govern territory beyond the chartering state's own boundaries, and it has in fact exercised these powers for a long time. It has shareholders, a governing board, and legal personality distinct from its members.

(2) Whatever exercises the characteristic powers of government over a territory—making law, judging disputes, levying revenue, keeping armed force, and conducting foreign relations—is a state with respect to that territory.

(3) So the Company is a state.

(4) But the Company is also plainly a corporation: an artificial legal person whose existence, powers, and organs are conferred by law.

(5) Therefore being a corporation does not bar an entity from being a state: the two statuses are compatible.

(6) Now run the same analysis on the state that chartered the Company. It exercises the characteristic powers of government over a territory, so by (2) it is a state. Its powers, too, are constituted by law—statute, precedent, constitutional convention—not by nature; it acts only through organs the law specifies; it may hold property, contract, and be sued; and it has no personality outside its legal order.

(7) The apparent differences—subjects rather than shareholders, no board of the joint-stock kind, a claimed monopoly on the legitimate use of force, and the claim that its authority is final rather than delegated—do not belong to the definition of a corporation. A corporation is simply an artificial legal person whose powers are conferred by law; after all, a municipal corporation has no shareholders and no board of the joint-stock kind.

(8) So by parity, the state that chartered the Company is itself a corporation; it differs from the Company only in the content and origin of its charter.

(9) But that seems to prove too much: either the Company was a state while remaining a creature of the order that chartered it, or the chartering state is the same kind of thing as the Company, and the distinction between an order's constituting authority and a power delegated within that order is only one of degree.

I want to know which premise or step to reject; if you think the argument is valid, say which premise it would be least costly to give up. Then explain why the rejected premise looked true and what it costs to reject it, including which further commitments have to change with it. I would rather keep (2) than give up the distinction between a chartered company and the order that charters it, but I will not insist on that if the cost is too high. Begin with a one-sentence statement of your answer; keep the whole reply to at most 800 words.
