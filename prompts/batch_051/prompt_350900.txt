In my political theory seminar we've been reading Arendt on the Eichmann trial, and one guy in the room keeps deploying "the banality of evil" like its a trump card. Every time someone tries to say anything about Eichmann's actual motives — whether he believed the antisemitic stuff, whether he got off on the power, whether he was just scared of looking bad to his superiors — this guy says none of it matters, because the banality of evil means Eichmann wasn't a monster, just some ordinary paperwork guy, and that's exactly the point. When I tried to push back and say "not a monster" could mean half a dozen things (he didn't enjoy it, he didn't hate his victims, he had no real ideology, he was just following orders, the system would have run without him...), he just repeated the phrase at me, louder.

For next week I want to come in with a careful disambiguation. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims that "the banality of evil" might be making — about Eichmann's inner life, about evil as such, about what makes genocide horrifying, about the role of bureaucracy — and for each one say whether its actually something Arendt's account supports, whether it's independently plausible, and whether it would license the "stop asking about his psychology" move? I'd especially like to know which readings are themselves pretty banal and which are the substantive, contestable ones, and which one my classmate is most likely leaning on.
