I've been chewing on a little theory about what the year zero of a calendar ought to be, and I'd like an honest assessment not a pat on the head. I call it the Standing Risk Rule. The idea is that an epoch isnt just a counting convention; it's the one date the whole system quietly marks as before and after, so it should sit at the most recent point after which some previously optional risk became permanent for every subsequent generation. By permanent I mean theres no going back, and by every subsequent generation I mean exactly that, not just the people alive at the time. Running the rule, I land at 1945, or better the stretch from the first controlled chain reaction under the stands in Chicago in 1942 to the bombs in 1945, because that's when the end of the species stopped being a thought experiment and became a standing feature of the world. A child born in 1930 could in principle plan a life without taking the self-inflicted end of everything into account; a child born in 1950 could not, and nor could any child since. The rule also tells me why the French revolutionaries attempt to restart the calendar at the founding of their republic was a different kind of thing—reversible, political, local in scope—and why the old epoch is unfit for a global calendar, since whatever its religious merits, it doesnt mark a threshold in the standing risk landscape. I know the obvious charge: I picked 1945 and then built a rule to launder it. So tell me straight: is the rule doing real work or is it post hoc? Has someone already worked out a serious criterion for choosing an epoch, under another name, and where does mine differ or collapse into it? And where does the rule break—does it imply the epoch should move again once a newer permanent risk arrives, and if so is that a bug or the point?
