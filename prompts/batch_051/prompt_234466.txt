An antimeme is an idea or piece of information whose content or framing makes it less likely to be remembered, copied, or taught. Consider a proposed institution aimed at one canonical kind of antimeme: the negative result. A scientific society creates a Dead Ends Registry with an annuity rule. Any researcher can deposit a negative result, a failed replication, or an abandoned approach, along with a one-page note on why it was abandoned. Each January the depositor's lab receives a fixed payment for every deposited item not yet activated; an item becomes activated in the year it is cited in at least three published articles or adopted by at least two independent working groups, at which point payments stop. The registry is funded by a flat membership tax, entries cannot be browsed or sorted by field (only by author name or exact title), and a small review board can retire entries it judges trivial, fraudulent, or redundant.

Walk through how this institution would actually behave. Who deposits, and do they lean toward genuine dead ends, near-misses, or manufactured nulls? How does the annuity interact with the standing incentives not to cite, replicate, or build on negative results? Where does it get gamed first—salami-sliced depositions, citation cartels that activate or deliberately refrain from activating entries, or elsewhere? Does the review board fix the failures or add new ones? Finally, what second-order effects might the registry have on what counts as a publishable result, and does it risk creating antimemes of its own?
