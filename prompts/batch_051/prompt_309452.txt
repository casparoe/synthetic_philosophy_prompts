I keep chewing on this argument against scientific realism and I can't tell whether it's airtight or whether I'm smuggling something in. Here it is:

(1) A theory never entails any observation statement all by itself; it only yields predictions when conjoined with a bunch of auxilary assumptions — instruments, initial conditions, background theory, ceteris paribus clauses.

(2) So when a prediction fails, what's falsified is only the whole package: at least one member is false, but the failure itself never tells you which one.

(3) By exactly the same logic, when a prediction succeeds the success can't tell you which member of the package deserves credit, so the observation doesn't differentially confirm the theory over its rivals — any piece of the package might be doing the work.

(4) Therefore no possible observation can ever make one theory more probable than its competitors.

(5) So empirical evidence gives us no rational basis for preferring one theory over another.

The conclusion seems absurd, but I can't locate the slide. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails, and under what reading of it? If it is valid, which premise should a scientific realist reject, and what would the correct version of that premise be?
