I'm teaching philosophy of law this term and keep coming back to a hypothetical I want checked. Suppose a constitution has exactly one provision on judicial review, and it reads: 'The Supreme Court shall have power to declare void any Act of the Legislature that is contrary to this Constitution.' The constitution says nothing about the size or composition of the Court. The drafters plainly had in mind statutes that violate a specific article—bills of attainder, discriminatory taxes, that sort of thing. Now suppose the legislature, without amending the constitution, passes a Judiciary (Reconstitution) Act that adds fifteen seats to the Supreme Court, fills them all within a month, and reassigns every constitutional challenge to a new panel made up only of the new appointees. Everyone agrees the Act doesn't violate any specific article. Does the review clause reach it—that is, can the Court void a statute whose only 'inconsistency' is that it would make future inconsistencies unreviewable? I dont care much about the enforcement politics. Walk me through the text, the purpose, and what the drafters would have said if asked, and tell me which should win. Then tell me: if the clause reaches the Act in self-defense, what stops that reasoning from swallowing any law that regulates the courts; and if it doesn't, hasn't the legislature found a lawful way to abolish judicial review without changing a word of the constitution?
