Two linguists in my department got into an argument at a workshop last week about what it means to call something a "rule" of grammar; the immediate subject was irregular morphology, suppletion, and sound change. I took down the exchange as best I could, but I cannot decide who had the better of it.

(1) The first said the verb "go" is enough to show that talk of grammatical rules is empty. No rule of English derives "went" from "go"; the past tense is supplied by an unrelated stem in suppletion, and a learner simply has to memorize the pair. If one of the most frequent verbs in English is an exception from top to bottom, then past-tense formation is not ultimately rule-governed.

(2) The second replied that this confuses a rule with the regularity of surface outputs. A rule can be real even when its application is blocked by listed lexical items: the default is "add -ed," and "go" is lexically marked as taking its past form from another stem. Calling "went" an exception presupposes the rule rather than refutes it, because an exception is an exception only relative to a rule.

(3) The first countered that "blocked by a listed item" makes the theory unfalsifiable: whatever does not fit can always be stored as an exception, leaving the rule with no work to do. Historically, the go–went pattern is a residue of sound change and semantic drift between formerly independent verbs, not something any synchronic rule produced. And children often produce "goed" before they learn "went," which suggests they apply a productive rule exactly where the language flouts it, so the "rule" is really a generalization with a hole, not a principle that governs the paradigm.

(4) The second answered that children's "goed" is evidence for the rule, not against it: overregularization shows a default rule is applied whenever the stored irregular form has not been acquired or retrieved, and the fact that "went" must be memorized as an override is precisely what a two-route model predicts. A pure analogy-and-memory account would not explain why errors converge on a single default. As for history, a synchronic grammar is not an etymology.
