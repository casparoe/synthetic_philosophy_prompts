I've been a long-time lurker on LessWrong, and at a meet-up a friend laid out an argument about the thermodynamic arrow of time that I can't find the flaw in. It seems to prove to much, because it ends up implying that my memories of the past are probably false. I've tried to steelman it into numbered steps so I can see exactly where it breaks, but each step still looks individually fine while the conclusion looks absurd.

1. The underlying microphysical laws are time-symmetric to an excellent approximation: if a process can run forward, the reversed process is also physically possible.
2. So the microphysics alone can't pick out a direction of time; any asymmetry between past and future has to come from statistical mechanics.
3. The usual statistical story is that entropy tends to increase because high-entropy macro-states are vastly more numerous than low-entropy ones. Relative to a low-entropy boundary condition, entropy is overwhelmingly likely to be higher on any side you look at.
4. But this reasoning doesn't itself say which direction is the future. Take any given non-maximal macro-state as a boundary, and entropy is overwhelmingly likely to be higher on both temporal sides of it.
5. Now take the macro-state of your brain right now, including all its apparent memories of a long past. Given only that macro-state and a uniform measure over the micro-states compatible with it, the overwhelming majority of those micro-states are ones in which the memories are a chance local fluctuation — a Boltzmann brain, briefly assembled out of the surrounding equilibrium rather than produced by an actual low-entropy history.
6. So, if the thermodynamic arrow is all there is to time, it is overwhelmingly likely that the past you remember didn't happen — that your apparent memories formed by chance moments ago.
7. But that can't be the right conclusion. A theory on which you should distrust all your memories is self-undermining, because your acceptance of the theory is itself arrived at through those memories.

My friend says the self-undermining objection is just cope and the conclusion follows whether I like it or not. I'd much rather keep premises (1) and (2) — and the general trustworthiness of memory — than accept (6), but I can't see which other step earns the rejection, or what giving it up would cost. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If the argument is valid as stated, which premise is least costly to give up? If you could write the answer in British English, I'd appreciate it.
