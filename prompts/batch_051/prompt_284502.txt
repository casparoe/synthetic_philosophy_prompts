Write a parody of a health-care advance directive, roughly 700 words, in which the signer—a competent adult with strong philosophical views about personal identity—tries to use the standard legal form to settle, in advance, every preference she can imagine changing. The document should begin in plausible boilerplate (a do-not-resuscitate instruction; a refusal of antibiotics for pneumonia if she would no longer be the person who signed it), and then escalate: a clause forbidding any future self from developing a taste for country music, a clause specifying how to treat the contented but severely demented person who will eventually inhabit her body, a penalty clause for later selves who revise her will, and provisions about who counts as her for purposes of honoring the document. Write it in the calm, euphemistic voice of a real legal-medical form, with numbered sections, definitions, and at least one place where the signer must initial.

The target of the satire should be the view that the person who exists now is a kind of legislator over the person who exists later—the overextension of what philosophers call precedent autonomy or a Ulysses contract—as well as the legal practice that enforces some earlier self-binding refusals while refusing to enforce others. The humor should come from getting the philosophical content right: a specialist in the literature on advance directives and personal identity should recognize the real doctrinal tensions being exaggerated, and a lay reader should still find it funny. Keep the irony deadpan and legible; do not break character, and do not have the form announce its own absurdity.
