I'm writing a paper on the ethics of forgiveness, and I keep getting stuck at what I initially thought was a merely terminological question. In the material I've been reading, people argue both about whether forgiveness can be obligatory — whether a victim could ever be under a moral requirement to forgive — and about whether it can be unconditional — whether someone can be forgiven without repentance, apology, or any change on the wrongdoer's part. My suspicion is that a surprising amount of this disagreement is secretly a disagreement about what the word picks out: whether forgiving is fundamentally an internal change in the victim, a communicative act addressed to the wrongdoer, a decision or commitment, or something else. Please act as a professional philosopher working on moral psychology and the interpersonal attitudes, and help me build a working definition of forgiveness that can do theoretical work in that debate rather than just tracking common usage. Start by brainstorming two or three rival analyses before settling on one, and say explicitly where each candidate analysis succeeds and where it strains.

Here are the cases I want the definition to get right, or at least to return a determinate verdict on. (a) Apologetic friend: a friend lies to you about something important, then apologizes without being prompted, explains herself, and works to repair the trust between you; you forgive her and the friendship mostly recovers. (b) Faded resentment: a coworker humiliated you years ago; you never addressed it, but the resentment simply faded with time and distance. (c) Excusing the wrongdoer: your sibling treated you cruelly during a family crisis; you later conclude that, given his mental state and the circumstances, his conduct was far less blameworthy than you had thought, and your anger dissolves because you no longer see him as having wronged you. (d) Forgiving out of duty: you have been badly wronged, and your religious tradition teaches that forgiveness is a duty; you commit yourself to acting and speaking toward the wrongdoer without hostility and you say you forgive her, even though you privately admit the resentment has not gone away. (e) Unrepentant wrongdoer: a former business partner betrayed you seriously; he is alive, unrepentant, denies he did anything wrong, and will never apologize, yet you decide to set aside vindictive resentment and genuinely wish him well. (f) Posthumous forgiveness: the same as (e), except the wrongdoer has since died without ever having acknowledged the wrong. (g) Third-party forgiveness: a friend says he forgives you for the serious lie you told his sister, even though she is the one who was wronged and she has not forgiven you herself. (h) Self-forgiveness: you sabotaged a colleague's project years ago; after long remorse and such amends as you could make, you say you have finally forgiven yourself. (i) Forgiveness without reconciliation: you forgive an ex-partner who was emotionally abusive, as part of a deliberate project of letting go of corrosive resentment, but you refuse any contact or reconciliation and you still want others to hold him accountable. For what it's worth, I am confident that (a) is forgiveness and (b) is not; I lean toward saying (c) is not forgiveness either, because it seems more like excusing or reinterpreting; and I have no settled view about (d) through (i), with (d) being the case that most makes me doubt the whole project.

Please give me, in prose, your considered definition and then run it through all nine cases, saying where it classifies correctly, where it gives a surprising answer, and where it gives no answer; then revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts. Because the point of the definition is to help with the questions I started from, also say what it implies about whether forgiveness can be unconditional and whether it can be obligatory, and flag the precise step on which those implications most depend. Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which counterexample or argument would lead you to a different definition or a different verdict on one of the cases, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
