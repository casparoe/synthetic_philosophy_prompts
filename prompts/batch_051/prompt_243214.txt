I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, so go easy on me if I've got the terminology off. I've been turning over a passage from Richard Rorty's "Response to Hartshorne," in Rorty and Pragmatism: The Philosopher Responds to His Critics, where he says: "I think of the course of human history as a long, swelling, increasingly polyphonic poem - a poem that leads up to nothing save itself. When the species is extinct, 'human nature's total message' will not be a set of propositions, but a set of vocabularies - the more, and the more various, the better." I've built a homemade theory on it that I call the polyphonic clause. The idea is that a novel's actual job isn't to represent the world or get a description right, but to enlarge the stock of vocabularies available to the species. A novel fails, on my view, when it leaves the language exactly as it found it, no matter how true its sentences are. Realism is just one vocabulary among many; a book earns its place in that polyphonic poem by making the whole thing longer, stranger, more internally contested. Truth is downstream of variety, not the other way around. My questions: is the polyphonic clause genuinely distinct from Rorty's antirepresentationalism, or have I just renamed his point and strapped a craftsman's ego onto it? Give me the three strongest objections to my theory, and tell me whether any of them is fatal rather than just uncomfortable. Where does the move from "more vocabularies" to "more is better" do too much work, and what does it get wrong about the difference between a vocabulary and a voice? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence then the reasoning, no preamble, no restating of the question.
