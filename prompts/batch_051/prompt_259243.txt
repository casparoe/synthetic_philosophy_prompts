You are an expert decision theorist and philosopher who works on choice under ignorance and interpersonal decision-making. Treat this as a reflective-equilibrium exercise: I will give you my considered verdicts on a series of simple choice problems, and I want you to find the principle—or small set of principles—that best captures them, testing each candidate case by case before settling on a proposal. Do not just assign each case to a textbook rule; look for the pattern behind the choices, including any threshold or tie-breaking rule it commits me to.

In every problem, the numbers are utility points, so larger is always better. In the personal cases I know the possible states of the world and the payoff of each option in each state, but I assign no probabilities to the states. In the distributive cases I choose between distributions across two people, and after the choice I will occupy exactly one of the two positions; there too I assign no probabilities to positions. The utility points in the distributive cases are interpersonally comparable.

Case 1 (personal, two states).
A: 10 in state 1, 0 in state 2.
B: 5 in state 1, 4 in state 2.
Verdict: B.

Case 2 (personal, two states).
C: 100 in state 1, 0 in state 2.
D: 80 in state 1, 1 in state 2.
Verdict: C.

Case 3 (personal, three states).

Verdict: F.

Case 4 (personal, two states).
H: 30 in state 1, 0 in state 2.
I: 10 in state 1, 9 in state 2.
Verdict: I.

Case 5 (distributive, two people).
J: person 1 gets 12, person 2 gets 0.
K: person 1 gets 5, person 2 gets 5.
Verdict: K.

Case 6 (distributive, two people).
L: person 1 gets 100, person 2 gets 0.

I am also torn about one further personal case, and I want a prediction from the principle rather than a post hoc classification:

Case 7 (personal, two states).
N: 50 in state 1, 0 in state 2.
O: 25 in state 1, 3 in state 2.
I keep flipping. The bottom gap is only three; the top gap is twenty-five.

I have already checked that pure maximin, pure minimax regret, a Hurwicz-style weighted average of best and worst, and equal-probability averaging each misclassify at least one of my verdicts, so none of them alone will do. Please give me: (a) the most natural principle, or small set of candidate principles, that fits my judgments, stated precisely; (b) an honest list of which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and whether any should be revised for coherence; and (c) what it predicts for Case 7—and whether that prediction depends on an underdetermined threshold that my current verdicts do not pin down. If the answer is that two different devices are doing the work, say so, and identify the smallest extra judgment that would settle which one I really hold.
