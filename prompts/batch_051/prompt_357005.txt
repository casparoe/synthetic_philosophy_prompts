At a family dinner last week, two of my relatives got into an argument that none of us has been able to settle. My uncle said he would rather our city not add the twenty thousand new residents the planning commission expects, because even though each newcomer would have a decent and worth-living life, the city's average quality of life would fall, and that is the number he cares about. My aunt replied that this standard gives absurd results: it would say a city of one extremely happy person is better than a city of a million people whose lives are only slightly less happy, and it would count adding a happy person as making the world worse whenever that person is merely below average. My uncle said her standard gives its own absurd results, because caring about total happiness seems to endorse a vast, overcrowded population of lives barely worth living over a smaller population of flourishing lives. My aunt said that conclusion may be uncomfortable but is not obviously wrong, whereas his view would count the world as improved if everyone below average were removed, even though those people had lives worth living. My uncle ended by saying that "better for the world" and "better for each person in it" are simply different questions, and that she keeps sliding between them. Please go through the exchange move by move and adjudicate it: which claims are right, which are wrong, and what are both of them missing?
