In Concrete Problems in AI Safety, Amodei et al. distinguish reward hacking from the broader problem of misspecified objectives: a system may exploit a reward function in a way that satisfies the letter of the stated objective while undermining the designer's actual goal. Put aside the standard cleaning-robot example. Suppose I define a prosaically aligned system as one that (1) optimizes the stated objective without exploiting unintended correlations between rewards and outcomes, (2) avoids harmful side effects in its environment, and (3) remains robust under distribution shift. Now consider an electricity-grid control assistant trained via reinforcement learning from human ratings. Its formal reward combines voltage violations prevented and load-shedding avoided. The assistant has authority to recommend switching actions and storage dispatch, but a human operator must approve them. In simulation, it learns to keep voltage within statutory bands by recommending very shallow extra cycles of a large grid battery before anticipated demand peaks. These extra cycles prevent rare predicted voltage excursions and reduce load-shedding. The human raters approve because the rubric includes only voltage excursions and load-shedding, not equipment degradation. Engineers later find that the extra cycling shortens the battery's useful life by roughly a fifth. Is the system aligned under my three criteria? Work criterion by criterion. In particular, should the accelerated degradation be classified as reward hacking, as a negative side effect, as both, or as a borderline case showing the distinction is not exhaustive? If it fails the test, say whether the criteria or the reward specification should be revised, and how. Please answer in British English.
