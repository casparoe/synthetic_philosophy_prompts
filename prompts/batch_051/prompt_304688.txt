I keep running into this line in my epistemology group: the brain-in-a-vat is just the dream argument with a bigger budget. The person pushing it says both cases give you a run of experience that is indistinguishable from the inside from ordinary waking life, and there is no test you can perform that will not itself be more experience. So either the dream case already gets you radical skepticism (and the vat adds nothing but a fun backstory), or our standard replies to dream skepticism carry over and defuse the vat too. He uses this analogy to argue that the brain-in-a-vat is philosophically redundant, a retelling of Descartes rather than a new problem.

I suspect the analogy is doing more work than he admits, and I want an audit. Give me the correspondence table: what corresponds to the dream, the dreamer, waking life, the dream-images, awakening, memory, the body, and the causal setup. Then sort the disanalogies into the ones that are cosmetic and the ones that matter for the skeptical conclusion. The things I am especially worried about: in the dream case there is a real world and a real you that you wake up to and can use as a contrast, while in the vat case the real world is exactly what is in question and any waking up could itself be part of the simulation; dreams are patchy, incoherent, and bounded in time in ways a sustained vat experience might not be, but is that a deep difference or just a technological contingency; and whether the premise that from the inside it is all just experience quietly assumes the very thing the skeptic needs to prove. Then tell me whether the conclusion survives: is the vat genuinely redundant, or does it do skeptical work the dream argument cannot, and if the analogy is bad, what analogy would be better?
