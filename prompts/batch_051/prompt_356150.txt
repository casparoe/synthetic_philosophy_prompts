Consider a variation on the ship-of-Theseus puzzle in which the persisting thing is a national currency rather than a wooden ship. A currency is a social kind: it exists only in virtue of collective acceptance, yet individual notes are physical objects that can be withdrawn, destroyed, or collected. Suppose that over several decades every note in circulation is gradually replaced with notes of a different material, size, and design, so that no original note remains in use. The currency is then officially renamed. Its issuance is transferred from the original authority to a newly created monetary authority. Finally, what stands behind it changes from a redeemable quantity of metal, to a government undertaking, to nothing beyond the settled practice of accepting it for wages, prices, and debts. Throughout, the public continues to treat this as one unbroken currency, while a collector who has saved all the withdrawn notes claims that she, not the central bank, possesses the original currency in its original form.

How should the standard responses to the ship-of-Theseus puzzle — identity as continuity of the same matter, identity as continuity of form or function, identity as spatiotemporal continuity, and identity as a matter of convention — be applied to this case? Which of the changes, if any, ends the currency's identity, and does the collector's hoard have a stronger or weaker claim to be the original currency than the circulating replacement? Does the example show that social kinds have different persistence conditions from natural artefacts, or merely that the same criteria are being applied to a different kind of fact? Please write your answer in British English.
