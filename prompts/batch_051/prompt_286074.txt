I've been collecting my friend Maya's verdicts on personal identity cases because she's halfway between a Hofstadter-ish view of the self as a pattern and a more ordinary view of the self as one unbroken stream of experience, and I want to know which way she actually tips. Her track record so far:

- Teleporter that scans you, destroys the original, and builds an exact copy far away: she says the person who steps out is not the person who stepped in. The original died.
- Your neurons get replaced one by one over many years with functionally identical artificial ones, and you stay conscious throughout: it's the same person the whole way, no question.
- A perfect copy of you is made while you're still alive: the copy is not you, it's a fresh center of experience, even though it has all your memories.
- After a close friend died, she said she could still hear him making grumpy comments inside her head, and that this was a real trace of him — but it wasn't him, more like an afterimage on the retina than a person still there.
- A split-brain patient whose hemispheres have been surgically disconnected: one person, not two, just a person whose parts aren't talking to each other.

Now the new case I want to predict. A woman was married for fifty years and got so braided with her husband that she carries around a running, generative model of him. She can produce novel jokes, judgments, and preferences that he never actually stated but that fit the grain of his mind. After he dies, the model keeps running on its own, sometimes against her own inclinations — his grumble about a friend, his veto of a purchase she wants to make. Maya has to say whether any part of him is actually still there, or whether it's still just an afterimage.

Give me your best guess about what Maya says, and more importantly tell me what rule or rules she's running — unbroken psychological continuity, graded pattern-instantiation, a deflationary view where the self is just a story, or something else — and which earlier verdicts each rule handles well or badly. Then make up the one further case that would cleanly settle which rule she really holds. Keep the whole answer under 400 words.
