I have been turning over a phrase I keep meeting while traveling: 'when in Rome, do as the Romans do.' I have watched it get used to settle arguments about whether a visitor owes it to locals to follow everyday conventions—not laws, but unwritten rules about tipping, queueing, and crossing the street. On one trip I saw the same shape of argument three times: a local said a visitor was in the wrong for not tipping table service the way locals do, for not picking up on how people form a line at a bus stop, and for jaywalking across an empty street while the signal said don't walk. Each time someone pushed back that these rules aren't posted anywhere and a stranger can't be expected to know them, and each time the first person ended the argument with 'when in Rome.' The proverb is clearly doing real work there—it is supposed to get from 'this is what locals do' to 'this is what a visitor should do'—but I can't tell which precise claim is carrying the load.

I'd like you to lay out the distinct principles that one sentence could be shorthand for: that following local ways is good strategy for getting what you want; that it is etiquette and the price of being treated decently; that visitors genuinely owe it to locals to follow their conventions; that locals have a claim to having their own norms respected on their home ground; that choosing to travel counts as consenting to whatever one finds; that flouting local ways expresses contempt even if no one is harmed. For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or just false, and whether that reading actually licenses the move from 'locals do this' to 'a visitor ought to do this.' Then say which reading the person who uses the proverb to settle the tipping argument is probably leaning on, and whether the proverb covers the harder cases: a convention a visitor has no easy way to notice, a convention that a substantial number of locals reject, and a convention with real moral stakes underneath it, like a queue where jumping ahead costs someone else time. I want the readings as a menu with verdicts, not one interpretation defended. Set aside the history of the proverb; I care about what it claims now.
