Question (30 marks). In the alignment literature, some authors argue that we should demand worst-case behavioral guarantees from AI systems, while others argue that only average-case guarantees are attainable. Answer the following:

(a) Define a worst-case guarantee and an average-case guarantee in your own words, using a language model that is claimed to be 'safe' as the example. (8 marks)

(b) Explain two distinct reasons why a guarantee that holds for most inputs from the training distribution can fail to reassure us in deployment. (8 marks)

(c) A defender of worst-case guarantees will often admit that such guarantees are still relative to a formal specification and to a class of inputs. Briefly explain what that relativity means, and give one reason it is hard to prove even such a relative worst-case property for a deep learning system. (8 marks)

(d) Suppose someone claimed to have a worst-case guarantee that an agent's goals, rather than its outputs, are aligned with the intended goals. Would this remove the need to worry about distribution shift? Answer in a few sentences. (6 marks)

Student answer:

(a) A worst-case guarantee says that the system satisfies the safety property on every input in the class of inputs the guarantee ranges over; an average-case guarantee says that it satisfies the property with high probability, or with low expected loss, when inputs are drawn from a specified distribution. For a language model, a worst-case safety guarantee would mean that no prompt in the certified class elicits the prohibited behavior, while an average-case guarantee would mean only that a randomly sampled prompt from the usage distribution is very unlikely to elicit it.

(b) First, an average-case guarantee concerns the probability mass of failures, not their severity. A model can be dangerous on a rare class of inputs and still look almost perfectly safe on average, yet those rare inputs may be the ones that matter most. Second, the distribution is unstable. Deployment prompts, especially from adversarial users, are selected to find precisely the tail where the average-case behavior was not certified, and a shifted user population need not match the distribution over which the average was taken. So an average-case guarantee gives little assurance about the inputs an adversary or a shifted population will actually produce.

(c) The relativity point means that 'safe on all prompts' can only mean safe on the set of strings one has formally declared to be prompts, under the formal definition of harm one has written down; the guarantee does not cover cases outside that specification. The reason it is hard to prove such a property for a deep learning system is that the guarantee would ultimately have to rest on the same finite training sample as the average-case estimate, and no finite sample can license a universal claim about a model with billions of free parameters.

(d) In principle, yes: a worst-case guarantee about goals would remove the distribution-shift worry. If the agent's objective function is certified to be the intended one whatever environment it finds itself in, then novel inputs will still be handled according to that objective, so the agent will continue to pursue the right goal even on out-of-distribution inputs. The remaining difficulty is not distribution shift itself but whether a goal-level guarantee could ever be secured; if it could be secured, the deployment distribution would no longer matter.
