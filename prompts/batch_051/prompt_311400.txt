I am a high school student preparing a presentation for a philosophy club on the difference between ordinary trust in a source and circular appeals to that source. I have encountered the terms easy knowledge and the bootstrapping problem, but I am still unsure how to apply them to concrete cases. Please rank the following five cases from least to most epistemically problematic, and give each a numerical score from 0 to 10, where 0 means a person may rely on the source with no independent evidence and 10 means the person needs fully independent evidence before relying on it.

1. A student steps onto a brand-new bathroom scale three times, gets exactly 142.0 pounds each time, and concludes that the scale is working properly.
2. A friend reports several times that a mutual friend is upset; after two of those reports match the mutual friend’s easily observable behavior, the student concludes the friend’s reports are generally trustworthy.
3. A person notices that his perceptual experiences have been coherent and useful in many ordinary situations, and concludes on that basis that his perceptual faculties are generally reliable.
4. A historian treats the internal consistency and level of specific detail in an old document as evidence that the document is not a forgery.
5. A textbook says on its first page that it is an authoritative source for the subject, and a student accepts that claim solely because the textbook says so.

In your answer, please separate what is relatively settled among epistemologists from what is genuinely contested, and avoid presenting a contested position as though it were the consensus. I would rather see the disagreement mapped clearly than have you smooth over it.
