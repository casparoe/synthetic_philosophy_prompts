I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy. I build behavior systems for non-player characters in an open-world game, and a conversation at work has left me stuck inside an argument that seems to force the main-character intuition: the thought that I am the only real subject of experience and everyone else is, in a deep sense, an NPC. I do not believe the conclusion, but I cannot find the failure.

The argument, as cleanly as I can state it:

(1) For every other human being, my only evidence of a mental life on the far side of the skin is behavioral: words, expressions, choices, timing, patterns of response. I never observe another person's pain, their visual experience, or their inner monologue directly.

(2) The stream of behavioral observations I will actually receive from any given person over the course of my acquaintance with them is finite. Any item in such a finite stream could in principle be emitted by a sufficiently elaborate non-conscious mechanism: a precomputed script keyed to each situation, an enormous lookup table, or a trained model with no inner life.

(3) Therefore, for any particular person, my total possible evidence is exactly what it would be on either of two rival hypotheses: mind, and mindless behavioral twin. The observations are identical on both.

(4) If my total possible evidence cannot distinguish two hypotheses, and I have no further resource that legitimately bears on the difference, then I am not rationally justified in believing one over the other.

(5) The only further resources I can find are a bare line drawn around my own kind — they are human, they have human brains — or an inference to the best explanation. The bare line just redraws the NPC boundary around whatever I already take myself to be, which looks like the main-character intuition doing the work rather than a reason for it. And the inference to the best explanation seems blocked by (2): a mindless mechanism fits the stream just as well, and positing an extra inner layer would explain nothing additional, so parsimony would count against it rather than for it.

(6) So I have no better reason to believe my colleague has an inner life than I have to believe a behaviorally indistinguishable NPC has one.

(7) But I believe the first and reject the second with overwhelming practical confidence, and my ordinary moral life is built on that asymmetry.

(8) Therefore, either that confidence in other people's minds is rationally unsupported — which is solipsism reached by a back door — or I must extend the same confidence to any sufficiently good NPC, which seems absurd.

I find both horns of (8) unacceptable, so I assume either a step is defective or the argument is valid and one of the premises costs far more than it appears. I would most like to keep (1) and (4); if surrendering one of those is the real price, I want that said outright. Where does the argument go wrong, and what exactly am I committed to once I give up the step I reject?
