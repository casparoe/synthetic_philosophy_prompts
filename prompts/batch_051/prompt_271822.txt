Lately I've been reporting on cliodynamics, the attempt to turn historical dynamics into something quantifiable and even predictable, and I keep coming back to one question about Peter Turchin's demographic-structural theory of secular cycles. As I understand the core argument, it was built for preindustrial agrarian states: population growth presses against the agricultural ceiling; real wages fall while land rents rise; the elite class expands faster than the number of elite positions, producing elite overproduction; state finances weaken; and the combination of popular immiseration and intraelite competition drives the wave of political breakdown that resets the cycle. The model, in other words, tacitly assumes a closed, land-based economy and a fairly stable institutional skeleton.

Suppose now you keep the same demographic-structural mechanism but try to run it in a modern, globally integrated industrial economy: the state borrows in its own fiat currency, runs transfer programs, imports food and labor, and its elites compete through educational credentials and positions in finance, administration, or the professions rather than through a fixed stock of aristocratic offices. I'm not asking whether Turchin's specific predictions about particular countries were historically correct; I'm asking what happens to the argument itself when the closed agrarian assumptions are relaxed. Does the mechanism still go through, does it need repair, or does it become inapplicable? Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. For each item, take a distinct premise of the demographic-structural argument and say whether the modern setting breaks it, preserves it, or forces it to be restated.
