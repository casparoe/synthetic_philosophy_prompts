I've been trying to settle how much of the "reality" of generations is just an artifact of institutions treating them as real, so here's a counterfactual I want a careful prediction about. Starting in 2030, every US federal statistical agency and all the major academic social-science survey programs adopt a binding rule: no tabulations, press releases, or papers may classify respondents as Boomers, Gen X, Millennials, Gen Z, or any named generation. If you want to do cohort analysis you have to use rolling five-year birth cohorts, continuous age effects, or something similar. Private companies, political campaigns, and ordinary people remain free to use generational labels however they like; they just loose the official data infrastructure and most academic social science behind them.

Predict how this plays out over the next forty years. Specifically: (1) does "Millennial" and its successors as a folk category wither, harden into a purely culture-war identity, or drift free from any empirical claims about behavior and attitudes? (2) Do the real differences people now attribute to generations—political attitudes, work norms, technology use, and so on—get redescribed as something else, and if so, what: age effects, period effects, class, educational timing, something else? (3) What happens to generational conflict in politics and workplaces: does it cool down, migrate to finer cohort language, or get replaced by some other us-vs-them axis? I want the mechanism for each link, not a story. Tell me which predictions you're most confident about, which are guesses, and what would have to be different about how social categories work for you to be wrong.
