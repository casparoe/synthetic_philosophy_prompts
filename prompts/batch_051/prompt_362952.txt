In a medieval philosophy reading group I've been sitting in on, two of the regulars got into it about Abelard's claim that sin lies in the consent, not in the act. The question on the table was whether this makes the outward deed morally irrelevant, and neither of them would budge. Their back-and-forth went like this.

(1) Rina said the claim commits Abelard to something strikingly revisionary: if you fully consent to a murder in your heart but are stopped by circumstance before you can act, your sin is already complete, so the person who actually goes through with it is not more blameworthy. She said that flies in the face of the way almost everyone thinks, since actually killing someone seems far worse than only wanting to, and it's the sort of intuition you can't just shrug of.

(2) Dev replied that she was flattening the view. Abelard still distinguishes the earlier stages of temptation and delight from full consent, and he has room for the act to matter indirectly: carrying a plan out is a way of re-consenting at each step, reinforcing the will, and creating scandal and harm. So the deed isn't inert even if guilt gets its start in the interior yes.

(3) Rina answered that this just concedes the main point. If each stage of the act is a fresh consent, the person who was prevented can run through the same renewals in his own head with just as much commitment, so the agent's guilt hasn't been shown to be any larger. Scandal and harm to victims change other people's situation, not the agent's degree of sin.

(4) Dev said maybe that's exactly what Abelard means: before God the deed adds nothing, while before human courts and in the community the act is all we can go on, so it is reasonable to treat the outward action as specially serious even though its seriousness is derivative of consent. He added that the intuition that killing is worse is one Abelard could accept, but only at the level of human punishment, not divine judgment.

They asked the rest of us to referee, and honestly no one could. I'd like an adjudication of the four moves point by point — who is right about what at each step, not just who wins overall — and also a judgment on whether Abelard's view really does have the revisionary implication Rina says it does. Also, say how confident you are in your reading and single out the point you're least sure of.
