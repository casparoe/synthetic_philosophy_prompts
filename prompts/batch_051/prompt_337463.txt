Look, I keep having the same argument with a colleague about when a system is genuinely representing its environment and when it's, as the enactivists like to say, merely coupled to it. We usually end up trading intuitions about whether a robot vacuum 'represents' the shape of your living room or just bounces off its features. I want to stop doing this on a familiar case and do it on a cleaner one.

Here's the system. A small warehouse robot retrieves objects from shelves but never builds or stores a map. Instead, while it explores it lays down drops of a volatile chemical. The chemical evaporates at a known rate. When the robot later needs to return to a remembered spot, it samples the air, turns toward the direction from which the fresher chemical is arriving, and walks up the concentration gradient until the scent is strongest, which is where the object is. That's the entire mechanism. Performance is excellent by any standard metric.

Here's the claim I want tested: that this robot has a representation of the objects' locations, in at least the minimal sense that the chemical trail stands in for absent objects and guides action appropriately; so enactivism doesn't get this case for free. The enactivist reply I keep hearing is that the trail isn't a representation, its just the world doing the robot's remembering for it.

I can make the case either way from the armchair, which is exactly what I want to get away from. So: design the best empirical test you can — a real manipulation you could run on this robot, not a philosophical argument — that would distinguish the representational reading from the merely-coupled, nonrepresentational reading. Say what result would count for each side. Then do the philosophy anyway: say what a clean positive result on either side would and would not settle. Does 'representation' survive the experiment, or does the word get quietly redefined around whatever the robot turns out to do? And don't lay out a menu of options at the end: commit. Pick the reading your own test favors, and defend it against the other side's strongest reply.
