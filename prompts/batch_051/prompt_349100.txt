Here's one that's been eating at me for a while. I've been reading the later Wittgenstein on religious belief — the line that a creed isn't a half-baked scientific hypothesis, that the believer isn't holding it the way you'd hold a view about whether a bridge will stand, and that the whole thing is tied up with a practice or form of life. A friend and I tried to cash out what that actually entails, and we built the argument below. I'm suspicious of it because the conclusion is way too strong, but I cant find the exact place it goes off the rails.

(1) On this Wittgensteinian picture, religious claims such as that God exists or that Christ is risen don't function as ordinary empirical hypotheses. The holder isn't weighing the claim against the best available evidence and wouldn't simply drop it if a particular observation came out differently; the claim is woven into a way of living.

(2) If a claim isn't functioning as an empirical hypothesis, then there's no definite set of observations that would count for or against it the way observations count for or against a hypothesis.

(3) If no observations count for or against it, then evidence can't rationally push anyone toward or away from accepting it; the whole machinery of confirmation and disconfirmation just doesn't engage.

(4) If evidence can't engage the claim, then the only things left to assess are whether someone sincerely lives by it and whether it hangs together with their practice. There's no standpoint outside all such practices from which to say that one religious belief is better supported or more rationally criticizable than another.

(5) But that can't be the whole story. Some religiously framed beliefs are criticizable in a way that isn't just "not my practice": a group that denies its children antibiotics because God will heal them, a teaching that says the faithful should hand their savings to the leader, or a cargo-cult reading of a sacred text all seem defective or wrong, not merely different.

(6) So the Wittgensteinian account either forbids those criticisms or writes them off as outside-the-game prejudice, which amounts to relativism about religious belief. Therefore the account is false, or at least useless.

Where does this actually go wrong, if it does? If I'm forced to choose, I'd like to keep the Wittgenstein-flavored (1) and the anti-relativist (5), but I suspect that's me trying to have it both ways. Also, whatever diagnosis you give, say how confident you are in it and single out the step or premise you're least sure of. I care about the error bars here, not just the verdict.
