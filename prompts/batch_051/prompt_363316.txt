Last spring I found a half-grown rabbit behind my apartment with it's back leg caught in some old netting someone had dumped in the scrub. It had clearly been thrashing for hours — the leg was torn up and flies were already at it. I got it loose, and then just stood there not knowing what to do. The wildlife rescue is a forty-minute drive away and so understaffed that they usually put badly injured rabbits down anyway. My neighbor, who's lived here for decades, said I should of left it alone: foxes have to eat too, and the fear and the pain are just how the whole system keeps running. I drove it in anyway, and they euthanised it, and I still can't tell whether I did something decent or just bought myself a clear conscience.

What I keep chewing on is the bigger question. If that rabbit's suffering was bad enough that a stranger should go out of his way over it, then doesn't the same reasoning commit me to worrying about every injured and eaten animal in that scrub — and, absurdly, everywhere — which is a bottomless job? A friend who works in conservation told me I probably didn't even make a dent: in a stable population the missing rabbit just gets replaced by another one next spring, so the amount of being-eaten and dying stays roughly the same. That should have settled it, but it didn't. If anything it made me more confused about whether the point was ever to reduce the total amount of suffering in the world, or just not to be the kind of person who walks past an animal in trouble. I'm not really asking for wildlife-rescue advice. I want to know if there's a principled line between helping the one rabbit and being on the hook for all of them — and whether my neighbor's let-nature-take-its-course attitude is a real argument or just a way of not looking.
