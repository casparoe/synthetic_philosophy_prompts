I keep getting in a tangle whenever someone brings Gödel into a debate about AI or the limits of logic, so let me ask this properly. People tend to gesture at two headline claims and then blur them together:

(1) Any consistent formal system rich enough to do elementary arithmetic will contain a sentence it can neither prove nor refute, even though the sentence is true when read in the standard way.

(2) No such system can prove its own consistency.

These clearly aren't the same claim, but I want to understand the gap between them, and in particular why (2) doesn't license the grand conclusions people build on it. Specifically, I keep hearing people move from "Peano arithmetic (PA) can't prove Con(PA)" to "so no machine could ever match a human mathematician", or "so PA's consistency is unknowable", or "so we can never justifiably trust PA". That second step feels way too fast, and I want you to show me exactly where it breaks. Could you walk me through: what a consistency statement actually is (a sentence about natural numbers and finite sequences of symbols, not a system's introspective self-assessment), what assumptions the second theorem needs (effectivity or recursive axiomatisability, being able to encode its own proof relation, and the consistency statement being written in the system's own language), and why "T doesn't prove Con(T)" doesn't mean "Con(T) can't be established from anywhere". It would also help if you said what it would take to prove PA consistent from outside — a stronger theory, presumably — and why that doesn't just push the problem back one step rather than making the original system off-limits. Get into the weeds; I want the actual distinction, not the usual simplified version.
