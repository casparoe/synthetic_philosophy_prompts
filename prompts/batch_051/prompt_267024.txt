I have been trying to work out whether I think human values are ultimately one thing or many, and I find that my gut responses to concrete cases will not line up consistently. A colleague of mine is building a decision tool that assigns every outcome a single scalar score, and she has been asking me to test whether I really object to it. When the choice is small and private—say, whether to spend the next hour reading a novel I like or going for a run—I do not object. These feel like different quantities of the same underlying good, and I would happily accept a trade-off: I would forgo a month of small reading pleasures for a large enough gain in fitness, and I believe there is a rate that would make the exchange exactly sensible. But when she frames a city council choice in her scalar vocabulary—an extra ICU bed versus a long-running community mural program, both described as wellbeing outcomes—I feel the framing is not just incomplete but wrong. And yet I also think the ICU bed is the better choice, not merely my preference, so I cannot cleanly say the two goods are incomparable.

I have the same unevenness elsewhere. I think a person who lets a close friendship wither because he gets slightly more ordinary pleasure overall from a widening circle of casual acquaintances has made a mistake, even if the pleasure number on the scalar account outweighs what the friendship contributed; the friendship does not seem to sit on the same scale at all. But I feel no resistance to the scalar framing when someone trades one friendship for another that is plainly more sustaining—there it seems exactly right to say: same kind of good, different amounts. And in some cases I refuse the arithmetic altogether: I do not want to calculate, or have a device calculate, how many extra minutes of a stranger's mild convenience would justify hurting my daughter's feelings, even though I would have no problem letting the same device govern a trade between my own convenience and a stranger's.

Can you extract the principle that best captures this pattern, or the small set of candidate principles I appear to be torn between? State it precisely enough that it says what I am treating as commensurable when, and what makes a choice correct when the goods are not commensurable. Tell me what it predicts about a case I have deliberately left unsettled: a pill that would slightly brighten my everyday mood for a year but slightly dull my capacity to be moved by music and by other people's lives—should I treat that as a simple trade I can compute, as a forbidden trade, or as a choice with a right answer that is not computed from any common scale? And be direct about which of my verdicts above the principle cannot accommodate, and which verdict you would have me revise to make the whole view coherent.
