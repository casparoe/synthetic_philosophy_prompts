Consider the following pair of judgments, which many people in dual-earner societies hold at once. A parent who places an infant in full-time paid childcare in order to keep a paid job is seen as a normally responsible working parent. A parent who places the same infant in the same childcare for the same hours in order to train seriously for a sport, write a novel, or pursue a demanding artistic project is seen as self-indulgent or neglectful—even when the family does not need the salary, and even when the unpaid activity is as demanding and arguably as valuable as the job. The two cases differ only in the reason for the parent's absence, which should seem curious. If the concern is that long hours of non-parental care harm the child, the harm cannot depend on what the parent happens to be doing elsewhere. If the concern is that parents have no right to pursue a separate life at the child's expense, paid work has no obvious monopoly on a life of one's own. Formulate the strongest principle that might nevertheless justify the asymmetry—candidate distinctions might appeal to necessity, to acting partly for the child's sake, to modelling reciprocity, or to contributing to the formal economy—and test each against the harder cases: a parent who uses the same hours to care for an aging parent, a parent who works from home while the child is in care, a household for which the salary is purely elective, and a parent whose unpaid activity produces clear social value. Then give an honest verdict: should the asymmetry be kept, narrowed, reversed, or abandoned, and what is the exact principle that draws the line?
