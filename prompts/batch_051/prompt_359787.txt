I help run a medium-sized discussion site built around a long-standing hobby community. Members earn points when other members upvote their posts, and the points are displayed next to every username and unlock small privileges. I have no training in economics or the social sciences, but over the past two years I have been keeping a private notes file on the ways the points system seems to bend behavior, and I have now sorted everything in that file into six headings. Reading other forum-administrator discussions and design postmortems, I usually find the same six headings wearing different phrasing.

Here they are. First, the number stops being a byproduct and becomes the target: people write what they expect will harvest upvotes rather than what they actually believe is useful or true. Second, the ledger itself is gamed: sock-puppet accounts, reciprocal-upvote rings, accounts that repost old popular material, and accounts cultivated and then sold. Third, a small number of early votes decides the outcome, because later readers vote partly on the existing score rather than on the post, and coordinated brigades can bury someone for reasons unrelated to the content. Fourth, people who expect downvotes often stay quiet, so reasonable but unpopular positions disappear before anyone has to argue against them. Fifth, accumulated points turn into permanent status: long-time high scorers get the benefit of the doubt, are treated as authorities in areas unrelated to how they earned the points, and keep compounding their visibility while newcomers cannot catch up. Sixth, the system selects for a certain kind of content, since quick, easily judged, instantly rewarding posts climb while slow, careful, or initially confusing contributions sink before they can gather points.

These six feel genuinely distinct to me, in the sense that a fix aimed at one would not touch the others. But I cannot shake the feeling that the list is not finished. I am looking for a seventh heading: a failure mode of karma-style reputation systems that is not on my list and is not a re-description, special case, or combination of the six above. State the seventh heading in a sentence or two and say what mechanism drives it. Then be honest with me: is this a real and robust failure mode, or does it sound separate but collapse into one of the six when examined closely? If it is real, does it suggest a design change that the first six do not?
