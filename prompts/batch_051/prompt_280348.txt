I co-edit the proceedings of a small venue that sits between natural language processing and science-and-technology studies, and our style guide contains this rule:

"Authors must not ascribe mental states—beliefs, knowledge, intentions, wants, or preferences—to computational systems."

The rule was written about a decade ago after readers complained that papers kept saying things like "the model noticed that …" as if a language model had a mental life, and until now it has mostly been enforced against authors who anthropomorphise without noticing.

We now have a submission whose author is explicit about using Dennett's intentional stance as a methodology. In the methods section they write: "We adopt the intentional stance toward the model: we treat it as if it were a rational agent whose beliefs and preferences are fixed by the prompt and training objective, because this yields compact, predictively useful descriptions. We do not claim the model literally has beliefs or preferences. When we write that the model 'prefers' short answers, this reports our modeling stance, not a property of the model's internals." The results section then freely says the model believes, prefers, hedges, and so on.

I'm arguing with the other editors about whether this breaches the rule. The clause's text seems to catch it—the paper does ascribe beliefs, even if it flags them as stance-relative—but the purpose of the rule was to stop readers being misled about what machines are doing, and this author is unusually careful not to mislead. The drafters clearly did not foresee someone explicitly adopting Dennett as a tool. Walk me through what the text of the rule, the purpose behind it, what the drafters would have said if asked, and what a reasonable reader takes the rule to mean each suggest, and then tell me which approach should govern our decision and what we should actually do (rewording, a carve-out, a warning, rejection?). Keep it under 850 words and start with your bottom line.
