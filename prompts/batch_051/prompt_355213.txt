In Jorge Luis Borges's essay A New Refutation of Time, he argues that if we accept both Berkeley's idealism about physical objects and Hume's skepticism about the self, we are forced, whether we like it or not, to deny that time is real. He does not present the argument as a formal proof, but I think the central line of thought can be reconstructed as a step-by-step argument, and I want to know whether the reconstruction is sound and whether it is faithful to what Borges actually says.

Here is the reconstruction:

1. If Berkeley is right, a material object is nothing more than a bundle of perceived qualities, and there is no underlying material substance that endures through time independently of being perceived.
2. If Hume is right, the self is likewise nothing more than a bundle of perceptions, with no persistent mental substance that stands outside those perceptions and observes their succession.
3. Therefore, if both premises are accepted, the entire world consists of perceptions and nothing else: no enduring material things and no enduring perceiving subject.
4. But a perception, considered entirely on its own, does not come labeled with a date; its temporal position is not one of its perceived qualities.
5. Moreover, two perceptions can have identical content: the same birdsong, the same pang of fear, the same patch of color can recur.
6. If two perceptions have identical content, and if neither a material substance nor a mental substance supplies an additional fact about their temporal order, then there is no fact of the matter about whether they are two successive perceptions or one perception repeated.
7. If there is no fact of the matter about whether identical perceptions are successive, then succession in general is not a feature of the world as it is in itself.
8. Therefore time, which is nothing but succession, is an illusion: an organizing habit we bring to the bundle of perceptions, not something that exists in the world or in the self.

Borges seems to treat this conclusion as following rigorously from Berkeley and Hume, even though he also recoils from it in places. I am inclined to think the weak point is step 6, because indistinguishability in content looks like an epistemological fact about what we can tell apart, not a metaphysical fact about what there is. But I am not confident of that diagnosis, and I also worry that my reconstruction may be importing premises Borges would not accept.

Is this reconstruction a fair account of the argument in A New Refutation of Time? Is the argument valid as stated? If it is not valid, say exactly which step fails and what the strongest available reading of that step would have to be. If it is valid, say whether the premises are true and which one carries the most weight. As you answer, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among careful readers of Borges, and do not present a contested interpretation as the consensus.
