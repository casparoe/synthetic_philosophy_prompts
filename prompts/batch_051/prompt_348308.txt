Idk if this has a name. I keep noticing a very specific kind of objection to markets, one that doesn't line up with the objections economists are usually willing to talk about. Take kidneys. Both sides fully informed, no coercion, no third party hurt — in fact everyone else benefits — and the objection doesn't weaken; it gets sharper, and it seems aimed at the fact that a price is attached to the organ at all. The same people who are uncomfortable with a market price for kidneys are often fine with reimbursing a live donor's exact travel costs and lost wages. So it isn't the transfer, and it isn't money changing hands; it's the price doing what prices do — flexing to clear supply and demand. I see the same shape in price gouging after a hurricane and, more murkily, in vote selling, where you could tell a story about harms to third parties so maybe that one doesn't fit cleanly. The wrong feels located in the act of pricing something, before and independent of whatever happens in any particular transaction. Does this family of objections have an established name, and how exactly does it relate to 'commodification'? If there's no precise name for this narrower thing, what would you call it, and what's the test for which goods trigger it and which don't? I mostly want the boundaries, not just a label.
