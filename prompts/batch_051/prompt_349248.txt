I'm a student who got pulled into a rabbit hole about AI safety via debate — the idea that two systems argue opposite sides of a question in front of a human judge, and even if the judge can't evaluate the whole issue at once, they can check small local steps, so the side defending the truth wins because the other side eventually gets caught in a contradiction or a false claim. I've been going back and forth on weather this is a real argument or just a story we tell about the setup. I tried to write it out as steps, and now I can't tell whether I've proven too much or nothing at all:

(1) Suppose there's a determinate true answer A, and one debater is assigned to defend A while the other defends not-A. They have equal time, equal evidence, and can attack each other's points.

(2) For any objection someone raises against a line of argument for the true answer, there is always another true statement that can be added to answer it without giving up A. For any line of argument for the false answer, if you push it far enough it must either make a claim contradicted by the evidence or make a claim that conflicts with something it said earlier.

(3) The human judge can't evaluate the whole question at once, but the judge can reliably check whether a single claim is contradicted by the evidence or by something that same debater said earlier.

(4) So, in a debate that runs long enough, the defender of not-A will eventually make a checkable local error and get rejected by the judge, while the defender of A can keep patching their case with true local moves. Therefore the judge ends up on the true side.

I keep suspecting that (2) is doing too much work — maybe it's true only in some vacuous sense about what true statements exist, not about what the debater can actually find or what the judge can actually follow. But I also wonder whether (3) is quietly giving the judge the very ability the debate was supposed to replace. Is the argument valid? Does it go through, and if not, which exact step fails? If you think (2) is false as written, give the strongest reading on which it comes out true and say whether the argument still reaches (4) on that reading.
