In a seminar on liberal political theory, a classmate and I repeatedly disagreed about whether Rawls's treatment of religious argument is internally consistent, and I would like you to help settle the issue. In Political Liberalism (1993), especially in the lecture on public reason, Rawls appears to hold that when citizens and officials argue about constitutional essentials and matters of basic justice, they should not rest their case on the particular religious or philosophical comprehensive doctrines they happen to accept. Instead, they should appeal to a shared political conception of justice and to public reasons that other reasonable citizens could in principle endorse. He treats this as part of a duty of civility: in the public political forum, an argument whose decisive premises are drawn from scripture, church teaching, or a comprehensive secular ideology would not count as a properly public justification.

Yet in his 1997 restatement of the public reason doctrine, Rawls seems to relax this requirement considerably. He allows that reasonable comprehensive doctrines, including religious ones, may be introduced into public political discussion at any time, subject to a proviso: the speaker or group must also present, in due course, suitable political reasons for supporting the same conclusion. On the face of it, that is a far more permissive rule, because religious arguments are admitted into the forum itself and are disqualified only if they never get an accompanying political justification.

Are these two positions really in tension, or is the later view just a clarification of what the earlier view had implied all along? Lay out the strongest readings on which they are consistent, the strongest reading on which Rawls changed his mind, and the strongest reading on which the proviso is an unstable compromise that still stacks the deck against religious citizens by requiring a secular translation of their views. Then give a verdict: which reconciliation fits Rawls's texts best, and which involves the least special pleading? Please answer in American English.
