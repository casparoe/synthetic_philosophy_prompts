I am preparing to interview a cognitive psychologist who defends the explicit-monitoring account of choking under pressure: the view that an expert's performance collapses under pressure because attention shifts to the step-by-step mechanics of a skill that has become automatic, rather than because of distracting worries about the stakes. Draft eight to ten interview questions that go beyond restating the position. Include at least one question pressing on the rivalry with distraction accounts, asking how she would handle evidence that pressure sometimes impairs performance by consuming working-memory resources in ways that look more like distraction than self-focus. Where her answer is predictable, write the follow-up you would ask next — for example, if she cites the familiar golf-putting finding that skill-focused attention disrupts expert performance while often improving novice performance, ask what that expertise reversal actually distinguishes: automaticity, the grain at which the action is attended to, or something else. Also include a question about the mechanism by which explicit monitoring is supposed to degrade execution — whether it decomposes an integrated motor program or forces a reversion to slower, piecemeal control. Include a question about what evidence would count against the account rather than merely supporting a rival. End by asking her to specify boundary conditions: for which skills, levels of expertise, and task demands she would expect metacognitive attention to improve performance rather than harm it, and what, if anything, follows from her answer for the broader claim that thinking about your own performance is generally helpful.
