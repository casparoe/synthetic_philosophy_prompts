Analyze the reshaping of the autism diagnosis over the past several decades — from a rare disorder of early childhood to the broad autism spectrum disorder of DSM-5, and the simultaneous rise of the neurodiversity movement, which treats autism as an identity rather than a disease — through the lens of Ian Hacking's looping effects. Take the concept as given rather than explaining it at length: classifications of people, unlike classifications of indifferent kinds, interact with the people classified, who revise their self-understanding, behavior, identities, and institutions in light of the label; this response changes the kind itself; and the process can iterate. I want the analysis to run in both directions. Spell out as precisely as you can where the loops are in this episode: how the expanding label reshaped self-understanding among those placed under it, patterns of stigma, access to services, and the formation of advocacy communities, and how those responses put pressure back on the diagnostic category — for example, in the folding of Asperger's syndrome and PDD-NOS into a single spectrum category and in the continuing dispute over whether the word disorder belongs there at all. Then identify where the model strains. Many self-advocates insist that autism is a neurologically real, lifelong difference rather than a constructed or transient condition. Does the looping-effect framework predict that move, accommodate it, or treat it as one more turn of the loop? Does the substantial evidence of genetic and neurological correlates make autism an indifferent kind, a counterexample to Hacking's distinction, or simply a different species of interactive kind?
