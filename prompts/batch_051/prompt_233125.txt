I have been thinking, on and off for a few years, about whether cultural things — jokes, songs, ways of cooking, greetings, beliefs about medicine — actually evolve the way living things do, or whether calling them "memes" is just a comforting metaphor. I think I have found a dividing line, and I would like an honest assessment of it.

My proposal is that a cultural item is a real unit of copying only if three conditions all hold. First, a new copy has to be made because an old copy was encountered; it cannot be independently re-derived from what the item is for. If two people separately hit on the same way of keeping a fire dry because it is the obvious solution to the same problem, that convergence is not copying. Second, the person making the new version has to be trying to match the old version as such, the way one tries to reproduce a tune note for note. If they are only trying to produce something that works — something that tastes good, keeps the rain off, or calms a restless child — then the older item is being used as evidence about what works, not copied. Third, something must keep the item's form stable across a chain of versions: a canonical score, a punchline that has to land, a ritual text, a rhyme or meter, a written recipe. Without that, small errors accumulate and the item drifts into a shapeless cloud of variations, and you never get the stable, distinct versions that selection could choose among.

On this test, a small class of cultural things passes and most fails. Jokes, songs, poems, slogans, standard rituals, and fixed catchphrases are copied in the strong sense. But most of what gets called cultural evolution — cooking techniques, farming methods, dialects, table manners, beliefs — fails at least one condition. A cooking technique is rebuilt from the goal of making food taste good, not from an image of the previous cook's movements; that is why it drifts. So my conclusion is that "meme" is a metaphor for most of culture, but for a narrow class of items whose form is self-stabilizing it is literal.

I have been calling this distinction the "copy test," to separate copying a thing from merely learning a lesson from it.

I am fairly convinced the distinction is real, but it feels too natural not to have been made before, and I would like to know whether someone has already worked it out under another name. I would also like to know where it breaks: what does my test wrongly count as a unit of copying, and what fails my test that nevertheless plainly evolves by something like random variation plus keeping what works? Is the third condition doing real work, or have I just smuggled in the conclusion by defining units of copying as things that hold their shape?
