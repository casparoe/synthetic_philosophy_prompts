A move i keep seeing in arguments about cutting off demanding parents is that the adult parent-child relationship is basically friendship plus some shared history, sometimes called the friendship model of filial duty. The analogy is doing real work: if you stop calling your mom enough and she guilt-trips you, that's supposed to be like an old friend guilt-tripping you for drifting apart, and since nobody thinks you owe a friend weekly calls just because you were close at 16, you dont owe your parents that either. I want you to stress-test how far this actually holds.

Start with the correspondence table: what plays the role of the shared history in a friendship, the mutual care, the voluntary entry and exit, the reciprocity expectations, the slow falling-out. Then go through where the mapping breaks. Flag separately the disanalogies that matter for the guilt-trip argument—the asymmetry of early dependency, the fact that the child never consented to the relationship or to the benefits, the way a parent's life-plan got shaped around the child, the fact that the relationship isnt really exit-able the way a friendship is—from the disanalogies that dont matter for that argument (blood ties by themselves, the fact that friends are usually peers, etc.). For each disanalogy that matters, say which premise of the argument it undercuts.

While youre at it, test the corollary people draw from the analogy: that what you owe tracks the quality of the current relationship, not the size of the past sacrifices, so a parent who sacrificed enormously but is now awful has no special claim. Does that corollary survive on its own?

Then give a verdict: does the conclusion—that obligations to adult parents are at most friendship-grade, so parents cant demand more than a friend could—survive the disanalogies, or was the analogy carrying the whole argument? If it partly survives, say what residual extra obligation (if any) the friendship model leaves standing, and whether you'd replace the analogy with a better one.
