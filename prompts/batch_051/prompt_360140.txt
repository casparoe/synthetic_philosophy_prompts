A city that has had conventional rent control is considering replacing it with the following scheme. No rent is capped outright. Each rental unit instead has a base rent, set freely when a new tenancy begins; for a continuing tenancy, the base rent remains whatever was in effect when that tenancy started. On each lease renewal the landlord may raise the rent by any amount. The permitted untaxed increase is the compounded value of the consumer price index plus two percentage points per year since the tenancy began. Any portion of a renewal increase above that permitted amount is taxed at ninety percent, and the proceeds are paid out as equal quarterly rent credits to every renter household in the city. Landlords may bank unused allowance: if they raise rent by less than the permitted amount in any year, the difference accrues and may be used to cover a later increase. Units less than ten years old are exempt while they remain with their original developer, and any unit is effectively exempt in the year a new tenancy begins, since the new tenancy defines a new base rent.

I want to know how this mechanism would actually behave, not whether it sounds fair in the abstract. Walk through the incentives for landlords, sitting tenants, prospective tenants, and developers. Where does it get gamed first — through turnover and tenant replacement, cosmetic renovations that pretend to create new units, condo conversion, reclassifying rent as fees, banking games, or something else? Does protecting renewals while leaving move-in rents free preserve the informational advantages of market rent at turnover, or does it shift the cost of the regulation onto tenants at exactly the moment they have least leverage? What does the equal per-household credit do on its own: is it capitalized into move-in rents, and does it make replacing a rent-controlled tenant a fiscal gain for the landlord? Does the banking provision reintroduce the large backloaded increases the tax was meant to prevent? Which failures of ordinary rent control does this design actually avoid, which does it retain or worsen, and can the remaining failures be fixed without turning the scheme back into an ordinary cap? As you go, separate what is well established about rent regulation from what is genuinely contested among people who study it, and from what is only a guess about this specific design; do not present a contested view as the consensus.
