I've been following the decisions of a state equal-treatment board that has to interpret an open-ended clause—employers can't discriminate on the basis of any "protected status"—and I want you to predict how it'll rule on a case that's about to come up. To do that you'll have to reverse-engineer what principle it's actually running on.

So far the board has found these to be protected statuses: race, sex, religion, disability, national origin, and sexual orientation. It has refused to recognize marital status, criminal record, and political affiliation as protected. Two decisions stand out. In one, it struck down a blanket no-headgear-or-headcoverings rule as religion discrimination against a Muslim employee, even though the rule applied to everyone; the board said a sincerely neutral rule can still discriminate when it forces someone to choose between their job and a practice tied to a protected identity. In another, it said a policy banning all visible tattoos was fine: self-decoration isn't a status, and the complainant couldn't tie the tattoo to any recognized ground.

The new case: an employer has a grooming policy that bans dreadlocks, locs, and afros longer than two inches, again for everyone. A Black employee says this is race discrimination because it essentially penalizes natural Black hair textures and styles associated with Black identity. The employer says no—hair is changeable and chosen, just like a tattoo, and the policy doesn't mention race.

I want you to:
- lay out the two or three principles that fit the board's record so far,
- say which principle gives the best account of the whole record,
- predict the board's likely verdict, sticking to it's own precedents rather than your own politics or what you'd want the law to be,
- name the closest past case and one further case (made up is fine) that would cleanly separate the leading principles,
- and tell me what would change your mind about the prediction, or where you're least confident.

Don't bother with actual case law or citations; I care about the internal logic of the board's record.
