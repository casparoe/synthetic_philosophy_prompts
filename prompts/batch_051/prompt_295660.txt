I'm a retired physicist auditing a philosophy of history course, and I offered to help with grading. The take-home question was:

"Explain hindsight bias and the narrative fallacy, and how they jointly distort historical explanation. Use one concrete historical episode. Then say whether the remedy is mainly just historians reconstructing what the actors knew at the time."

One student wrote this:

Both notions are about how knowing the outcome leaks back into the story you tell. Hindsight bias, also called the knew-it-all-along effect, is the tendency to judge a decision as obviously wrong after the fact because we know it produced a bad outcome; the classic experiments show that people recall being more certain in their earlier predictions than they actually were. The narrative fallacy is our habit of trimming events into a tidy causal story, with a beginning, middle and end, and dropping whatever doesn't fit. In historical explanation the two reinforce each other: once the historian knows how things turned out, the result starts to look inevitable, and the narrative fallacy supplies the neat causal chain that makes it look even more inevitable. A good example is the 2008 financial crisis, where after-the-fact accounts string subprime lending, the rating agencies, and derivative trading into a single chain, so the crash reads as something regulators and bankers should plainly have seen. So yes, the remedy is mainly for historians to reconstruct what the actors actually knew at the time and to stick to that. If a historian strictly separates ex ante information from ex post information, hindsight has nothing to feed on and the narrative fallacy loses its material, so the distortion disappears. That may make for less enjoyable reading, but historical explanation is causal reconstruction, not story-telling.
