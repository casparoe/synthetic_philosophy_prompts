I work on ML platform stuff and have no formal philosophy background, but I keep getting dragged into arguments about what "alignment" should even mean. I've been chewing on an argument that the narrow definition — the system does what the person using it actually intended, nothing more — is the only one that makes engineering sense. Here it is:

(1) A target you're going to optimize against has to be checkable at runtime. If you can't tell whether the system hit the target, you can't train against it, write evals for it, or debug failures.

(2) The only checkable form of "human values" is a person looking at the concrete situation and saying what should happen. Once they've said what should happen, that's just a statement of intent; the "value" part doesn't add anything you can test.

(3) So a system aligned with human values would be observationally indistinguishable from a system aligned with the intentions of whichever person is doing the saying in each case.

(4) If two targets are observationally indistinguishable in every possible situation, then as far as building the system goes, they're the same target.

(5) Therefore value alignment collapses into intent alignment, and the only defensible version of the alignment problem is the narrow one: get the system to do what the operator intended.

I have a feeling something goes wrong in the back half of the argument, but I can't make the objection precice, and honestly (4) feels airtight to me. Is the argument valid? If not, tell me exactly which step fails, give the strongest version of that step on which it would be true, and say whether the conclusion still follows.

When you answer, lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — your verdict and, if it's invalid, which step fails — then the reasoning. No preamble and don't restate my question back to me.
