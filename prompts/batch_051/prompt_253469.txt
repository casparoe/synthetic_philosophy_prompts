Over the past few years I have kept an informal record of how a friend of mine—call her Mara, who teaches a course on practical rationality—answers questions about lottery-like choices. Her stated verdicts include these: she paid $1 for a 1-in-100 chance of winning $200; she paid $10 for a 1-in-a-million chance of winning $50 million; she refused to pay $30 for a 1-in-1,000 chance of winning $20,000; she refused to pay $10 for a 1-in-a-million chance of winning $1 million; and she paid 50 cents for a one-in-ten-billion chance of winning $60 billion, saying the upside was large enough to justify the small price. I find the last one especially interesting because it sits right at the edge between "tiny probability, enormous payoff" reasoning and ordinary prudence.

Next week her seminar will put a new case to her: should she pay $2 for a one-in-a-quadrillion chance of winning $50 quadrillion? I care less about the bare verdict than about the rule behind it. The obvious candidates are plain expected-value maximization (treating the money stakes as directly measuring value); a bounded-value rule on which money beyond roughly $10 billion adds nothing further; and a probability-threshold rule on which chances of about one in a trillion or less are treated as effectively zero. Which rules, or what nearby variants, are consistent with her full record? What does each predict about the new case? Give your best guess about how she will respond and why, and identify a further case that would most cleanly distinguish the candidates the new case leaves unresolved. Keep your answer to at most 400 words; a compact table for the rules' predictions is fine.
