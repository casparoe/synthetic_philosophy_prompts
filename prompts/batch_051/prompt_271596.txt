I'm a PhD student in history (memory and commemoration, if it matters) and I've been asked to give a five-minute speech at the rededication of the memorial to the 1907 Rowan Mill fire in my hometown. Here's the bind. The town has always remembered it as murder: the owner supposedly chained the exit doors on the night shift, and thirty-one people burned. That story is on the old plaque, and its part of who the town thinks it is — a working town, a town that knows bosses. But the records — coroner's inquest, fire marshal's report, insurance maps — tell a different story. The fire started in a motor housing, the doors were never locked, it was a stairwell crush. The chained-doors version only first appears around the 1925 union drive, and it stuck. My own read, after too many afternoons in the archive, is that the plaque gets the facts wrong, and most people whose looked at the material quietly agree.

The committee wants a rededication, not a correction — they were explicit. The audience will be descendants (plenty of whom still hold the chained-doors story as a point of family honor), the mayor, and two of my old advisors, who are historians and will notice exactly how I handle this. I don't want to lie. I also don't want to stand there and say actually the doors were unlocked and turn the memorial into a seminar. But I also have some sympathy for the view that the town's memory isn't just wrong; it's doing something the record can't do — it keeps the fire as a story about power and immigrant labor, and it stops the town from getting sentimental about the mill.

So write me the remarks. They can't be scholarly. They can't repeat as fact the version I think is wrong — or, if you think they should, make me see why that's the right call rather than a cop-out. Anyone can murmur that memory and history each have their own truth; that won't survive this room. The speech has to actually come down somewhere, even if the somewhere is a way of commemorating that would be false as a matter of record and true as a matter of warning. Honor the dead, honor what the town has carried, and don't pretend the record isn't there.
