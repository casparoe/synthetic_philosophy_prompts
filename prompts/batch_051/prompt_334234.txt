I keep noticing a conversational move that I don't have a precise name for, and I would like to know whether it has one, and how it sits within philosophical work on what a speaker communicates beyond the literal meaning of their words. Here are two plain cases.

In the first, a seller of a used machine is asked whether it is in working order. She answers, "Every part in it is original," which is true; the buyer takes this to mean that the machine works. She knows it does not. When the buyer later complains, she says, "I never said it worked—I said the parts were original."

In the second, an official is asked whether a plan will cut services. He answers, "Every household will retain access to a local office," which is true; the audience reasonably takes him to mean that services will not be cut. Cuts are planned.

In the clearest cases, the move has three features: the sentence used is literally true; it is selected because the hearer will draw a further conclusion from it; and the speaker later defends himself entirely by retreating to the literal sentence, treating the further conclusion as something the hearer merely "read into" it. This is not lying in the usual sense, since the stated sentence is true. It also seems different from ordinary hinting, where the hint can be known to both parties as a hint. Here the speaker relies on the hearer's not seeing that the remark was selected as a device for producing the conclusion.

I understand there is a standard philosophical view, attributed to Grice, on which what a speaker means is not exhausted by what the sentence says, but is fixed in part by the speaker's intentions—including an intention that the hearer recognize what the speaker is doing. But in this move the speaker seems to intend the hearer to form a conclusion while also intending the hearer not to recognize that the remark was chosen for exactly that purpose; if the hearer recognized the intention, the device would fail. So I cannot tell whether the analysis treats the further conclusion as something the speaker meant, or rather as a belief the speaker merely caused without telling the hearer anything.

My questions are: (1) Does this move have a standard name of its own, distinct from the broad label "misleading" and from lying by omission? If the best-established name comes from behavioral ethics or psychology rather than philosophy of language, I would like to know that. (2) On the Gricean analysis as I have described it, does the speaker mean the further conclusion at all, and is that a stable way to draw the line between telling someone something and merely getting them to think it? (3) Where are the edges—for example, the speaker believes the further conclusion but still wants deniability; the hearer draws an unintended conclusion; the hearer sees exactly what is being done and plays along anyway; or the further conclusion is all but certain even though the speaker tries to keep it offstage? A bare name will not satisfy me; I want the carving—what this phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its boundaries sit.

If I have run two or three different phenomena together, or if the Gricean view I am half-remembering is contested enough that reporting it as "the" standard account would mislead, please say so plainly rather than trying to make the pieces fit. Where a term is used loosely or differently across fields, flag that and tell me how confident you are in the labels and distinctions rather than making them sound more settled than they are.

Please write the response in American English.
