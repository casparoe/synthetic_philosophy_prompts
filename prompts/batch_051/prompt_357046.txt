John Stuart Mill, in the opening chapter of *Utilitarianism*, writes:

"Were it not so, there would be no science more precarious, or whose conclusions were more insufficiently made out, than algebra; which derives none of its certainty from what are commonly taught to learners as its elements, since these, as laid down by some of its most eminent teachers, are as full of fictions as English law, and of mysteries as theology. The truths which are ultimately accepted as the first principles of a science, are really the last results of metaphysical analysis, practised on the elementary notions with which the science is conversant; and their relation to the science is not that of foundations to an edifice, but of roots to a tree, which may perform their office equally well though they be never dug down to and exposed to light."

I keep coming back to this passage while thinking about a question in philosophy of mathematics: what counts as a legitimate mathematical object? Mill's point about algebra seems to describe the actual historical situation pretty well—people happily used imaginary numbers, infinitesimals, divergent series, and the curves of coordinate geometry long before anyone could say what those things were, and the definitions often came after the practice. So I want to turn his roots-and-tree image into a concrete set of criteria.

Please write, as the main body, a list of desiderata that a theory of legitimate mathematical objects must meet. The theory should count as adequate for the purpose of explaining and adjudicating the following sorts of cases: compass-and-straightedge constructions versus curves that need a whole motion or a transcendental equation to be produced; infinitesimals before and after Robinson; complex numbers before and after they were given a geometric or algebraic home; the move from "constructible" to "existent because consistent"; and objects only known through indirect reasoning, like the roots of equations that cannot be expressed by radicals. For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark whether it is non-negotiable or just a preference of a particular camp (constructivist, Platonist, formalist, etc.). Then say which combinations of desiderata are jointly unsatisfiable or at least in serious tension—that is the part I most want to see. Don't give me a scorecard of existing theories; I'll do that myself.
