One of the beats I keep coming back to as a journalist who covers science and ideas is the applied-rationality crowd: the people running workshops where you drill a premortem or a goal-factoring technique the way a dojo drills a stance. The pitch is that rationality is a trainable skill with something like katas—repeatable cognitive moves that eventually transfer to messy, high-stakes decisions outside the training room. The skeptical take is that the transfer is mostly illusory: you can get very good at produceing clean probability calibration on workshop-style questions and then still be wildly overconfident about whether to leave a job, trust a collaborator, or fund a project. I want to write a piece that forces the question of whether this is a real research program or a subculture with good jargon, and I can't quite tell which open questions would most settle that.

So here is what I'd like from you. Suppose a small foundation is willing to fund three or four researchers for three years, call it $2 million total, for conceptual and empirical work on whether and how rationality can be trained like a martial art. Please rank the open questions you would put on their plate—about practice, katas, transfer, measurement, mechanisms, whatever you think is actually live—and defend the ordering. For each, tell me why it lands there: is it upstream of the other questions, tractable with current methods, likely to change what practitioners do day to day, or likely to change whether outsiders should take the whole scene seriously? Also say what kind of result in the next couple of years would move it up or down your list. I care more about the reasoning than the menu, since I'll use it to decide which researchers and labs to quote, not to produce a survey.
