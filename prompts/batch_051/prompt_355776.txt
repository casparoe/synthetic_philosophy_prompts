Back when I did biology I treated the species question as settled: a species is a group of actually or potentially interbreeding populations that's reproductivly isolated from other such groups. Since retiring I've been reading the philosophy side and now I'm not sure my gut even follows that. I wrote down my verdicts on a bunch of cases to see if there's a principle hiding under them:

- Greenish warblers around the Tibetan plateau: adjacent populations interbreed, but where the two ends meet they don't, so I call the ends two species.
- Bdelloid rotifers, asexual for millions of years: I still call them species even though there's no interbreeding anywhere in the picture.
- Dog and wolf: seperate species, even though they interbreed and have fertile pups, because they've been under very different selection and, I don't know, just are different.
- Polar and brown bear: separate species because hybrids are uncommon and the two stay distinct where they overlap.
- Neanderthals and us: different species, full stop, even with the interbreeding.
- A fossil lineage that grades slowly from one form to another: I'm fine drawing the species line at a convenient point even though it's arbitrary, but I also feel the lineage is really one thing.

Is there a principle—or a small set of them—that gets all of these, or nearly all? If the answer is pluralism, what are the jobs the different concepts are doing, and how do I keep that from just licensing whichever verdict I happen to like? What would the principle say about the one-vs-four giraffes thing, and which of my verdicts, if any, does it force me to drop?
