I am a teaching assistant in an undergraduate phenomenology course. Next week I have one fifty-minute section with second-year students; most are analytic-leaning and suspicious of what they regard as continental obscurantism, and they have read — without discussion — the early tool-analysis in Heidegger's Being and Time, including the workshop, the hammer, and the broken tool, but have not yet worked with the terms ready-to-hand and present-at-hand in class. Do not explain the distinction to me. Plan how I should teach it in that single session.

The plan should specify: the order in which the pieces must be introduced and what each depends on — in particular, what must already be in place before the broken hammer can carry philosophical weight rather than remain an anecdote about annoyance; the one concrete example or scenario that will do most of the work — whether to keep the hammer or replace it, and what that example must have and must lack for this audience; the misconceptions these students will predictably form, in the order they are likely to arise, and the exact point in the sequence at which each should be headed off (I suspect at least these three: ready-to-hand is just flow or muscle memory; present-at-hand is what is really there; breakdown is only a vivid word for noticing something when it stops working); a question for the last five minutes whose answer would show me whether they have got the distinction as one about how things show up rather than one about attention or psychology; and what you would deliberately leave out at this level, tell them you are leaving out, and why.
