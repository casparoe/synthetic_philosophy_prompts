I am drafting a statement of my views on privacy for a seminar and want a consistency check before I circulate it. My current commitments are these. (a) Privacy is best analyzed as an individual's control over access to information about herself. (b) If my neighbor trains a camera on my bedroom all night, then deletes the footage without watching it and tells no one, he has still violated my privacy. (c) If a stranger observes and remembers what I voluntarily do in a public square, he has not violated my privacy. (d) Privacy matters only because it serves autonomy and intimate relationships; it has no value beyond those goods. (e) Whether a given disclosure wrongs me depends on the relationship between the parties: the same fact about my health can be a violation when my physician passes it to a data broker and unobjectionable when a friend recounts gossip about me. (f) Privacy and secrecy are distinct, since much of what is private about me is not secret. I suspect that at least two of these commitments pull against each other, but I keep finding arguments that reconcile them, so I cannot locate the real problem. Please identify which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only seem to be, rank the tensions by severity, and say what the least costly revision is and what it costs.
