Okay so I'm a philosophy of mind PhD student, and my advisor works on pain. Shes been giving a series of off-the-cuff verdicts about whether various states are bad for the person, and I'm trying to reverse-engineer her rule before I have to TA her seminar. Her verdicts so far:

- An ordinary toothache the person hates and wants gone: bad.
- Pain asymbolia, where the patient can tell you where the pain is, how intense, how sharp, but is completely indifferent to it and doesn't want it to stop: not bad.
- Someone on morphine who says the pain is still there but doesn't bother them: not bad.
- A deep depression with no localized pain at all, just a horrible feeling the person wants to escape: bad.
- A runner's side stitch that's mildly unpleasant and annoying: bad.

The new case she's going to be asked about is a patient who feels a pain as intensely unpleasant — the felt awfulness is intact — but because of some weird frontal-lobe thing the normal urge to escape or stop it is completely gone. If you ask them whether they want it to continue, they sincerely say they don't mind either way and would be fine if it never stopped.

What's she going to say about that case, and more importantly what rule is she running? The candidates seem to be something like: badness is in the sensory quality itself, badness is the felt unpleasantness, or badness is the aversion/desire for it to stop. Which rule best fits her track record, what does each candidate predict about the new case, and wich further case would cleanly settle whichever rules are still alive?
