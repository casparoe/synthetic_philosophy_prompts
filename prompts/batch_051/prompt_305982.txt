Okay, here's one I've been turning over since I fell down a rabbit hole about Greek oath rituals and then argued about it with my roommate, who's an econ person and would not stop asking what it does to contract enforcement. The setup is the myth that oaths sworn on the river Styx bind even the gods: a god who swears falsely on the Styx is laid out flat for a year, unable to breathe, cut off from nectar and ambrosia, and then excluded from the other gods' councils for nine more years. The Greeks already treated everyday oaths as backed by divine punishment, but the Styx one is different because even the gods can't wriggle out of it. So here's the counterfactual: starting around 700 BCE, any human who swears by the Styx and breaks the oath suffers exactly that penalty, visibly and within a few days. It happens regardless of status, city, or wealth, and the god enforces the plain public meaning of the words at the time of swearing — not the swearer's private intention and not any spin they put on it afterward. Nothing else about the world changes.

Does having one reliable deity-backed oath make oaths in general more valuable, or does it mostly drive the action into the gaps: ambiguous wording, conditional oaths, swearing by cheaper gods, getting a proxy to swear for you, figuring out exactly what counts as a breach? What happens to ordinary oaths, trust, and status once a working divine sanction is sitting right in the middle of the polis, and how do tyrants, sophists, and people who dont believe in the gods cope with it?
