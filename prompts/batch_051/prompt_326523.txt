Below is a working characterization of meaningful human control over the use of force, meant as a set of criteria rather than a formal definition. For the purpose of this exercise, say that a use of force is under meaningful human control only if:

(1) a human operator or commander can veto or override the system's decision;
(2) the operator has enough accurate, intelligible information to understand what the system is doing and why;
(3) the operator has enough time to form a judgment that is independent of the system's recommendation;
(4) the operator faces a genuine choice among alternatives, rather than a ceremonial confirmation of a decision that is effectively already made;
(5) the operator's action or inaction makes a difference to whether force is used in the specific case; and
(6) the operator can genuinely, rather than only nominally, be held responsible for what happens.

Now consider a fictional automated counter-rocket, artillery, and mortar defense deployed at a forward border outpost. The system detects incoming projectiles and is set to fire automatically at anything it classifies as an incoming munition unless the human operator at the console presses HOLD during the engagement window, which is roughly four seconds. During that window the console shows a low-fidelity optical feed, the bearing and estimated range of the object, and the firing solution, but not the predicted impact point or a confidence score for the classification. Pressing HOLD requires two confirmation steps and automatically opens a legal and performance review of the operator; it also removes that interceptor from the engagement. Operators are trained that the system reacts faster and classifies more accurately on aggregate than any human, and that most HOLD interventions leave the outpost less well defended. In practice, the HOLD mechanism is not purely decorative: operators have occasionally used it in time to stop intercepts of friendly drones that the system misclassified as hostile. But attempted HOLDs far more often come after the window has closed, and the review process is widely experienced as faintly punitive.

Does the operator of the system exercise meaningful human control over the use of force, on the six criteria above? Audit the case criterion by criterion, and for each criterion give a clear verdict — yes, no, or genuinely underdetermined — with a brief justification. Where the verdict is no, say whether the failure lies in the criterion, in the design of the system, or in the surrounding practices of training, review, and procedure. Then say whether the case shows that the criteria themselves need to be revised, for instance by adding a condition about the circumstances in which a veto power can actually be exercised, or by distinguishing control from accountability. Finally, state how confident you are in your overall verdict, and identify the single criterion judgment you are least sure about.
