I'm bothered by an asymmetry in how we hand out blame and forgiveness. If a guy humiliates his coworker in a staff meeting, the security guard who saw it, a stranger who hears about it later, and the victim's friend can all say "that was wrong," and we treat each of them as entitled to blame him; nobody asks whether the speaker was the one wronged. But if the security guard or the stranger says "I forgive him," we hear that as confused, even insulting to the victim: forgiveness looks like something only the person wronged can give. Both are responses to the same wrong, so why is blame open to everyone while forgiveness is reserved to the one who was wronged? Is there a principle that justifies this, or is it an inconsistency we should give up—say, by letting anyone who was hurt by the wrong forgive their own share of it? If you'd keep the asymmetry, defend it against the case where the humiliation happened in front of the victim's teenage kids and they were plainly hurt too: do they get to forgive him, and for what?
