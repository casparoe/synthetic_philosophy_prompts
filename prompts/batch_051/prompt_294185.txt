I have been thinking about how a philosophical argument can be hostage to its era's picture of the technology it is arguing against, and I would like you to work through one case of that. From the 1960s onward, the Gödelian anti-mechanist tradition, associated especially with Lucas and Penrose, has argued that human mathematical understanding cannot be wholly captured by any mechanism. The canonical form of the argument runs roughly as follows. Suppose some machine M, understood as a consistent formal system, produces the arithmetic truths that a human mathematician can produce. By Gödel's incompleteness theorems, there is a sentence in the language of arithmetic that M cannot prove if M is consistent, but a suitably reflective human standing outside M and surveying it can see that the sentence is true. Hence the human's mathematical knowledge outruns M. Whatever its merits, the argument was framed under a specific picture of what a machine is: a determinate system of axioms and explicit inference rules, enumerable and inspectable, for which a Gödel sentence can be constructed.

Today's most capable systems differ sharply from that picture. A large language model trained on enormous corpora of human mathematical writing is not handed an axiom set or an explicit proof procedure; its behavior is a function of billions of weights, it is not straightforwardly consistent, and there is no obvious way to associate it with a recursively enumerable theory for which a Gödel sentence could be formulated. Nevertheless, such systems produce passable proofs, revise candidate arguments when prompted, and can discuss Gödel's theorem with apparent facility.

Does the Lucas–Penrose argument survive this shift from the formal-system paradigm to trained, non-axiomatic systems? I want you to identify precisely which premise comes under pressure: the idea that any mechanism worth considering must be a consistent formal system, the idea that a human has a principled way of recognizing the truth of the relevant Gödel sentence, or some other load-bearing assumption. Then assess whether the argument can be repaired, for example by treating a trained network's fixed weights as encoding a finite-state mechanism that some larger formal system can describe, or by weakening the conclusion to a claim about the limits of algorithmic explanation rather than formal unprovability. If no repair succeeds, say exactly what dies and whether any philosophically interesting residue of the anti-mechanist challenge remains.
