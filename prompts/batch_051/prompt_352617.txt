I'm trying to settle something that came up while I was reading a popular explainer on why people vote. The piece summarized the paradox of voting like this: 'Voting is irrational in any large election: your vote only matters if the two sides are otherwise perfectly tied, and the probability of that is essentially zero, so the expected benefit of casting a ballot is negligible no matter how much you care about who wins. Since voting has at least some small cost—getting to the polling place, standing in line, missing part of your lunch break—a rational person will always stay home. The fact that millions of people vote anyway is a paradox, and the standard explanation is that they are making a mistake or just doing it for the warm glow. This should also make us wary of treating election results as a meaningful signal of what the public wants, since the people who turn out are behaving irrationally.'

I've always suspected this line of thought proves too much—by the same logic you would never write to your representative, recycle, or do any small thing that mainly helps a large group—but maybe economists really do think all of that is irrational too. Could you go through the summary sentence by sentence and tell me which parts a careful person working on this would accept, which are overstatements or conflations, and weather the final claim about election results actually follows from the accurate parts? I'm not asking for a reading list; I just want to know whether the summary is fair enough to rely on.
