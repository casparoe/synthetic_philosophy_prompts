I'm a software engineer who works mostly on distributed systems and backend infrastructure — message queues, rate limiters, replicated data stores, consensus protocols, retry and backoff policies — and I have no formal background in philosophy. I've been reading about the ethics of individual contributions to collective harms (climate emissions, meat consumption, voting), and I keep getting stuck on something called the inefficacy objection: roughly, that a single act — one flight not taken, one burger not eaten, one vote not cast — makes no difference to the relevant outcome, so the individual has no reason to refrain or to act. The replies I've seen look different enough that I want them pulled apart. There is the expected-difference reply: even if my act almost certainly changes nothing, its expected impact is nontrivial because there is some small chance it is the act that falls at a threshold (the ton of emissions that pushes a system past a policy threshold, the consumer-demand tipping point, the deciding vote). There is the threshold/causation reply: harms arrive at thresholds, and by acting at the margin I take on some probability of being the difference-maker. There is a newer-sounding reply I only dimly grasp: that an act can help or participate in a collective outcome even when it does not make a difference to whether that outcome occurs, and that the objection wrongly assumes difference-making is the only thing that matters. And there is the older appeal to universalizing the act — what if everyone did that — together with the familiar problems that appeal is supposed to face.

I'd like two things from you. First, restate the inefficacy objection and each of those replies — including what actually distinguishes them, not just their conclusions — as faithfully as you can in the vocabulary I work in every day: aggregate load versus individual requests, retry floods and thundering herds, rate limits and circuit breakers, autoscaling thresholds, quorum writes and redundant replicas, idempotency keys and exactly-once effects, cache stampedes, congestion collapse. I am fluent in this vocabulary, so use it precisely rather than as decoration. Second, give me a residue report: which of the arguments translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion (and exactly where the distortion sits), and which have no counterpart in the target vocabulary at all. For each item that fails to survive the translation, tell me whether that reflects a gap in my tooling — something important it does not model, such as responsibility or normativity rather than measured aggregate impact — or whether it exposes a place where the philosophical argument was leaning on an ambiguity that the engineering language forces out into the open. In short: is the inefficacy objection ultimately a distributed-systems problem I already know how to reason about, or is it pointing past the request/load analogies at something they cannot touch?
