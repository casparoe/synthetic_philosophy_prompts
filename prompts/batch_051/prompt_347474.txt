I am trying to settle a dispute about my university's academic integrity code, which was last amended in 2003 and plainly drafted before current language models were foreseeable. The rule at issue reads: "No candidate shall submit as the product of their own intellectual work any essay, exercise, or examination response that has been composed, in whole or in material part, by another person, or which has benefited from assistance of a kind or degree that renders the submitted work not genuinely the candidate's own." A student in an epistemology seminar is assigned an essay on the conditions under which one can reasonably accept what one is told by experts. They compose the essay themselves: the examples, structure, and sentences are their own, and no model-generated prose appears in the final text. However, they use a language model at two points. First, they ask it to interrogate an early outline—not to give answers, but to generate questions like "What is your argument against the view that trusting testimony always requires independent evidence?" and "If trust in experts is not blind, what exactly distinguishes it from ordinary inference?" Second, after writing a full draft, they ask the model to identify the three paragraphs a critical reader would find least clear and to restate the burden of each in one sentence, without suggesting content. The student then revisits the assigned readings, cuts one paragraph, and rewrites another. The essay is their own at the level of sentences, but the model structured their inquiry and targeted their revisions.

Does the student's submission fall within the clause's prohibition? I want you to work through the clause on each of the main interpretive approaches—the ordinary meaning of its words, its purpose, what its drafters would most plausibly have said if asked about this practice, and what a reasonable reader of the policy would understand it to forbid—and to say which approach, if any, reaches this student, and whether a coherent reading of the clause can do so without also catching plainly permissible activities such as consulting a human tutor or using a writing center. I am less interested in the disciplinary outcome than in the conceptual question: can "not genuinely the candidate's own" be given a reading that tracks the difference between using help to produce prose and using help to manage one's own thinking?
