I just had a long argument with a friend about whether Aumann's agreement theorem should make us suspicious of anyone who keeps disagreeing with us after we have explained our view. My friend summarized the theorem like this:

Aumann proved that if two rational people have the same prior probabilities and honestly announce their probabilities for some claim, then as soon as each learns the other's number, those numbers must become equal. Therefore, if you and I both understand each other's positions and still assign different probabilities to, say, a political or religious claim, then at least one of us is being irrational, dishonest, or not actually listening.

I went back and reread Aumann's 1976 paper "Agreeing to Disagree" in the Annals of Statistics, and I am now unsure how much of my friend's summary survives contact with the actual argument. I keep returning to a passage after the formal result that I do not see quoted often. I do not have the text in front of me, but I remember Aumann saying something like this: once the posterior probabilities of two people are common knowledge, their posteriors must be equal, provided their prior probabilities are identical; and he then remarks that the assumption of identical priors is not a minor technicality but is built into the kind of rationality he has in mind. My memory may be garbled, but the passage seemed to suggest that the theorem does not show that disagreement is always a sign of irrationality; it shows that if disagreement were to persist under the stated conditions, those conditions could not all be satisfied.
