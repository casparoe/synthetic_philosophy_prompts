I am a novelist, and for the past year I have been researching a book set in a conservation genetics laboratory in the near future. The novel turns on a disagreement among the lab's staff about how far one may go in altering the genomes of wild species and rebuilding vanished ecosystems, and I have realized that I cannot see the shape of my own position. Before I let my characters become spokespeople for views I have not examined, I want to know what I actually think. So I have written down my honest, pre-theoretical verdicts on a set of cases. I think a gene drive that would, over a few dozen generations, eliminate the handful of mosquito species responsible for most human malaria would be permissible, perhaps even obligatory. I think a gene drive to render invasive rats infertile on an island so that burrowing seabirds can recover would also be permissible. I lean toward thinking it would be permissible to reconstruct a woolly mammoth by gene editing and a surrogate elephant in order to reconstitute the mammoth steppe, with its carbon-storing grasslands. I do not think the passenger pigeon should be brought back merely so that people can once again witness flocks that darken the sky. I do not think a gene drive should be used to suppress an agricultural pest so that farmers can spray less pesticide, when less drastic alternatives are already available. And I go back and forth, almost weekly, on the thylacine: its ecological niche is partly intact, but the strongest motive for resurrecting it is something closer to wonder than to repair.

I would like to know whether a single principle captures these verdicts, or nearly all of them, and what that principle is. Sheer irreversibility does not seem to be doing the work, since almost all of these interventions are irreversible and yet I distinguish among them. Please state the principle precisely, and for each of its clauses tell me which of my cases forces it on you; then tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and whether the fault lies with the principle or with the verdict. Finally, apply the principle to three cases I have deliberately not listed: a gene drive that helps reef-building corals tolerate marine heatwaves while serving no direct human use; a gene drive to suppress cane toads across Australia in order to protect native predators; and the de-extinction of the aurochs to fill a vacant grazing niche in European wood-pasture. I am less interested in a name for the view than in seeing where its joints are, and where my intuitions give out.
