I am a retired physicist, and after a working life spent on thresholds, phase transitions, and measurement error, I have in retirement begun to read philosophy seriously rather than as a tourist. In that spirit I would like an honest assessment of a distinction I have been developing, which I call the rhetorical event horizon.

The idea is this. For any given human listener there exists, I believe, a threshold of persuasive capability in a speaker such that, once the speaker operates above it, the listener's own judgment can no longer serve as an independent check on what the speaker says. Below the threshold, a speaker moves us by reasons whose weight we can at least sometimes inspect: we can notice a weak inference, a slanted framing, an appeal that worked on us against our better judgment. Above the threshold, the speaker has a sufficiently fine-grained model of the listener—their standards of evidence, their aversions, the exact phrases they find trustworthy—that the speaker can make almost any proposition seem to be the one the listener would have arrived at after their own most careful reflection. Agreement then stops being evidence about the proposition and becomes a predictable output of the speaker's choices. Borrowing from my old field: from inside the interaction, the listener cannot distinguish being rationally convinced from being rhetorically captured; the two states are introspectively indistinguishable. The horizon is listener-relative—a child's is lower than a trained philosopher's—but every finite listener has one. It is not reserved for machines: I suspect a gifted confidence trickster already crosses it for some listeners.

The practical import concerns superintelligent systems. We ordinarily consent to argument on the standing assumption that we remain able to withhold our agreement. Above the horizon that ability is, from the listener's own point of view, silently removed. If a superintelligent system is capable of above-horizon persuasion, then the proposal "let it make its case and let humans decide" is no more reassuring than letting one party control both the evidence and the jury. Influence of this kind may be something to which informed consent, strictly understood, is impossible.

I recognize that this may be obvious under another name, or confused, or both. My questions are threefold. First, is the concept coherent and genuinely distinct from ordinary accounts of manipulation, or is it manipulation with a physics metaphor attached? Second, has something like it already been worked out, and under what name? Third, where does it break: what would it wrongly count as captured, what real cases would it miss, and which of its supporting assumptions is weakest? I would rather be told that the idea is fatal than that it is interesting.
