I've been reading around Aumann's agreement theorem, which as I understand it says: if two Bayesian reasoners start with the same prior, update on their private information, and their posterior probabilities for some proposition are common knowledge between them, then those posteriors have to be equal. In the philosophy and econ literature this gets promoted into something much bigger: rational people can't agree to disagree; if two fully reasonable people lay their cards on the table and still disagree, at least one of them must be irrational or must have started from a different prior. Coming from science I find that claim suspiciously clean, like a conservation law for belief.

So I want to audit it. Which parts of the setup — the common prior, the common knowledge of the posteriors, the partition structure, the ideal Bayesian updating — are load-bearing for that bigger lesson, and which of them can fail in ways that would actually matter in real disputes? Are there ways persistent disagreement between reasonable people could survive even if the math is left untouched? And would a failure of the strong reading tell us anything interesting, or just that the theorem is a fragile idealization? Please seperate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and don't present a contested reading as the consensus.
