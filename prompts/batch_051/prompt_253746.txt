I sit on the city council of a city of about 90,000 people, and in two weeks the council votes on a resolution I have to take a position on. I want to circulate a short written opinion to my colleagues before the meeting, and I'd like you to draft it. I'm genuinely torn.

Section 14-209 of our municipal code, enacted in 1961, makes it an infraction to operate a commercial vehicle inside city limits without an annual cartage license from the city clerk. The license costs ten dollars; the fine for operating without one is up to two hundred and fifty dollars per day. The section was enforced for a while in the 1960s and early 1970s, but the clerk's office has no record of issuing a single cartage license after 1978, and code enforcement has no record of a citation under the section after 1981. The staff who run our current vehicle and business licensing had never heard of the section until a resident dug it out of the code last spring. The city attorney calls this desuetude and says the section was never repealed and is still facially valid, though she has advised that a court might find it unenforceable given the history of non-enforcement.

In the decades since, thousands of businesses have operated openly without a cartage license: plumbers, florists, landscapers, food trucks, and now app-based delivery drivers. Many of them hold current city business licenses, and some hold permits under a 2015 ordinance that regulates food trucks and street vending, but none holds a cartage license. The clerk's office has no forms, no register, and no published rules for issuing one.

Last month a group of council members introduced a resolution directing code enforcement to begin enforcing section 14-209 in ninety days, starting with app-based delivery drivers and food trucks. The resolution was introduced after a neighborhood association complained about delivery vans and food trucks crowding residential streets, and its sponsors say enforcing the old license would give the city a way to get commercial traffic under control while raising money for street repair. They estimate enforcement could bring in roughly four hundred thousand dollars a year in fines and fees. The clerk's office says it would have to build the licensing system from scratch.

The council is split three ways. One group says an ordinance does not expire just because we stopped enforcing it, and beginning to enforce it now is the only way to restore the rule of law. A second group says enforcing a law that everyone reasonably forgot would be arbitrary and unfair, and would in practice be a revenue grab aimed at the easiest targets. A third group says we should repeal section 14-209 outright and, if we want to regulate commercial delivery and vending, pass a new ordinance with notice and hearings.

Please act as an expert in the philosophy of law and the rule of law, with a practical feel for how municipal government actually works. Begin by brainstorming the strongest considerations on each side, including ones I haven't named. Then produce a single polished decision memo of about 800 to 1,000 words that I can circulate. It should recommend a concrete course of action for the upcoming vote — adopt the resolution, amend it, reject it, or reject it and do something else — with a clear bottom line and a time frame. It should give the reasons for the recommendation and honestly engage the strongest consideration on the losing side, not a weak version of it. Reason from the facts I've given you; don't pad the memo with citations to case law or books.
