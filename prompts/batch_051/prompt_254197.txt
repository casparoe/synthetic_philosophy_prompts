I've got an epistemic rule I've been following for a few years and I want to know if its defensible. In my circle, maybe ten or fifteen people are each in a position to notice and act on some big idea before everyone else — publishing the argument, launching a project, warning people, whatever. My rule is: if I think the idea is at least 60% likely to be right and would matter a lot if true, I act on it unilaterally instead of first checking with the others. My reasoning is that group discussion has its own failure modes: cautious people slow everything down, social pressure flattens novelty, and if I've honestly done the analysis I shouldn't need permission to say what I found. A friend says this is exactly the setup for the unilateralist's curse, where lots of people each using a "good enough" bar will collectively take actions almost nobody in the group would actually endorse. That sounds plausible, but I can't tell whether it applies to my case, since I'm not saying the group should act — I'm saying each of us should act when our personal bar is cleared. Is my policy defensible? If it isn't, is there a principled version — like the bar should rise with the number of people who could act unilaterally, or with how independent our judgments are — that keeps what I like about the rule without the curse? And how much should I care that our judgments are probly correlated because we read the same stuff and talk all the time? Oh, and please answer in American English.
