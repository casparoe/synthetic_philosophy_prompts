I am preparing a single fifty-minute lecture for a first abstract algebra course whose students have seen the definition of a field, complex numbers, and polynomial rings, but have not yet met group actions or Galois theory. The lecture is meant to settle, early, a confusion I see surfacing for the rest of the year: in what sense it does or does not matter which root of a polynomial one calls "the" root. I want to build it around x² − 2 over Q. The two roots are algebraically indistinguishable over Q: any polynomial with rational coefficients satisfied by one is satisfied by the other, because the map a + b√2 ↦ a − b√2 is an automorphism of Q(√2) fixing Q and swapping the roots. Yet students meet √2 and −√2 as if positivity were part of one root's identity. I want the lecture to make them see that the positive-root choice is inherited from extra structure — the ordering of the real numbers and a chosen embedding Q(√2) → R — rather than from the field itself, and that the general moral is this: an object is canonical when it is fixed by all the symmetries of the situation, so the set of roots of a polynomial is canonical even though no individual root is.

I am asking for a teaching plan, not the lecture. Please specify: (1) the order in which the ingredients must appear and what depends on what — I have imagined a sequence like posing the puzzle without relying on the word "positive"; proving the conjugacy fact that rational polynomials cannot separate the two roots; constructing Q(√2) as the quotient Q[x]/(x²−2) and noticing that the coset of x is neither of the familiar roots until an embedding is chosen; introducing the automorphism explicitly; and only then isolating exactly which extra structure breaks the symmetry — but I want you to correct or improve that order and say why. (2) The one carrying example and what it must have and must lack, plus a judgment on whether a contrast case such as x³ − 2, where the real cube root feels singled out but is algebraically in the same position as the others, would help or muddy things. (3) The misconceptions to expect and where in the sequence each should be headed off, including at least these candidates: that positivity makes √2 the natural algebraic choice; that the decimal expansion 1.414... distinguishes a root intrinsically; that indistinguishability means identity; that complex conjugation shows the phenomenon is special to C; and that the whole question is merely verbal since nothing changes when labels are swapped. (4) A short, specific in-class check for understanding that would show the idea has actually landed. (5) What I should explicitly refuse to explain at this level — I assume Galois theory proper — and how to say so without making the topic feel truncated.
