im a phd student in ancient philosophy and next month I have to give a twenty-minute paper to a stoicism reading group that meets in my department. The regulars are a weird mix: maybe five academic Hellenists who can correct my Greek, two clinical psychologists who keep saying CBT is just Stoicism with the metaphysics taken out, and the rest are modern-Stoicism people who came in through the self-help route — most have read Epictetus and Marcus in translation, a few are serious about it. I want to be ready for the discussion, because the same sentence is going to land totally differently on each part of the room.

The paper defends this thesis: the dichotomy of control as it circulates in the modern revival is a misreading of Epictetus. Encheiridion 1 does not sort things by how much causal influence you have over them — that's the popular version's test, and it's why the popularizers keep having to wedge in a third category of things you partly control. Epictetus sort by what kind of thing they are. Only the activities of the ruling faculty — assenting, judging, desiring, having an impulse — are eph' hēmin, up to us; external outcomes are not, even the ones you can partially influence, and on a strict reading even whether your own limbs follow your intention. Then comes the provocative half: I argue the influence-test version, though bad exegesis, is often better as therapy than the accurate view, which points toward resignation. So I defend a two-level picture: get the exegesis right, but dont practice the exegesis. I expect the Hellenists to hate the second half, the practitioners to be baffled about why the first half matters, and the psychologists to be the swing voters.

Please write my Q&A prep sheet in these sections, in order, with roughly these lengths:

1. The room (120 words) — the two or three camps in the audience, what each camp needs from me, and where each will stop listening.
2. Objections in order (500 words) — six to eight objections, each labelled with who asks it, phrased the way they'd actually put it, plus one sentence on how much damage it does and why.
3. Concede up front (120 words) — what I should give away in the first two sentences to buy goodwill, and how to say it without gutting the paper.
4. Hold the line (140 words) — the two claims I refuse to soften even under pressure, and why.
5. The reply that has to land (140 words) — the single response the whole paper depends on, worded once for the Hellenists and once for the self-help crowd.
6. Where I'm soft (130 words) — the weakest moves in my own argument that they're most likely to find, and how to preempt or at least not be blindsided.
7. The question I'm praying nobody asks (60 words) — and why it would hurt.

Dont bother drafting the talk itself; I only want the prep sheet.
