I serve on my university's Food Systems Advisory Committee, which reports to the vice chancellor for campus services and sets purchasing standards for dining halls and campus catering. We are reopening our seafood policy. Our existing standard covers wild fisheries — stock status, bycatch, gear type — but is silent on the welfare of farmed fish and crustaceans. A working group has proposed that the university require all farmed finfish and farmed shrimp bought for campus meals to come from producers meeting a short list of welfare conditions: finfish must be rendered insensible by percussive or electrical stunning before slaughter rather than being gill-cut while conscious or asphyxiated on ice; shrimp must be stunned before slaughter rather than packed alive into an ice slurry; and shrimp must come from hatcheries that do not use eyestalk ablation, the removal of one or both eyestalks of female broodstock to induce spawning.

The committee is split. Some members argue that the evidence that fish and decapod crustaceans experience pain is genuinely contested, and that the university should not add costs and disrupt supply chains on speculative grounds. Others reply that uncertainty cuts the other way: if the animals are sentient, these are serious harms; alternatives already exist commercially; and our own procurement ethics place the burden on avoiding practices that may cause grave suffering rather than waiting for scientific consensus. A third group worries that the standard as written would let the university adopt a humane-sounding commitment with no verification mechanism, purchasing a clean conscience while the supply chain stays unchanged; some members also doubt that animal welfare belongs inside a sustainability standard at all rather than being a distinct value that will muddy the trade-offs.

As the member responsible for drafting the decision memo, I need a formal recommendation the committee can act on: adopt the working group's standard as proposed, reject it, or adopt a revised version with staged implementation and verification requirements. The memo should have an actionable bottom line, spell out why the recommended option is preferable, and address the strongest case for the losing position rather than dismissing it.
