A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether a committee of moderately informed people is, as a group, more reliable than its best individual member. The argument began because our department is considering a policy under which grant proposals would be scored by a panel of non-specialists instead of a single expert reviewer. My friend defended the policy by appealing to Condorcet's jury theorem: if each voter is even slightly more likely to be right than wrong, and they vote independently of one another, then the probability that a majority is correct increases with the size of the group and approaches certainty. I objected that this is a theorem about idealized voters, not a claim about any actual committee. Real panelists are not independent — they share training, read the same journals, and sometimes talk before voting — and the premise that each is more likely right than wrong is not something we can verify from the outside, since it is defined relative to a standard of correctness that is usually exactly what is being contested. We ended up stuck on a more basic question: is the claim that majority rule tracks the truth under realistic conditions an empirical claim at all, and if so, how would one go about testing it?

Please act as an expert in collective epistemology and the philosophy of social science, and think through this carefully. Treat the claim under examination as follows: majority rule has a genuine epistemic advantage over individual judgment — not merely that Condorcet's theorem is mathematically true, but that real groups approximate its conditions often enough for the advantage to show up in practice. Before committing to a design, briefly brainstorm the different things someone might mean by testing such a claim, and say which interpretation is the most charitable and interesting. Then set out the strongest empirical test you can construct: specify what data you would collect (for example, a battery of questions with independently verifiable answers), what comparison you would run, what result would count in favor of the claim, and what result would count against it. Finally, be explicit about what even a positive result would and would not settle — in particular, whether it would license deferring to majority decisions in cases where there is no independent gold standard, and whether the independence condition can itself be tested or only inferred.
