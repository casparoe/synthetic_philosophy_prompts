Suppose a midsize American city amends its charter so that every capital project above $500,000 is decided by the following procedure. Each January, a citizens' budget assembly of 120 randomly selected residents is convened for a single four-month term; members are paid a salary, former members are ineligible to serve again, and members may not trade in the markets described below. In its first month, the assembly selects exactly four measurable welfare indicators for the coming fiscal year and, for each, a directional threshold that the city is to try to meet within twelve months—for instance, at least 80 percent of priority-one ambulance responses arrive within nine minutes, at least 75 percent of city parks are rated in good condition by the municipal auditor, no more than 1,000 eviction filings occur, or at least 85 percent of reported potholes are repaired within thirty days. The charter stipulates that meeting any one of these thresholds is worth $50 million. Between March and April, any city department, member of the city council, recognized union, or petition signed by 1,000 residents may propose a capital project. Each proposal receives a seven-day, publicly subsidized prediction market open to everyone. For each indicator, two contracts trade: one pays one dollar if the project is approved and the indicator meets its threshold at month twelve; the other pays one dollar if the project is rejected and the indicator meets its threshold at month twelve. The expected value of approving the project is the sum, across all four indicators, of $50 million times the difference between the prices of the two contracts. The project is approved exactly when that expected value exceeds the project's five-year amortized cost. An independent audit office with no stake in any project collects and publishes the indicator data; the assembly may not propose, amend, or negotiate projects.

Analyze this mechanism as it would actually operate, not as its designers hope it would. What does it incentivize among the assembly when it chooses indicators and thresholds, among the departments, unions, and other sponsors who design and time proposals, and among the traders, including city employees, contractors, and outside speculators? Identify the first places it would be gamed and the second-order effects that follow—for example, projects engineered to move numbers rather than deliver value, threshold placement, market manipulation, coordination among sponsors, Goodhart-style pressure on the indicators, and the behavior of a one-term assembly. Which failure modes can be fixed with narrower rules without destroying the mechanism, and which are intrinsic to deciding budgets through conditional prediction markets? End with a verdict on whether the procedure mostly selects projects residents genuinely value or mostly selects projects that are good at moving the numbers.
