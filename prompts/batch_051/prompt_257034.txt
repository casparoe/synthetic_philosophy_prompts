I have been trying to work out whether a comparison I noticed between moral psychology and medicine is a genuine structural parallel or only a loose analogy. The philosophical side comes from John Rawls's discussion of envy and resentment. Rawls treats envy proper as a vice: it is the disposition to be pained by another person's good simply because that good is theirs, not because one has been wronged. Resentment, by contrast, is a moral feeling: it is a response to something that actually is unjust. But Rawls also allows that some envy is excusable when it is a reaction to inequalities that unjustly injure people's self-respect — inequalities a society should have prevented or repaired. So within the same family of unpleasant social emotions, feelings can be condemned as vices, treated as reliable moral signals, or seen as understandable and informative responses, and the difference turns on whether the feeling is tracking a real injustice. The medical side is pain. Acute pain is normally a reliable signal of actual or threatened bodily damage, which is why doctors generally take it seriously. Chronic and phantom-limb pain show that an aversive sensation can persist after healing, or arise without any matching injury, so that the feeling itself becomes the problem rather than a guide to one.

The structural parallel I think I see is this. In both cases there is an aversive state whose felt unpleasantness does not by itself settle whether it should be respected as a report of external harm or treated as something to be managed away. In both cases the state has a proper object — injustice for these social emotions, bodily damage for pain — and its warrant depends on whether it is tracking that object accurately. In both cases we therefore have to answer two separate questions: whether the feeling is genuinely felt, and whether it reveals a real harm in the world beyond the feeling itself. Is that mapping accurate, or am I reading too much into the shared vocabulary of signals and warrant? If the parallel is deep rather than superficial, what would have to be true of both sides for anything to transfer between them — for instance, could the clinical distinction between acute, informative pain and chronic, misleading pain cast light on the thought that some resentments indicate real injustice while other, similar-looking feelings are merely envious? And where exactly does the analogy break down, for example in the fact that injustice is a contestable normative property while bodily damage is comparatively mind-independent?
