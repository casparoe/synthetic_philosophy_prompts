I have been going back and forth with two friends about whether social sanctions that the state never touches—deplatforming above all—are best analyzed as punishment, and one of them keeps anchoring the debate in an analogy I want you to stress-test. His line is that deplatforming is essentially a modern, decentralized version of Athenian ostracism: a community notices that someone has made himself intolerable to its common life and withdraws the shared platform, not to inflict suffering but to protect the space. Because ostracism is usually described as a measured political institution rather than a penalty—a fixed term of removal decided by a vote, with no criminal charge, no confiscation of property, and no formal imputation of wrongdoing—he concludes that deplatforming should likewise be classified as community self-defense rather than as punishment, and that calling it punishment is a category error that makes a protective practice look more oppressive than it is. My other friend calls this a strained analogy: a content platform is private infrastructure, not a polis, and deplatforming tends to be permanent, intensely moralized, and imposed by a few firms rather than by a citizen quorum.

I would like an audit of the analogy rather than a tally of loose objections. Work out the explicit mapping: what in the contemporary case fills the role of the polis, the ballot (the inscribed potsherd), the threshold of participation, the fixed term of exile, and the studied refusal to treat the expelled person as a wrongdoer. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the conclusion from the ones that are merely cosmetic. In particular, identify what the source domain quietly imports—formality, time limits, proportionality, the absence of moral stigma, a bounded civic community—that the target may lack, and explain whether each imported feature is load-bearing for the claim that deplatforming is not punishment. Finally, say whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and what better analogy, if any—excommunication, shunning, boycott, trespass from private premises, or something else—would carry the claim more honestly.
