Here's a pair of ordinary judgments that sit uneasily together. Most people think a person who loses their spouse and feels no grief at all is missing something — we treat the absence of that painful emotion as a defect, and we'd think a life that never contained grief was poorer, maybe even a failure of love. But those same people treat someone who is swamped by sadness for months, even when the sadness has a real object (the end of a relationship, the state of the world), as having a problem to be cured; there we reach for the language of disorder and want the feeling gone. So one painful emotion is counted as part of a good life and another as noise. I want the strongest principle you can find that generates both verdicts. Try candidates like fittingness to the object, proportionality, controllability, action-readiness, and whether the person would endorse having the feeling; say which one survives pressure from the others. Then tell me whether the principle you end up with is closer to the Stoics (emotions are worth having only insofar as they guide action, and the sage would not grieve) or the romantics (at least some painful emotions are constituents of the good life). Finally, run the principle on the hard case of a man whose daughter died twenty years ago and who still weeps every time he mentions her. Answer in British English.
