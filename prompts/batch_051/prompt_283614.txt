I keep going around in circles in my reading group about Sherlock Holmes: everyone's confident, but the confidence cancels out. We all agree that "Holmes doesn't exist" is true, and also that "Holmes is a detective" is true in the stories. Fine. But when we try to state the rules for what makes a sentence true in a fiction, someone always produces a case that breaks the rules: the detail never stated but taken for granted by every reader (Holmes has a liver; the London of the stories has gravity), the story that contradicts itself where we still don't want every sentence to count as true in the fiction, which is where naive "just take what the text says" views blow up, the narrator who is lying or mistaken so that "in the fiction, X" comes apart from "the story says X", the impossible-in-real-life plot that's unremarkable inside its genre, and the fact that "Holmes doesn't exist" and "in the stories, Holmes exists" are somehow both fine.

So before I read any more theories—pretense accounts, closest-possible-world accounts, abstract-object accounts that treat Holmes as an invented thing—I want the spec first. Write the desiderata that any adequate theory of truth in fiction has to meet, where "adequate" means it can handle ordinary talk and inference about stories rather than just formal semantics or lit-crit. For each requirement give the case or puzzle that forces it, and mark whether it's non-negotiable or just one camp's preference. I especially want two things: (1) which pairs of desiderata might not be jointly satisfiable, since that would explain why this debate never lands, and (2) which single requirement does the most work ruling out the standard views. Also keep the requirements about the name "Holmes" itself (empty reference, negative existentials) distinct from the requirements about the "in the fiction" operator—my group keeps running those together and I want them untangled. Skip the scorecard; I'll map the theories onto the list myself. And write it in American English.
