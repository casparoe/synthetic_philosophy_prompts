I recently had a long argument with a friend about whether clicking "I agree" on a standard-form online contract—a clickwrap contract of adhesion—should count as genuine agreement, or merely as a legally convenient substitute for agreement. My friend maintained that the empirical findings settle the matter against real agreement: almost nobody reads the terms, so almost nobody can be said to agree to them. I resisted that inference, on the grounds that agreement need not reduce to a private mental state of informed assent; it may also be a public act with conventional force, and contractual assent is a legal status as much as a psychological fact. We eventually conceded that the data alone could not settle the conceptual question, but neither of us could say precisely what kind of study would get any traction on it.

I would like you to take up that question. Treat the following as a conceptual claim with an empirical edge: a user who clicks "I agree" to a standard-form online contract has thereby agreed to its terms. Design the strongest empirical investigation you can of this claim. Be concrete: specify the population, the conditions or manipulations, the measures, and the principal comparison. Say what pattern of results would count in favor of treating the click as genuine agreement, and what pattern would count against treating it as such. Then distinguish what the investigation can show from what it cannot: what aspect of the claim would even a clean positive result leave unestablished, and what aspect would survive even a clean negative result? In particular, address whether "agreement" is best understood here as a psychological state, a speech act, or a legal status, and explain how that choice changes what any empirical test can and cannot settle.
