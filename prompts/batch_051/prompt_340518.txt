I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar called Computing, Fairness, and Power, with about 18 students and one 75-minute meeting. In two weeks we will hit the formal literature on algorithmic fairness, and I want the students to discover, before they read any of the proofs, that the fairness criteria they find individually compelling are jointly unsatisfiable in the ordinary case. Here is the idea I want them to feel in their hands rather than hear from me: suppose a binary risk score is used to make yes/no decisions — predicting, say, who will reoffend, default on a loan, or clear a hiring screen — for two groups that differ in their base rates (the raw share of each group that turns out positive). The score is calibrated when, at every score level, the same fraction of people who receive that score actually turn out positive in each group, so that a score of 6 means the same risk regardless of group. In that setting, unless the score is a perfect predictor, you cannot simultaneously equalize the two kinds of mistake across groups — the false positive rate (flagging someone who would not have turned out positive) and the false negative rate (passing over someone who would). Equalize both error rates and calibration has to break somewhere; keep calibration and at least one error rate will differ between groups. That is the trade-off I want the activity to make vivid, and I want students to run into it through their own design choices rather than by being told it exists.

Design a classroom activity — a small-group game played with pre-printed cards or slips of paper, explicit scoring rules, and a payoff scheme that does not require real money — in which groups act as competing vendors of a risk score for two applicant pools with visibly different base rates. Each group must actually decide how to sort its applicants into a small number of risk buckets, post its statistics on the board (positive rate within each bucket, false positive rate, false negative rate), and try to satisfy a checklist of three desiderata that they are told are all reasonable. The rules, incentives, and constraints should be arranged so that the incompatibility shows up in the posted numbers before it is named: groups that manage equal error rates will have buckets that mean different things in the two pools, and groups whose buckets are calibrated across pools will show unequal error rates. The time pressure, or a small reward for being able to report "all boxes checked," should tempt them to re-bin or fudge, which becomes its own debrief point about institutional pressure to claim fairness. Include materials (exact card-deck compositions), roles, rules, a minute-by-minute timeline for 75 minutes, and a debrief that maps what happened in the room onto the impossibility result I described. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it, and be frank about which feature of the formal theorem the activity cannot reproduce — for instance, the theorem covers every possible predictor satisfying the criteria, not just the few scoring rules the class happened to build — and how the facilitator should supply that gap.
