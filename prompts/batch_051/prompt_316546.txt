I've been reporting on embryo selection for a while now, and the beat just shifted from "testing IVF embryos for single-gene disorders like cystic fibrosis or Huntington's" to startups selling polygenic scores that rank embryos on diabetes risk, schizophrenia risk, and even things like predicted educational attainment. I want to run an interactive "what do you actually think?" decision tree alongside the piece, because I've stopped trusting the usual labels: plenty of people who would never call themselves eugenicists still think you should pick the embryo without the fatal mutation, and plenty of people who are fine with selecting against disease get quietly squeamish about a "polygenic health index." I want to sort readers by their answers, not by which word they'd put on a sign.

So: build me a decision tree from the logical space rather than from a list of -isms. Start by working out three or four branching claims that actually do the sorting, something in the neighborhood of: whether parents have a reason, or an obligation, to pick the available embryo with the best expected life; whether the disease/enhancement line is doing real moral work; whether the state belongs in the decision at all; and whether selecting against a trait expresses something insulting even if no individual is harmed. Dont feel locked to those if a better split emerges. The tree should include, as leaves, at least these actual positions: the view that embryos have full moral standing, making all selection wrong; the strong disability-rights/expressivist critique of selecting against deafness or Down syndrome; a treatment-only or "healthy child" view that permits preventing serious disease but not selecting for normal-range variation; a pure procreative-autonomy view that leaves it all to parents; procreative beneficence in its strong form (an obligation to select the best-off child among those that will actually be born) and in its weaker pro tanto form (a real but easily outweighed reason); liberal eugenics as a public/political stance (the state may normalize and permit, but not coerce); and the old perfectionist eugenics that made better traits a population-level project. If the tree throws up an unoccupied combination—say, someone who is fine selecting for enhancement but objects to selecting against disability—tell me whether it's incoherent, merely unattractive, or actually defended and the tree just can't reach it, and flag any two leaves the tree genuinely cannot separate. To test the branches, work in these cases concretely: (1) two usable embryos, one with a mutation for a uniformly fatal childhood disease and one without; (2) a Deaf couple using IVF who specifically request an embryo with a deafness-associated variant because deafness is their family's language and culture; (3) a startup's "general health index" where the couple picks the embryo with slightly higher predicted educational attainment or income; (4) a public health system that funds screening and issues guidance telling clinics to favor the embryo with the best expected wellbeing. My readers are science-literate but not philosophy-trained, so phrase each branch as a claim they can answer yes or no to without knowing the labels. Aim for somewhere around ten leaves, maybe a couple more, each with one sentence on its most awkward commitment. I'd rather the tree over-report its limits than fake a clean sorting—if two positions come apart only in a case I haven't included, say so.
