I am a philosophy professor who convenes a research group on the philosophy of artificial intelligence, and for several months one participant, a machine-learning researcher, has been closing down abstract debates by invoking the same slogan: "intelligence is just compression." The slogan has gradually acquired the authority of a settled principle. Last week it did real work on two occasions. A student asked whether a system that simply stored every input it had ever encountered and later retrieved the stored answer should count as intelligent; the researcher replied that such a system compresses nothing and therefore cannot be intelligent. Later, when the discussion turned to whether intelligence requires an inner perspective or a self-model, he dismissed the question as confused, on the grounds that the only thing that matters is whether a system finds shorter descriptions of regularities in its input. I am sympathetic to something like this idea—prediction and compression are linked in ways that clearly matter—but I suspect the slogan is being asked to bear more weight than any one precise thesis can carry.

I would like you to disambiguate what "intelligence is compression" could actually be claiming. Lay out a menu of candidate theses: computational ones, such as the claim that any system that predicts efficiently thereby compresses data, or that prediction and compression are formally intertranslatable; psychological ones, such as the claim that finding compressed representations of experience is the function of cognition; and stronger eliminativist ones, such as the claim that intelligence consists in nothing over and above compression, so that questions about selfhood or understanding are idle. For each candidate thesis, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible claim, or an overstatement, and identify the assumptions that separate the stronger readings from the weaker ones. Then apply the menu to my colleague's two moves: which readings, if any, justify excluding a pure memorizer, and which readings, if any, actually dissolve the question about whether an inner perspective or self-model is required? I especially want to know whether the slogan can be true in a weak sense while failing to license one or both of the argumentative uses to which it was put.
