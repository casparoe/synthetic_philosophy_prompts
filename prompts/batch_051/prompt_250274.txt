I am giving a paper next month at an economic methodology workshop, and I expect a difficult discussion period. The thesis I will defend is this: Milton Friedman's F-twist in his 1953 essay should be reconstructed not as the global claim that unrealistic assumptions are harmless, but as a functional claim about how assumptions earn their keep in a model. On my reading, an assumption's lack of realism carries no weight against a model only when two conditions are met: the assumption functions within a model whose predictive target is stated explicitly, and the model's predictive success for that target has been demonstrated empirically. The F-twist therefore gives no licence to false assumptions in models used to explain mechanisms or to guide policy counterfactuals; at the same time, it protects maximization models from the objection that agents do not literally solve optimization problems.

The audience is mixed. Roughly half are applied microeconomists who are Friedmanite in practice and will resist being told that their working models are merely instruments. There are also two economic theorists who regard methodology as a distraction and will look for ways the reconstruction has no consequences. Finally, three philosophers of science who work on idealization will be suspicious of any reading that seems to let economists off the hook by reinterpretation. Everyone in the room knows the standard objections: that predictive success is too weak a criterion, that models are underdetermined by data, and that assumptions matter for explanation even when they do not matter for prediction.

Write me a Q&A prep sheet for that discussion period, not a full defense of the paper. For each objection, phrase it in the way this audience would actually put it, indicate who is likely to raise it, order the objections by how likely and how damaging they are, state what I should concede and what I should hold, and give a two-minute reply with a note on where that reply is weakest. Include the objection I am hoping nobody asks. My argument sketch, in brief, is as follows. I distinguish a theory from a model. I argue that only models, not whole theories, have predictive targets sharp enough to trigger Friedman's reasoning. I use that distinction to absorb the standard counterexamples in which false assumptions led to predictive failure. And my central move is to reinterpret Friedman's talk of treating agents as if they solved an optimization problem as a claim about the division of inferential labour inside a model, rather than as a claim about the truth of the assumptions.
