so last night me and a friend spent like two hours arguing about whether math actually explains things in nature or just describes them after the fact. he kept hammering the periodical cicadas: sure, they emerge every 13 or 17 years, and sure, those are prime, but the real explanation is natural selection and the primes are just a byproduct of the life cycle, not doing any explanatory work. I kept trying to say no, the number-theoretic fact is doing genuine explanatory work, and we got absolutly nowhere.

anyway, I teach an upper-level philosophy of science seminar, 12 students, 90 minute sessions, and next week I want to run a session where they feel this instead of hearing me argue with a wall. can you design a structured game or simulation around the cicada case? the main constraint is that they should discover for themselves that prime-numbered life cycles beat composite ones at avoiding periodic predators, before I ever name prime or indispensability. I want the math to show up in the rules and payoffs, not in the debrief. something like teams choosing emergence periods, a predator cycle or two, population counts, and enough simulated years that the pattern comes out. maybe 45 minutes for the game and 30 for debrief, with the last part of the debrief connecting it to the honeycomb case (bees minimizing wax with hexagons) and then to the indispensability argument.
