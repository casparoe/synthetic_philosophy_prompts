I recently watched two friends argue, over several rounds, about a third friend's decision, and I could not settle it myself.

Our friend Dana keeps four hens she took in when a small egg operation shut down. The birds are healthy, have a secure run, and get veterinary care; Dana does not cull them and does not buy more. They lay eggs almost every day. Dana, who had avoided eggs for years on ethical grounds, has begun eating the ones her own hens lay, and she gives the surplus away. Two other friends, Mateo and Priya, argued about it in front of me.

Mateo opened by saying Dana had quietly abandoned her principles. She refuses to treat animals as sources of products for humans, yet she now takes the output of her hens' reproductive systems for herself. She could instead feed the eggs back to the hens, which would also return some of the calcium the hens lose in laying. By eating the eggs, she sends the signal that an animal's body is still, at bottom, a legitimate resource for people once the circumstances look clean enough.

Priya replied that Dana creates no market demand, funds no operation, and harms no bird. The eggs will rot or need disposal regardless. The moral problem with eggs has always been the industry behind them — confinement, the culling of male chicks, the purchase itself — not the egg as an object. To refuse to eat an egg from a hen you feed and shelter is to mistake the object for the relationship that produced it. It is like freeganism: taking discarded food does not fund the system that discarded it.

Mateo answered that "creates demand" cannot carry the whole weight. If a rescued cow had to be milked for her own health, would Priya drink the milk? If a sheep's overgrown wool had to be shorn for her comfort, would she spin it into clothing? If a beloved dog died of old age, would she use the leather? Each of those uses would also involve a "waste" product. What Dana has become is not a guardian who happens upon a byproduct but a guardian who also benefits from the animal's body, and "guardian plus beneficiary" is how farmers have always described themselves.

Priya said those other cases have to be judged one at a time, by whether the byproduct must be removed anyway for the animal's welfare and whether any demand is being fed. Wool that must be shorn and would otherwise be thrown away is not made more respectful by being thrown away; refusing to benefit from it is a kind of ritual purity, not a protection of the sheep. She added that the cow and dog challenges are aimed at provoking disgust rather than tracking a moral difference.

Mateo then said the eggs are not waste until someone decides to call them waste. Laying is costly to the hen, and the respectful default is to reduce or interrupt laying where that is feasible, not to consume the result and call it a byproduct. He also said Dana's rule could not be made general: if everyone were willing to eat eggs only from well-kept rescue hens, there would be nowhere near enough such eggs to go around, so the practice is an exemption that depends on the continued existence of the very industry she rejects.

Priya granted that the rule fails as a general one but said that is not the sort of test she accepts. Accepting a donated kidney or buying a secondhand coat also cannot be made general in that blunt way, and that does not settle whether either act is wrong. What matters, she argued, is whether Dana's own act involves harm, demand, or complicity. Mateo has not named the feature that makes it wrong; he has only shown that it can be made to sound unappetizing.

I came away unable to decide; whichever side had just spoken struck me as right. I would like someone to referee the exchange point by point: for each of the main moves above, say who is right about what and to what degree, which moves are substantive and which are merely persuasive, and then give your own verdict about Dana's choice and about who argued the better case.
