In a seminar on the rule-of-law ideal, one participant defended a court's decision to invalidate a piece of emergency legislation by reaching for the old adage that ours is a government of laws, not of men. A second participant replied that this is threadbare rhetoric: laws do not draft themselves, and judges interpreting them inevitably rely on judgment, discretion, and contestable values, so the adage cannot be literally true. I would like to get straight what that sentence could actually be doing. Lay out the distinct precise claims the slogan might be used to assert, arranged from the most trivial and platitudinous to the most substantive and contestable. For each reading, say whether it is true, false, or true under specific conditions, and explain what it would take for a legal system to satisfy it. Then say which of these readings, if any, would actually answer the objection that decisions are always made by persons, and which readings overclaim in ways a defender of the rule of law does not need to endorse. In particular, address whether the slogan forces one to say that law can bind officials independently of their judgment, or whether a weaker reading — about generality, publicity, non-retroactivity, and the difference between arbitrary will and the application of standing norms — is enough to give it point.
