I teach an upper-level philosophy of language and metaphysics seminar and I am writing the final exam. Candidates will have 90 minutes, and the question is worth 25 marks. Please write one essay question on bivalence and truth-value gaps that cannot be answered by reciting Aristotle's sea-battle discussion. It should force candidates to show that they understand why rejecting bivalence and rejecting excluded middle are different moves, that they can operate a toy formal semantics in which one fails, that they can identify a serious cost of such a semantics, and that they can explain why supervaluationism is attractive but not free of problems. Then give a model answer of about 700 words and a marking scheme built around five must-hit points, the marks each is worth out of 25, and a one-line note on how weaker candidates typically get each point wrong.

Question

In On Interpretation, chapter 9, Aristotle reasons from the thought that if every assertion about a future contingent event, such as the assertion that there will be a sea-battle tomorrow, is already true or already false, then the event is already fixed, and he draws a conclusion that appears to restrict truth or falsity for such assertions. The passage is often invoked in debates about whether every statement must be true or false.

Your task is to address the following.

1. Distinguish the principle of bivalence from the law of excluded middle. Explain why a classical logician can regard them as inseparable only because classical semantics has exactly two truth values, while someone who introduces a truth-value gap must say explicitly which of the two principles fails.

2. Provide a three-valued semantics for a toy language containing only atomic sentences about future contingents, negation, conjunction, and disjunction, in which bivalence fails and excluded middle is not a logical truth. Specify the truth values, the designated values, and the truth tables, and show how the table for disjunction assigns a non-designated value to the instance p or not-p when p has the intermediate value.

3. Give one serious objection to this three-valued semantics that is not merely the observation that it departs from classical logic. Explain what the three-valued theorist would have to say in reply and why the reply is not fully satisfactory.

4. Explain how a supervaluationist can accept that bivalence fails while still counting every instance of p or not-p as true. Identify one specific problem this creates that a two-valued classical semantics does not have.

5. Return to Aristotle. State one reading of the sea-battle passage that is compatible with your answers, and identify one idea sometimes attributed to that passage that modern apparatus allows us to reject or refine.

Model answer

The principle of bivalence is a semantic principle: every declarative sentence is exactly one of true or false. The law of excluded middle, in its logical form, is the claim that every instance of the schema φ or not-φ is true, or valid, or assertable. In classical semantics every atomic sentence receives exactly one of two truth values, and the truth tables for negation and disjunction ensure that φ or not-φ always receives the top value. The two principles come apart as soon as a third non-designated status is allowed. One may deny bivalence by saying that some sentences are neither true nor false, while retaining excluded middle if the logic is set up so that φ or not-φ still counts as true even when φ itself lacks a classical truth value. Conversely, in a simple three-valued logic one can reject both bivalence and excluded middle by treating the intermediate value as non-designated and by letting disjunction take the maximum of the values of its disjuncts.

For the formal semantics, let the set of truth values be {1, ½, 0}, with 1 the only designated value. Interpret 1 as true, 0 as false, and ½ as neither true nor false. Negation maps 1 to 0, 0 to 1, and ½ to ½. Conjunction is the minimum of the two values, and disjunction is the maximum. For an atomic sentence p about a future contingent matter, let v(p) = ½. Then v(not-p) = ½, and v(p or not-p) = max(½, ½) = ½. Since ½ is not designated, this instance of excluded middle fails. The schema p or not-p is not a logical truth in this semantics because there is an allowed valuation on which it receives a non-designated value. To make the point explicit, the disjunction table relevant here is: if both disjuncts are ½, the disjunction is ½; if one disjunct is 1, the disjunction is 1; if one is 0 and the other ½, the disjunction is ½; and if both are 0, it is 0. The failure occurs exactly when both disjuncts are intermediate.

A serious objection to this three-valued account is the assertion problem. If the sentence p, such as the prediction that there will be a sea-battle tomorrow, is neither true nor false now, then what is a speaker doing when she asserts it now? Ordinary assertion seems governed by a norm that one should assert only what is true, and one should deny only what is false. But p is neither true nor false, so neither assertion nor denial seems appropriate. Yet we routinely make predictions, accept bets on future contingent events, and treat people as having said something correct or incorrect when their predictions are later borne out or not. The three-valued theorist can reply that assertion norms should be revised: perhaps p may be asserted when it is at least not false, or when it is useful given all available evidence. But this reply weakens the connection between assertion and truth that many theorists treat as constitutive. It also leaves the three-valued account embarrassed by retroactive assessment: when the sea-battle does occur, it is natural to say that the person who predicted it had spoken truly all along, but a truth-value gap account must tell some story about how an assertion that was not true at the time became true later, or about how truth-bearers change their truth status with the passage of time.

Supervaluationism takes a different approach. A sentence is considered true, or supertrue, if it is true under every admissible classical valuation, false or superfalse if false under every such valuation, and otherwise neither supertrue nor superfalse. For the future contingent p, some admissible valuations or future histories make p true and others make p false, so p itself is neither supertrue nor superfalse. But under every admissible classical valuation, p or not-p is true, because every classical valuation validates the disjunction. So p or not-p is supertrue even though neither disjunct is supertrue. In this way the supervaluationist keeps the law of excluded middle as a valid form while denying bivalence for the bare claim p.

The cost is that supervaluationist truth is not compositional with respect to the standard connectives. Whether a compound sentence is supertrue is not determined by the supertruth status of its parts. The sentence p or not-p is supertrue, while each of p and not-p is neither supertrue nor superfalse. But two other sentences whose parts have the same local statuses could compose in a different way, depending on how the indeterminacies are distributed across admissible valuations. Consequently the connectives are not truth-functional in the way students of classical logic expect, and logical consequence must be defined globally over valuations rather than locally over the values assigned to components. This makes the semantics more cumbersome and raises puzzles about whether supertruth is really a notion of truth rather than a technical surrogate.

Aristotle's passage can be read as rejecting bivalence for future contingent assertions without necessarily rejecting excluded middle: he may be saying that neither the assertion that there will be a sea-battle tomorrow nor its negation is already true, while still allowing that the disjunction one of them or the other is in some sense guaranteed by the law of excluded middle, depending on how one formalizes it. The idea that avoiding determinism requires rejecting bivalence, however, can be rejected or refined. Modern discussions separate the semantic question of whether a future contingent proposition has a truth value now from the metaphysical question of whether the future is fixed. An Ockhamist can hold that the future contingent proposition is already true or false now, while insisting that its truth does not necessitate the event, because truth is not the cause of the event and bivalence does not by itself imply determinism. Supervaluationism also shows that one may deny bivalence without giving up excluded middle, so Aristotle need not be saddled with a wholesale rejection of classical logic.

Marking scheme

1. Distinguishing bivalence from excluded middle (5 marks). The candidate must state that bivalence is a semantic principle about exactly two truth values, while excluded middle is a logical principle about the truth or validity of φ or not-φ, and must explain how adding a third value can drive them apart. Weaker candidates treat the two as synonyms or infer that rejecting one entails rejecting the other.

2. Reconstructing the sea-battle reasoning and returning to Aristotle (5 marks). The candidate must show that the passage can be read as targeting bivalence for future contingents without necessarily rejecting excluded middle, and must identify at least one modern refinement, such as the Ockhamist point that bivalence need not imply determinism. Weaker candidates simply say Aristotle denies that future contingent statements are true or false and stop there, or they conflate truth with necessity.

3. Producing a correct three-valued semantics and showing excluded middle fails (5 marks). The candidate must specify values, designate 1, give tables for negation, conjunction, and disjunction, and demonstrate that p or not-p has value ½ when p is ½. Weaker candidates introduce a third value but still let disjunction or negation produce a designated value for p or not-p, or they fail to identify which values are designated.

4. Raising a serious objection beyond mere non-classicality (5 marks). The candidate must articulate a problem such as the assertion problem, the loss of truth-functionality, the status of truth-bearers over time, or propositional attitude ascriptions, and explain why the three-valued theorist's reply is not fully satisfactory. Weaker candidates offer only the objection that classical logic is abandoned or that three-valued logic seems strange, without developing an argument.

5. Explaining supervaluationism and one cost of preserving excluded middle without bivalence (5 marks). The candidate must define supertruth over admissible classical valuations, show how p or not-p is supertrue while p and not-p are not, and identify a cost such as non-compositionality or the disputed claim that supertruth deserves to be called truth. Weaker candidates say supervaluationism simply declares p or not-p true by stipulation, or they confuse not supertrue with superfalse and therefore misstate the position.

Total: 25 marks. An A answer will contain all five moves with the technical details correct; a B answer will contain the first three but will be vague or partly mistaken on the objection or the supervaluationist cost.
