I am a novelist. In the book I am writing, the central character is a working mathematician who regards the four-colour theorem as true beyond any doubt, yet refuses to say that she knows it on the strength of the computer-assisted proof. While drafting her position I built the following argument, and it now seems to prove more than I intended; I cannot find the false step.

(1) A mathematical proof is a finite sequence of statements, each of which is an axiom or follows from earlier statements by an elementary rule of inference.
(2) The computer-assisted proof of the four-colour theorem is such a sequence, but no single human mind has surveyed the whole of it; the unsurveyable case analysis was executed by machine.
(3) If knowledge by proof required one mind to survey the entire sequence, the four-colour theorem would not be known.
(4) The four-colour theorem is known; mathematicians rightly treat it as settled.
(5) Therefore what proof-knowledge requires cannot be that someone surveys the whole. What matters is that a finite, mechanically checkable sequence exists and was in fact checked by some reliable procedure.
(6) If that is all that matters, then a sufficiently reliable black-box device that reported that a proof exists for some conjecture, but exhibited no sequence at all, would also give us mathematical knowledge of that conjecture.
(7) It would not. If a black box announced that the Riemann hypothesis had been proved, we would not thereby know the Riemann hypothesis.
(8) So we must surrender either (4) or (7): either the four-colour theorem is not known after all, or a bare machine oracle can transmit mathematical knowledge.

I find I want my character, and myself, to keep both (4) and (7), so I read the argument as a reductio of something upstream. My unease centres on the move from (5) to (6), but I cannot say what epistemic difference remains between a sequence no one has fully inspected and a certificate no one can inspect. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost?
