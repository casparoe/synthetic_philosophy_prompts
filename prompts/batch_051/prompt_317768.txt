I am a student in a university course on set theory and mathematical logic, and we have just covered Zorn's lemma. In our presentation, Zorn's lemma says: if a nonempty partially ordered set has the property that every chain in it has an upper bound in the set, then the set contains a maximal element. I constructed the following argument, and I cannot find the step at which it fails, even though the conclusion is clearly false. I would appreciate a careful diagnosis rather than a restatement of the definitions.

Let P be the collection of all countable subsets of the real numbers, ordered by inclusion.

(1) P is nonempty and is partially ordered by inclusion.
(2) Take any chain C in P, and let U be the union of all the sets in C. Since every member of C is a subset of U, U is an upper bound for C.
(3) The set U is countable, because it is a union of sets each of which is countable, and a union of countable sets is countable.
(4) Therefore U is an element of P, so every chain in P has an upper bound in P.
(5) By Zorn's lemma, P contains a maximal element M.
(6) Suppose M is not all of the real numbers. Then choose a real number x not in M. The set M ∪ {x} is still countable, and it is a strict superset of M within P, which contradicts the maximality of M.
(7) Hence M is the full set of real numbers, so the real numbers are countable. But Cantor's diagonal argument shows that the real numbers are uncountable.

Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting that step commit me to? Please write your answer in American English.
