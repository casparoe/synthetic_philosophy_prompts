I'm a PhD student in behavioral econ, and I've got a decision rule I've never actually tried to justify. I won't take a gamble when I can't put numbers on the probabilities, even when the known-probability option pays less under the most natural prior. Last week a friend offered me a choice: draw from an urn with exactly 50 red and 50 black balls and win $20 on red, or draw from an urn with 100 red and black balls in unknown proportions and win $25 on red. I took the known urn. If I use a 50/50 prior the second one has higher expected value, but I don't want to pull a prior out of thin air, so I won't. I do this all the time, and I think of it as a discipline: don't gamble on ignorance, keep your uncertainty quantified. A colleague says it's just Ellsberg-style ambiguity aversion, and that it can make my choices jointly incoherent—like, I'd also take the known urn if the winning color were black, which means I'm acting as if the ambiguous urn is less than 50% red and less than 50% black at the same time. Is my rule defensible as a policy for managing my own ignorance, or is it a bias I'm dressing up as prudence? Is there a version of it that keeps the benefits without the incoherence? I don't care much whether I'm violating some axiom; I want to know if this is a good policy for someone who is genuinely uncertain about the contents of the urn. Also: please separate what's settled here (I think the fact that people exhibit this pattern is pretty settled?) from what's genuinely contested among decision theorists, and don't present a contested view as the consensus.
