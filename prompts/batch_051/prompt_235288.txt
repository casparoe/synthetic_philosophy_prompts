I recently had a long argument with a friend about Lewis’s modal realism. I was defending it as a serious metaphysical proposal, while my friend kept calling it an unfalsifiable science-fiction ontology and insisting that on Lewis’s view the word “actual” ends up doing no work. Afterwards I found this summary in an introductory book, and I want to know how much of it I can rely on when I continue the argument.

Here is the passage:

“Lewis’s modal realism says there are infinitely many possible worlds, and each is a concrete universe as real as our own. The actual world is not metaphysically special: to call a world actual is just to say it is the one you are in, like saying ‘here.’ For any way things could have been, there really is a world like that, containing flesh-and-blood objects and people. For example, ‘there could have been flying pigs’ is true because some world contains flying pigs, and ‘you could have been a plumber’ is true because a counterpart of you in another world is a plumber, where a counterpart is whoever in that world is similar to you in the relevant respect. Because there are no spatiotemporal or causal links between worlds, what happens in other worlds cannot matter to what happens in ours. Lewis admits this is strange but thinks it earns its keep by reducing possibility, necessity, counterfactuals, properties, and propositions to talk about worlds and their contents; this also shows that modality is not a matter of our conventions.”

I am suspicious of three things in particular. First, the claim that “for any way things could have been” there is a world seems to smuggle in a principle of plenitude under the cover of a definition. Second, the claim that Lewis reduces properties and propositions to “talk about worlds and their contents” sounds like it lumps together analyses he would keep apart. Third, the final clause that this “shows that modality is not a matter of our conventions” feels like editorializing rather than exposition. But the book is by a professional, and my friend says I am just rationalizing.

Please audit the passage for fairness. Separate what is accurate, what is a common simplification, what Lewis explicitly denied or would resist, and where the wording overstates a modest claim. In particular, tell me whether each of my three suspicions is well-founded or would be rejected by a careful Lewisian, and say what a defender of modal realism should object to first in the passage and what, if anything, my friend has genuinely right.
