I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've spent the past few months reporting on how different publics relate to their governments. A claim I keep returning to is this: in countries where public institutions are broadly competent and honest, the Scandinavian default of institutional trust — extending confidence to agencies, courts, and elected bodies until specific evidence of betrayal appears — is a more reasonable stance than the American default of suspicion, in which public institutions must repeatedly earn confidence and are presumed unreliable until proven otherwise. More strongly, I suspect the actual gap between Nordic trust and American suspicion is only partly explained by differences in institutional performance; part of it is a difference in default attitude, and the Nordic attitude is the more reasonable one.

I'm tempted to defend this claim in a long-form piece, but before I commit I want to know exactly what it is risking. I don't need authors or citations; I need the strongest case for the claim's being false, sorted by how much damage each item would do if established.

Please do the following:

1. Reconstruct the claim carefully, separating three things that often run together: that trust is instrumentally useful for governance; that trust is epistemically reasonable — well-calibrated to available evidence about institutional performance; and that citizens ought, as a civic matter, to adopt one or the other posture as a default.

2. Identify what would have to be true for the claim to fail under each reading. For instance: evidence that high trust is largely a cultural inheritance that persists after institutional quality declines; evidence that Nordic trust rests on historically contingent conditions such as small, relatively homogeneous societies, low inequality, or a particular welfare-state bargain; evidence that American suspicion is precisely what keeps American institutions as accountable as they are; or cases in which high-trust publics update too slowly to corruption, cover-ups, or administrative failure. These are starting points, not a ceiling.

3. Distinguish the defeaters that would be decisive from those that would be merely awkward. Which findings would force a defender to abandon the claim, and which would only force them to qualify it?

4. Be honest about insulation. A defender might say that trust is reasonable exactly where institutions are trustworthy and suspicion is reasonable where they are not, so the comparison never really pits attitude against attitude. If the claim collapses into that near-tautology under pressure, say so, and tell me whether that collapse is fatal to the claim as I originally framed it or merely a clarification of it.

5. Close by telling me what a committed defender of the claim would themselves concede counts against it, not only what critics would say, and rank those concessions from fatal to manageable.

I would personally be most moved by evidence that high-trust publics are systematically slow to notice institutional decline, or that the Scandinavian pattern depends on background conditions that cannot be separated from the trust itself. But I don't want my priors to anchor the map; I want it honest even where it points away from the conclusion I find attractive.
