I'm a philosophy professor, and I've been keeping an informal casebook on a colleague's rulings about slurs. To avoid using real ones, she mostly works with a made-up slur: the word krand for Krandians, an invented ethnic group. Her track record, as I've written it down:

- You krand, said as an insult: a slur, derogatory.
- He's not a krand, he's one of the good ones: still derogatory; the negation and the compliment don't cancel it.
- Is the new neighbor a krand?: still derogatory; asking doesn't launder it.
- She said I was a krand, reported secondhand: still derogatory; you can't clean it by attributing the word to someone else.
- A Krandian comedian using krand in a joke about Krandians, to a mostly Krandian audience: not a derogatory use; that's reclamation.
- A linguist mentioning krand in a paper about slurs: not a derogatory use.
- jerk said with exactly the same contempt: not a slur, just an insult.

Next week a student is going to ask her about a kid who grew up in a community where krand is just the neutral word for Krandians, nothing nasty attached, and who innocently describes someone as the nice krand lady next door. Predict her verdict: does that use count as a slur or not? Then say which theory her track record is actually running—wether the derogation lives in the word's conventional semantic content, or in speaker attitudes and social prohibitions, or some mix—and give me the further case that would settle which one she's really committed to.

And if I've set this up badly—if is it a slur? is actually several questions and her track record doesn't force one answer—say that flat out instead of humoring me.
