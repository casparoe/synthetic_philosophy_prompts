I'm a linguistics PhD student (philosophy of language side of the department), and I keep seeing the same gloss of the generics litarature in passing. The intro chapter of a textbook I'm reading summarizes it like this:

Take Ducks lay eggs and Mosquitoes carry malaria. Both are true even though most ducks never lay eggs and almost no mosquitoes carry malaria. That is the puzzle of generics. The resolution, now fairly standard, is that generics aren't quantifiers like most or all. A generic predicates a property of a kind rather than of its members: Ducks lay eggs says the duck kind has the property of laying eggs, and that property counts as holding of the kind when it is in the relevant way normal, defining, or characteristic of the kind. So Ducks lay eggs is true while Ducks are female is false, and Mosquitoes carry malaria comes out true because carrying malaria is a characteristic feature of the mosquito kind even if almost no individual mosquito has it. Since generics track what is normal or characteristic rather than what is statistically typical, they naturally lead hearers to treat rare but striking properties as typical, which is why the social-cognition people warn that generic talk about social groups is dangerous.
