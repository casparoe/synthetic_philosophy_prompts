I am preparing a single 90-minute session for a public-library reading group of intelligent non-specialists — a retired nurse, a civil engineer, a parish administrator, a part-time magistrate, and a few others — none of whom have studied philosophy and most of whom hold the firm, unexamined view that people ordinarily deserve the praise and blame they get. I want to explain Galen Strawson's Basic Argument against ultimate moral responsibility so that the group genuinely grasps the regress and can run it themselves, rather than merely hearing it described and then rejecting it as an intellectual curiosity.

Please give me a teaching plan, not the explanation itself, and structure the response in the following sections, in this order, with roughly the indicated lengths:

1. Sequence and dependencies (about 40%). Lay out the components in the order they must be introduced, and for each step say what earlier step it depends on and why it cannot come sooner. Include the opening move, made before any thesis is stated, so that the audience does not hear the conclusion as the absurd claim that nobody ever really acts or chooses. The plan must be built so that the argument's independence from determinism is a load-bearing insight introduced at the right moment rather than a footnote near the end. Say also when and how the qualifier ultimate in "ultimate moral responsibility" gets cashed out, since the whole session turns on it.

2. The carrying example (about 20%). Specify the one example or case that will carry the argument through the entire session: what it must have and what it must lack, and how the same example is revisited at each step of the sequence. It should be a small, everyday moral failure rather than a dramatic crime, and you should explain why that constraint matters for this particular audience.

3. Misconception map with timing (about 25%). For each misconception this audience is likely to bring or form — for instance, hearing the argument as "everything is determined, so nothing can be blamed," or treating "he could have done otherwise" as a stopping point, or inferring that accepting the argument would dissolve all practices of blame, punishment, and praise — say the point in the sequence where it will predictably surface and how the sequence should absorb it at that point rather than leaving it for a Q&A at the end.

4. Check for understanding (about 10%). Describe what the group should be able to do or say by the end that would show the regress has actually landed: not that they can restate the premises, but that they can run the argument on a case they have not seen and explain in their own words why neither an earlier act of self-shaping nor an injection of randomness stops the regress.

5. What to leave out (about 5%). List what a full treatment would include that you would deliberately omit at this level — such as the wider compatibilism debate or the detailed relationship between Strawson's argument and other regress arguments — and say why each omission protects the aims of the session.
