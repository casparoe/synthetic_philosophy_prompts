After I retired from analytical chemistry I started reading philosophy, mostly about language, and I got particularly stuck on the question of untranslatability, whether some concepts just don't map cleanly from one language to another. That reading became unexpectedly practical because I now sit as the non-clinician scientist on our hospital's research ethics board. We are reviewing a study of a culturally adapted counseling intervention for persistent low mood after childbirth, carried out through the hospital's long-running maternal health partnership in a highland community in Papua New Guinea. The participants' first language has no settled vocabulary for several ideas the protocol leans on: depression, placebo, random assignment, and the right to withdraw from research. The consent process has to happen in that language, or the study collapses, since you cannot get meaningful consent in a language people only half command.

The investigators' translation consultants have proposed two options. Option one keeps the English terms in the consent conversation and glosses them each time in the local language: depression gets explained as something like a heavy sadness in the chest that does not lift for many months; placebo as a pill with no medicine in it, given only so the comparison is honest. Option two uses local idiomatic expressions that come closer to how people actually talk, but the investigators admit the meanings shift at the edges. The closest local phrase for persistent low mood also carries a suggestion that the person has neglected family obligations, and the closest expression for placebo amounts to trick medicine, which the community advisors on the team say could sound insulting. Half the board thinks option one is the only defensible way to protect informed consent. The other half says tacking an English word onto a gloss does not solve a translation problem, and that using expressions people actually understand is more respectful even if the meanings are not identical. I keep going back and forth myself. Could you draft the board's descision memo before Friday? It should land on a clear recommendation, approve the consent materials in one format, approve them with conditions, or reject the study as proposed, and it should give the real reasons rather than just splitting the difference, while taking seriously the strongest argument for whichever option loses.
