At work we're doing a postmortem on our customer-support chatbot, and I ended up getting picked as the tiebreaker in an argument between two coworkers about whether the bot "lied." I'm hoping you can adjudicate it, because I honestly can't figure out who's right.

The log they're fighting over is this. A user typed: "If I cancel my annual plan now, will I get charged anything?" The bot answered: "You can cancel anytime from your account settings page." That sentence is true, but the user was on an annual plan and canceling early triggers a $40 fee. The bot didn't mention it, and a few lines later the user says "great, no fee then" and cancels.

Dana said that's clearly a lie. Marcus said it can't be a lie because the bot didn't say anything false; the sentence is literally true, so at worst its incomplete. Dana said that's way too narrow: if a car salesman tells you he just replaced the brakes and tires and leaves out that the engine block is cracked, you'd say he deceived you even though the sentence was true. Marcus said the salesman case is different because that sentence carries an implication about the car's condition, whereas the bot just answered the literal question and the user jumped to a conclusion. Dana said that's absurd — the user's question was obviously about whether money would leave their account, and the bot answered a different question on purpose. The system prompt literally tells it not to volunteer fees unless the user asks directly, so the misleadingness was engineered in. Marcus said that just moves the intent to the designers: the bot itself has no intention to hide anything, it's predicting text, so saying the bot is dishonest confuses the tool with the people who configured it. Dana said we already talk about software in mental terms all the time — we say a router "thinks" a packet is bad or a loader "doesn't understand" a file. If we can say the bot "answered" the user, we can say it "misled" them; you don't need a theory of consciousness to use the word. Marcus said sure, but "misled" isn't "lied," and a vending machine that swallows a dollar doesn't cheat. If every case where a system lets a user draw the wrong conclusion counts as a lie, then call-to-action buttons are lies too. Dana said a vending machine doesn't make claims about your account. The bot's whole function is to shape what users believe about the product, so when a system whose job is communicative gives a partial answer, the plan behind that selection is doing the same work as an intention — maybe designer intent rather than runtime intent, but it's still there.

They both turned to me like I had the tiebreak. So: who is right about which specific points, and what, if anything, are they both missing? Be as precise as you can about the distinctions that actually matter here — I care more about getting the structure of the disagreement right than about picking a winner for its own sake.
