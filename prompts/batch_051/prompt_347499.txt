Last night I got into a long argument with a friend about political parties, and I cant stop turning it over. The thing that stuck with me is an asymmetry. Individually, we all seem to think that letting a group do your thinking is a vice: we use "partisan" as an insult, we roll our eyes at politicians who simply vote the party line, and we praise the one who votes their conscience or breaks with their own side. But the same people (me included) would say a democracy with no parties at all—just millions of unorganized independents—would be unstable or unworkable. A party is, among other things, an organized machine for getting people to adopt positions as a bloc, for whipping the ones who drift back into line, and for packaging political judgment into a few team options. So the small-scale vice looks like the large-scale necessity.

My friend had been reading Simone Weil and kept pushing the hard version of this: she said it isnt a manageable tension but a straight contradiction, that parties are by their nature machines for suppressing independent thought, and that we only treat them as legitimate because theyre old and familiar. I kept trying to defend parties as a practical compromise—they aggregate preferences, make governments accountable, give voters a usable choice. She said I was just describing the status quo and then calling it a justification. Honestly by 2 a.m. I wasnt sure she was wrong.

I want you to actually adjudicate this rather than just explain both sides. Specifically: is there a principle that justifies holding both judgments at once, or is the asymmetry a piece of collective doublethink? If you defend the asymmetry, defend it against the hard case of a party whip forcing a representative to vote a line the representative privately thinks is wrong—which is just the individual vice, organized and given a salary.

Give the response in four sections, in this order and with roughly these lengths:

1. The asymmetry (150 words): state it as charitably and precisely as you can.
2. The best case that its justified (300 words): the strongest principle that could underwrite judging individuals one way and parties another.
3. The best case against (300 words): why that principle might fail, including the strongest version of the Weil-style line.
4. Verdict (200 words): which side should win, and whether we should keep the asymmetry, reform it, or give it up.

No citations or reading list needed—just argue it.
