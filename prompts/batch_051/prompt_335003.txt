You are an expert on the psychology of skill acquisition and transfer. Please think slowly and carefully, separate the different questions tangled together here, and treat both sides as serious before you rule. I am not a specialist, but I want a rigorous referee's report.

On a long drive, two friends of mine spent an hour arguing about whether reasoning can be trained the way martial arts are. One friend, call him Marcus, defended the idea. He said a martial artist drills a kata thousands of times so that in a real confrontation the component movements fire automatically; rationality training aims at the same thing. You repeat exercises — spotting fallacies in short artificial arguments, translating verbal claims into probabilities, asking what the base rate is — until those moves fire by themselves inside real disagreements.

Lena disagreed. In a real fight, she said, nothing looks like the kata: the distance, timing, and stress are all different, and strikes practiced in the air often collapse under resistance. That is exactly the transfer problem. Someone who can spot a fallacy in a neat paragraph will still rationalize when their own job or relationship is on the line. If anything, the drills create an illusion of competence: people learn to talk fluently about motivated reasoning while continuing to do it.

Marcus replied that she was attacking a straw man. No serious trainer thinks kata alone produces a fighter; kata is combined with sparring. The right comparison is not kata alone but kata plus sparring, and the drills exist to install pieces that live sparring then pressure-tests. You would not say a boxer should stop shadowboxing just because shadowboxing is not a fight.

Lena said the martial-arts analogy actually cuts the other way. The fighting systems with the best competitive records spend less time on formal choreography and more time on alive drills, where a partner genuinely resists and adapts, so the problem is not predictable. Even shadowboxing is not really a kata; it is improvised and self-correcting in a way that a fixed fallacy-spotting worksheet is not. That is exactly what most rationality exercises lack: the alive version. Until someone builds the sparring equivalent of rationality training — adversarial debate where the other side adapts, pre-registered forecasts — pointing at martial arts does not yet justify the kata side.

Marcus answered that those alive versions do exist and are the interesting part, but formal drills still earn their keep because they break reasoning into small reusable components: noticing an equivocation, separating a factual claim from a value claim, asking what would count as evidence. A jab transfers across contexts precisely because it is a small component, and rationality drills target components at that same grain. So the burden shifts back onto her: she should show that these specific components do not transfer, instead of asserting that nothing general transfers.

Lena replied that the burden sits the other way, because the far-transfer claim is the one in need of evidence, and the record of general problem-solving instruction is not encouraging. She added that the whole martial-art framing smuggles in the conclusion: a martial art is a training system whose drills demonstrably produce fighters, and the fight supplies an honest scoreboard. Reasoning mostly has no such scoreboard, so a person could drill for years and never find out whether it worked.

They asked me to referee, and I could not untangle it. Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each move, say what it gets right, what it gets wrong or too quickly, and rate it on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, briefly justifying each rating. Then say who is more right overall, and identify the strongest point each side never made.
