I am drafting a novel in which a character develops a monothematic delusion after a stroke, and I want his state of mind to be psychologically precise rather than a stock portrayal. While planning the character, I have been collecting my own verdicts about a series of cases, and they no longer add up.

- The man who, after an accident, calmly reports that his wife has been replaced by a near-identical impostor, and who continues living with her and accepting care from the woman he calls an impostor: I cannot help calling him a believer — he assents to the proposition and will argue for it — yet the belief is curiously inert in his daily choices.

- The woman who reports, with every appearance of sincerity, that she is dead and that her body is a corpse, while still eating and making plans for the next month: I do not hear a belief; I hear an experience of her own body reported in the idiom of a fact — nearer to 'I feel dead' than to 'I believe I am dead.'

- The man who denies that his left arm is paralyzed and explains its refusal to move with obviously false confabulations: I lean toward saying he believes the arm is fine, though his behaviour suggests a corner of him knows otherwise.

- The husband who is convinced his wife is unfaithful without evidence, and who treats every neutral detail as confirmation: I call that an ordinary false belief, maintained unreasonably — not a different kind of state.

- The woman who says the thoughts in her head are not hers but have been inserted there by a neighbour's machine: I do not take her to be expressing a belief about the neighbour; she is reporting an altered sense of ownership of her own thinking, with a mistaken explanation attached.

My working rule has been functionalist: a person believes that p only to the extent that the state plays the role a belief that p should play, guiding expectations and action and shifting with evidence. That rule would force me to say the first man does not believe his wife has been replaced, since he treats her as his wife in almost every practical setting; but I do not want to give that verdict up. The same rule does not trouble me in the case of the jealous husband, who is equally insensitive to evidence, and that asymmetry bothers me. Is there a single precise principle — perhaps one on which delusions require both an anomalous experience and a failure to evaluate it, or one on which these reports are often avowals of experience rather than beliefs — that captures all or most of my verdicts? State the principle, say what it predicts for cases I have not listed (for instance, someone who says his flat is bugged but never checks for devices, and someone who says he is the reincarnation of a historical figure and restructures his whole life around it), and tell me honestly which of my verdicts I would have to surrender.
