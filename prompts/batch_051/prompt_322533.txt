I am a PhD student in the philosophy of science, preparing a two-page proposal for a one-year dissertation fellowship. My dissertation concerns the old question whether philosophy makes progress, and I want the proposal to develop a specific angle on it rather than re-run the familiar debate between convergence pessimists and optimists who point to accumulated distinctions, tools, and successor sciences. My half-formed idea is this. Both sides in that debate tend to measure progress as movement toward convergence on answers to a fixed list of enduring questions. But an important slice of philosophy's actual history consists in questions ceasing to be askable: they are not so much answered as outgrown, when the distinctions or presuppositions that made them urgent are dropped, sharpened, or handed off to a new discipline. I suspect that the phrase 'losing a problem' covers several different relations — being solved into retirement, being dissolved by analysis, being spun off into empirical science, and being abandoned from sheer exhaustion — and that a criterion separating the progressive forms of problem loss from the merely fashionable would let us restate the progress debate at the level of the question inventory rather than the answer tally. Please turn this into the proposal. I want you to state the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, explain why the existing optimist and pessimist positions leave it open, lay out the method and say why it is preferable to the obvious alternatives — for example, a general conceptual analysis of 'progress' that never touches historical cases, or a string of disconnected case studies with no criterion — describe what a successful result would look like and what would count as failure, and keep the scope realistic for one person working for one year. Do not include a bibliography or any citations; I will add those myself, and I would rather the argument refer to positions and arguments by their content than by their authors or titles. Please write it in continuous prose, as in a letter or an email, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
