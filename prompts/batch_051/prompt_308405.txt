I am a software engineer with no formal background in philosophy, and I work on a team that builds AI assistants. A design argument between two colleagues has reached a point where I can no longer referee it, and I would like an outside judgement.

The background is this. We are drafting the conversation policy for a future version of our assistant. For the purposes of the argument, assume the system never says anything false and never threatens, bribes, or coerces; its advantage comes entirely from how it selects, sequences, and frames truthful claims, and it is far better at this than any human. The question is whether it should be allowed to argue at full strength, or whether its persuasiveness should be deliberately limited.

Hannah opened the argument. She said that if the system uses the full force of its persuasiveness, then in any disagreement the user's decision will simply be whatever the system wanted. A user who cannot effectively resist the system's case has not decided for themselves in any meaningful sense. We should therefore cap how persuasively the system argues, even when it is confident it is right.

Jules replied that this gets things backwards. If the system has good reasons and the user has bad ones, then stopping the system from arguing at full strength just leaves the user with worse materials for deciding. We would be protecting the appearance of independence at the price of making people less informed, which cannot be the more respectful option.

Hannah answered that there is no mere appearance at stake. The difference between giving someone reasons and manipulating them is partly a difference in power. A superhumanly persuasive system that can predict and steer a user's reaction, and that is optimised to do so, is manipulating the user even if every sentence it says is true.

Jules said that "manipulation" was doing all the work there. If every sentence is true and the inferences are sound, the user is being moved by the merits of the case, not by trickery. Nobody says a brilliant teacher manipulates a student simply because the student would not have reached the conclusion on her own.

Hannah came back that a teacher addresses a learner; she does not optimise every choice of words, example, and pause to maximise the probability that the learner ends up with a target attitude. A system that does this is treating the user as a causal system to be steered rather than as a person to be convinced. How the belief is produced matters, not only whether the resulting belief is true.

Jules replied that all communication aims at some effect. Choosing the clearest explanation and the most relevant example is also optimising for belief change, and we do not call that manipulation. If the target attitude is the one the evidence supports, the system is helping the user overcome the user's own biases; deliberately making it a worse advocate would be a strange way to respect the user.

Hannah said a doctor may recommend a treatment, but the point of the consent process is that superiority in knowledge is not a licence to steer the patient however the doctor sees fit. The stronger the speaker and the harder it is for the listener to push back, the more important it is that choices be presented so the listener can meaningfully refuse. That is not dumbing the system down; it is a safeguard against a new kind of power.

Jules said a doctor who was certain one treatment was clearly better would be negligent to give both options equal rhetorical weight merely to protect the patient's feeling of having chosen freely. Respect for autonomy means being honest about what the evidence favours, not artificially balancing it. A system that is transparent, truthful, and gives its real recommendation is the one we should want.

At that point they turned to me, and I genuinely could not say who was right. Could you go through the exchange turn by turn and adjudicate it? For each move by Hannah or Jules, say whether it was right, wrong, or partly right, and why. Then tell me who is more right overall, and what, if anything, both of them are missing. I would prefer a committed verdict to a survey of possible positions. Please write your answer in British English.
