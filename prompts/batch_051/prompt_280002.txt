Read Ursula K. Le Guin's short story The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas as a test case for the paradox of deontology. The paradox, roughly, is that a morality built on agent-relative constraints both forbids an agent from doing certain things and, at the same time, seems to forbid the agent from minimizing the occurrence of those very things: you may not kill one innocent person even if that killing is the only way to prevent several other killings. In the story, the whole city's happiness and decency depend on one child kept in filthy, abject misery, known to all the citizens. The citizens did not create the arrangement, and most do not personally enforce it; they know of it, accept it as the condition of their own flourishing, and remain. A few walk away, one by one, and the story declines to say where they go or whether their departure changes anything for the child.

I want a reading that treats both the story and the deontological literature with full seriousness. Specifically: does the arrangement put pressure on the doing/allowing distinction, given that ordinary citizens cause nothing, merely know and benefit? Is a citizen who remains treating the child as a mere means, and if so does walking away answer that charge or simply exchange complicity for abandonment? Finally, does Le Guin add anything to the paradox of deontology that the standard trolley-style cases miss, or does she merely restate them with better prose? Where the deontological reading must strain or ignore parts of the text, say so directly.
