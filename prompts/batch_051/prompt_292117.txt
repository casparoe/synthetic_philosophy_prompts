I spent most of my career as an ecologist and have only recently turned to moral philosophy, and a question at the boundary of the two disciplines has been troubling me. Consider the proposition that, over the next half-century, clearing one hectare of mature temperate broadleaf woodland and allowing it to regrow as a mosaic of grassland and scrub will produce less total suffering among the sentient wild animals living on that hectare than leaving the woodland undisturbed would. My own credence hovers around 0.35, but I do not much trust it. Please give me your credence in that proposition and, more importantly, decompose the reasoning behind it. The sub-questions I think matter are at least these: whether the lives of wild animals in either habitat are on average worth living or net negative; whether mature woodland and early-successional grassland–scrub differ systematically in the density of sentient life, in the dominant causes of death, and in the typical lifetime welfare of their inhabitants; whether the deaths and displacement caused by the clearing itself contribute a cost large enough to dominate the long-run difference; whether cross-species and cross-time comparisons of welfare are well-defined at all; what discount rate, if any, the comparison should apply to future suffering and over what horizon; and whether ecosystem-level properties such as naturalness, biodiversity, or species richness belong in the welfare ledger or should instead be kept separate. For each sub-question give a rough probability and a sentence or two of justification, show how they combine into your final number, and then tell me which sub-question the overall estimate is most sensitive to and what evidence would move it most.
