I am a novelist, and I am drafting a book whose central crisis is a decision to blow the whistle. The character—call her the engineer for now—works in quality assurance at a small medical-device firm. She discovers that the firm submitted manipulated durability data to the regulator before approval. The device is already in wide use, and the company's own internal analysis puts the cumulative rate of a serious, surgery-requiring failure in the low single digits over five years. Her attempts to escalate internally have been rebuffed, and the company has reminded her of her confidentiality and nondisclosure obligations. The plot turns on whether she takes the internal analysis to a journalist, and on how she understands what she would be doing to the people she works with.

To sharpen the moral terrain, I asked two friends to argue the case in my presence while I stayed out of it. Ruth is a patient-safety advocate; Samuel is a former corporate lawyer who now teaches business ethics. What follows is a condensed, lightly edited version of their exchange.

1. Ruth: "Going to the press would be a betrayal—perhaps a justifiable one, but a betrayal all the same. Loyalty is not merely an instrument for producing good outcomes. It is partly constitutive of the engineer: she has spent years accepting the trust and company of her colleagues, and breaching that trust would change who she is before it changes anything else. Betrayal carries a moral weight of its own, so the question is not only whether leaking produces more benefit than harm; it is whether she may break faith. For that, a projected failure rate is not enough."

2. Samuel: "I do not deny that betrayal has weight. But faith has conditions. Loyalty to a firm is owed in virtue of the ends the firm actually serves. When it falsifies safety data, it ceases to be the enterprise she was loyal to. And the firm broke faith with her first; loyalty is not a one-way tribute."

3. Ruth: "The firm broke faith, but her colleagues did not. If she leaks, people in her department who had no hand in the fraud may lose their livelihoods or be drawn into litigation and investigation. You are willing to injure the innocent in order to relieve the engineer's conscience. If her loyalty to those colleagues as persons matters, that is exactly what should stop her."

4. Samuel: "That assumes the colleagues' interest is to remain ignorant. They are being made unwitting participants in a fraud, and if the truth emerges later their position will be worse, not better. In any case, her disclosure does not cause their injury; the firm's decision to ship a device on falsified data causes it. Refusing to help maintain a dangerous secret is not the same as betraying anyone."

5. Ruth: "That relabeling will not survive the moment of decision. If she leaks, she can foresee with near certainty that the consequences will land on those colleagues; a person is answerable for the foreseeable consequences of her act even when she wishes they were otherwise. Notice also the asymmetry: the harm to patients is years away and probabilistic, while the breach of a promise she made when she signed the nondisclosure agreement is certain and immediate. It is strange to trade a certain wrong for an uncertain good. The loyal course is to keep pressing internally, at risk to herself."

6. Samuel: "No one can promise to keep the kind of secret that makes her party to concealing a fraud on the public. The nondisclosure agreement is a contractual instrument, not a vow of silence in all circumstances, and on any reasonable reading it cannot bind her to hide conduct that endangers patients. Uncertainty cuts the other way too: regulators and clinicians act on exactly this kind of projected risk, and if she waits until the failures are certain, she will have allowed the harm she could have prevented. That is not caution; it is a quiet kind of betrayal."

7. Ruth: "What troubles me is the world your view leaves behind. Once betrayal can be traded away against uncertain harms, loyalty stops being a distinctive reason and becomes an item in a cost-benefit ledger. But the point of loyalty is precisely that it binds when the calculation points the other way. You have not adjudicated between loyalty and safety; you have dissolved loyalty into safety."

8. Samuel: "I have not dissolved it; I have located it. Loyalty is owed to persons and to the worthwhile ends an institution serves, not to an institution's power to keep its misconduct quiet. The engineer can honor what she owes her colleagues—by giving them early warning and helping them prepare—without helping the firm hide what it did. What you are defending looks to me like the form fidelity takes when a wrongdoer is using it."

Neither would move from these positions, and I genuinely could not tell which moves were sound. I would like you to adjudicate the dispute as it was actually made, movement by movement, rather than write a general essay on whistleblowing.
