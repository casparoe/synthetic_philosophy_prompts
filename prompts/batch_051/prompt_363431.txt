I'm a journalist who covers science and ideas, and I've got a rule I've been applying more and more when I decide what to write about. If a field doesn't have a clear feedback mechanism—some way that wrong results get corrected fast through experiment, prediction, or practical use—I hold its claims to a much higher bar before I'll cover them. My thinking is that fields with fast feedback advance faster, because errors get filtered out without having to rely on people's good judgment or intellectual honesty. A philsopher friend told me to read Mill's On Liberty, and I got stuck on this line:

"If the opinion is right, they are deprived of the opportunity of exchanging error for truth: if wrong, they lose, what is almost as great a benefit, the clearer perception and livelier impression of truth, produced by its collision with error."

That sounds like Mill thinks even false or sloppy ideas can make our grip on truth sharper by forcing us to answer them. If that's right, my feedback-loop rule might be a kind of censorship of my own attention: it would keep me away from fields where progress is slow and messy but still real, and it might cut me off from the very collision Mill says improves understanding. So: is my rule defensible as a way to spend limited reporting time, or is it a bias dressed up as a quality filter? Is there a version of the rule that keeps the benefit—avoiding noise—without screening out slow fields that still produce truth through collision? How would I tell from the inside whether I'm applying the rule to protect my beliefs rather than to allocate my attention?
