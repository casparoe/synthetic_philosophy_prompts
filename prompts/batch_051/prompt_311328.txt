I'm a retired physical chemist, and after a lifetime of reaction paths and free-energy landscapes I've started reading philosophy, so please bear with me if my terminology is off. I'm trying to turn a half-formed idea into a short essay, working title 'free-floating rationales,' and I'd welcome comments on whether there's actually an argument here and where it fails.

My notes so far:

- In science we routinely say a process has a 'rationale' without positing anyone in charge: the rationale for a river braiding is that the flow can't carry the sediment load, so it splits. The rationale for a protein folding the way it does is that the folded state is a minimum of the free-energy landscape. These aren't just causes in the billiard-ball sense; they're explanatory shapes, the kind of thing you can misidentify before you get them right.

- Philosophers mostly reserve 'reasons' for minds. I want to defend the weaker claim that some reasons are free-floating: they obtain whether or not any agent registers them. The reason not to keep burning fossil fuels (that doing so harms future people) was a reason before climate science, not simply when we noticed it.

- The obvious objection is that I'm conflating explanatory reasons ('the reason the pipe burst was rust') with normative reasons ('you have reason to replace the pipe'). I think the distinction is real but not the last word. Maybe both are species of a single genus: a consideration that makes something intelligible as the thing to be, do, or expect. The difference is whether the target already comes with a direction, standard, or success condition.

- If that's right, some of the mystery around normativity deflates. A norm would be a free-floating rationale that a self-monitoring system can track, fail to track, or mistrack. An agent's reason would just be a free-floating rationale that has found a reasoner to be right or wrong about it.

- My own worry: I may be laundering idealizations back into the world. Free-energy minima and fitness landscapes are our models; if they're not literally 'out there,' then free-floating rationales may just be bookkeeping, and the whole essay collapses into a metaphor.

- Another worry: there may be no unified intelligibility relation. Reasons for physical processes, for beliefs, and for actions have seperate logics — belief-reasons get defeated by evidence, action-reasons by competing ends, physical rationales by boundary conditions — so the genus may be empty.

I'd specifically like comments on: (i) whether I'm smuggling normativity into physics by using words like 'rationale'; (ii) whether there's a non-question-begging test for when a free-floating rationale counts as normative rather than merely explanatory; and (iii) which part a philosopher would attack first and what that line of attack would be. I'd also appreciate any structural suggestions. No need to point me to readings; I want to work this out from the ground up.
