I'm giving a talk next month to a standing seminar on the future of governance, and I want a prep sheet for the question period, not a restatement of the talk. My thesis is that futarchy—the proposal that a democratic stage first fixes the welfare metric by which policy outcomes are to be judged, and that prediction markets then select the policies expected to score best against that metric—fails at the very point where it promises a clean division of labor between values and beliefs. I will argue that choosing the metric is not a small administrative step but a substantive value commitment. The metric cannot be left to the markets, since the metric defines what the markets are supposed to maximize. Any usable metric is a bundle of indicators with stipulated relative weights—income, life expectancy, leisure, health, some measure of self-direction—and setting those weights is exactly what a theory of the good does. The market prices reached under that metric are therefore forecasts conditional on one contested evaluative hypothesis, not a value-neutral guide to means. My conclusion is that futarchy does not avoid the hard problem of democratic value choice; it buries that problem in the choice of metric and then reimports it disguised as prices.

The room is mixed and not lined up behind me. Roughly half are political theorists and historians of self-government whose commitment is that democratic practice has non-instrumental worth; they will accept my conclusion readily and may fault me for granting futarchy's outcome-centred frame at all. Two regulars are governance engineers—they see ordinary legislatures chiefly as failed information-aggregation systems, know the futarchy idea well, and will defend the ideal against any argument they can present as an implementation problem. One is a sociologist of quantification who studies how metrics reshape the practices they are supposed to measure; she will be sympathetic to the circularity worry but may press that my argument is conceptual while her field shows these failures empirically.

Please give me the prep in the following sections, in this order, to these rough lengths.

1. "The likely objections, in the order they'll come" (roughly 250 words). Phrase them as those audience members would actually phrase them; for each, give a one-sentence reply and an honest note on where that reply is weakest. Mark which objections have real force and which can be handled cheaply.
2. "Concede and hold" (roughly 120 words). What I should grant up front without losing the thesis, and what I must not grant, with a sentence of reasoning for each.
3. "The reply that has to land" (roughly 150 words). Identify the single answer on which my whole thesis depends, give its strongest form, and then tell me the weakest point in it that I should prepare to defend rather than hope no one notices.
4. "The question I'm hoping nobody asks" (roughly 100 words). State it, and give me the best salvage line if it surfaces.
5. "Settled vs. contested" (roughly 100 words). Separate the claims in sections 1–4 that are broadly settled among people who take futarchy seriously from those that are genuinely live; where my talk depends on a contested reading, say so plainly and note what sort of argument would need to carry it.
