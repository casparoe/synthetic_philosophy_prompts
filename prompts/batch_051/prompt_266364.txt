For most of my career I have taught a course on the historiography of the French Revolution, and I remain struck by how little traction its central interpretive debate has produced in decades. The social interpretation, in its classical form, explains the Revolution as the political expression of class struggle: a rising bourgeoisie confronted an aristocratic reaction while peasant grievances gave the conflict its force, so that the events of 1789 and even the Terror become intelligible as moments in a larger social transformation. The ideological interpretation reverses the order of explanation: political culture changed first, through new languages of the nation, popular sovereignty, and republican virtue, and these ideas reshaped the very interests they are supposed merely to express. The contingency interpretation denies that any such large cause was necessary: once the king's attempted flight, the outbreak of war, and the harvest failures arrived at the wrong moments, the Revolution was pushed along paths no structural or ideological story could have predicted. All three camps accept roughly the same sequence of events, and each has a standing way of redescribing the others' favored facts as real but incidental. The Terror is where the disagreement concentrates most sharply: each camp must say why the Revolution devoured so many of its own partisans, and each regards the others' answers as either ad hoc or circular.

My question is not which interpretation is right, but what kind of disagreement this is. Is it partly verbal, with the three camps operating with different definitions of revolution, class, or explanation and therefore never quite engaging one another? Is there a hidden counterfactual question doing the real work — for example, what the Revolution would have become without the war or the failed harvests — that the surviving evidence cannot settle? Do the sides differ about what an explanation of a singular historical event must look like, one side demanding large structural or ideological causes and another treating narrative and contingency as explanatory on their own? Or is the debate, at bottom, a disagreement about the meaning of the Revolution for the present, in which each camp is defending a normative picture of politics through historical argument? My working hypothesis is that several of these diagnoses operate at once, but I have not found a clean way to separate them. I want a diagnosis of the structure of the impasse, grounded in the characteristic commitments of each camp, and an account of what would count as progress given that diagnosis — a distinction, a question that could in principle be answered, a change in the terms of debate — or, if you conclude that the impasse is permanent, an argument for why that should be expected.
