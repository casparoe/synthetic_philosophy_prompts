I have a friend who read a bunch of epistocracy stuff last summer and now every argument about democracy arrives at the same line. The other night it was: "You wouldn't let a random person take out your appendix, so why would you let a random person vote on trade policy?" His case is that we already accept competence requirements all over the place—surgeons, pilots, electricians, even hairdressers where we live—but when it comes to voting, which affects way more people and can do way more damage, we insist on zero requirements. So he wants a competency test for the franchise, or at least weighted voting where people who know more get more say, and he thinks this is just the ordinary logic of licensing carried over to politics.

When I pushed back that a patient can choose their surgeon and can opt out of surgery entirely, while a citizen cant opt out of the state's decisions, he said that only makes the case stronger: since the stakes are universal and nonconsensual, the bar should be higher, not lower. That move felt off to me, but I couldn't say exactly why, and I'm aware some of my discomfort might just be democratic nostalgia.

So before I go back to argue with him, I want to know how far the surgery analogy actually holds. Lay out the mapping explicitly—a table is fine—surgeon vs. voter, patient vs. the governed, medical boards vs. whatever would oversee voter competence, malpractice vs. bad policy. Then say where the mapping comes apart, and which disanalogies matter for his conclusion and which are just window dressing. In particular, address the consent and exit point, the difference between technical expertise and contested values, and the fact that elections pick representatives rather than policies. Give me a verdict: does the licensing argument for epistocracy survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or does it depend on quietly importing features of medicine that politics doesn't have? And if there's a better analogy for the epistocrat's point, give that too. Keep the whole response under 1,200 words.
