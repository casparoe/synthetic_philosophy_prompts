I've spent years reading rationalist and effective-altruism blogs and forums, so I already know how the open-borders case is usually run and where its internal disagreements lie: place premiums and the gains from moving workers from low-productivity to high-productivity places, keyhole solutions that move people rather than institutions, the standing cautions about trusting aggregate GDP figures that have not been checked against credible identification, and the running dispute over whether migration or some other intervention does more good per marginal dollar. Next month I am presenting to an audience that is not that crowd, and I do not need another steelman of the open-borders side.

I'm presenting a position paper to a joint workshop at a center-left policy institute. The room will be mostly comparative welfare-state scholars whose work centers on universalism, decommodification, and social citizenship, plus empirical labour economists who work on the labour-market and fiscal effects of immigration, two people from the institute's political-strategy team who think in terms of the median voter and the next three election cycles, and one demographer who works on pension sustainability and population ageing. I am defending the following thesis:

A large, sustained increase in low-skilled immigration does not have to undermine a generous welfare state, provided the welfare state is reorganized along contributory rather than residence-based lines. The apparent fiscal and political conflict between immigration and the welfare state is largely an artefact of universal, noncontributory entitlements. If core old-age, health, and unemployment programs are built around contribution records, and if new arrivals phase into noncontributory assistance gradually or through an explicit transition fund, a developed welfare state can absorb far more low-skilled migration while becoming more solvent and more politically durable, including for the native poor.

The paper's argument sketch, which the objections can target, runs like this:

1. The largest gains from low-skilled migration come from moving individual workers from low-productivity to high-productivity locations, and these gains are real and large at the individual level even when aggregate estimates are disputed.
2. The fiscal and political objections are strongest precisely for low-skilled migration into welfare states that award noncontributory benefits on the basis of residency alone.
3. Moving the core programs to contribution-based entitlement restores the link between what workers pay in and what they receive, lowering the fiscal cost of each additional migrant and weakening the incentive to migrate for benefits.
4. A gradual or partial phase-in of noncontributory support for new arrivals preserves most labour-market gains while limiting short-run fiscal exposure.
5. Natives are mostly unaffected in their own entitlements, because most already have substantial contribution records; the reform primarily changes the rules for new entrants.
6. Contribution-based programs are more credible to native voters than open-ended eligibility, so this arrangement is more politically sustainable than the current standoff between pro-migration economic arguments and anti-migration welfare-state concerns.
