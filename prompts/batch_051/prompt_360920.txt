I am a law student preparing a paper on the rule of law, and I have recently become preoccupied with the fact that legal systems are full of statutes nobody enforces — laws that seem dead until some official decides to revive them for a specific case. I read Shakespeare's Measure for Measure for the first time while thinking about this, and it struck me that the play is almost a case study in the problem of desuetude: the Duke has let Vienna's strict sexual offenses go unpunished for years, and the supposedly upright Angelo revives one of those dormant laws to sentence Claudio to death for getting his betrothed pregnant. I want to know whether the play genuinely illuminates the legal-philosophical issue, or whether it is a misleading analogy dressed up in dramatic conveniences.

Could you give me a careful reading of Measure for Measure through the lens of desuetude and the rule of law? In particular, does the play suggest that a long-unenforced law loses its binding force, or that such a law remains valid and may legitimately be revived in the name of consistency and deterrence? Is Angelo objectionable because he ignores desuetude, or because he enforces selectively, hypocritically, and for corrupt motives — and can the play itself separate those charges? I would also like you to identify where the play captures something the theoretical literature on desuetude tends to underplay: for instance, the reliance of ordinary citizens on settled practice, the way selective revival of a dead law is an exercise of power, or the element of pretense in maintaining that the law has always been in force. Finally, I would like you to say where the play resists the frame: its comic ending resolves everything through the Duke's disguises, substitutions, and pardons rather than through any legal principle, and I want the honest part of the reading to acknowledge that. I am less interested in a plot summary than in a reading that takes both the dramatic logic and the philosophy seriously, and that is willing to conclude that Shakespeare complicates the doctrine instead of merely illustrating it.
