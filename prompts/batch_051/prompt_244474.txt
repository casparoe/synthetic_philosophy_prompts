I chair the drafting subgroup of my university system’s standing committee on human-subjects research, and we are revising the statement of ethical principles that governs our institutional review boards. Our current draft opens by declaring that research participants have inherent, inalienable natural rights to dignity, autonomy, and protection from harm. One faction among the IRB chairs wants to keep that language because they believe it gives the document moral force that goes beyond any particular regulation. Another faction, led by a member with legal training, has circulated a short excerpt from Jeremy Bentham’s Anarchical Fallacies. In it Bentham argues that talk of natural rights in political declarations is a way of dressing up preferences as unchallengeable law: a right that exists before law can be asserted by anyone for anything, and adding that it is imprescriptible or inalienable only makes the claim harder to answer while doing no practical work. On that basis she wants the revised statement to avoid any appeal to rights not explicitly created and limited by the protocol, the consent process, or the common rule. The rest of us are genuinely stuck, because we find the natural-rights language both rhetorically important and philosophically suspicious. Draft a decision memo from our subgroup to the full committee recommending whether the revised statement should ground its protections in a declaration of natural rights or in enumerated, institutionally enforceable rights. Treat the strongest version of Bentham’s objection seriously rather than as mere cynicism, engage honestly with the case for retaining natural-rights language, and end with concrete preamble wording we could actually adopt.
