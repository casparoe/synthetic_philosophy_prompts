I work as a journalist covering science and ideas, and I'm researching a piece about why sophisticated, well-informed people so often disagree. A source recommended Aumann's agreement theorem, which I knew by name, and sent me a popular explainer that summarizes the result this way: "Aumann proved that two fully rational people cannot disagree once each knows the other's probability for a claim. Suppose Alice announces that she puts the chance of recession at thirty percent while Bob says sixty percent; each knows the other's estimate was formed by Bayesian reasoning on private evidence, so each is rationally compelled to revise in light of what the other thinks, and as they continue to exchange their updated estimates, the gap must shrink until their probabilities coincide. This is why economists treat persistent disagreement among well-informed people as evidence that at least one of them is reasoning badly."

I have a nagging sense that the actual theorem requires something about a common prior, and that the result is a condition on beliefs at a single moment rather than a story about convergence through repeated conversation, but I can't tell how much this matters. Could you audit this summary for me, claim by claim? I'd like to know which parts are faithful to the formal result, which parts are defensible simplifications, which are just wrong, and what a philosopher of probability or formal epistemologist would and would not conclude from the theorem about real-world expert disagreement.
