In the surprise exam paradox, the puzzle is usually introduced with one standard announcement: "There will be an exam next week, and you will not know which day it will be until the exam is handed out." I first met it in an epistemology seminar, where the instructor wrote that sentence on the board and then used it interchangeably with the claim that the teacher both tells the truth and takes the class by surprise. I want to know what that sentence actually asserts before the paradox is allowed to do any work. Separate the precise epistemic claims hiding in it—for instance, the difference between saying that no student will know the date, that no student can know the date, and that the date is not deducible from the announcement itself. For each reading, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or false (perhaps self-undermining). Then say which reading is actually doing the work when the students argue backwards from Friday, and whether any single precisification makes the announcement both guarantee the exam and generate a genuine contradiction rather than a merely unexpected outcome. I would rather have a menu of precisifications with verdicts than one preferred reading or a recap of standard solutions. Finally, say where your own verdict is least stable: which precisification of "know"—or of "the announcement is common knowledge"—or which treatment of the induction step would most plausibly change your assessment, and where the strongest objection to that assessment lies.
