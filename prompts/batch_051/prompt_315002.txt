Last evening I had a long and ultimately inconclusive argument with a close friend, and I would be grateful for a careful adjudication of it. She is a serious birdwatcher, and her central claim was that learning to identify birds has changed the character of her visual experience itself. When she began birding, she said, a distant warbler was merely a small, nondescript grayish shape; now the same bird simply looks to be a bird with a pale eye-ring, wing bars, and a distinctive posture. The field marks, she maintained, are present in the seeing, not inferred after the fact. I resisted this. I suggested that what has changed is where she directs her attention, what she remembers, and how quickly she interprets the incoming pattern; the light reaching her eyes is the same, and her knowledge is doing its work after perception rather than inside it. She replied that this appeal to an 'after' is carrying too much weight, and that the familiar example of two lines with arrowheads, which continue to look unequal even after one has measured them, shows only that one particular illusion resists belief, not that perception in general is insulated from what we know. She also pressed the case of radiologists and chess players, who report that years of training have changed how a scan or a board appears to them; she thought it would be special pleading to reclassify all such reports as changes in interpretation alone. I answered that the reports cannot themselves settle the question, because a change in attention, memory, or judgment would produce the same verbal reports and the same improved performance, so one needs some way of disentangling experience itself from everything downstream of it.

We could not agree on how to draw the line among changes in attention, changes in judgment, and changes in the visual experience itself. I would like a careful treatment of who is right, or at least of how the dispute ought to be framed. In particular: what would it take for a belief, expectation, or piece of trained expertise to count as changing perception itself, rather than merely changing what one does with a perception? Are there cases that decide the question in either direction? And is the distinction between 'perception' and 'its interpretation' stable enough to do the work I was asking of it?
