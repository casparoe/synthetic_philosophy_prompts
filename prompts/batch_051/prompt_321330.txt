Take the standard birthday problem: n people are chosen independently with birthdays distributed uniformly over 365 days, and we ask whether some pair shares an exact birthday; at n = 23 the chance is about one half. Now vary the setup to model how coincidence anecdotes actually arise. A person meets n people and regards herself as having witnessed a striking coincidence if any pair matches in any one of the following categories: (a) same exact calendar date, (b) same calendar month, (c) same weekday of birth, or (d) birthdays no more than three days apart, where distance is measured around the circular year and zero distance counts. For each category separately, give the approximate smallest n at which the probability of at least one matching pair exceeds one half, and state and justify any approximations you use. Then show that, once a match in any one of the four categories is allowed to count, a group far smaller than 23 is already more likely than not to yield some apparent coincidence, and support this with the strongest simple bound you can justify. Does the classic exact-date analysis mislead when the category is effectively chosen after looking at the data? Finally, state the epistemological rule this variation illustrates about when an anecdotal coincidence should be treated as evidence of something more than chance.
