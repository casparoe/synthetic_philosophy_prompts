Lately I've been struck by the fact that the old saying "saying it doesn't make it so" feels obviously true in some cases and obviously false in others. A friend used it recently to dismiss an apology I'd made: the words were "just words," so the apology itself didn't count. But in that same week I watched a colleague end his employment by saying "I resign"; a registrar change two people's legal status by saying "I now pronounce you married"; and a referee turn a ball into a goal with a shout. So the slogan can't be a flat assertion that words never change things. I'd like you to separate out the distinct, precise claims that "saying it doesn't make it so" could be making—which are trivial, which are substantive and defensible, which are false—and then say which of those readings my friend was actually relying on, and whether it supports his conclusion that my apology didn't count. I'm especially interested in what has to be true beyond the words themselves: why does a resignation said in anger, or a marriage pronounced by a random wedding guest, do nothing, while the same words in the right setting change what everyone is entitled to expect? A menu of readings would be more useful to me than a single interpretation.
