I'm taking a class on Roman thought and we've been reading Cicero's On Old Age — well, reading it in translation and mostly skimming the parts where Cato talks to Scipio and Laelius, if I'm being honest. I came away with a rough sense that Cicero wants to answer the standard complaints people make about getting old, but I wasn't sure I'd understood the actual argument. My roommate, who took a similar class last year, explained it to me like this:

"So Cicero's whole point is that old age isn't just harmless, it's actually the best stage of life. People give four reasons why old age sucks — it removes you from active affairs, it weakens the body, it destroys your ability to enjoy pleasure, and it brings you close to death — and Cicero shows that every single one of them is false. The old are actually better at politics because they govern through judgment and authority rather than physical strength. Bodily weakness doesn't matter because virtue is the only real good. Losing bodily pleasures is even a benefit, since pleasure is basically a kind of slavery and young people are all addicted to it. And being near death is a relief, because nature has set a fixed limit on life and a wise person wouldn't want to hang around too long anyway. So if an old person is miserable, that just proves they're not virtuous enough."

Something about this feels off — it makes Cicero sound weirdly smug, like he's claiming old age is a straight upgrade and anyone who complains is just a bad person.
