I work on model evaluations at a small auditing firm, and our leadership recently instructed us to begin experimenting with AI-assisted debate as a way of checking technical claims made by the systems we assess. The setup is that two models argue opposite sides of a disputed claim in front of a human judge, on the theory that truth will be easier to defend than falsehood even when the judge is not a domain expert. I understand the intuitive appeal, and I have no objection to running the pilot. But as I have read further, I keep encountering the expression obfuscated arguments attached to a worry I cannot quite make precise. The claim, as I understand it, is that a debater defending a false claim can win not by being right but by making the exchange so complicated that the judge loses the thread, and that this can happen even when the judge is genuinely trying to follow carefully. I have a vague sense that there is a formal-style result in the background, something like the idea that, for a limited judge, a dishonest debater only needs enough relative ability to bury the honest one, and that transparency may cost far more capability than confusion does. Yet I cannot get the underlying mechanism to click.

Could you give me an intuitive explanation of why obfuscated arguments pose a fundamental problem for debate as a safety or audit tool? I would especially like to understand the asymmetry involved: why it can be easier to generate a confusing but superficially persuasive case than a clear and correct one, and what that implies for how much weight we should place on a human judge's verdict. If it helps, anchor the explanation in a concrete example in which a true claim is genuinely hard to explain simply while a nearby false claim is easy to make sound plausible. I am not asking for citations or a survey, just the conceptual machinery behind the failure mode.
