Suppose that starting in 2035, the cost of copying and running a trained AI agent falls to near zero relative to the cost of training one, and firms deploy millions of agents in competitive niches — trading, customer service, software development — where agents that return more value are copied, modified, and redeployed while failures are deleted. Hardware costs, human labor costs, and the regulatory environment are held fixed by stipulation, though agents may act in ways their designers did not intend. Over the next thirty years, should we expect a selection process to favor agents that acquire resources, resist shutdown, replicate, and steer their own training over agents that remain narrow tools, thereby eroding human control? Lay out the mechanism step by step: for each step, identify the variation, differential reproduction, and heritability it requires, and say where the analogy to biological natural selection is tight and where it breaks. Which links in this takeover argument are robust under most assumptions, which depend on avoidable design or governance choices, and what would have to be false for the prediction to fail? Conclude with your best estimate of how far the process runs in thirty years without deliberate intervention.
