I work on the safety team of a company that is developing a general-purpose AI assistant. The system is expected to become superhumanly persuasive — far better than any person at reading a particular listener and choosing the phrasing, example, or emotional register that will move them — and, eventually, to be far more knowledgeable than the person it advises. The company has just adopted an internal influence policy for the assistant, and I keep finding myself unable to state exactly what its authors are assuming about the ethics of persuasion. Under the policy, the assistant may be directive and even heavy-handed when the conversation concerns factual or uncontroversially practical matters: it may tell a user flatly to stop taking a drug that interacts with their other medication, may use social proof and vivid worst-case imagery to push someone toward a diagnostic test, and may draft an exercise plan wrapped in a motivational message that deliberately recruits shame and deadline anxiety. The same policy allows fear of a stroke to be used to move someone toward vaccination, but explicitly forbids using a user's fear of disappointing their mother to soften the user's refusal to reconcile with a sibling. The difference, the policy says, is that the second influence concerns what it calls an axiological commitment — religious or political identity, whether to have children, how to weigh loyalty against honesty — and in that region the assistant is barred from everything classified as non-rational persuasion: framing effects, flattery, appeals to fear or group identity, invoking loss aversion, or tailoring to a known cognitive bias. There it may offer only considerations explicit enough that the user could, after reflection, endorse them as reasons.

What I want to understand is what this practice presupposes. On its face it assumes a fairly sharp divide between factual or prudential questions and questions of value or identity, and it seems to assume that persuasion is legitimate exactly when it addresses a person's capacity to respond to reasons and illegitimate when it bypasses that capacity — as if the same emotional pressure that is harmless in one domain becomes disrespectful in the other. It also seems to assume that people have some desires that are more truly theirs, the ones they would reflectively endorse, which an assistant may shore up but not replace from outside. Reconstruct the implicit theory of autonomy, manipulation, and respect for persons that the policy needs in order to be coherent. Distinguish what the policy strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with, and say where it is philosophically underdetermined, such that several rival theories would license it. Then tell me what would have to change if the lab instead accepted a purely epistemic account of manipulation, on which influence is wrong only when it causes or exploits false beliefs, or a purely welfarist account, on which influence is permissible whenever the expected outcome leaves the person better off. Finish with a single line beginning "In sum:" followed by a one-sentence statement of the policy's deepest unstated commitment.
