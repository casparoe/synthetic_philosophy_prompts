I'm a longtime reader of rationalist blogs and forums, and I've been asked to write up my considered view on the intelligence explosion and takeoff speed for a small group blog. I want a consistency check on the package I've converged on, not another survey of the debate.

Here is the package:

1. Continuity at the macro level. I expect no clean threshold where artificial general intelligence arrives. Publicly visible aggregate measures—benchmark trends, the share of economically relevant tasks that can be automated, and eventually GDP-relevant output—will keep improving along an accelerating but smooth trend, not a step function.

2. The pace is set by serial wall-clock constraints. The binding bottleneck on speed is not raw algorithmic insight or compute, but the time required for training runs, hardware fabrication and deployment, organizational integration, procurement and regulatory lag, and real-world feedback loops. These set a floor under how quickly the transition can physically happen.

3. A slow economic explosion still occurs. After our systems become useful contributors to AI research and to production, the global economy's doubling time will shrink from decades to on the order of a year or less, within roughly a decade. So I believe in an explosion in the growth-rate sense, but without any compute-overhang-style overnight jump.

4. Recursive self-improvement is overrated. I expect the main driver of capability gain to be larger and better training runs plus broad deployment across the economy; a single system introspecting on and rewriting its own source code strikes me as a minor mechanism at best.

5. Moloch wins at the margin. Labs, firms, and states will deploy capabilities as soon as they can be made to work, which means the frontier will be in use before it is understood; safety, interpretability, and alignment will be lagging indicators throughout the transition.

6. There will be a long, legible fuse. Because the serial constraints in (2) are themselves measurable in advance, I expect years of visible acceleration before anything truly transformative, enough that even slow-moving actors could in principle see it coming and shift resources.

Does this hang together? I'm too close to it to tell whether I've reconciled a soft-takeoff bottleneck story with an economic explosion and race-to-deploy dynamics, or whether I've just written down six individually plausible theses that quietly cancel. Please audit the set rather than lecturing me on the literature: go through the pair-by-pair matrix, separate the genuine tensions from the merely apparent ones, rank the genuine tensions by severity, and for each say what would have to give and what the cheapest revision would cost. Then tell me which single revision would do the most to make the package coherent.

For calibration: for each tension you flag, say how confident you are that it is genuine, and at the end single out the point in your audit you are least sure about.
