I keep a list of verdicts about originals, copies, and forgeries, and I want to know whether any one principle captures them. (1) I would value an authenticated Vermeer enormously, and a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of it that no expert could distinguish almost not at all. (2) I would happily hang a good, honest reproduction on my wall and feel I lose nothing aesthetically. (3) A fake that passed as a Vermeer for years, once exposed: I thought its collapse in value was right — it was never a Vermeer — yet it kept, and even gained, value as an artifact of its own history. (4) A painting authentically by a master but ninety percent repainted after a fire makes me uneasy: I still call it the master's work, but I cannot say why with confidence. (5) A work executed mostly by the master's assistants under the master's direction I value less than an autograph work but far more than a forgery. (6) For a digital image, where I know which file the artist first saved but every copy is bit-for-bit identical, I find I do not care which one I own; the distinction seems idle. My working hunch is that an original's special value is the artist's achievement rather than anything perceptible, but that sits badly with (2) and (6). Is there a coherent principle behind these verdicts, which of my verdicts does it force me to revise, and what would it say about a perfect copy of a painting produced by a machine in full public view, with no intention to deceive?
