I work in information security—authentication, integrity, provenance, trusted hardware—so I will ask in those terms. Some philosophers writing about deepfakes treat recordings and photographs as an epistemic backstop for testimony: when witnesses or officials contradict one another, we can in principle check the tape, and the availability of that fallback is part of what stabilizes the surrounding economy of testimony. The worry is that deepfakes undermine this backstop, since a recording can no longer be presumed authentic in the absence of an independently verified chain of custody. Restate this argument in the vocabulary of my field as faithfully as you can—root of trust, non-repudiation, signing and verification, threat model, trust on first use, key continuity, replay and replacement attacks, provenance logs—and then give me a residue report: which parts translate cleanly, which parts you had to distort to fit, and which parts have no counterpart at all. I especially want to know whether the philosophical problem is just an authentication and provenance problem that better cryptography, watermarking, and attestation will eventually dissolve, or whether something remains even with perfect provenance. If something remains, tell me whether that residue reflects a blind spot in the security vocabulary or a genuine feature of the philosophers' worry that the vocabulary cannot express.
