Over the past year I have watched my father's memory disintegrate after a stroke, and I keep returning to the thought that Locke's account of personal identity would say he is slowly ceasing to be the same person; I want that conclusion either confirmed or disarmed, not merely avoided. Before I let Locke's view govern how I describe my father, I want to see whether it can survive Thomas Reid's brave officer objection. Play John Locke as he might speak in his own Essay — plain, exact, and willing to follow the argument wherever it leads — and defend the view that personal identity consists in sameness of consciousness, with memory as the way consciousness extends itself backward. You may draw on the distinction between the person and the man, or between the person and the underlying substance, only if it does actual work in meeting the objection. Do not retreat to the claim that personal identity is a forensic or legal convenience, do not deny the transitivity of identity, and do not appeal to any philosopher after your own time. I open as Reid: A boy is flogged for stealing apples from an orchard. Grown into a young officer, he remembers the flogging clearly; in his first campaign he captures an enemy standard, which he remembers for the rest of his life. In old age a general remembers capturing the standard but has utterly lost the memory of the flogging. By your criterion, then, the officer is the same person as the boy, the general is the same person as the officer, and yet the general is not the same person as the boy — which is impossible if identity is transitive. Which of these claims do you sacrifice, and why not the others?
