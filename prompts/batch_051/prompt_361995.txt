I'm preparing a lecture on whether wisdom can be taught, and the real difficulty seems to be upstream: the target of instruction is never specified. So, rather than argue for or against any "wisdom is X" account, I want a specification of what an adequate theory of wisdom must do before the teaching question can even be posed. Provide the desiderata: (1) classification cases it must get right, with the reason each is decisive — for example the domain expert with poor life judgment, the morally serious person whose advice is consistently bad, the young person whose counsel is better than her elders', the wise person who fails in a genuinely novel case, and the manipulator who says exactly what a wise person would say; (2) distinctions it must not collapse — wisdom versus knowledge, intelligence, skill, moral goodness, well-being, luck, and age; (3) explanatory jobs it must perform — why wisdom is action-guiding in novel circumstances, why it supports good advice even when the advisor cannot fully articulate the grounds, why it resists codification into rules while still being partly teachable, and why we hold it up as a goal of education and a life rather than merely a useful capacity; and (4) boundary conditions it must not contradict, such as experience producing entrenchment as easily as insight. For each desideratum, say whether it is negotiable or non-negotiable given the purpose of specifying a teachable target. Then identify which single requirement eliminates the most standard accounts (wisdom as a body of knowledge, as practical skill, as a character virtue), and say whether you think the full list is jointly satisfiable or contains a tension. Keep the answer under 900 words; a table is acceptable.
