I have been thinking through the Rietdijk–Putnam argument, which is often taken to show that special relativity forces a block-universe view of time on us: all events, past, present and future, are equally real, and the passage of time is not an objective feature of the world. I want to determine whether the argument, as I reconstruct it, actually goes through. Let S be the event of my composing this sentence now, and let F be an arbitrary future event on my own worldline, say the event of my finishing this paragraph. Working in Minkowski spacetime, choose an event E that is spacelike separated from both S and F: for example, take E to lie halfway in coordinate time between S and F and far enough away spatially that both separations are spacelike. It follows from the Lorentz transformations that E is simultaneous with S in one inertial frame and simultaneous with F in a different inertial frame.

The argument then runs as follows:

(1) S — the event of my writing this now — is real now.

(2) If two events are simultaneous in some inertial frame, then those two events are equally real; no frame is privileged, so a judgement of simultaneity made in any frame is as good as one made in my rest frame.

(3) By the construction above, E is simultaneous with S in one inertial frame, so E is equally real with S (from 2); and because S is real now (from 1), E is real now.

(4) By the construction above, E is simultaneous with F in another inertial frame, so F is equally real with E (from 2); and because 'equally real' is transitive, E's being real now carries over so that F is real now.

(5) Since F was an arbitrary future event, every future event on my worldline is already real now; and what goes for my worldline goes for any other, so future events generally are already real. The future is therefore no less real than the present — which is just the block-universe conclusion.

I suspect steps (3) and (4) are where the trick hides — something about 'real' quietly changing its meaning between frames — but I cannot say exactly what goes wrong, and part of me suspects the argument is valid and that I am merely resisting its conclusion.

Is this a valid argument? If it is valid, say why steps (3) and (4) are not as suspect as they appear and why the conclusion follows despite its strangeness. If it is invalid, identify exactly which step fails, explain whether the failure is an equivocation on 'real', a failure of transitivity in the relation being used, or something else, and give the corrected version of the step. In either case, say whether the argument can be repaired so as to put genuine pressure on presentism or a growing-block view — or whether it only threatens views already committed to a frame-independent notion of existence. Please write your answer in British English.
