Here is the practice as I understand it. A single hospital can arrive at the same legal word — "dead" — in two different ways, and the difference becomes especially visible when the person is an organ donor.

The first way is what I have seen called brain death. A patient has suffered a catastrophic brain injury, and after a set of tests a doctor determines that the brain has irreversibly stopped working altogether. The person is then declared dead even though the heart is still beating (kept going by a ventilator) and the body is still warm. Organs can then be taken from that person.

The second way is what I have seen called cardiac death, often in the context of donation after the heart has stopped. A patient is not brain-dead but is on life support, and the family and doctors have agreed to withdraw that support. The ventilator is removed, the heart stops, and after a short mandatory observation period — a few minutes with no heartbeat — the person is declared dead and organs can be taken. In that second case, as far as I can tell, nobody tests the brain at the moment of declaration; the judgment rests on the heart having stopped and stayed stopped.

What bothers me is that the same institution seems to use different ideas of what death consists in, depending on which donation route a patient is on. If what matters is that the brain has irreversibly stopped, then the few-minutes-of-no-heartbeat rule looks too quick and untested, because a stopped heart does not by itself tell you at the bedside whether the brain has irreversibly stopped in the way the first route demands. If what matters instead is that the heart and circulation have stopped, then the first route looks strange, because the heart may still be beating when the person is declared dead and when organs are removed. And underneath both there seems to be a further question the waiting period only partly settles: whether the point is that the body has actually stopped and will not start again on its own, or that it could not be started again no matter what anyone did. Those two sound alike, but they are not the same, and I am not sure which one the practice needs.

So my question is: what understanding of death does this whole practice — both routes taken together — actually rest on?
