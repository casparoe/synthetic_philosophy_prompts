I have been weighing whether to invest a substantial amount in a business a former colleague is starting. I have spent several weeks with the documents, the financials, and the market data, and on that basis I would put the chance that the venture is sound at around one in five. The complication is that I have agreed to spend time with the founding team next month, and I know from repeated experience that I revise sharply upward in settings like that: the demo, the team's confidence, and the sheer likability of the people all move me more than they should. A few years ago a similar visit left me assigning something close to 90 percent to a deal that later collapsed, even though the underlying paperwork had not changed. So I can predict, with real confidence, that after this visit I will think the odds are good.

My question is what to do with that prediction now. On one hand, it seems odd to think: "I expect that in a month I will believe this is a good deal, and I also believe now that it probably is not." If I already know I am going to hold that belief, on the basis of information I have not yet encountered, shouldn't I move some of the way toward it now? On the other hand, I have good reason to think that the person I become in that environment is not a reliable evaluator; if anything, that person's judgment should be discounted, and the sensible move might be to precommit against whatever I think after the visit. My instinct is that the right answer must distinguish between cases where my future self will know something I don't and cases where my future self will merely be biased, but I am not sure that line can be drawn from the inside, since the future self always seems like the one with more information. What should I actually do, and is there a principle that determines how much weight my present self should give to the anticipated beliefs of my future self?
