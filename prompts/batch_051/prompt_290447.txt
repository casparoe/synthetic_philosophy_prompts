I'm in a philosophy of biology reading group and we've been stuck on the species problem for three weeks now. The thing is, the empirical examples don't settle anything: ring species, asexual lineages, hybridizing oaks, chronospecies in the fossil record. Every time someone brings up a case, the same people draw different conclusions from it. We keep coming back to this sentence from Darwin's On the Origin of Species, Chapter 2: "No one definition has as yet satisfied all naturalists; yet every naturalist knows vaguely what he means when he speaks of a species." It's striking that the sentence still works, but I can't tell whether that's because the problem is deep or because people keep using the word for different jobs.

My working theory is that the debate has stalled because the sides aren't actually answering the same question: some are asking what species really are out there in nature, others are asking what the most useful unit is for conservation, taxonomy, or communication. But maybe that's too quick. Diagnose the structure of this disagreement. Is it verbal, substantive, about standards of evidence, or about which cases count as data? Use what the main species concepts actually say—biological, phylogenetic, ecological, and maybe the older morphological view Darwin was responding to—but don't give me a survey; give me a diagnosis of why the dispute is stuck and what, given that diagnosis, would actually count as progress.

I want exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction, no conclusion, and no bullet-point hedging. Each point should identify one layer of the impasse and say what would move it, or why nothing would.
