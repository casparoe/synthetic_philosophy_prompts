I'm a novelist doing research for a book built around a forgery, and I've gotten stuck on a question about how anyone manages to know anything about the distant past, because my plot depends on documents with murky origins and I want to get the epistemology right rather than just lean on the standard be-skeptical line. So I keep coming back to the Hitler diaries affair in 1983 as a test case: the magazine Stern announced it had found Hitler's private diaries, supposedly salvaged from a transport plane that crashed near Dresden in the last days of the war; the magazine had paid an enormous sum and put its prestige behind the scoop; a respected historian, Hugh Trevor-Roper, initially vouched for them; and then within weeks, forensic work on the paper and ink, along with objections from other historians, showed the whole thing was a forgery by a dealer named Konrad Kujau.

I'd like you to analyse this episode as a case study in source criticism, understood as the tradition running from Herodotus's open canvassing of conflicting reports and Thucydides's preference for eyewitnesses and suspicion of the fabulous, down to modern external and internal criticism. In particular: what should a source critic have demanded about provenance, chain of custody, physical authenticity, and internal consistency before treating these diaries as Hitler's, and which of those demands does the affair show that even expert gatekeepers can skip when they're excited? Then flip the lens: what does this case reveal about source criticism itself—about it's dependence on institutional incentives, on critics not already wanting a source to be real, and about where the method goes silent? I want a disciplined mapping that runs both ways, source criticism onto the affair and the affair back onto source criticism, without turning into a general lecture on skepticism.
