I've been turning over the American practice of the "designated survivor." During the State of the Union, one eligible member of the cabinet is quietly taken to a secure, undisclosed location while the President, the Vice President, the Speaker, the President Pro Tempore, and nearly the whole cabinet sit together in the House chamber. The idea is that if the Capitol were destroyed there would still be a President — no gap, full legal authority, the works — and the next morning the country would just carry on. What bothers me is how this sits next to the story we normally tell about where authority comes from. We say a president gets the right to rule from the voters, or at least from winning an election under rules the voters accept. The designated survivor never got a single vote for that job. They were appointed to run, I don't know, the Department of Agriculture or whatever, and on a normal day they'd be way down the list. We learn afterward who it was, and nobody seems especially bothered that this person might have woken up as president without any popular say. So the practice seems to presuppose that legitimate authority doesn't actually come from elections or ongoing consent, but from something else — a chain of offices, maybe, or the sheer fact that the state must not stop for five minuts. What theory of political authority and continuity does this practice actually presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, then separate what the practice strictly needs from what it merely fits with. Could someone with a thoroughgoing consent-based, bottom-up view of legitimacy still run the designated-survivor program, or does it quietly rule that view out? And tell me what the practice would have to change into if we replaced its implicit theory — emergency elections, a pool of pre-elected successors, letting the offices sit empty for a few days, whatever follows. Then give me a one-sentence summary as the last line, beginning with "Summary:".
