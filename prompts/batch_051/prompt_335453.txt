I am taking a course on free will this term, and one week was devoted to Dennett's discussion of the digger wasp (Sphex). After the seminar, a classmate offered the following summary of what he took the argument to be. I'll reproduce it here because I suspect it is partly right and partly a distortion, and I would like a careful audit.

He said: "Dennett's point is that free will just is the opposite of sphexishness. The wasp is a little deterministic machine: drag the cricket to the burrow, check the burrow, drag it in; if you move the cricket while it is checking, it resets and repeats the whole routine without ever noticing. Because its behavior is rigidly fixed and unresponsive to the changed situation, the wasp isn't free. Human beings are free to the extent that we can notice novelty, stop, reconsider, and select a different course of action. So the real threat to free will isn't determinism; it's rigidity. In fact, any sufficiently flexible system — a well-designed robot, say — would count as free on this view. The standard objection is that this changes the subject: calling flexibility 'free will' says nothing about whether we could have done otherwise in exactly the same circumstances, which is what the free will problem was supposed to be about."

Please go through this summary claim by claim. Among the things I'd like separated: (1) does Dennett actually define free will as the absence of sphexishness, or is the wasp serving a narrower purpose in the argument? (2) Would he accept the inference from sufficient flexibility to free will, or does his account require something more than behavioral flexibility? (3) Does he grant the "changes the subject" charge, and how does he handle the demand that free action require the ability to do otherwise in the exact same circumstances? I am not asking for a reading list, just whether the summary is fair and where I should be cautious.
