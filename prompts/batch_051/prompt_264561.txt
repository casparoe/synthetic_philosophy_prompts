I teach a seminar on documentary photography, and each year the same question overtakes the room. I have become convinced by the transparency account of photography: the view that photographs are transparent in a fairly literal sense, so that when we look at a photograph we see the thing photographed, much as we see through a telescope, a periscope, or a closed-circuit television feed. The argument that moves me is a continuity argument. Light reflected from a scene is gathered by glass, recorded on a sensitive surface or sensor, and later displayed to the eye; if a mirror lets me see my own face, a periscope lets me see over a wall, and a live CCTV feed lets me see the lobby of a bank, then drawing a line at still photography looks arbitrary. The special epistemic authority we grant photographs — in courtrooms, in newsrooms, in mourning — seems to me to rest on exactly this: a photograph is not merely a picture of an event but a surviving trace that puts us in a kind of visual contact with it.

And yet, whenever I hold a photograph of my grandmother, who died before I was born, I cannot convince myself that I am seeing her. What I am aware of is a small, flat, carefully printed sheet of paper; she is absent. She is not present to me, not located in my spatial environment, not available to my gaze in the way even a distant mountain seen through a telescope is. The theory tells me this hesitation is a confusion: seeing is a matter of being linked to an object by an information-bearing causal channel, not of proximity or simultaneity, and my grandmother's pastness should be no more of an obstacle than a galaxy's distance. But the intuition does not dissolve, and I do not think it is mere sentimentality. It feels as though it tracks something the transparency account leaves out — something about presence, or about the difference between being in a perceptual situation with a particular and inspecting a durable representation of one. The manipulation objection sharpens this: if a photograph has been digitally altered, I can be in the same visual state while the causal chain to the scene is broken, and the transparency account seems to make that difference invisible from the inside, which worries me.

I am not asking which side wins; I am asking for a negotiated verdict. Give me the strongest version of the transparency case and the strongest version of the intuition that we do not see through photographs, or at least not in the full sense. Then say what it would take for the intuition to be the thing that goes — what kind of error would it have to be shown to be — and what it would take for the theory to be amended or abandoned. Finally, tell me whether there is a reading of 'seeing' on which both claims survive, and what such a reading would have to sacrifice. Approach this as a professional philosopher of aesthetics and perception. Think carefully; before writing your final answer, work through the considerations on each side and decide what would justify each move.
