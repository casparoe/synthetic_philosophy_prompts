i'm pulling together a reading group on the socialist calculation debate and we've spent the last couple sessions on the Austrian side of it, Mises and Hayek against Oskar Lange and the market socialists. i've been collecting the anti-Lange arguments that keep coming up, and i think i've got the usual ones down, but i keep geting told there's at least one more distinct argument and i can't tell if that's real or just people being vague. here's my list so far, in my own words:

one, the calculation argument: without private ownership of the means of production there's no actual market for capital goods, and without that there are no prices for the means of production, so planners can't do the comparison that picks the least-cost way of producing anything. the prices Lange's central planning board announces by trial and error are being minted out of nothing rather than emerging from real bids and offers.

two, the knowledge argument: the information you'd need to run an economy never exists all in one place. it's local and dispersed and often tacit, like a shift manager noticing a particular machine can be jury-rigged to run faster this week, and a lot of it can't be written down and shipped up to a board. real market prices are what let people act on that local knowledge without anyone having to collect it centrally, and a Lange board pretending to set prices is basically assuming the knowledge problem is already solved.

three, the incentive argument: even if the board somehow got the information, the managers in a Lange-style system are public employees with no residual claim, so nothing actually makes them minimize cost, shut down a failing line, or resist pressure from above. the person who gains when the loss is avoided is missing from the room.

four, the discovery argument: Lange's trial-and-error procedure is set up for a static problem where the goods and techniques are already known and you're just trying to find the right prices. the actual hard part of economic life is discovering new products, new methods, new wants. a board that tinkers with parametric prices can't imitate entrepreneurs betting on hunches and getting punished for betting wrong, and that's not a rounding error in the model, it's the main event.

five, the accountability argument: if the state owns the capital, the people who replace bad managers are politicians and bureaucrats rather than profit and loss. you get soft budget constraints and the discipline of failure never really bites, so the price mechanism turns out to be decorative because nobody has to adjust when the numbers say they're failing.

so my question is, is there a sixth argument against Lange-style market socialism that isn't just a restatement of one of these five, and not just a special case or a mashup of a couple of them? if there is, i'd like to hear it laid out, plus some sense of how it relates to the ones i listed and whether it's actually any good as an objection or just sounds clever. and please write it as plain paragraphs, no headings or bullets or bold, like an email back to me.
