I recently took over an upper-level course on philosophical method, and the issue I keep returning to is what, precisely, thought experiments contribute to an argument. My students raise a reasonable worry: if a familiar case merely makes a reader feel that a thesis is right, why should that feeling count as evidence rather than rhetoric? In preparing the course I have found it useful to separate three roles. A case functions as an illustration when it makes a thesis vivid or concrete for someone who already accepts the thesis. It functions as an intuition pump when it is engineered to provoke a pre-theoretic judgment in a reader who began neutral. And it functions as evidence when its internal structure is claimed to be sufficiently isomorphic to some relevant portion of the real world that a judgment reached inside the case can, under a stated principle, be exported to the target phenomena. What I cannot yet pin down is whether these roles can be built into a single scenario, and whether a small change to a case can genuinely upgrade it from one role to the next.

Rather than keep trading examples with a colleague, I would like a purpose-built specimen. Pick a philosophical thesis with live defenders and critics, and construct a thought experiment that meets four constraints. First, in its base form the scenario should illustrate the thesis for someone already inclined to accept it while leaving a neutral reader largely unmoved. Second, changing exactly one structural feature of the scenario should turn it into an intuition pump that elicits the intuition that the thesis is true even in a reader who began neutral. Third, in this modified form it should also be plausible for a defender to offer the scenario as evidence for the thesis, because the single changed feature mirrors an identifiable feature of the real-world phenomena the thesis concerns. Fourth, the scenario must stay mundane: no fantastical elements, no stipulated superhuman judges or perfect predictors, no science-fictional technology, and no more than a short paragraph when presented.

After presenting the scenario, show that it meets the specifications. State the thesis and the argument the case is meant to serve. Identify which feature of the base scenario does the illustrative work and which feature of the modified scenario does the intuition-pumping work. Spell out the principle or structural mapping under which the modified scenario would count as evidence, assess whether that evidential claim is weak or strong by your own standard, and explain what happens if the changed feature is reverted — does the case fall back into mere illustration, and why? Finally, give the strongest objection a critic could raise against treating the modified scenario as evidence, and say what would have to be true of the real world for the defender to have a fair reply.
