I'm a retired wildlife biologist — thirty-some years doing fieldwork, mostly on ungulates and their predators. I've recently started reading philosophy, animal ethics mostly, and I've been trying to write down where I've landed on wild animal suffering. Since I'm new at this I'd like an audit: tell me which of these commitments are actually in tension with each other, which only look like they are, and what the cheapest repair would be. Rank the tensions by severity rather than making them all sound equal.

1. Suffering is bad wherever it occurs. A given amount of pain in a wild animal matters just as much as the same amount in a pet or a person; species membership doesn't discount it.

2. Wild nature contains an enormous amount of suffering — predation, parasitism, starvation, disease, the works. Most wild animals don't die of old age; they die young and badly. I don't have a number for it, but it isn't a rounding error.

3. The fact that something is natural carries no moral weight for me. A lion eating a gazelle alive is not made okay by being natural. Saying it's just nature never settles an ethical question.

4. Even so, I'm against large-scale interventions in wild ecosystems — predator control, mass vaccination, genetically rewiring predators, that sort of thing. We simply can't predict the downstream consequences, and the history of ecological tinkering is full of surprises. For something that big and irreversible the default has to be no.

5. I do support small, local, monitorable interventions: pulling an animal out of a fence, euthanizing one that is clearly dying in pain, supplemental feeding during a one-off winter die-off. The causal chain is short and we can watch what happens.

6. The unit of moral concern is the individual animal's welfare. A species or an ecosystem is not the kind of thing that can suffer, so it's flourishing can't by itself outweigh the suffering of individual animals.

7. At the same time, I think biodiversity loss is a real crisis and I support the standard conservation toolkit — protected areas, habitat restoration, reintroducing apex predators. I thought bringing wolves back to Yellowstone was a good thing, mainly because of what it did for the ecosystem.

8. I think the burden of proof is always on leaving preventable suffering alone: if I can help without making things worse, I should, and a general don't-meddle stance is not by itself a reason.

That's where I am. Which pairs are in real tension, which only appear to be, and what is the least costly single revision that would clean up the most?
