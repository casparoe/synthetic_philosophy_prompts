I am giving a talk next month to my department's political theory colloquium, defending this thesis: a view that assigns enormous expected moral weight to the far future does not, by itself, license the kind of centralized, discretionary control over present populations that we associate with totalitarian rule. The move from "the far future matters more than the present" to "we may suspend ordinary consent and procedure now" rests on conflating axiology with political authority, since a claim about what makes outcomes good does not, without a separate argument, grant anyone standing to coerce. My sketch of the argument is that political authority needs its own account of legitimacy, and that the very features of far-future reasoning that produce the huge expected values — deep uncertainty about who will exist, what they will want, and which present people are being sacrificed for which future benefits — are exactly the features that make it impossible to show that a would-be coercive power would track its model rather than its own interests. The audience is mostly liberal, deliberative-democratic, and neo-republican political theorists who are inclined to see longtermism as technocratic utilitarianism rebranded, plus a few analytic moral philosophers who accept consequentialism and will suspect I have helped myself to deontological constraints I have not earned; none of them identify as longtermists. Give me a Q&A prep sheet as exactly five numbered items, each no more than two sentences: the first sentence states an objection phrased as a member of this specific audience would phrase it, ordered from most to least damaging, and the second gives my best reply, what I should concede if anything, and the point where the reply is softest. Include among the five the question I am most hoping nobody asks, and do not add an introduction or conclusion.
