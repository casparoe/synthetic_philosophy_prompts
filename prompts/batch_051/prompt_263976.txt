I find myself torn between a theory I think is right and an intuition I cannot give up. The theory is the Everettian package: quantum mechanics with no collapse postulate, just the unitary evolution of one wave function, with decoherence-induced branching doing the work that "measurement" is supposed to do. Its virtues are systematic — it dissolves the measurement problem, removes the privileged status of the observer, and never adds a stochastic process to the basic dynamics. I also find the decision-theoretic response to the Born rule problem about as compelling as the package itself. On that approach, once decoherence has supplied a preferred basis and amplitude weights, an agent who must choose among bets before branching is forced by ordinary coherence constraints — transitivity, dominance, indifference to irrelevant details of the branching structure — to weight branches by their squared amplitudes. Other candidate weights fail stability under splitting and merging: branch-counting is not even well defined, since the number of branches depends on an arbitrary choice of coarse-graining, and amplitude-to-the-fourth weighting makes the weight of outcome A change when an unrelated branch B is subdivided. Squared amplitude is the unique weight that survives these constraints, which makes it look less like an added postulate and more like the measure any rational agent in a branching world would bet with.

And yet I cannot shake the intuition that all of this is a sophisticated answer to a question that, inside the theory, has no literal sense. If every outcome is realized, then before the branching there is no fact about which branch "I" will end up in, and afterward there is a successor of me in every branch; nothing chancy happens and no observation could tell me I was wrong. The decision-theoretic argument, I worry, shows at most that squared amplitudes can be made to function as probabilities if I first treat my branch successors as objects of self-locating uncertainty. It does not, by itself, show that I should so treat them. And the self-locating step looks as though it purchases coherence at a hidden metaphysical price: it needs some criterion of which successor is "me," or which branch I should locate myself in, of exactly the sort the theory's parsimony was supposed to avoid. So I am left with a formal story I believe and an intuition that probability talk in a fully deterministic multiverse is a category mistake.

I do not want a verdict that simply says the Everettian is allowed to call these probabilities. I want to know what each resolution would cost and what would justify it. Specifically: what would it take for the intuition to be the thing that goes — that is, what would show that it is a residue of single-world thinking about chance, and that a rational agent's betting weight is all "probability" ever was, inside or outside many-worlds? What would it take for the theory to be the thing that goes, or to be amended — for example, what would show that the derivation establishes only that the Born rule is consistent with rational preference, not that it has any normative or explanatory force, or that the self-locating premise is a cost the package cannot absorb? And is there a stable reading on which both survive — the derivation is valid and important, while the intuition that there are no single-world chances is fully preserved? Please give me a negotiated verdict that says what would justify each move rather than merely picking a side, and indicate how confident you are in what you claim, singling out the point you are least sure of.
