I have been thinking through a problem in constitutional interpretation, and I would welcome a careful treatment of it. A constitutional monarchy whose settlement dates from the introduction of responsible government contains the following article:

"Article 31. The Monarch shall be the head of the Realm and shall represent its unity, but shall take no part in the Government of the Realm. All powers, prerogatives, and acts of state belonging to the Crown shall be exercised only by the Prime Minister or by a Minister of the Crown; no such power, prerogative, or act of state shall be exercised by the Monarch in person; and nothing done by the Monarch shall have any force or effect in law unless it is countersigned by the Prime Minister."

The article was drafted in an era when the only instruments available to a monarch were legal instruments — appointments, proclamations, assent, dismissal — and each of them could be controlled by requiring a ministerial countersignature. The present monarch, who is young and has an enormous social-media following, has begun publicly opposing a bill introduced by the Government that would place the national broadcaster under the oversight of a board appointed by ministers. In a series of video addresses posted on the royal household's official channel, she calls the bill a threat to free broadcasting and urges members of the upper house to vote against it; the videos have not been countersigned, create no legal rights or duties, and are not acts of state in the strict sense. Yet polls have shifted sharply, several members of the upper house have announced that they will now oppose the bill, and the prime minister has accused the monarch of governing through the screen.

Does Article 31 apply to this conduct? Please argue the question from (i) the ordinary meaning of its words, (ii) the purpose of the article within the constitutional settlement, (iii) what the drafters would most plausibly have said if asked about a head of state who could address the entire electorate each evening without the countersignature of any minister, and (iv) what a reasonable reader today would understand the article to promise. Is there a coherent reading under which the videos are constitutionally unobjectionable, and, if so, does that reading show that the article protects the wrong thing? Which of these approaches should govern here, and why?
