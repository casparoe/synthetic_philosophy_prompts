I am writing a paper on the heterogeneity problem for hedonism, and I need a thought experiment built to a specification. The background problem is this: if pleasure is the only intrinsic good, a hedonist must say what all pleasant episodes have in common, yet pleasures look radically heterogeneous — the taste of a wine, relief at good news, the satisfaction of seeing a proof come out, a runner's high, taking pleasure in another person's good fortune. Three views in the literature give different answers. On the felt-quality view, all pleasant episodes share, or approximate, a distinctive felt quality — a hedonic tone. On the attitudinal view, what makes an episode pleasant is not a feeling but a propositional attitude: to be pleased is to take pleasure in, or enjoy, some state of affairs, and what we call a sensory pleasure is just taking pleasure in a sensation. On the desire-based view, a mental episode is pleasant just in case the person has an intrinsic, non-instrumental desire that that very episode itself be occurring, for its own sake, at the time.

I would like a single ordinary-life case — a train ride, an office, a family dinner; nothing clinical — in which the felt-quality view and the attitudinal view both deliver the verdict that the person is experiencing pleasure at the moment, while the desire-based view, by its own criterion, denies this. Further constraints: the person should be reflective and articulate, but not in any unusual state — no dissociation, no drugs, no exotic neurology — and the case must not get its force from the person's having philosophical views about pleasure. The case should be no more than a paragraph, and the verdicts should fall out of the ordinary structure of the person's attention and concerns.

Please do three things. First, state the case. Second, run the check: for each of the three views, say which episode is being evaluated, apply the view's criterion, and show without stipulation why the first two yield 'pleasant' and the third yields 'not pleasant.' Third, be honest about the machinery: identify the feature of the case that is doing the work, explain what happens if that feature is removed — in particular, whether the case then collapses into a standard pleasure case that all three views handle the same way — and say how a defender of the desire-based view would most plausibly resist the case, whether by overriding the agent's report, rejecting the description of the case, or arguing that the case equivocates on 'feels pleasant.'
