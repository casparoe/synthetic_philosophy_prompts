I'm a retired natural scientist, and the more philosophy of science I read the more I keep circling the old multiple-discovery problem. The standard picture—Newton and Leibniz on the calculus, Darwin and Wallace on natural selection—is that once the prerequisites are in place, a discovery is more or less inevitable, and near-simultaneous independent discovery is the evidence. I find that picture deeply attractive: it makes discovery a lawful, almost deterministic process rather than a lottery of unique genius, and it neatly explains the recurring priority fights between people who never talked to each other. But I also can't shake the intuition that some discoveries are lonelier than that—one person makes a sideways move nobody else was close to, and if you removed that person the idea might not arrive for decades or ever. I also have a stubborn sense that priority and credit sometimes track a real difference between opening a door and walking through a door someone else left ajar. And the thing that nags me most is methodological: how could we even test the inevitability claim, when we only observe the discoveries that happened and not the ones that failed to happen or arrived late? I dont want a verdict on Newton versus Leibniz. I want to know which should give way—the theory or the intuition—and what would justify each move. Please answer in exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no introduction or conclusion: the strongest case for the inevitability view, the strongest case for the intuition, what would justify my dropping the intuition, what would justify amending or abandoning the theory, and whether there's a reading on which both can stand.
