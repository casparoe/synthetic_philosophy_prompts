I've been going back and forth with a friend who thinks the norm of assertion is just truth—the propriety of the speech act is settled by whether what you said is true, not by what was in your head. I've landed on the knowledge account instead: you assert properly only if you know what you're asserting, where knowing requires belief, truth, and whatever anti-luck condition turns out to be right. But I want to know how exposed this view really is, and I don't want the usual tour of lotteries and Moore-paradox sentences.

What would count against the knowledge norm, in a way that should actually move me? Give me the cases, linguistic data, and theoretical pressures that pull toward the truth norm, the belief norm, the justified-belief norm, or something more contextualist. For each, tell me whether a committed knowledge-norm person can absorb it cheaply, absorb it with a patch, or has to concede real damage—and also flag any alleged counterexample that should just be dismissed because the knowledge norm was only ever a necessary condition, not a sufficient one. I'm especially interested in less-discussed trouble: retraction and disagreement data, selfless assertions where someone states what they don't privately believe but their evidence strongly supports, warranted assertions that rest on false lemmas, deliberately misleading true assertions, and cases where the propriety of an assertion shifts with conversational stakes or what the hearer needs. Then rank the defeaters from 'this could sink the view' to 'this is an awkward data point its defenders will just footnote.' End with an honest verdict: has the knowledge norm been protected by auxiliary hypotheses to the point that nothing could refute it, and if so, is that a worse problem for a norm of assertion than it would be elsewhere in philosophy? I'd rather have six sharp defeaters than a survey. Write the answer in American English.
