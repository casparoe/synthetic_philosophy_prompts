People seem to have two different settings for making tradeoffs, depending on whether they say the tradeoff out loud. A person choosing between two jobs will happily weigh pay against commute, autonomy, and interesting work—nobody bats an eye, even though there is no shared unit for those things. But ask that same person how much money it would take for them to miss their kid's only high school play, or to end their closest friendship, or to move their aging parent into the cheapest available facility, and the reaction is that the question is offensive or confused, as if some things are beyond pricing. Yet people do take the higher-paying job that makes them miss the play, drift away from the friendship for a better position, and choose the cheap facility. So we have an asymmetry: making the tradeoff with your life is treated as normal, while stating the trade in explicit terms is treated as a category mistake. Is there a real distinction between these two, or is the taboo on explicit pricing just a way of not having to look at what we are already doing? If you think the taboo is justified, say exactly what goes wrong when the trade is made explicit that doesn't go wrong when it's made by choosing the job. If you think it isn't justified, say whether we should keep the taboo anyway as a useful social fiction or try to get rid of it.
