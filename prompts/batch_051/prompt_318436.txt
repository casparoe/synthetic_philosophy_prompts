You are a philosopher specializing in the ethics of gratitude and in the analysis of arguments by analogy. Think through the question carefully, and begin by writing out the intended correspondence between source and target before you evaluate anything.

In ordinary moral thought people constantly speak of a debt of gratitude, and this idiom often does real argumentative work: it is used to infer that gratitude is an obligation, that its size tracks the benefit received, that it can be discharged by a suitable return, and that the ungrateful person is a kind of defaulter. For example, the metaphor is invoked to insist that children owe their parents determinate returns for earlier care, or that someone whose success rests on luck and past sacrifice owes payback to those who made it possible. Audit this analogy. First set out the mapping explicitly — benefactor to creditor, beneficiary to debtor, benefit to loan, grateful response to repayment, ingratitude to default. Then stress-test the analogy against the following: (1) benefits conferred accidentally, recklessly, or with ulterior motives, where the beneficiary receives something only through chance; (2) benefactors who cannot be repaid or who never aimed at this particular beneficiary, such as the dead, ancestors, or past generations; (3) the thought that gratitude, unlike a typical debt, cannot be paid off and made even, and may instead open or sustain a relationship; and (4) the fact that ordinary debts can be demanded, transferred, and enforced, whereas gratitude seems to lose its point when extracted. Say which disanalogies are decisive for the conclusions the idiom is used to support, which are merely incidental, and which features of the source domain the analogy quietly imports into gratitude. Then give a verdict: how much of the conclusion that gratitude is a debt-like obligation, proportioned to the benefit and dischargeable through repayment, survives the audit? If the comparison misdescribes gratitude, say what a better comparison would be and why it fares better.
