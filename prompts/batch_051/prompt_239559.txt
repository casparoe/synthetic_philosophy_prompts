I keep going back and forth on whether veganism is actually required outside rich countries, so I wrote my reasoning out as steps. I half-suspect there's a trick in it somewhere but I can't find it.

(1) Using animals for food is wrong only when the person has a workable alternative that doesn't cost them something comparable.

(2) For a goat herder in a dry region where crops fail often, plant-based alternatives aren't workable; going fully vegan would mean real hunger or malnutrition, which is a comparable cost.

(3) So that herder isn't doing anything wrong by keeping and killing goats.

(4) But how big a cost has to be to count as comparable should be judged from inside the person's life. A person in a rich city who gives up meat loses a lot of everyday pleasure, family meals, and convenience, and to them that also feels like a big cost.

(5) So by the same principle, the rich-city person isn't doing anything wrong either.

(6) That seems too easy, because then the principle excuses anyone who would really miss the thing they're giving up.

(7) So something in the argument has to give.

I suspect the slide is between the herder's cost in step 2, which is about hunger and nutrition, and the rich person's cost in step 4, which is about how attached they feel to their food. But I also worry that drawing that line is just me being elitist about who's suffering counts. Is the argument valid? If not, which step actually fails, and can the principle be fixed without it excusing everyone?
