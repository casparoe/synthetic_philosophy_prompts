I have no training in philosophy, but for some time I have been turning over a view that I find both compelling and unsettling. The view is that the self is not a thing but an abstraction: something like a centre of narrative gravity. Just as a centre of gravity is not a material part of an object yet does genuine explanatory work in mechanics, a self is not a part of a person that could in principle be inspected, but is the organising point around which the events of a life are made into a coherent story. On this view, what I call myself is produced by the stories I tell about my life and the stories others tell about me, rather than being the source of those stories.

I find this hard to resist for several reasons. It explains why my memories are selective and revised in retelling, why I present different selves to different people, and why my sense of who I am can change after a conversation without any single moment of change. It also addresses something that has always bothered me: whenever I introspect, I never actually find a self; I find perceptions, feelings, and thoughts, but no inner thing that has them.

And yet I cannot bring myself to believe it. The intuition that I am a single, persisting thing is firmer than my attraction to the theory. When I am in pain, there is someone the pain is happening to, and that someone is not a story. When I make a promise, I take myself to be the same person who must keep it tomorrow. I also suspect the analogy is doing more work than it should bear: a centre of gravity has a determinate location fixed by an object's mass, whereas the centre of a narrative seems to depend on who is telling the story and for what purpose. If the self is only a centre of narrative gravity, and the story can be retold around a different centre, then whether I exist would seem to depend on the telling; but I am certain my existence does not depend on any telling.

I want to know which of these should give way, or whether there is a reading on which they are compatible. Present the strongest case for treating the intuition as a by-product of how a brain models its own activity and therefore something to discount; present the strongest case for treating the intuition as tracking something the abstraction view omits; and then say what would justify each move—abandoning the intuition, amending the theory, or keeping both—rather than simply choosing a side. Please write your answer in British English.
