We judge unwarranted optimism and hope for the same outcome in strikingly different ways, and I want to know whether the difference can be justified. An optimistic belief held on weak evidence is treated as an intellectual failing: we call it wishful thinking and think the person ought to correct it, whatever comfort it brings. Yet hope for the very same improbable outcome, held on the very same evidence, is treated as a moral achievement: we call it resilience or courage and treat anyone who tries to talk the person out of it as cruel or obtuse. A patient with a small chance of recovery is thought deluded for believing he will recover, but admired for hoping he will; a citizen whose political cause is all but lost is thought naive for believing it will win, but principled for hoping it will.

Is there a principle that generates both of these ordinary judgements, or are we simply giving one irrational attitude a respectable name because we like what it does for people? I want the strongest candidate principle you can construct, not a survey of positions. In particular, address the following dilemma. If hope is a belief-like or probability-like state about how likely things are, it seems to inherit the epistemic criticism aimed at optimism, and the asymmetry collapses. If hope is instead a desire or motivational state towards an outcome, explain which epistemic standards, if any, apply to it, and whether that lets hope escape the charge of irrationality without becoming merely decorative. Then test your principle against hard cases: a person who sustains hope by avoiding evidence; a person who spreads hope to others at some cost to their accuracy; and a person whose hope systematically outruns their planning, so that they hope but never act. Say whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up.
