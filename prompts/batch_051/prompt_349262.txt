im putting together a little syllabus on Du Bois and art, and i keep tripping over one paragraph in W. E. B. Du Bois's "Criteria of Negro Art" (the Crisis piece, October 1926). here's the passage:

Even as you visualize such ideals you know in your hearts that these are not the things you really want. You realize this sooner than the average white American because, pushed aside as we have been in America, there has come to us not only a certain distaste for the tawdry and flamboyant but a vision of what the world could be if it were really a beautiful world; if we had the true spirit; if we had the Seeing Eye, the Cunning Hand, the Feeling Heart; if we had, to be sure, not perfect happiness, but plenty of good hard work, the inevitable suffering that always comes with life; sacrifice and waiting, all that -- but, nevertheless, lived in a world where men know, where men create, where they realize themselves and where they enjoy life. It is that sort of a world we want to create for ourselves and for all America.

Everyone summarizes this essay as the one where Du Bois says art is propaganda and that he doesnt care much for the art-for-arts-sake crowd. but this paragraph sounds like he's after beauty and self-realization almost for their own sake, and i cant work out whether that's compatible with the propaganda demand, whether it's the deeper criterion the propaganda is supposed to serve, or whether it's just a genuine tension in the essay. can you walk me through who "we" is here (black Americans? black artists? all America?) and how the paragraph bears on the propaganda claim? and if the real target is the kind of world he describes in this passage, does that mean the white audience that wants Negro art to be protest first and beautiful second has the thing backwards? does Du Bois actually settle on one criterion for black art — political effect, or truth-and-beauty — or does he need both, and does that two-step hold up?
