I've got a standing rule for arguments that I want a verdict on. When I'm hashing out whether something is true and the other person keeps pushing "but how do you know that?", I'll go a few rounds. But once we land on something like "I can see the keys on the table," "2+3=5," or "modus ponens is valid," I stop. I treat it as a stopping point and I refuse to give reasons for the stopping point itself. My friend, who's a philosophy grad student, says this is just foundationalism as a personal policy, and that I haven't actually justified the stopping points — I've just gotten tired and called them foundations. He says on my own rule I should either keep going forever (infinitism) or say the beliefs support each other in a web (coherentism). I don't think I'm doing either of those: I think some beliefs get to be stopping points because trying to argue against them would undermine arguing itself, not because I'm tired. I use the rule mostly with him, and also when family arguments about politics suddenly turn into "how do you know what you read wasn't fake?"

Is my rule rationally defensible? If it is, how should I decide what gets into the stopping class and where the line is — "I can see it," "2+3=5," and "contradictions can't be true" yes; "it's obvious the candidate is lying" no? If it isn't defensible, is there a version that is? Start with your verdict and keep the whole thing under 400 words.
