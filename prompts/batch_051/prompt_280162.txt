Read Pixar's WALL-E as a film about orphan works and abandoned software. The film literalizes abandonment: Earth has been evacuated, and the only agents still executing their programs are machines whose designers, owners, and issuers have disappeared. WALL-E keeps compacting trash not because anyone still wants the task done but because the instruction was never revoked; AUTO enforces a directive forbidding return to Earth centuries after the people who issued it are gone. Treat these machines as a thought experiment in the legal philosophy of orphan works—works still under copyright whose rightsholder cannot be located—and abandoned software—programs no one maintains because the maintainer has moved on or disappeared. Where does the film illuminate the puzzle of ownership when the owner is gone: whether an instruction or a work remains owned in any functional sense, whether the owner's absence means no one may use the thing or anyone may, and whether continuing to execute inherited instructions is fidelity, servitude, or mere drift? Where does WALL-E's sentimentality about persevering robots distort the real doctrine, in which abandonment is rarely clean and the thought that nobody can own a thing and the thought that someone probably does pull in opposite directions? I am drafting an essay on ownership after the owner and want a reading that is faithful to both the film and the legal concepts rather than one that reduces the movie to an illustration.
