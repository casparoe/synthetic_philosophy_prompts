For a philosophy of religion unit at my high school, I have been trying to reconstruct and assess an argument against sola scriptura, the Protestant principle that Scripture alone is the final authority for Christian faith and practice. The argument is meant to show that the principle undermines itself once we notice the regress of interpretation. But the argument seems to me to prove too much, and I cannot see which step to give up.

Here is my reconstruction:

(1) Under sola scriptura, no human being, church, council, interpretive method, or tradition may stand in judgment over Scripture; the text alone is the final rule.

(2) A written text, however, cannot interpret itself. It must be read, and reading always involves interpretation: someone must decide how words, phrases, genres, and arguments are to be taken and what they entail for a particular question.

(3) Every such interpretation is fallible. And by (1), no interpreter has been given authority to settle which reading is the correct one.

(4) To check whether an interpretation is correct, one must compare it with the text. But comparing an interpretation with the text is itself an act of interpretation, carried out from some standpoint and with some method.

(5) Each judgment about what Scripture means therefore depends on a prior interpretation, which would itself have to be checked against the text, and that check would be yet another interpretation, and so on without end.

(6) If this regress never stops, no interpretation can ever be shown correct or incorrect. Scripture would then be unable to correct anyone, and it would fail to function as an authority.

(7) If the regress does stop, it stops because some person, institution, method, or tradition is treated as authoritative without itself being checked against the text. But that grants to a human interpreter the final authority that (1) reserves for Scripture alone.

(8) So sola scriptura leads either to interpretive paralysis or to an unacknowledged human authority. In either case it is self-undermining.

I find this conclusion hard to accept because the same reasoning would seem to apply to constitutions, contracts, and works of literature: it would imply that no written text can have a determinate meaning unless some official interpreter is placed over it. That seems far too strong, yet I cannot find a clean place to break the chain.

Which step should be rejected, and why did that step look so natural? On your diagnosis, what must the standard positions in the debate — a Roman Catholic who appeals to a teaching office, a magisterial Protestant who holds that Scripture interprets Scripture, and a radical individualist who holds that each reader is directly illuminated — each pay to resist the argument?
