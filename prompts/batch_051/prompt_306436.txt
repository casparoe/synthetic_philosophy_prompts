My roommate and I were arguing about how mathematical proofs actually get accepted, and I realized I dont have a clear picture of what the practice commits people to. My vague impression was that a journal referee is supposed to check a paper line by line, like a really finicky proof checker, but my roommate (a grad student) says that's basically not true: referees read for the main idea, whether the technique hangs together, whether any gap looks patchable, and nobody expects them to catch every subtle mistake. At the same time, universities beat into undergraduates that you have to show your work, and they give partial credit for what sure looks like procedural correctness — sometimes almost none for a correct answer with no visible reasoning. And then there are formal proof assistants, which really do check every step, but lots of mathematicians are weirdly reluctant to say a computer-verified result has been understood, or even, sometimes, that it's a proof in the full sense.

I'm trying to figure out what view of mathematical understanding is presupposed by the journal refereeing practice in particular. Does accepting a proof on the basis of a referee's judgment presuppose that understanding a proof means grasping the idea or the structure of an argument rather than being able to verify each inference? Or is that just a time-saving compromise with no philosophical commitments? And is the teaching practice of grading shown work consistent with whatever theory of understanding the refereeing practice presupposes? If not, what would have to change in one or the other for the practice to be coherent?
