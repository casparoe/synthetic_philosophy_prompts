Retired chemist here, been in a philosophy reading group for a couple years now. Lately we've been on higher-order evidence, and my friend Dana presented an argument that seems to prove something absurd, but none of us can locate the mistake. I'd like a committed answer—tell me which premise to drop and why, not just a menu of options.

Here's the setup. Suppose I've just finished a long lab-notebook calculation—say, estimating the yield of a reaction—and I believe the answer is P on the basis of my first-order evidence E: the measurements, the arithmetic, the chemical reasoning. I then remember that I slept four hours last night, or that I've made arithmetic slips before. That gives me higher-order evidence H: evidence about how good my first-order evidence or my response to it actually is.

Dana's argument runs like this:

(1) If my total evidence includes H, then my rational confidence in P should be based on my total evidence, not just E.
(2) When H indicates that my first-order assessment is unreliable, my total evidence supports P less strongly than E alone would.
(3) So I should lower my confidence in P.
(4) But this same move can be made for any belief, because I always have some higher-order evidence of my own fallibility—fatigue, bias, past mistakes, or just the general truth that humans are fallible.
(5) So for any belief I form, I should be less confident than my first-order evidence suggests.
(6) But if I take that higher-order doubt seriously, I should also doubt my judgment about how much to lower my confidence—since that judgment is just another one of my cognitive outputs—and so on, producing either an infinite regress or a collapse in confidence.
(7) Therefore, I can never rationally hold a firm belief in anything based on my own reasoning.

That conclusion can't be right, because it would make careful science and mathematics impossible. I'd most like to keep the thought that I can rationally be pretty confident the yield is 72% when I've done the calculation twice and checked the units. So which premise is really doing the bad work? And if I reject it, what exactly does that cost in the standard higher-order evidence debate—which views do I end up committed against or forced to revise?
