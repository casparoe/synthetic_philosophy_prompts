I'm a decision theory PhD student and I've been going back and forth on a Pascal's-mugging variant that I haven't seen worked out, and I'd like your take on how the standard analysis changes.

In the usual setup, a stranger threatens to inflict an enormous amount of suffering on an astronomically large number of people unless you hand over $20. The expected-utility calculation says even a tiny nonzero probability of that threat makes the expected harm swamp the $20, so you should pay; then he immediately escalates, and your off to the races.

Here's my tweak. Before making the big threat, the mugger gives you a demonstration. In a bare room you've searched yourself—no props, no electronics—he causes a sharp pain behind your left eye, maybe a 5 out of 10, for exactly ten seconds, three times, on your command. You do your due diligence and can't find any trick, stage magic, or sign you're losing your mind. Then he says: pay me $100 now, or I'll give that same ten-second headache to 10^20 people at once. (If you'd rather run it with the original's absurdly huge number, go ahead.) To keep the arithmetic simple, suppose you'd pay $1 to prevent any one person from getting that headache, and no double-counting or budget weirdness applies.

What I want to know is whether this makes Pascal's mugging better or worse for the expected-utility view. For someone who thought the original argument was sound: does the demonstration justify paying, and does the escalation problem get even harder, since each larger threat now comes from someone with a verified causal power instead of a random crank? For someone who thought you should assign threats like this zero probability: does the demonstration force them to concede anything, or can they still refuse without being irrational? In particular, I'm curious what the posterior probability on the 10^20-person threat would need to be for the $100 payment to be rational, and whether any reasonable prior, conditional on the demonstration, gets you there.
