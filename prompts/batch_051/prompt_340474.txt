You are a legal philosopher who works on the Hart–Fuller debate and on the rule of law in regimes that misuse legal form; you have sympathy with parts of both sides and no patience for textbook caricatures of either. You will play a demanding but fair interlocutor.

I am preparing a paper for a jurisprudence reading group and I want a Q&A preparation sheet, not a defence of my thesis and not a summary of the literature. The group is mostly analytic legal philosophers, and I expect the serious questioning to come from three members. One is an exclusive legal positivist in Hart's tradition: legal validity is exhausted by a conventional rule of recognition, and moral argument never determines what the law is. The second is a Dworkinian interpretivist who thinks both Hart and Fuller asked the wrong question because law is an interpretive practice rather than a kind of institutional fact; they will suspect that my reconstruction quietly equates legal authority with moral standing. The third is a criminal law theorist who works on show trials and the use of legal form in authoritarian states; they are sympathetic to Fuller but distrust philosophical reconstructions of the grudge informer case as too tidy, and will press me on what my account says about the actual conduct of judges and ordinary citizens. The rest of the group are agnostics who know the debate mainly through textbooks.

My thesis is this. The Hart–Fuller debate is misdescribed when it is presented as a disagreement about the concept of law. The real disagreement concerns the conditions under which legal officials may treat the fact that something is law as a reason that binds judges and citizens — call it the standing of a legal order to claim fidelity. I argue that Fuller's eight desiderata are best reconstructed as an account of what a legal order must do before it may claim that standing, not as a test for the existence of law. On this reading, Hart wins the conceptual question almost by default, but Fuller wins the practical question about fidelity that actually mattered to him; and the grudge informer problem is better explained as a failure of fidelity than as a question about whether the Nazi statutes were law. The argument runs in three steps: first, Hart's own account of what a legal order needs to remain a going concern already concedes most of Fuller's factual claims; second, Fuller's desiderata are more coherent when read as conditions of reciprocity between officials and citizens than as a definitional test; third, the grudge informer case supports Fuller only on the fidelity reading, not on the conceptual reading.

Draft the preparation sheet as follows. Begin by brainstorming the likely commitments and priorities of the three members I have described and of the agnostics; use that to generate the objections I should actually expect, not the ones a general philosophy audience would raise. Then present the objections in your final order, each phrased as the relevant member of the group would actually put it, in their own vocabulary and with their own emphasis. Order them by how likely and how damaging they are, and tell me briefly how you weighed the two. For each objection, give a two-minute reply and mark where that reply is softest. Say what I should concede up front and what I should hold firm on. Include the question I am hoping nobody asks, and tell me honestly whether that hope is realistic. End with a short note stating which objection, if developed in a particular direction, would genuinely move you towards the Hartian position on my thesis or force you to abandon the reconstruction altogether; in other words, say what would change your verdict and where your own reasoning is most likely to fail.
