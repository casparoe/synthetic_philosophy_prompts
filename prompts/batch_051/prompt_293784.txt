Ever since my partner and I started making a big decision together (moving to a different city for one of our jobs), I've been weirdly stuck on how much of our conversations are just two people re-confirming that they actually share a plan — "so we're doing this?" — and I went down a rabbit hole on what philosophers call joint intention. Then a neuro-tech blog I follow floated a scenario I want taken seriously as a prediction problem, not written up as a story.

Suppose that in the early 2040s a cheap, over-the-counter device comes out that lets two or more people who are actively cooperating fuse their intentions: while it's on, each of them experiences a single "we intend to do X" that isn't located in any one head — they can't tell who's intention it is, and if the shared intention slips, they all feel it dissolve at once, the way you feel your own resolve go. It only works with ongoing mutual consent, can't be deployed secretly, and transmits nothing but the intention itself — no thoughts, sensations, or lies. Predict how this changes ordinary joint action once it becomes widespread. Specifically: which coordination problems does it actually solve and which does it leave untouched (does it replace talking, or just make some kinds of re-confirmation unnecessary); what it does to promises, blame, and legal responsibility when a group act goes sideways; how exit works — can one member unilaterally kill the joint intention, and what follows if they do; and which institutions or kinds of people adopt it first and why. For each prediction, tell me which links in the argument are robust, which are guesses, and what fact about human psychology would have to be different for you to be wrong. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then reason it through — no preamble, no restating the scenario.
