I am a novelist at work on a book in which a character builds an AI system whose task is to explain the decisions of a much larger AI, and then must decide who explains the explainer. In researching this, I have found that the underlying debate seems to have stalled in a distinctive way. One side holds that this kind of interpretability is in principle more tractable than interpreting human minds, because a trained model is a fully observable artifact: its weights, activations, and training history are all available, and a sufficiently capable AI could turn that detail into a faithful mechanistic account of why the target behaves as it does. The other side replies that an account produced by an AI is itself the output of an opaque learned system; unless the interpreter is itself interpreted, its explanation cannot be trusted, and the project threatens to become an endless chain of interpreters explaining interpreters. What puzzles me is that neither side appears to deny the other side's factual premise; they part ways over whether the chain can stop and over what would count as understanding at the end of it. My working hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that the real disagreement is not about technical feasibility but about what interpretation is supposed to deliver: prediction, control, a humanly intelligible story, or something closer to introspection.
