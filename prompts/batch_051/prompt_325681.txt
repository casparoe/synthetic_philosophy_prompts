I am a software engineer working on a system that logs user actions and attempts to infer from those logs what users actually want. The part I keep getting stuck on is conceptual rather than technical: in inverse reinforcement learning, a given run of observed behaviour is typically consistent with an enormous family of reward functions, because almost any trajectory can be made optimal by some assignment of rewards. The data therefore underdetermine the preferences, and I notice my colleagues splitting into camps about what that underdetermination actually shows. I run a small reading group of engineers on this issue, and I want to sort its members by what they believe about cases, not by which label they happen to like.

Please produce a 'which position on inferring preferences from behaviour do you actually hold?' quiz for that group. Give eight to ten questions. Each question should be a concrete case or a claim, for example: a route planner that always takes the quickest road; a user who says she wants to read long books but always clicks on short videos; a delivery robot that goes around the block when a door is blocked; two reward functions that make the same demonstration optimal and differ only on states the agent never visits. For each question, provide two or three answer options, phrased so an engineer can answer without knowing any philosophical vocabulary. Build the questions around the distinctions that actually move the debate: whether a preference is something read off behaviour or a hidden state behind behaviour; whether there is a fact of the matter about the 'true' reward when several rewards explain the behaviour equally well; whether constraints, habits and social norms should be modelled separately from reward rather than folded into it; whether underdetermination should be carried as uncertainty and responded to conservatively rather than resolved with a simplicity prior; and whether a single scalar reward is even the right object to infer in the first place.

Map the answer patterns onto at least the following positions, though you may rename them if you think the space is better carved:

- the revealed-preference view, on which preferences just are the ordering that rationalises choice, so there is nothing hidden left to infer;
- hidden-state realism, on which there is a genuine preference behind the behaviour, the data underdetermine it, and assumptions such as simplicity or maximum entropy should select the best candidate;
- the no-fact-of-the-matter view, on which the choice of reward function is a modelling decision to be judged by downstream usefulness rather than by truth;
- the constraint-sensitivity view, on which behaviour is a mixture of goal and feasibility, and folding constraints into reward is a serious inferential error;
- the ambiguity-preserving view, on which we should keep a distribution over reward functions and act cautiously under it rather than collapse it too early.

After the scoring key, include a brief note on the logical space: which combinations of answers are unoccupied, and whether each empty combination is empty because it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored; and which pairs of leaves the quiz cannot reliably separate. The audience is practising engineers with machine learning experience and no formal philosophy background, so explain the labels without being condescending. Please do not pad the answer with citations or further-reading suggestions; the reasoning should stand on its own. Aim for roughly 1,200 words, and write the whole answer in British English.
