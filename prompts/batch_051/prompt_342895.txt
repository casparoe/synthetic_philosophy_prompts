I'm on a small audit/red-team group that reviews black-box optimizers—trading algorithms, recommender systems, autonomous agents—before they go live. For each one I get about three days, a few hundred probes, and no access to weights or code. My report has to answer two questions: what is this thing actually maximizing, and what action space is it actually searching over? I've learned the hard way those two are easy to conflate: a dumb search strategy can make a sensible objective look perverse, and a misspecified objective can look like a quirky search process. Write me a reusable procedure—a checklist or set of tests in a fixed order—for getting from observed behavior to a defensible claim about objective and search space, built around the fact that behavior underdetermines both. Include which probes to run first, what each result can and cannot show, how to tell objective-driven behavior from search-driven behavior, and where the procedure should just output "cannot be determined from this evidence." Then apply it to two or three made-up cases, including one where the procedure forces me to tell my manager there's no single objective we can pin down. I don't want a survey of methods; pick a sequence, give the reasons for it, and flag the steps that are judgment calls dressed up as tests.
