Democratic backsliding is a term I reach for constantly, but I have realized that I could not say precisely what it picks out. People apply it to military coups, stolen elections, court-packing, media capture, and sometimes merely to election results they dislike; critics charge that it is no more than a partisan label. I want you to propose a working definition of democratic backsliding that does real classificatory work: it should distinguish backsliding from ordinary democratic disagreement and from full democratic breakdown, and it should not reduce to a synonym for government I disapprove of. Then test your definition against the following cases, saying where it gives the right answer, where it gives no answer, and where it forces you to revise or defend a surprising verdict.

Case A. A governing party wins a constitutional supermajority in a free election, then uses it to rewrite the electoral law in its own favor, forcibly retire judges and replace them with loyalists, and turn the public broadcaster into a party organ. Elections continue, but observers begin rating them formally free yet not genuinely fair.

Case B. An incumbent narrowly loses an election, falsely claims it was rigged, pressures election officials to locate additional votes for him, and his supporters storm the legislature to stop certification. Certification is delayed but eventually completed, and the incumbent leaves office under legal compulsion.

Case C. A party wins office promising strict migration controls and steep tax cuts, enacts those policies through normal legislative procedures, and is voted out four years later.

Case D. A dominant party never cancels elections and allows real opposition parties to run, but over three decades it has made campaign finance, media ownership, and district boundaries progressively more favorable to itself. It keeps winning national majorities with well under half the vote while the opposition wins only a few cities.

Case E. After a violent coup attempt, an elected government declares a state of emergency, imprisons not only the plotters but also many peaceful critics, and then holds a constitutional referendum during emergency rule that abolishes the prime ministry and concentrates power in the presidency.

Case F. A government lowers the mandatory retirement age for senior judges, doubling the number of vacancies that open within eighteen months, and changes the appointment rules so that the executive and its legislative majority can fill them immediately. It defends this as a long-overdue modernization of a corrupt, self-dealing judiciary; the depleted courts cannot effectively review the changes.

Case G. No formal rules change. Elections run on time with high turnout and occasional alternation of power, but public trust in the process collapses, false claims of rigging become conventional, and a large minority declares the last election illegitimate.

My own intuitions are firm at the extremes: Case A is clearly democratic backsliding and Case C is clearly not. I genuinely do not know how to classify B, D, E, F, or G. Please propose the definition, run it through all seven cases, and then tell me which of my intuitions you would overturn, whether the term is doing more than one job, and what a neutral observer applying your definition would need to establish before calling a case backsliding rather than mere partisan dislike.

End your response with a single line of the form: Final definition: <your one-sentence worked-out definition>.
