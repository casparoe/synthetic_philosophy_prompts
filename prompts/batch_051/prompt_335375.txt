I'm teaching Peter Weir's The Truman Show in a philosophy course on mass culture, and I want to anchor the film in one of the less-anthologized sentences from Adorno and Horkheimer's chapter "The Culture Industry: Enlightenment as Mass Deception" in Dialectic of Enlightenment:

"The attitude of the public, which ostensibly and actually favours the system of the culture industry, is a part of the system and not an excuse for it."

My problem is that The Truman Show seems simultaneously to confirm and to disown the sentence. On the one hand, the diegetic audience's devotion to the program looks like a textbook case of a public whose enthusiasm is part of the system: the show runs on their appetite for Truman's everyday life, and the control-room scenes make clear that their attention is what sustains Seahaven. On the other hand, the film's closing moments appear to exempt its real audience—us—from that complicity by letting us cheer Truman's escape, as if we were never the ones watching. I want a careful reading that uses the passage to open up specific elements of the film: the reaction shots of the in-film viewers, the control-room apparatus, Truman's discovery of the set, and the final exit. Where does the film genuinely dramatize what the passage describes, where does it strain against the theory, and does Adorno and Horkheimer's logic suffice for the ending, or does the film reveal something the essay's framework cannot accommodate? I do not want the film flattened into an illustration of critical theory, and I would rather an honest critic concede the points where the film escapes the passage than force it to fit.
