I'm having an argument with a friend about epiphenomenalism, and he sent me this summary to show I should stop defending it:

"Epiphenomenalism says consciousness is a by-product of the brain. Your pain doesn't cause you to wince or say 'ouch'; the underlying brain state causes both, and the feeling just tags along, like the whistle on a steam train. Fine. But now ask why you believe you're in pain. That belief is itself a brain state, caused by earlier brain states, not by the pain. So the fact that the belief's content matches your actual feeling can't be explained by that feeling; a careful epiphenomenalist has to say the match is a lucky coincidence, and natural selection couldn't have shaped a report that tracks a cause that does nothing. Since our introspective reports obviously do track our feelings, epiphenomenalism refutes itself."

I've read enough to know the steam-whistle analogy is the standard epiphenomenalist move, but I can't tell whether the last step—that the view makes correct introspective reports a 'lucky coincidence'—is right, or whether he's running together two different claims. What should I concede, what's being misstated, and does the paradox of phenomenal judgment still give me trouble after the fair version is cleaned up?
