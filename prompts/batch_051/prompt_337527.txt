Take Nozick's experience machine, but change what the machine sells. Instead of a menu of any experiences you want, it sells one package: you keep on living your actual life in the real world, except every time you would have felt sadness, grief, or anxiety, the feeling is dialed down to 30% of it's ordinary intensity. Everything else—your memories, the losses themselves, your choices, how you look to other people—stays the same. You still attend the funeral and lose the job; you just feel almost nothing about it. Nozick's main complaints about the original machine were that it's all a simulation, so you'd be cut off from doing things for real, from being a certain kind of person, and from contact with a deeper reality. None of those seems directly touched, since nothing here is simulated. So does the anti-machine conclusion survive the change? Is a hedonist forced to say the package makes your life better? Does a desire-satisfaction or objective-list view have a real objection, and if so what exactly is the good being lost? Work through each of the three standard views of well-being, and say which of Nozick's complaints, if any, still have force.
