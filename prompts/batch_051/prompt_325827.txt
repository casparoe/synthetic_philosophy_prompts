I've been working through the puzzle of the statue and the lump of clay for a philosophy elective at school. Every treatment I have found uses the same stock example: a sculptor shapes a lump of clay into a statue and then squashes it, and we are meant to have the intuition that the statue is destroyed while the lump survives. That asymmetry is then used to support the claim that the lump and the statue must have been two distinct material objects all along, since one of them apparently has a property—the capacity to survive being squashed—that the other lacks. I would like to understand how much of that argument is genuinely about material constitution and how much is an artifact of the particular change being described.

Please construct a thought experiment in which, starting from an ordinary statue made from a lump of clay, a single realistic process produces the reverse intuitive verdict: the statue survives and the original lump does not. Constraints: the scene should be an ordinary school art classroom or potter's workshop, and the process should be one a patient person could carry out in a single afternoon; no instantaneous replacement of matter, no magic or exotic means, and no changing mid-case what counts as the statue or the lump in order to secure the result. The removed clay should remain present in the scene, for instance in a bucket, so that the question is about the identity of the original lump rather than about the destruction of matter as such. The result should arise from the structure of the scenario rather than being stipulated.

After you present the scenario, walk through exactly what each of the following positions would say about it and why: (a) the coincidence view, which holds that the statue and the clay are two distinct material objects that occupy the same place and differ in their persistence conditions, and (b) the one-object view, which holds that there is only one material object there, describable under different sortals—kind terms that supply criteria of identity and persistence—so that apparent conflicts about what survives are really conflicts in how the story is sorted. Then identify the single feature of the case that does the work by reversing the standard asymmetry, say whether removing that feature collapses the case back into something like the standard squashing case, and finish by saying how a defender of the standard case as an argument for coincident objects would attack your scenario, and how damaging you think the attack is.
