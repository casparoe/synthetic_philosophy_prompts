Suppose that in 2015 a mid-sized country (call it Meridia) goes through a Stoic-revival craze and rewrites its tort law around the dichotomy of control, taken literally as a liability rule: you owe damages only for what was up to you — the reasonableness and riskiness of your choices — and never for how things turn out. A surgeon who makes the same error pays the same weather the patient dies or recovers fully; a drunk driver pays the same whether she hits a mailbox or a school bus. Courts must therefore work out, case by case, which parts of a bad outcome were genuinely within the agent's control. Predict, with your reasoning visible, how Meridia's legal system and ordinary life look by 2060: which claims get brought, how the control inquiry reshapes evidence and procedure, how insurers, hospitals, employers, and regulators adapt, and whether people end up taking more care, less care, or a different kind of care. Flag which predictions you'd bet on, which are guesses, and what would have to be true for the reform to backfire.
