A friend and I spent an evening arguing about whether addiction is "really" a disease, and the dispute kept running into a larger asymmetry that neither of us could justify. Neither of us hesitated to call hypertension a disease: a blood-pressure cuff reads a number above a threshold, and that seems to settle the question, even when the person feels well and the reading chiefly marks a statistical risk of future harm. But when a condition presents in conduct, emotion, or desire — depression, ADHD, addiction, a personality disorder — the conversation changed register. We found ourselves demanding a biological marker before granting the label, and in its absence we talked as if the condition might be only an unusual or disapproved way of living, however much misery it caused. Beneath this lay the familiar divide between naturalism about disease — the idea that a disease is an objective failure of biological functioning, discoverable independently of our values — and normativism — the idea that calling something a disease always involves a judgment about which states, feelings, and ways of living are bad. The same person, I have noticed, can be an unembarrassed naturalist about the body and a reluctant normativist about the mind: somatic conditions are spoken of as discovered facts, psychiatric ones as negotiated judgments. I want to know whether anything principled holds those two attitudes apart, or whether the difference is only the residue of an older picture in which bodies belong to nature and minds to persons.

Put the question in its sharpest form. Take two people equally distressed and equally unable to carry on their ordinary lives. One has a thyroid disorder with a clear blood marker and is counted as ill without further argument. The other's condition appears only in mood, sleep, and behaviour, has no reliable biomarker, and is met with the familiar mixture of sympathy and the quiet suspicion that the person may simply be at the unlucky end of normal variation, or failing to cope. Which way should the correction run? Should the realism we feel in the thyroid case be extended to the psychiatric case, so that the second person is ill in the same factual sense as the first, only beyond what our instruments can currently measure? Or should the value-ladenness we sense in the psychiatric case be read back across medicine, so that even the thyroid case turns out to involve a judgment that certain ways of functioning matter? Work out the strongest naturalist and the strongest normativist answer, test each against the asymmetry, and say whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up.
