Standard story: modern science didn't just stop talking about final causes; it argued them out of existence. Aristotle thought a complete explanation of a thing includes what it's for, but since the scientific revolution the respectable line has been that purpose-talk is at best harmless shorthand. Organisms look designed, but natural selection plus efficient causes does the real explaining, and a thing's being for such-and-such never does causal work. Darwin is the usual exhibit: design without a designer, so teleology is redundant.

Now look at how we actually describe trained machine-learning systems. A transformer is just matrix multiplications, backprop, and gradient steps; nobody thinks there's a ghost in the thing. But the most predictive, compact descriptions of what it's doing are hopelessly teleological: the model is trying to lower the loss, the reward function is what the agent is about, and if you rewrite the objective the behaviour changes with it. This isn't pop-science anthropomorphism; it's how the people doing the math talk while building it. The four causes even seem to map over a little too neatly: the training data is the material, the architecture is formal, the training run is efficient, and the loss function sits there looking like a final cause.

So run the old anti-teleological argument in earnest and tell me what survives it. Which premise does a loss-minimising system actually put pressure on? Is the loss a genuine final cause of the systems behaviour, or is it just efficient causation in gradient form with a teleological gloss on top? If the argument survives intact, say exactly what a phrase like 'trying to minimize the loss' is doing, and whether something like Aristotle's final cause has snuck back in under another name. If it's dead or needs repair, give the repaired version and say what it can no longer rule out.
