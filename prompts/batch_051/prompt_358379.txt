Right, I need a second opinion before our next compliance commitee meeting. We've built up a half-dozen precedents over the past two years on gifts and favours, and I genuinely can't work out which principle we've been running. I'll give you the track record, then the case that's coming up.

1. Ghana borehole. We cleared a programme officer who accepted a goat and a strip of kente cloth from a village association after their borehole grant had closed. It was public, it was customarily given as thanks after the fact, and refusing it would have damaged the relationship.

2. Delhi clearing agent. We reprimanded a logistics officer who accepted a gold bangle from a clearing agent while the agent's customs paperwork was still sitting with him. He said it would've been rude to refuse. The committee said the live discretionary matter plus the concealment made it a bribe.

3. London theatre. We cleared a UK project officer who accepted two theatre tickets, worth about £150, from a supplier after a tender had been awarded. She'd logged them.

4. Guatemala permit. We reprimanded a country director who gave cash to a local mayor's wife shortly before a permit decision. He claimed it was ordinary local goodwill. The committee said the timing and the pending permit made it an attempted bribe, whatever the etiquette.

5. Gujarat visas. We reprimanded a field officer for lending the charity's Land Rover to a local party official at weekends in exchange for speeding up staff visas. Bribery in kind, they said.

6. Christmas hamper. We cleared a finance assistant who accepted a £45 hamper sent to the whole finance team by an insurance broker, logged on the register. No live business with the firm.

Now here's the one on the agenda: a programme director in Nigeria is getting married, and at the wedding a contractor whose company has bid for a large clinic-construction tender, still undecided, gave her an envelope of cash worth around £2,000. It was given publicly, matches normal wedding practice, and went into the wedding gift book. She doesn't sit on the procurement panel, but her deputy chairs it. She says she had no role in scoring the bid, refusing would have caused real offence, and the gift wasn't tied to any promise of help.

What should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves? Which precedent is the closest analogue, and if the precedents genuinely underdetermine it, what principle would we be adopting either way? And is there a further case that would cleanly separate the remaining readings? Half the room thinks any rule we adopt is us imposing a London standard on Kano; the other half thinks the whole point is not to have local opt-outs.
