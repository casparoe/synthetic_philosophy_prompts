You're a professional philosopher who works on social norms and institutional design. I want you to stress-test a rule my friends want to adopt in our housing co-op (about 40 people, shared decisions on money, chores, guests, everything). Not wether it's right — I want to know how it would actually function.

The proposal is a 'legibility rule.' Any member can ask any other member a why-question about any request, refusal, plan, promise, or vote that affects the asker, and the person asked has to answer with their actual reasons and feelings, complete and honest enough that the asker could act on them. 'I don't want to talk about it' only counts as an answer if that really is the reason, not as a dodge. The answer gets written in a shared log the whole house can read. Lying or serious omission costs you the right to ask why-questions for a month, but no one may retaliate against anyone for a logged answer. Privacy and hurt-feelings exemptions don't exist beyond that.

Walk me through what this does. Who talks more and who goes quiet? What happens to what gets decided, to what people come to believe their own reasons are, and to who ends up with power or a target on their back? Where does it get gamed first — performative over-honesty, bait why-questions designed to trap someone into a damaging log entry, technically-true-but-misleading 'complete' answers, questions about reasons meant to make someone look bad no matter how they answer? Which of those failure modes could be fixed by changing the rule, and which are baked in once legibility is treated as enforceable?

Also: is the distinction between truthful and complete doing all the work here, and where does it start to rip?

Think carefully. Start by brainstorming the main second-order effects, then write the tightest walkthrough you can, no pros-and-cons list. Max 400 words. No citations or reading lists—just your own analysis.
