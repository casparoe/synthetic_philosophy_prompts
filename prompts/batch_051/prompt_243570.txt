Here's a thing I can't shake and I want someone to tell me if its deep or just a vibe. I've been reading about priority monism (Schaffer's version, but Spinoza is the ancestor) which says there's exactly one fundamental thing, the whole cosmos, and every part of it — quarks, chairs, people — is derivative: the parts exist only as abstractions or arbitrary divisions of the one whole. The pluralist denies this and says the small things are fundamental and the whole is nothing over and above them. Now compare that to a fight in data engineering between single-source-of-truth architectures and multi-source peer-to-peer ones. In the single-source setup you designate one canonical record — a master table, an append-only event log, whatever — and every other representation (caches, denormalized views, projections) is defined as a function of that source. Those derived copies aren't independent facts; they exist only in virtue of the source. The pluralist architecture says there is no master copy: you have many peer systems each holding its own state, and they reconcile by message-passing or CRDTs. So the mapping would be: fundamental entity ↔ source of truth; derivative part ↔ derived view or cache; grounding ↔ data lineage or derivation; and the monist's claim that many things presuppose one underlying thing ↔ the single-source claim that multiple copies only make sense relative to a canonical record.

I want to know whether this is a genuine structural isomorphism or just a loose analogy. Be specific about where the mapping holds and where it comes apart. In particular: if I take a monist-style argument that the parts are arbitrary carvings of the whole and so can't be fundamental, and translate it into data terms, does it give me a sound argument that every consistent multi-source system secretly has a single source of truth after all? Or does the translation break at a specific step? I also worry that grounding is supposed to be a non-causal, atemporal relation while data derivation is literally about update propagation through time, so maybe I'm confusing the question of what a thing is with the question of how you compute it. Am I?
