Until my aunt's stroke I thought dying was just your heart stopping. After the stroke, the doctors said her whole brain had no activity, but a ventilator was breathing for her and her heart kept beating on its own; she was warm and her chest still moved. I thought she was already gone, and when my family finally let them stop the machines I didn't feel like she died then, that had already happened. But a friend's brother drowned and is now in something they call a permanent vegetative state: he breathes on his own, opens his eyes, has sleep-wake cycles, but they say there is no awareness and no chance of any coming back. I don't think he's dead, even though the guy he used to be seems gone. And I can't quite put my finger on the difference between him and my aunt.

There are some other cases I've been going on gut feeling about. A man's heart stops during surgery for maybe ninety seconds before they restart it: I'd never say he died on the table, he was just in danger. Another man's heart stops at home and can't be restarted, and the paramedics declare him dead on the spot, no brain scan or anything: that feels right even though his brain cells might still have had some life in them for a few minutes. Then there's locked-in syndrome, where someone is fully aware but almost completely paralyzed: obviously alive, even if an outsider might think there's nobody home. And the cryonics case, people frozen after their hearts stop, hoping future medicine can fix what killed them and revive them. I'd say they'r dead now, and if one got revived a hundred years later she would have been dead the whole time, not "suspended" or alive all along. But that makes me wonder whether my "irreversible" idea gives the word dead too much to do, since what's irreversible depends on what technology somebody happens to have in the future.

So is there a single principle about what death consists in that gets all those verdicts, or close? If not, tell me which of my gut calls are in tension with each other and which ones I should probably drop. And whatever principle is left over, what does it say about my aunt, my friend's brother, the man on the operating table, and a person whose entire brain is destroyed except the brainstem that keeps them breathing, which is the case I keep waking up thinking about?
