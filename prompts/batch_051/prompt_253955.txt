For econ class we’re doing public goods and tragedy of the commons, and my teacher gave us this paragraph from Hume’s Treatise of Human Nature, Book 3, Part 2, Section 7 (“Of the origin of government”):

“You have the same propension, that I have, in favour of what is contiguous above what is remote. You are, therefore, naturally carried to commit acts of injustice as well as me. Your example both pushes me forward in this way by imitation, and also affords me a new reason for any breach of equity, by shewing me, that I should be the cully of my integrity, if I alone shou’d impose on myself a severe restraint amidst the licentiousness of others.”

He said Hume is basically giving an early free-rider argument. I want to set it up as numbered steps and see if it actually goes through, because me and my freinds keep getting stuck on whether the last step follows. Here’s the argument I think Hume is making:

(1) People know that following the rules of justice keeps society stable and is in their long-term interest.
(2) But people’s passions respond more to what’s close and vivid than to what’s far off, even when the far-off thing matters more.
(3) So when an immediate chance to cheat or free-ride comes up, the near-term gain beats the remote benefit of obeying.
(4) Once a person expects others to cheat, he gets an extra reason to cheat too: if he’s the only one exercising restraint, he’s just being a sucker.
(5) Therefore rule-breaking becomes common, and society gets dangerous and unpredictable.
(6) The fix is government, which can make punishments immediate enough to outweigh the temptation.

But step (6) seems to require the very thing he says people can’t do: prefer a distant good over a near temptation. If people can’t resist near-term gain, how do they ever get together to create a government that restrains them? Does this argument actually show society collapses without government, or only that there’s a collective action problem? Is the argument valid? If not, which step exactly fails, and why? And if it is valid, would informal things like reputation or social norms fix the problem too, or does Hume’s reasoning rule those out?
