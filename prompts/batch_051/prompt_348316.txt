I've been developing a distinction I call same-source versus independent-source evidence, and I want it checked before I build anything further on it. The idea is this: evidence for the reliability of a belief-forming mechanism is same-source when it is produced by that very mechanism. Standard bootstrapping is the paradigm case. I look at a table, it looks red, I correctly believe the table is red, and from that belief I infer that my color vision got this one right; repeated across many occasions, this yields the conclusion that my color vision is generally reliable. The problem is usually posed against a reliabilist background — a true belief produced by a reliable process counts as knowledge, even if the subject cannot independently certify the process — which seems to leave the reliabilist unable to fault the reasoning. I suspect the fault lies not in reliability or in perception but in same-source evidence, and that the easy-knowledge cases (from the table is red, infer it is not a white table under red light) have exactly the same structure. Take the distinction in its strongest, most precise form, then tell me: is it coherent enough to do that diagnostic work, or does it collapse into something epistemologists already draw, such as epistemic circularity or premise- versus rule-circularity? And where does it break — give the clearest case where same-source evidence is plainly legitimate, or where independent-source evidence still bootstraps. End by saying which consideration would most likely change your overall verdict, and where your assessment is most fragile.
