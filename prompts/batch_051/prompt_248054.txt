I'm a novelist, and I've worked myself into a proper tangle over the word "fragile". In the scene I'm drafting, a glassblower's apprentice argues that a chandelier that has survived a century of air raids, minor earthquakes and two careless owners can't be fragile, because fragile just means "would break if given a modest knock", and the ugly thing never does. But that feels wrong to me: it's still thin glass, still the sort of object you'd call fragile, it just keeps being protected or getting lucky. A philosopher friend tells me this is an actual debate, with "finks" and "masks": cases where a thing has a disposition even though the standard "if you did X to it, it would Y" claim comes out false. I'd like a working definition of "fragile" that gets the following cases right (I've marked the verdicts I'd fight for, where I have one):

1. A bone-dry crystal wineglass dropped onto a stone floor from chest height: it shatters. Clearly fragile.
2. A rubber dog toy dropped the same way: it bounces. Clearly not fragile.
3. The same crystal wineglass packed inside a foam-lined crate: dropped in the crate from chest height, it doesn't break. I still want to call it fragile.
4. A delicate glass vase on a spring-loaded shelf: every time it starts to fall, the shelf fires a cushion underneath it, so it never hits the floor. Fragile or not? I genuinely can't decide.
5. A wineglass that looks identical to the first one, but its glass is laced with a compound that hardens on sudden impact, so the very impact of dropping it would make it stronger, not shatter it. Is it fragile?
6. A thin glass rod wrapped in layers of packing tape: you can bend it, drop it, rap it against a wall, nothing happens. I'd still say it's fragile underneath, but that may be me cheating.
7. A steel anvil. Not fragile.

Propose a definition, run it through all seven, and say where it forces you to contradict me. If your definition gives a surprising verdict on one of my cases, either defend it or revise the definition — I'd rather have a notion I can use in the novel than win an arguement with an imaginary glassblower.
