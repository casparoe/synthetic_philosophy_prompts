So two friends of mine, Sam and Noa, got way too invested in an argument at a barbecue about a hypothetical betting scheme. The scheme: every week, you put your whole current bankroll on a fair coin flip. Heads, you gain 50%; tails, you lose 40%. Each flip is independent, and any winnings are automatically reinvested, so next week's stake is the new total.

Sam starts: "It's an obvious yes. The expected value is positive: 0.5(1.5) + 0.5(0.6) = 1.05, so each dollar becomes $1.05 in expectation per week. Over 52 weeks that's 1.05^52, around 12.6 times your money. Only someone who hates free money turns that down."

Noa says: "That 12.6 is an average over every possible sequence of flips, but a person only actually lives one sequence. A typical sequence has about 26 heads and 26 tails, which leaves you with (1.5^26)(0.6^26) = 0.9^26, roughly six cents on the dollar. The expected value is being dragged up by the tiny probability of an absurd run of heads. If you're asking what will probably happen to you, you should be looking at something like the median path, not the mean."

Sam: "Saying 'what will probably happen to me' is just risk aversion wearing a statistics costume. If you're risk-neutral, positive expected value is the definition of a good bet. Noa clearly isn't risk-neutral, which is fine—she can scale the stake down, say to 10% of her bankroll each week, and take the positive expectation in smaller, less scary slices. But she can't tell me the all-in version is mathematically wrong."

Noa: "Scaling down changes the bet, so it doesn't rescue the all-in version. With all-in reinvestment, what compounds over time for a single wealth path is the geometric mean per flip, not the arithmetic mean. The geometric mean is sqrt(1.5 × 0.6) ≈ 0.949, so the all-in player is multiplying their money by about 0.949 per flip on the typical path—losing about 5% per flip—no matter what the ensemble average says. The gap between the 1.05 arithmetic mean and the 0.949 geometric mean is what the whole argument is about."

Sam: "By that logic you'd also refuse a single one-off flip with the exact same payouts, since it has the same arithmetic and geometric means. And if your real rule is 'maximize expected log wealth,' then you need to defend that utility function as something other than your personal risk aversion. Otherwise you're not correcting my math, you're just telling me you're more scared of losses than I am."

Noa: "A one-off flip is different because there's no compounding, so the time average and the ensemble average don't come apart there the way they do over 52 reinvested flips. For a multiplicative process, the thing that determines whether a single path tends to grow or shrink is the expected log return, not the log of the expected return. That's not a taste; it's what the dynamics identify."
