You are a constitutional lawyer and a philosopher of antidiscrimination law, expert in the concept of suspect classifications and in equal-protection reasoning. Think carefully and argue precisely; before giving your recommendation, briefly brainstorm the candidate principles that fit the entire track record, then say which one you would adopt and why.

I sit on the Equal Treatment Bench of the Supreme Tribunal of Vellum, which enforces a constitutional equality clause. The Bench has long distinguished between classifications on "suspect grounds," which receive strict scrutiny and are almost always invalidated, and classifications on other grounds, which receive rational-basis review and are usually upheld. But our precedents do not settle which feature makes a ground suspect, and we must now decide a new case consistently with our own earlier rulings.

Our earlier rulings are these:

1. Osei v. Ministry of Public Employment. The Ministry reserved senior civil-service posts for persons of "indigenous Vellic ancestry." We struck the statute down as a classification on a suspect ground. We emphasized that ancestry is not chosen and that non-indigenous groups have endured long-standing discrimination and political exclusion.

2. Feldbaum v. Civil Aviation Board. A regulation compelled commercial pilots to retire at sixty. We upheld it under rational-basis review. We said age is not a badge of historic subordination, and age-based line-drawing often tracks legitimate ability concerns.

3. Mazar v. Ministry of Education. A regulation barred members of the Kuldunite confession, a small religious minority, from teaching in public schools. We struck it down as suspect. We noted that the Kuldunites had suffered centuries of persecution and remained a small, insular community with little political influence; we did not require members to renounce their faith as the price of equal treatment.

4. Rens v. Municipal Housing Authority. A rule denied public housing to daily tobacco smokers. We upheld it as not suspect. Smoking, we said, is a behavior within the person's control, and smokers are not a group with a history of state-sponsored subordination.

5. Dzimitrowicz v. National Jury Service. A statute excluded persons with epilepsy from jury service. We struck it down as suspect. The judgment stressed that epilepsy is unchosen, that persons with epilepsy have faced a long history of medicalized exclusion and stigma, and that they are too dispersed to form an effective political bloc.

The case now before us is Aurelius Municipal Council v. Darianar Community Association. The Council passed a by-law denying eligibility for a new child allowance to any household whose first language, as recorded in the civil registry, is Darianar. The Darianar are a small minority — roughly one percent of the population — who began arriving in Vellum about twenty years ago. They are concentrated in a few municipal wards, have almost no representation in elected office, and are widely described as political outsiders. However, there is no history in Vellum of enslavement, segregation, or state persecution of the Darianar as a group, and the record discloses no entrenched societal stigma of the kind Vellum has directed at indigenous minorities or at the Kuldunites. The Council maintains that the allowance is simply administered in Vellic, and that a household may change its registered first language only if it genuinely uses Vellic at home, which most Darianar-speaking households do not.

If the Bench wants to decide this new case consistently with its five precedents, how should it rule on the by-law, and what would each competing principle predict? Which of the five precedents is the closest analog under each candidate principle? What principle would the Bench be adopting with each possible holding? Finally, specify a further hypothetical case — not before us now — whose resolution would most cleanly separate the surviving principles.
