I'm grading a short-answer question in a "reasoning with data" class and want a second opinion on one student's answer. The question was:

"A hospital compares two surgical teams, A and B, on routine and complex procedures. For routine procedures, team A succeeds 90% of the time and team B succeeds 85% of the time. For complex procedures, team A succeeds 60% of the time and team B succeeds 55% of the time. Yet when the hospital pools all procedures together, team B has the higher overall success rate. Explain how this can happen, and say what it tells us about comparing aggregate success rates between hospitals."

The student wrote:

"This is Simpson's paradox. Team A is better at both types of surgery, but team B looks better overall because the pooled rate is a weighted average and the two teams do very different mixes of procedures. Team B does mostly routine cases, where success is high for everyone, while team A does a lot of complex cases, which drags their average down. The pooled comparison is confounded by case mix, so the overall comparison is spurious in the same way a correlation is spurious when a hidden variable drives it. The lesson is that you should always trust the dissaggregated rates and ignore the pooled rate."

Could you grade this out of 10 and give the point-by-point comments you'd actually write on it, not just a general impression?
