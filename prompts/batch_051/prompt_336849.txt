An otherwise humane person will watch undercover footage from an intensive pig farm with evident distress, sign a petition against farrowing crates, and then order farmed salmon and a whole crab at dinner the same evening. The salmon was reared in a crowded net pen and killed by being hauled into air and left to asphyxiate; the crab was in all likelihood boiled alive, a method that would be regarded as cruelty if applied to the pig. The person knows this in outline, and it does not move them, or at least does not move them enough to change the order. I keep returning to this asymmetry and I want to know whether anything justifies it, as opposed to merely explaining it.

The obvious candidate is that fish and crustaceans either cannot suffer in a morally significant way, or suffer so much less that the comparison with the pig is simply a mistake. But notice how that defence functions: it is usually offered by people who have not examined the evidence, and it places the burden of uncertainty on the creature rather than on the diner, which sits oddly with how moral caution normally works when the evidence is contested. A second candidate is that the difference lies in cognitive sophistication rather than mere sentience; a third is that we simply cannot attend to every animal, and fish are where attention gives out. The difficulty is that the third candidate seems to make the practice worse, not more defensible: the greater the number, the greater the harm, not the smaller.

I am not asking for a summary of the empirical literature on fish sentience. I am asking for the strongest principle that generates both judgements, tested against the hardest nearby case: a fish and a mammal killed by materially the same method, the only difference being the species. If the principle fails there, say so. Should we keep the asymmetry, reverse it, or give it up?
