I've been arguing with a friend about the sorites paradox for a couple weeks and I ended up with a distinction I want someone to test. I'm calling them ostensive predicates and criterial predicates. An ostensive predicate is one you basically learn by being shown clear positive and negative cases and then left to extrapolate: 'heap,' 'bald,' 'chair,' 'red.' A criterial predicate is one you learn by being given a checklist or decision procedure: 'prime number,' 'bachelor,' 'eligible voter,' 'misdemeanor under the relevant statute.' My claim is that ostensive predicates are always sorites-suseptible—there will be a chain of tiny differences from a clear yes to a clear no with no non-arbitrary place to draw the line—while criterial predicates are only sorites-susceptible if at least one of their criteria is itself vague. Otherwise the checklist just sets the line, even if we sometimes don't know the facts well enough to apply it.

The payoff I'm after is a prediction about which predicates generate paradoxes and which don't, and a way of saying the sorites is really about how meanings get transmitted (examples vs. rules) rather than about truth-value gaps or unknowable cutoffs. I'd guess this is already in the literature under a name I don't know—tolerance, open texture, family resemblance, something like that—but I want to know whether the actual claim is true, not just familiar-sounding. Things I'm worried about: 'adult' is learned ostensively by kids but has a legal cutoff, so is it a counterexample or does the law just replace the predicate? 'chair' is ostensive but maybe a hidden functional criterion does the work. And 'tall' seems to need a comparison class, which doesn't fit either box cleanly. Give me the strongest version of my distinction, say what it gets right, and then break it as hard as you can.
