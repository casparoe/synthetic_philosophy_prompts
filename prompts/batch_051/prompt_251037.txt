You're an expert on decision theory and a professional philosopher, and you're good at turning formal ideas into checklists that actual humans can run. Think carefully, and before you give the final procedure, brainstorm a couple of candidate procedures and say why you're rejecting them.

I'm a junior in high school and the treasurer of my student council. We make spending decisions every few weeks — dances, fundraisers, spirit week, club fairs — with budgets from a few hundred to a couple thousand dollars. The problem is that alot of the time we genuinely can't assign probabilities: a brand-new assistant principal has to approve our event and we've never met her, a vendor might flake, a new venue has no track record, a TikTok post might blow up or get zero views. So we're choosing under ignorance, not risk.

I've read a little about three rules for deciding under ignorance. Maximin says pick the option whose worst possible outcome is best. Minimax regret says pick the option that minimizes how much you'd regret your choice once you find out what happened, where regret is how much better some other option would have been. And then there's the Rawls vs Harsanyi fight, at least as I understand it: Rawls, behind a veil of ignorance, goes maximin, while Harsanyi says to give every possible state equal probability and pick the option with the best average payoff. I don't know which of these, if any, should govern a student council.

Please write me a reusable decision proceedure my council could actually run in 20 minutes before a vote. It should be a checklist or a flowchart in words: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it hands the decision to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, and about where the procedure will misfire. Don't just tell me it depends — the whole point is that we need something we can run when we're tired and the meeting is running long. There are five of us, none of us know statistics, and our advisor has to sign off on anything over $300, so the procedure has to be simple enough that she can see why we made the call.

Then run the procedure on three cases:
1. A bake sale we know will make about $200, versus a car wash that could make about $500 if the new assistant principal approves it and loses us the $150 supplies deposit if she doesn't. We've never met her and have no idea which way she leans.
2. Booking the hotel ballroom for the spring dance ($900, we know it's fine but it's boring) versus a new rooftop venue ($400, could be amazing or could be a mess — bad sound, no rain plan, an owner who overbooks). No track record at all.
3. Buying merch in bulk for a brand-new event (cheap per item but we're stuck with boxes of unsold stuff if turnout is low) versus buying small batches at a higher per-item cost and reordering if it goes well.

Make sure at least one of these comes out as an answer the council is going to hate, and say which one and why. Also tell me what the procedure can't do — what would still be left for us to fight about after we've used it.
