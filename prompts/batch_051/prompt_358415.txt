Act as an epistemologist who works on higher-order evidence and rationality. Before settling, brainstorm a couple of ways my policy could go wrong, then give me your verdict in under 200 words.

Here's a policy I've been running for a while. When I've actually done the work—sat down, gone through the arguments, weighed the objections, and formed a considered view—I don't let evidence about my own condition move my confidence. Tired, stressed, a bit drunk, outnumbered by people I respect: none of it matters directly. At most it's a reason to go back and re-do the first-order reasoning. If I re-check and find no flaw, I stay exactly where I was. My thinking is tht my considered view is already my best handling of the first-order evidence, and higher-order evidence is only a general fact about how often people in bad states get things wrong—not a fact about the question itself. If I let it move me directly, I'd be beleiving something while also thinking the process behind it was unreliable, and that seems like epistemic akrasia. My friend calls this a shield for my opinions. He says the entire point of higher-order evidence is that it rationally pressures you even when you can't spot the first-order mistake—since you obviously can't always spot your own mistakes.

Is this policy ever defensible? Is there a restated version that keeps the benefit (not being pushed around just because I'm tired or outnumbered) without walling off legitimate evidence? And is there any way to tell from the inside whether I'm being principled or just protecting myself?
