I've been arguing with my roomate who works in NLP, and I've started using a term I made up: 'shadow translation.' My claim is that a statistical translation system doesn't translate; it renders. Here's the distinction. Rendering is producing a target-language sentence that is the kind of sentence a human translator would probably write for the source — right words, right idioms, plausible syntax, natural collocations. Translating is something stricter: it means carrying over what the source actually asserts and denies, and what a speaker would be answerable for in saying it — all the stuff a smooth sentence can quietly drop. For example, a system might turn 'I don't exactly disagree' into a flat agreement or a flat denial depending on which is more probable, and a benchmark of the usual kind would never notice, because those benchmarks test smooth target-language strings — they test rendering. So my little theory is that the whole 'does the model understand?' debate is misframed. It doesn't matter whether the system understands. Translation is a normative relation: a translation inherits obligations from the source text, and a purely statistical process optimizes the wrong thing no matter how good it gets. I gather this is in the neighborhood of Hofstadter's complaint that statistical translation is shallow.

Has it already been worked out under another name in philosophy of language or translation studies? And where does it break — what's the hardest case where my 'answerability' requirement either dissolves or rules out something that is obviously translation?
