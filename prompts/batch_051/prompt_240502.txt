After a year of our reading group ending every session in the same fight, we're forcing the two most stubborn members to coauthor one document. The topic is transformative experience, and the camps are as follows. One of them — call her the acquaintance firster — thinks that for a choice that is both epistemically transformative (you can't know what the outcome is like from the inside until you've had it) and personally transformative (having it would change the very values you'd use to judge it afterward), there is no well-formed expected-utility calculation to make: the utilities are missing because the first-personal aquaintance is missing. So testimony from people who have done it can narrow things but can never close that gap, and the most honest thing to say is that the choice is rationally underdetermined. The other — call him the sufficer — thinks the gap is real but grossly overstated: you can rationally rank the options by combining a broad, honest sample of first-person reports with your own stable higher-order preferences (you mostly want to become a person whose values you can endorse later), and he insists that treating the status quo as the safe default is just hiding the ball, because refusing to change is itself a choice with the same missing first-personal information.

Write the joint statement they should have produced. It should have: (1) a numbered list of claims both can sign without wincing — not the thin stuff like some experiences change people, but the substantive points about evidence, utility, and how much precision a utility ranking actually needs; (2) a disagreements table in which each row gives the exact subquestion, the firster's answer in wording she accepts, and the sufficer's answer in wording he accepts, plus a column marking whether the disagreement is substantive or just a fight over words; and (3) a section on cruxes: the two or three subquestions such that settling them would genuinely move at least one camp, with for each crux a concrete case or bit of evidence they both agree bears on it and what each side predicts in advance. I'd push them to consider a crux about whether post-hoc endorsement by people who took the transformative path counts as evidence about the rationality of the choice ex ante, and one about whether there's really an asymmetry between acting and refraining, but add whatever cruxes a good version of this document would need.
