I recently started volunteering with a project that is developing a first orthography for a language that has until now been spoken only. The committee has a recurring argument that always ends the same way: someone says the new script should "write it the way it sounds," and the room treats that as the end of the discussion. Last month the disputed case was a consonant that is pronounced one way at the end of a word and another way in the middle, because the same morpheme alternates between, say, [t] and [d] depending on what follows. One member argued we should keep a single symbol so that related words remain visually connected; another replied that we are building an alphabet, and the whole point of an alphabet is to record pronunciation, so we should write two symbols, one for each pronunciation. The slogan carried the day, but I suspect it is concealing several different claims that nobody separated.

Please take apart the one-liner "write it the way it sounds." Lay out the distinct precise theses it could be asserting: about biunique letter–sound correspondence, about surface pronunciation versus underlying morphological identity, about what an alphabet is a notation for, about how far orthographies should track phonemes as opposed to morphemes, about what makes a script learnable, and anything else relevant. For each thesis, say whether it is a tautology, a testable empirical claim, a design principle, or a misleading formulation; how defensible it is; and whether that particular thesis would actually support the conclusion that the alternating consonant should get two symbols rather than one. I want a menu of readings with verdicts and, in particular, an account of where the phrase "the way it sounds" does illegitimate work when it is used to settle questions that are partly about morphology, not just sound.
