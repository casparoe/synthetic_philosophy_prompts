You are a philosopher of cognitive science with a strong background in both philosophy of mind and the empirical study of perception. I am trying to work through a puzzle about the timing of conscious experience, and I would like you to reason through it with me carefully. Consider the color phi phenomenon. In the standard demonstration, a red dot is flashed briefly at one location, and shortly afterwards a green dot is flashed briefly at a nearby location. Subjects do not report two stationary flashes; they report a single dot that appears to move across the intervening space and change from red to green roughly halfway along its apparent path. That is already strange, since no moving dot was physically presented. But the deeper puzzle is temporal: the color change is represented as occurring in the middle of the apparent motion — earlier in the represented sequence than the onset of the green dot — even though the information that the second dot was green could only have arrived at the brain with, or after, that second flash. The brain seems to have inserted later-arriving information into an earlier part of the experience.

There is an elegant way of dissolving the puzzle, associated with Daniel Dennett, which says that the two available ways of telling the story differ only verbally. On the Orwellian telling, the subject first consciously experienced the dot as red and moving, and then, once the green flash was processed, the brain retroactively edited the memory so that the subject now recalls the dot as having changed to green mid-path. On the Stalinesque telling, consciousness itself was delayed: the brain waited for all the relevant information before constructing a single integrated experience, so there never was an original conscious experience of an all-red moving dot — the first conscious episode already contained the color change. What attracts me to the Dennettian line is that it refuses to posit either an unverifiable moment at which something actually became conscious or a homunculus in a Cartesian theater to whom the final version is presented. It treats the two stories as alternative descriptions of the same underlying processing and concludes that the question cannot be answered because there is nothing real to answer.

And yet I cannot shake the intuition that there is a fact of the matter here, and that I know it from the inside. It seems obvious that either I was conscious of the moving dot before the green flash's information arrived — in which case the later editing was Orwellian — or I was not — in which case the construction was Stalinesque. These are different histories of my own stream of consciousness, and one of them is true of me. I do not see how a difference in the timing of my own conscious awareness could be a distinction without a difference. So which should give way: the theory that dissolves the distinction on the grounds that no evidence could ever tell the two histories apart, or the introspective certainty that my experience has a determinate temporal structure? Walk me through what each concession costs, whether there is a reading on which the theory and the intuition are compatible, and what would justify debunking the intuition rather than amending the theory — or the reverse. Please think carefully, and begin by briefly reconstructing each side at its strongest before giving your verdict.
