I am giving a workshop paper in three weeks to a university ethics center's reading group. The paper defends this thesis: in the standard sweatshop case, where a worker takes an export-factory job that is better than her actual alternatives even though conditions are harsh, pay is low, hours are long, and exit is costly, the exchange is consensual and mutually beneficial relative to the worker's feasible options, yet it can still be exploitative in the moralized sense of taking unfair advantage of background vulnerability; that exploitation is a real normative defect but is not by itself enough to make the transaction wrong or to justify consumer boycotts and import bans, because the decisive wrongs sit upstream in background poverty, monopsony, and missing labor protections rather than in the micro-level wage bargain itself. The audience is about twelve people: four broadly Kantian moral philosophers, two development economists who accept the standard comparative-advantage and better-than-the-alternatives story, three global-justice ethicists who work on structural injustice, and one Catholic social teaching scholar. I expect the Kantians to push on whether consent under constrained circumstances validates anything; the economists to ask what policy could make workers better off without destroying the jobs; the global-justice people to argue I have mislocated the wrongness; and the Catholic scholar to press on dignity and a just wage.

Give me a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the thesis. Write it as a numbered list of exactly five items, each item one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion. Order the items by how likely and how damaging the objection is, phrase each objection the way this particular audience would put it, identify the likely speaker, say what I should concede and what I should hold, give a short reply, and note where the reply is weakest. Among the five, include and label the question I am hoping nobody asks.
