You are a professional philosopher who specializes in moral philosophy and in formal models of collective action. I would like you to think carefully about the following problem, and I want you to begin by briefly reconstructing the dialectical structure of the relevant argument before you press on to your own analysis.

Consider Derek Parfit's harmless torturers thought experiment. In its well-known form, a thousand people each press a button once; each press increases an electric current by a microamount and thereby produces an increment of pain that is too small for the victim to notice on its own; after all thousand presses, the victim is in agony. The case is used to test the inefficacy objection and its limits: each individual can plausibly say that their own press makes no perceptible difference, and yet the thousand presses together cause serious harm. The same structure recurs in debates about climate change, meat consumption, and voting, where a single omitted flight, a single refused hamburger, or a single ballot can seem to make no difference to the aggregate outcome.

Now consider a precise variant. Keep the thousand agents and thousand buttons, but replace the smooth accumulation of imperceptible pain with the following payoff structure. The victim's pain system has a threshold T, drawn uniformly at random from the integers 1 through 1000 before anyone acts, and unknown to the agents. If the number of buttons pressed is less than T, the victim feels no pain; if the number pressed reaches T, the victim immediately feels an agony worth 1000 units of harm, and all subsequent presses add nothing. Every agent knows the setup, knows that T is uniform over the integers 1 through 1000, but does not know which value T has taken. Assume that each agent presses exactly once, and that each agent, when deciding, holds fixed that all 999 others will press.

Work through how the moral analysis changes between the original case and this threshold variant. In particular: (i) state precisely the sense in which each press in the original case is said to make no perceptible difference; (ii) holding fixed that the other 999 agents press, compute the probability that a single agent's press changes what the victim feels, and then compute the expected felt harm attributable to that press; (iii) explain how a sophisticated defender of the inefficacy objection might argue that the variant still does not give any individual a decisive reason to refrain, and how an act-consequentialist might reply; and (iv) say what the contrast between the two cases shows about the difference between making no ex post difference and making a small expected difference, as that distinction bears on the climate, meat, and voting versions of the objection. Do not settle for a vague verdict; keep the ex ante and ex post perspectives clearly separated and state which one is doing the work at each step.
