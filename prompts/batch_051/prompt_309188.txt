I'm preparing a decision tree for a graduate seminar on sovereignty and international institutions, on the question of what it is for a state to join a customs union or confederation and whether—and in what sense—its sovereignty survives membership. The tree should take readers from a starting commitment and sort them into named positions through forced-choice questions about cases. Aim for six leaves, with no more than eight branch questions, and do not let the tree collapse into a standard list of "EU theories."

Start from this split: once two states have given a joint body the power to set their common external tariff, can the sentence "both states are still sovereign" still be true? Build the tree from the logical space of answers rather than from a list of established labels. First identify the distinctions that actually do the work—for example, whether sovereignty is divisible, whether exit must remain available if a member is to count as sovereign, whether a non-state institution can itself be sovereign over a transferred competence, and whether "sovereignty" is the right notion at all—then locate the positions in the resulting space, and mark any combinations of answers that nobody occupies, briefly diagnosing each empty combination as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

The leaves must include at least these five: the indivisible-sovereignty or delegation view, on which sovereignty cannot be divided and the state keeps it while the union exercises only delegated powers; the pooling view, on which the member states exercise sovereignty jointly over the transferred matters; the split-sovereignty view, on which the union itself can be sovereign over external trade while the member states remain sovereign over other matters; the post-sovereignty or functionalist view, on which the question "who is sovereign?" should be replaced by talk about competences; and the recognition-based view, on which sovereignty is a status conferred by other states and internal transfers of competence do not change it. If any two of these cannot be separated by a question in the tree, say so explicitly and explain where the tree would misclassify someone.

Use at least the following cases in the branch questions: (1) a customs union whose council sets the external tariff by majority vote, and a member state is outvoted yet must apply the tariff; (2) a union from which a member cannot withdraw without the consent of all the others—can that member still count as sovereign?; (3) the union signs a trade treaty that binds a member state that voted against it; (4) a confederation whose central authority is revocable by any member at will and cannot act directly on individuals. Every branch node should be phrased as a question a student could answer without already knowing which position it leads to, and the names of the views should appear only at the leaves. Each leaf should carry one sentence on the view's most awkward commitment in light of the cases above.
