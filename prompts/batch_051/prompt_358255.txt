I cover comparative politics and the erosion of democratic institutions, and I keep running into the same problem on deadline: "democratic backsliding" is used for everything from court-packing and media capture to ordinary policies the writer simply dislikes, while genuinely dangerous changes get described in hedged language until they are hard to reverse. Before I use the term again, I want the specification that a defensible theory of democratic backsliding has to meet, where the purpose is fixed: a journalist deciding on deadline whether the term applies and what evidence must be present before she writes it. Give me the requirements list, with the case or consideration behind each. A serviceable account must capture the cases it cannot afford to miss and exclude the cases it cannot afford to swallow — the past fifteen years in Hungary, Poland, and Turkey, and the recent dispute over the United States. It must respect distinctions a crude account collapses, such as an illiberal policy outcome versus dismantling the institutions that could reverse it, a lost election versus an unfree one, and polarization versus declining public support for democratic norms. It must also say which observable changes are load-bearing — electoral integrity, judicial independence, media capture, executive aggrandizement, or something else — and who, if anyone, is entitled to decide that the threshold has been crossed. Finally, tell me which requirements are non-negotiable and which are the preferences of one camp, which single requirement does the most work to keep the term from being a partisan label, and whether any two requirements cannot be jointly satisfied. Keep any scorecard of existing accounts brief; the criteria are what I need.
