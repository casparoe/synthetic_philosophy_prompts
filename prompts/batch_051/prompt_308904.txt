I have come to read Marx's treatment of technology and automation as a rejection of technological determinism. On my view, for Marx, machinery never creates value, however much material wealth it helps produce; it only transfers its own value bit by bit through depreciation. Under capitalism, automation matters primarily as a lever for relative surplus value: it cheapens the goods workers need, thereby shrinking the necessary labor time within a working day of fixed length, and it throws workers into a relative surplus population whose competition disciplines the wages of those who remain. The emancipatory potential of the same machinery — a shorter working day, less toil — is real, but it is realized only through changed social relations and class struggle, not by the technology itself. What I want is an honest map of what would count against this position. Which historical and economic developments would genuinely tell against it — the long-run decline of the working day under capitalism, rising real wages and expanding skilled technical labor alongside mechanization, the cheapening of wage goods, or capitalism's recurrent ability to generate new labor-absorbing sectors rather than a permanent reserve army? Which of these would a committed defender of my view concede as counting against it, and which are merely complications the view can absorb? I would also like to know whether the position has been qualified into unfalsifiability, and if it has, whether that is a problem for a thesis of this kind. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward.
