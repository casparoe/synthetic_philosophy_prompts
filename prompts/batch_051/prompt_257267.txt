Ordinary civil dating runs ...1 BCE, 1 CE, 2 CE...: there is no year 0, and the interval from the start of 1 BCE to the start of 1 CE is one year even though naive subtraction across the boundary (1 minus −1) gives two. Astronomers who must do arithmetic with dates quietly adopt a different, coordinate-style scheme (...−2, −1, 0, 1, 2...), in which 0 is 1 BCE and −1 is 2 BCE, so that subtraction yields actual durations. Yet the same societies that omit year zero are perfectly comfortable with zero-based counting elsewhere: the Unix clock counts seconds from 0 at 1970-01-01T00:00:00 UTC, and programmers index arrays from 0. What view of time, of number, and of what it is to date an event does the no-year-zero convention presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory, distinguishing what the convention strictly presupposes from what it merely fits with and where it is philosophically underdetermined. In particular, say whether the astronomers' negative years are a mere notational repair of the same underlying picture or a genuine change in what a year-number means, and say what would have to change — in calendar design and in ordinary historical thought — if we replaced ordinal year-counting with a single coordinate axis anchored at an origin chosen on purpose rather than inherited.
