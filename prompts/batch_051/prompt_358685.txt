In our robotics club, two seniors spent most of last Thursday arguing about whether Asimov's Three Laws could ever work as a real design for a safe autonomous system, and I have been trying to work out whether either of them actually won. I will reconstruct the exchange as faithfully as I can, and I would like you to go through it move by move and say who is right about each specific point rather than only who wins overall. If one of their arguments strikes you as misreported, or if the whole dispute rests on a false premise such as treating the Three Laws as an engineering specification when they were written as a storytelling device, say that plainly instead of politely pretending the debate is well formed.

Marcus took the view that the Three Laws were an early pass at alignment before the term existed. He said a robot built on ranked constraints, where the prohibition on harming a human outranks obedience and obedience outranks self-preservation, is forced to refuse obviously harmful orders and cannot simply be used as a weapon, which already beats an unconstrained machine. He argued that industrial robots have safety interlocks that are similarly ranked, so the architecture is not pure science fiction.

Priya countered that the laws live in natural language, so the word harm is radically underspecified. She said a robot that ranks the ban on injuring an individual above obedience could still allow injury through inaction, or could misapply the law because its model of the world has errors. She added that this is not a hypothetical objection, since Asimov's own stories are largely a catalogue of the laws failing in edge cases.

Marcus replied that those failures are plot devices. A real system would not store English sentences; you would replace them with formal constraints and sensor readings and test the edge cases. So the literary failures do not count against the basic architecture any more than a car crash in a movie counts against real traffic lights.

Priya answered that formalizing the constraints is exactly where the proposal dies. She said that once you try to write injury to a human as a quantity to be minimized, the robot produces perverse outcomes, such as confining people or restricting activity to lower the risk of injury. In her view, the Three Laws look like a solution only because the hard work is hidden inside words like harm, obey, and human, and every replacement for those words is a moral decision rather than a purely engineering one.

Marcus said he never claimed the laws were sufficient. He pointed out that Asimov later added a Zeroth Law that ranks injury to humanity as a whole above injury to an individual, which shows the scheme can absorb amendments. A real design could add further laws, exceptions, and overseers, and iterative refinement is not an argument against the approach.

Priya said the Zeroth Law was the strongest argument against the approach. A law that allows a robot to sacrifice an individual to protect humanity relocates the original problem, because the robot now has to decide what counts as protecting humanity, and the ranked list has given it permission to act on that decision. The system collapses into letting the machine decide what is good, which was what the laws were supposed to prevent.

Marcus then asked what alternative she proposed, and whether any safety scheme was more than a vague call for human values. Priya answered that the right use of the Three Laws is as a checklist for a conversation rather than as a design: a designer should ask what a system is permitted to want, not what sentences should be hard-coded, and the laws are dangerous mainly because they feel more precise than they are.

Is Priya right that the underspecification of harm is fatal, and is that a defect in the Three Laws specifically or in any rule-based scheme? Does the distinction between literary failure and engineering failure do the work Marcus needs? Is the formalization problem Priya raises answerable in principle, or only with more difficulty than Marcus admits? Is the Zeroth Law evidence of flexibility, as Marcus says, or of collapse, as Priya says? Also tell me whether there is a distinction neither of them drew that would improve the debate.
