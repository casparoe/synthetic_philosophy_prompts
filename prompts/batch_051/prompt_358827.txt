A friend of mine has a whole set of strong opinions about what counts as courage, and I've trying to reverse-engineer the rule. Here are four of his verdicts.

He says a soldier under fire who is terrified but moves to help his pinned-down squad is obviously courageous. He says a guy who free-solos a cliff face for a sponsorship deal and says he doesn't really feel fear anymore is not courageous—its just recklessness and ego, because the aim poisons it. He says a nurse who keeps showing up during an outbreak, exhausted and afraid, partly because she needs the paycheck, is still courageous; the mixed motive doesn't cancel it. And he says a bystander who calls the cops from his locked car while watching a fight is a good citizen but not brave, because he never exposed himself to any risk.

Now a new case: a very experienced firefighter walks into a collapsing house to pull out a child. He's fully aware of the risk and decides to go in, but by his own account he wasn't afraid in that moment—he's been in hundreds of fires and the fear just wasn't there. So, danger: yes. Good aim: yes. Felt fear: no.

What should my friend say about the firefighter if he wants to stay consistent with the four earlier verdicts? Which of the four is the closest analog, and if the earlier verdicts genuinely underdetermine it, what rule would each possible answer be committing him to? Give your best guess, and say what a further case would have to look like to settle which rule he actually holds.
