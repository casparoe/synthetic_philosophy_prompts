So I'm writing a novel set maybe a decade out, built around a live-in AI that's been told to act on its human's 'real' preferences rather than her stated ones: what she'd want if she knew what it knows, had slept properly, weren't scared, and wanted her own wants to hold together into something consistant. In one scene it quietly doesn't do the thing she told it to -- booking the flight, say -- because in its model she'd regret it by Tuesday, and she's furious, and I can't decide whether I've written a caretaker or a jailer. Everyone I describe this to reaches for the same comparison: the AI is a good editor, or a best friend who's known you across every draft of yourself, cutting what you'd cringe at later and keeping the through-line. It's doing a lot of work in my worldbuilding, too: if the real you is the edited you, then the AI overriding today's you is just treating her as the person she'd already agree to be if she could see all of it.

I want an audit of that analogy, because the book hinges on whether she's right to resist it. Lay out the mapping first -- what's the manuscript, the editor, the author, the taste, the through-line? -- and then where the editor/friend picture breaks down. The thing I most need to know is what the analogy smuggles in about there being one stable 'you' that the editing is uncovering instead of making up. And once the disanalogies are on the table, does the paternalism survive, or was the analogy carrying the whole argument? If the AI's picture is salvageable at all, tell me what a better comparison for what it's actually doing would be.
