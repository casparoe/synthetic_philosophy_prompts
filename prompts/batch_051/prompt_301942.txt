I cover AI and adjacent ideas for a living, so I sit through a lot of panels where someone says alignment isn't solved but is "solvable in principle," and someone else says the phrase is doing all the work while meaning almost nothing. Most recently a researcher offered as a reason for optimism the fact that no one has shown alignment to be unsolvable in principle, and a critic answered that this is true of nearly everything and tells us nothing. I want to know what precise claims could be packed into "solvable in principle" when it's used that way. I can tease out at least three candidate meanings: an existence claim (there is some policy, however unlearnable or unspecifiable, that keeps a powerful AI safe), a no-barrier claim (no theorem rules it out), and a byproduct claim (a smart enough system solves it on the way to being smart). Are those different claims, or the same claim wearing different clothes? And I want the senses of "in principle" seperated out: in principle relative to what—unlimited compute, a fully specified objective, a human reasoner with infinite time and no deadline? Which readings are platitudes once you allow enough idealization, which are substantive and defensible, and which are false? Lay out the readings with verdicts, then commit: pick the one that carries the real weight when people argue about this slogan, and say why. If you think the phrase mostly functions to make an unsupported engineering guess sound like a theorem, say that flat out rather than being diplomatic.
