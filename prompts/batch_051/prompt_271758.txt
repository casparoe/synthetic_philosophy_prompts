I've been trying to straighten out whether Ryle's category mistake charge against Cartesian dualism actually does any independent work, and I'd like comments on the setup and on whether the argument goes through. My rough essay idea is this. There seem to be two different things people call category mistakes. One is a kind of type error: an expression of one logical type gets slotted into a position that calls for another, the way saying that Saturday is in bed, or that the average taxpayer has two point four children, goes wrong before truth or evidence can even get a grip. The other is a substantive misdescription: you put something in the wrong explanatory class, like treating being north of something as if it were an object with mass, or treating a rate as if it were one of the parts of the system it describes. That second kind expresses a view; it may be false, but it is not nonsense. Ryle's complaint about the Cartesian picture, the hidden ghostly thing supposedly pulling the levers behind behavior, looks to me like the second kind, not the first. But he often writes as though the dualist has made a logical or linguistic blunder, which would be the first kind. If I'm right, the category mistake charge doesn't refute dualism; it mostly advertises that Ryle has already bought a physicalist picture of the mental. If I'm wrong, then I don't see how to make the stronger charge stick without first defending a theory of which types mental and physical expressions belong to, which is exactly the problem the dualist though was still open.

I'd like comments on whether these two kinds of category mistake really come apart or collapse into each other, whether it's fair to read Ryle as running them together, and whether either horn leaves him with anything more than a rhetorical point against dualism.
