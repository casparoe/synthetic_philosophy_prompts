I am a novelist, and I am currently writing a novel whose central character is a systems programmer who insists that, when she holds a large program in her head, she is not translating from English into code; the structure of the program is, she claims, the very shape of her thought. That has forced me to think seriously about whether programming is a language of thought, and I want an answer I can argue with rather than a survey of the available positions.

My confusion is deepened by a much older writer. In the Introduction to Leviathan (1651), Thomas Hobbes writes:

For seeing life is but a motion of Limbs, the begining whereof is in some principall part within; why may we not say, that all Automata (Engines that move themselves by springs and wheeles as doth a watch) have an artificiall life? For what is the Heart, but a Spring; and the Nerves, but so many Strings; and the Joynts, but so many Wheeles, giving motion to the whole Body, such as was intended by the Artificer?

I understand the surface point: the commonwealth is an artificial man, and sovereignty is its soul. But I am not sure what the passage commits Hobbes to about thought itself.

What I would like from you is twofold, and I ask you to be committed rather than balanced. First, how strongly should we read this passage? Is Hobbes saying that the activities of a living body are exhaustively mechanical in the way a watch is mechanical, such that the right arrangement of parts would produce actual thought, or is he offering a controlled analogy and nothing more? Take a definite interpretive position and defend it. Second, apply the reading you defend to the question I began with: if Hobbes is right, does programming count as a language of thought, or does it become something more like building an artificial body that can act without thinking? Say plainly where Hobbes's text has to be stretched to speak to modern programming, and say which of your claims you take to be the least secure.
