Suppose that, this year, a wealthy democracy that has for decades pursued an integration policy modeled on French laïcité — strict state secularism, no conspicuous religious symbols for pupils or public officials, no public funding of religious schools, and an official discourse of assimilation into a shared civic identity — enacts in a single package a Canadian-style regime of reasonable accommodation and official multiculturalism. The new framework imposes a legal duty on public institutions to accommodate religious difference up to the point of undue hardship, funds religious schools per pupil on equal terms with secular schools, and revises curricula and public ceremonies to give explicit recognition to the country's constituent cultural groups. Assume the legal change is stable for a generation. Give a reasoned prediction of how the society's integration regime actually operates twenty years later, addressing the public schools, the labor market, majority attitudes, and the internal politics of minority communities. Distinguish the predictions you regard as robust from those that depend on contestable assumptions, and say where your confidence is lowest and what would have to hold for you to be wrong.
