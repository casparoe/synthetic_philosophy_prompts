Right, I'm marking a midterm for my philosophy of science module and I'd like a second pair of eyes on one script. The question I set was:

Explain what inference to the best explanation (IBE) is, and give at least one concrete example from the history of science. Then set out the 'bad lot' objection and evaluate whether it shows that IBE cannot be a rule of rational inference.

Here's what the student wrote:

Inference to the best explanation is the rule that tells us to accept the hypothesis that, if true, would best explain our evidence. For example, when nineteenth-century astronomers noticed anomalies in Uranus's orbit they inferred the existence of Neptune, because an unseen planet was the best explanation of the data; Darwin's theory was likewise accepted partly because it best explained the fossil record, biogeography, and homologies. The general pattern is: given evidence E and competing hypotheses H1...Hn, infer the one that scores highest on the explanatory virtues — simplicity, scope, precision, fit with background knowledge.

The main objection is van Fraassen's 'bad lot' argument. Our comparison can only show that the chosen hypothesis is the best of the hypotheses we have thought of, not that it is true; the true hypothesis may not be in the lot at all. So IBE cannot be a truth-tracking rule of inference.

I don't think this objection works. The explanatory virtues are not merely aesthetic: simpler hypotheses have higher prior probabilities, so by Bayes' theorem the hypothesis that best explains the evidence will also tend to be the most probable. As van Fraassen himself notes in distinguishing the 'loveliest' from the 'likeliest' explanation, explanatory loveliness is a reliable guide to probability. And since IBE has in fact been the engine of successful science, it has a track record that justifies trusting it as a rational rule.
