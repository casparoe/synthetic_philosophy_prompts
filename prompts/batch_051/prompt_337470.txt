I’m in an ancient philosophy class and we’re working through Zeno’s arrow paradox. The professor gave us this paraphrase of Aristotle’s response in the Physics, Book VI, chapter 9. Aristotle reconstructs Zeno as saying something like: a thing is at rest when it occupies a space equal to itself; a flying arrow always occupies a space equal to itself at any instant; so it is at rest at every instant; so it is at rest for the whole flight. Aristotle then objects that the argument treats time as if it were made up of indivisible instants, but time is continuous, so the inference to “at rest throughout” doesn’t go through.

Then a guy in my study group summarized Aristotle’s objection like this: “Aristotle refutes Zeno by pointing out that rest requires a thing to be in the same place across two different instants. Since the arrow is never in the same place across two instants, it is not at rest at any instant; therefore it is moving at every instant. Zeno’s real mistake was thinking that instantaneous velocity is incoherent, whereas Aristotle shows it’s fine.”

I feel like these two interpretations are not the same, and the second might be smuggling in modern ideas about instantaneous velocity. Can you go through the study group guy’s summary sentence by sentance and tell me what a philosopher working on ancient physics would accept, what they’d wince at, and whether either version actually answers Zeno’s paradox? If the professor’s paraphrase is itself misleading, say that too.
