Two friends of mine have been arguing for months about a meditation tradition one of them belongs to. The practitioners describe a state they call a direct, nonconceptual insight into the nature of awareness. They insist that the insight cannot be put into words—not because they lack the vocabulary, but because it is in principle the kind of thing that resists propositional form; anything they say is, they claim, at best a pointer to what they mean, not a description of it. My friend says he has reached this state and now knows something he cannot state. Our other friend, a neuroscientist, says this is a familiar story: a strong feeling of clarity and significance can be produced by many things, from sleep deprivation to certain drugs, and she sees no reason to treat it as knowledge, since there is nothing to check or communicate.

I want to know how much of this disagreement can be settled by evidence rather than by stipulating a definition of knowledge. Imagine you could recruit a large group of practitioners who make this ineffability claim, along with a control group with no such training. Design a concrete research protocol—using behavioral tasks, measures of skill transfer, physiological recordings, blind expert ratings, longitudinal follow-ups, or any other methods—that would discriminate between two hypotheses: first, that practitioners have acquired a kind of non-propositional knowledge; second, that they are reliably producing a powerful but contentless experience. For each component of the protocol, say which result would support which hypothesis, and state exactly what that result would still fail to show. Finally, identify the point at which the two hypotheses stop being separable by evidence because the dispute has shifted to the concept of knowledge itself.
