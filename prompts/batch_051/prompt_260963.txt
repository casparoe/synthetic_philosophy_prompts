I teach philosophy, and for years I have followed a private rule about how I let myself pursue questions. Before I will take up a question in earnest, I ask whether reaching a settled answer would alter anything I would do, decide, teach, or recommend. If it would not, I drop the question, even when it continues to pull at me. The rule hardened after an embarrassing episode last term: I lost most of an evening trying to determine whether a small river in the region where I grew up had been navigable before it was canalized in the nineteenth century. I found a tentative answer, and then it struck me that if the answer had been the opposite, nothing in my conduct or outlook would change at all. I had satisfied a curiosity that was, by my own test, worth nothing. Since then I have applied the test more ruthlessly, and I am mostly satisfied with it.

A colleague has challenged me, however. She says my policy treats knowledge as though its only value were instrumental, as though a truth that would change no decision of mine were worthless to me, and as though curiosity were merely an itch to be ignored rather than a faculty pointing toward something good to have for its own sake. I feel the force of this. But I also worry that the unlimited alternative is no alternative at all: stated without restriction, it seems to license a finite and overburdened person in spending attention on anything whatsoever, simply because he happens to want to know. I do not mean to deny that knowledge can be good apart from its uses; I mean only to ask whether someone in my position is entitled to treat the question "would a settled answer change any decision?" as a standing filter on inquiry.

What I would like from you is an assessment of the policy as a policy, not a general account of the intrinsic value of knowledge. Is it defensible for a busy person with many practical obligations to use decision-relevance as a filter, and can the filter be restated so that it keeps its discipline while leaving room for what my colleague is pointing at? Please respond in continuous prose, as in a letter or an email: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
