I recently listened to a retrospective on the Oakland school board's Ebonics resolution and have not been able to stop thinking about it, in part because I grew up speaking a variety that my own teachers corrected as bad grammar, and I now work in a university writing center where I am expected to correct students' prose. As I understand the case, the board argued that many of its African American students spoke a distinct, rule-governed language variety rather than a defective or lazy version of English, and it proposed that teachers use their knowledge of that variety to help students learn standard academic English. The public reaction was fierce: some heard the board as endorsing broken English, many linguists replied that the variety was as systematic as any prestige dialect, and the board ultimately clarified that it was not actually going to teach in Ebonics. Nearly everyone involved came away unhappy.

I would like you to analyze this episode as a test case for the prescriptivism–descriptivism controversy, treating the two positions as tools rather than slogans. In particular: what did each framework commit the board, the commentators, and the linguists to about what bad grammar is; how did each framework handle the distinction between describing a variety as systematic and deciding that a school should institutionalize it or correct away from it; and where in the episode did descriptivism, even as an accurate scientific picture, prove unable to settle the educational and political questions on its own? The part I most want to understand is whether the episode shows that bad grammar is really a social judgment about speakers being mistaken for a property of their sentences, or whether there is some residual, non-social sense of grammatical failure that neither side managed to articulate. I would prefer a disciplined mapping over a general survey, and I want you to say plainly where each framework goes silent rather than forcing it to speak.
