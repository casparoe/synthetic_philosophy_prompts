As part of a project on long-run predictions and their falsification, I have been trying to work out whether Malthus is best read as offering a single forecast or as making a claim about explanatory priority: that the crucial question is not whether population is checked, but which means of checking it are operative and how much they constrain improvement. In the first edition of *An Essay on the Principle of Population* (1798), he writes:

> It is an obvious truth, which has been taken notice of by many writers, that population must always be kept down to the level of the means of subsistence; but no writer, that the Author recollects, has inquired particularly into the means by which this level is effected: and it is a view of these means, which forms, to his mind, the strongest obstacle in the way to any very great future improvement of society.

I want to assess whether that explanatory project survives the demographic transition. In most industrialised societies, fertility fell substantially through conscious limitation of births within marriage, rather than through the positive checks or through the delayed-marriage moral restraint he emphasised, while agricultural productivity repeatedly shifted the apparent level of subsistence upward. One might say this makes Malthus 'right but early': for long stretches of history the Malthusian mechanism did roughly operate, and modern growth simply relaxed the constraint. But if the means that Malthus treated as the strongest obstacle to improvement turned out to be a historically local arrangement rather than a necessary feature of human populations, what content does 'right but early' still have? Does the demographic transition rescue Malthusianism by showing only that population is always checked by some means or other, or does it falsify the substantive mechanism he actually offered? I am interested in the epistemology of long-run predictions, not in demographic history for its own sake. Please also say what would change your mind: which consideration or argument would lead you to the opposite verdict about whether this is a rescue or a falsification, and where your reasoning is most likely to fail.
