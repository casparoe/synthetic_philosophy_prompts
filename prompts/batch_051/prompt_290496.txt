I cover environmental and consumer ethics for a general-interest magazine, and I'm working on a piece about a claim I keep hearing: that the real problem with individual climate and food choices is causal — a single person's action never makes a difference, so it can't be required. A source sent me a widely shared online explainer that puts the philosophical side of this especially sharply, and I want to know how much of it I can safely build on. The explainer summarizes the "inefficacy objection" like this: "Philosophers who work on collective action have made a strong case that an individual's contribution to a large-scale harm does no actual good: one less cheeseburger does not reduce the number of animals the meat industry raises, and one skipped flight does not change the trajectory of the climate. If we judge actions by what they in fact bring about, then eating the burger and refusing it have the same effects, so neither can be morally better than the other. On this basis, many moral philosophers conclude that individual consumption choices are not in themselves right or wrong, and that anyone who still defends a duty to refrain is forced to say we are obligated to do something we know has no effect. The argument is standardly extended to voting: because one vote virtually never decides an election, voting is causally as pointless as consumer choice. The practical upshot is that guilt over a single flight or burger rests on a confusion, and the real moral work lies with policy and collective action rather than with individuals."

I'm inclined to think the causal observation is right but that the explainer's leap from "no single act causes a detectable difference" to "so individual choices aren't really right or wrong" is doing more work than the argument can support. But I'm not trained in this literature, and I don't want to be sloppy in the other direction either — perhaps much of the closing advice about policy rather than individual guilt does survive. Please audit the summary claim by claim. Separate what is well established, what has to be qualified and how, what a philosopher pressing the inefficacy objection would still insist on after your corrections, and what a defender of individual duties would most plausibly say. Be explicit about which of the explainer's practical conclusions — about guilt, voting, and the priority of policy — survive the audit and which need hedging.

Structure the answer in five titled sections: (1) "The sound core" — roughly 250 words on which claims are accurate, for whom, and under what assumptions; (2) "Where the explainer slips" — roughly 300 words on the overstatements, conflations, and scope creep, with the most important ones first; (3) "What a proponent of the objection should still insist on" — roughly 200 words on the strongest version of the inefficacy objection after the corrections; (4) "What a defender of individual duties should say" — roughly 250 words on the most credible replies and how far they get; (5) "What I can responsibly write" — roughly 150 words, phrased as reportable claims with the necessary hedging.
