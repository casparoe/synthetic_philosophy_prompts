You are a professional philosopher working on collective responsibility and the ethics of war. Please think through the following argument carefully; if it helps, begin by laying out the possible points of failure and what each rejection would cost, and only then settle on a diagnosis.

I am a retired chemist who has been reading the historiography of the First World War, and I have constructed an argument that seems to me to prove too much, but I cannot locate the error. Here it is, roughly as I would number the steps.

(1) A nation can be held responsible for a war only if the war was brought about, to a significant degree, by that nation's deliberate decisions — its alliances, assurances, plans, ultimata, mobilizations — taken for reasons it understood and in pursuit of its own perceived interests.

(2) In the First World War, for each German decision that the strong Fischer thesis singles out — by which I mean the thesis that in 1914 Germany's leaders deliberately risked and to a large degree willed a general war for expansionist reasons — at least one other great power took a decision of the same general kind: an unconditional assurance to an ally, annexationist war aims, the decision to treat the July Crisis as an opportunity rather than only as a threat.

(3) Parity: if a decision of a given general kind would make one power responsible for the war when it took it, then a decision of the same general kind would make any other power similarly responsible when it took it; if it would excuse one, it excuses the others.

(4) Therefore the standard reasons for singling out Germany either establish that several powers are jointly responsible in the same way, or they establish nothing about Germany in particular.

(5) The second horn dissolves war responsibility altogether. The first horn abolishes the difference in kind between a state that drives a crisis and a state that reacts, since the reasons become fully symmetric. Either way, the distinction on which blaming Germany depends disappears.

Taken as a free-standing argument, this seems to me a valid reductio of the strong Fischer thesis. My difficulty is that the same template, retargeted, would dissolve responsibility in wars where I am convinced one party is the aggressor. So something must be wrong; I just cannot say which step it is.

For the purpose of this exercise, grant me premise (2) as a reconstruction of the standard case for parity rather than a settled archival claim; my worry is conceptual, not historical. I would most like to keep premise (1), since I find the idea that responsibility must trace to deliberate choice almost definitional, but I will give it up if it is the true culprit.

Which premise or step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost for the project of holding a nation responsible for a war?
