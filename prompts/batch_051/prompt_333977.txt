I have a rule when reading early modern science: if I learn that a thinker took astrology seriously, I treat that as strong reason to lower my expectations for their broader claims. I don’t reject their achievements outright, but I start with the suspicion that their standards of evidence were porous, and I let that suspicion decide which books I invest time in. Recently a friend who is sympathetic to astrology challenged me over Kepler. I had known Kepler cast horoscopes, but I had filed that under economic necessity: every court mathematician had to produce them. My friend insisted I was underestimating the extent of Kepler’s investment. According to him, Kepler wanted to reform astrology rather than abandon it: he rejected the conventional zodiac and much of the house-based machinery as arbitrary, but tried to rebuild astrology on what he took to be physical and geometrical causes, above all the aspects understood as angular relations between planets, linked to his harmonic ideas. That has made me uneasy, because my rule lets me stop investigating precisely the case that might test whether astrology was separable from early modern astronomy in the first place.

What I want to know is whether my rule is rationally defensible as a time-saving epistemic policy, or whether it is a bias disguised as a heuristic. Is involvement with a now-rejected practice a reasonable negative prior about a historical thinker’s general reliability? Does Kepler represent an exception that should make me abandon the rule, or does his case simply show that even an extraordinary mind can be partly captured by a bad framework? I would also like to know whether the rule can be restated in a form that keeps whatever benefits it originally had without the cost of ruling out Kepler-like cases in advance.
