I'm a retired atmospheric scientist — thirty years of trying to squeeze signal out of noisy, short, non-stationary records — and I've recently gotten into philosophy. Something I keep turning over is the precautionary principle. As I understand it, it says roughly: if some new intervention could cause big, possibly irreversible harm, the lack of full scientific certainty isn't a good reason to hold off on protective action, and the burden of proof sits with the folks who want to do the intervention.

Here's the parallel I can't shake. In my old line of work you'd formalize exactly that situation as a decision under uncertainty: two states (the thing is dangerous / it isn't), two acts (allow / block), a prior, and a loss function. If the loss for 'allow when dangerous' is made much larger than the loss for 'block when safe', and you put a somewhat skeptical prior on 'safe', the expected-utility-maximizing rule is to block unless the evidence for safety is strong. That just is the precautionary principle, isn't it? In fact it starts to look like the principle does no work at all; its just a rhetorical wrapper around choosing an asymetric loss function and a conservative prior, and the real argument is always about the numbers.

But I suspect that's too quick. For one thing, the principle is usually invoked when you don't have a well-defined probability distribution at all, so the expected-utility machinery may not even apply. Also, 'block the intervention' has costs too — foregone benefits, substitute risks — and the principle as normally stated seems to screen those out, which a loss function can't let you do for free.

So: is the reduction of the precautionary principle to asymmetric loss plus a conservative prior a genuine structural identity, or is it superficial? If it's deep, what if anything transfers from the weird properties of precautionary reasoning over to decision theory? If it's superficial, what exactly does the expected-utility reconstruction leave out? Please answer in American English.
