In my seminar this week we went over Tarski's hierarchy argument for the liar, and a student asked something that left me less sure of the whole thing than I'd like. The usual story is: in a classical language that can talk about its own syntax, a serviceable truth predicate satisfying the T-scheme gives you the contradiction, so truth for a language has to live one notch up in a metalanguage, and that's why the hierarchy never bottoms out. Fine.

But here's the development I want someone to work through. Suppose you drop the bivalence assumption and let truth be graded — a real number in [0,1], or a probability/degree of truth — and suppose the language allows self-referential sentences whose value is a fixed point, so the liar settles at a stable intermediate value rather than flipping between true and false. This isn't just an idle variation; plenty of systems now assign confidence to their own claims, and fuzzy/continuous semantics have ben around long enough to be worth taking seriously. What I want to know is whether Tarski's hierarchy argument survives that change, or whether it was really an artifact of bivalence all along. Which premise exactly does the graded setting put pressure on? And is there a repaired version that still says something of the old conclusion — e.g., that even a graded language can't contain a full, coherent, unrestricted truth predicate for itself? If the liar just sits at 1/2, has the paradox stopped doing any work, or has it only moved somewhere else?
