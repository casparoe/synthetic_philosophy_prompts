I sit on the scientific advisory committee of a mid-sized mental-health research funder, and we're staring down a grant application that has split us cleanly in two. The applicant wants to run a three-year trial of a metacognitive-training package for people with persistent delusions: the intervention treats a delusion as a badly justified but ordinary belief, and it has patients practice sizing up the eidence for and against the delusion the way you'd interrogate a hunch — keeping a diary of confirmations and disconfirmations, testing one element at a time, that sort of thing. The application's rationale says flatly that delusions are beliefs in every sense that matters: they guide action, people avow them, and they shift, slowly and unevenly, in response to reasons. One reviewer on the panel has been fighting this tooth and nail. He's pushing what he calls the two-factor view: a delusion starts with an anomalous experience — say, a flat, uncanny feeling that your spouse's face doesn't belong to her — and then a second factor, a bias toward accepting bizarre explanations, turns that feeling into the conviction that she's been replaced by an impostor. On his account the delusion isn't a belief held for bad reasons; it's a symptom of a broken belief-forming system, and trying to reason it away is like trying to reason with a fever. He wants us to reject the proposal and steer the money into a neuroimaging study of the same population instead.

This came to a head at last week's meeting, where we spent forty minutes arguing about what a delusion even is before anyone got around to the budget. I've been asked to draft the committee's decision memo — recommend funding the trial, rejecting it, or funding it with conditions — and it should be a document the applicant could genuinely respond to: our reasons laid out plainly, our residual worries named, and an honest statement of the strongest consideration on whichever side loses. I'm not looking for a literature review or references, just a defensible institutional decision.
