I've spent the past month arguing with two historians of science about the standard doublet cases — Newton and Leibniz, Darwin and Wallace. One of them insists that these near-simultaneous discoveries are the visible record of a deeper fact about knowledge: once a field has accumulated the right materials, techniques, and problems, the major result is, as she puts it, in the air. Had Newton died as a child, someone else would have produced something close to the calculus within a generation; had Wallace's letter never reached Darwin, someone else would still have formulated natural selection around the same time. My other friend counters that this is unfalsifiable storytelling dressed up as history of science, because we cannot rerun the past and every counterfactual about dead geniuses is underdetermined by the evidence. My own suspicion is that some components of the inevitability claim are testable and others are not, but I would like someone to draw the line precisely rather than gesture at it.

Treat my first friend's claim as a testable-in-part claim about scientific discovery: roughly, that the timing of major discoveries is a property of the state of the field — its problems, tools, and distributed knowledge — rather than of irreplaceable individuals. Be concrete: say exactly what data would be collected, what comparisons would be made, what outcome would count as evidence for the inevitability claim, and what outcome would count against it.
