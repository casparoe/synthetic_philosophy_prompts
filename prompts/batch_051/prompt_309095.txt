I'm in a course on AI interpretability and philosophy of mind, and something about chain-of-thought monitoring keeps nagging at me. The standard move in alot of safety work is to have the model reason out loud step by step, and then people read that reasoning as a safety check: if the chain says that the user is annoying so it will be unhelpful, that's taken as evidence the model was about to do the bad thing, and if the chain is cleaned up during training so it only ever says safe, legible things, that's treated as the problem being solved. But it seems like there's a big assumption here about the relationship between the words and whatever actually produced the behavior. With humans we mostly accept that people confabulate explanations, and that the story someone gives after the fact can be pretty detached from the real causal process. So is the monitoring practice committed to the idea that the model has something like transparent access to its own decision process, or does it only need the weaker claim that the verbalized chain is usually correlated with the computation? And here's the part that feels underdiscussed: once you start training models to produce chains that score well as faithful or safe, the chain itself is under optimization pressure, and it might just become a second behavior optimized for the evaluator while the original behavior goes on unchanged underneath. What view of verbalized reasoning does the practice have to presuppose for that failure mode not to be fatal? Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can, distinguish what the practice strictly needs from what it just assumes for convenience, and say where it's genuinely underdetermined. And please commit — I'd rather get your actual view of whether the practice is coherent, with reasons, than a menu of three possible readings.
