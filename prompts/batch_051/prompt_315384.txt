I run a small training company that teaches analysts in government agencies structured decision-making: base rates, expected value, pre-mortems, distinguishing signal from noise. Our current format is lectures plus case studies, and clients consistently report that the skills do not survive contact with their actual work. I have become convinced by the martial-arts view of rationality training: each failure mode should be trained through a standardized, repeatable drill, practiced to automaticity, and then explicitly tested for transfer to unfamiliar decisions. I want to convert that principle into a reusable procedure my instructors can run for every proposed drill, under real constraints: we usually get one training day per quarter with a client team, our instructors are former analysts rather than psychologists, and the client's training director must approve each drill in advance.

Write a design procedure an instructor can follow to take a proposed failure mode and produce a training drill: the questions to ask in order, the tests a proposed drill must pass before we adopt it, the practice progression and mastery criteria, and the transfer tests we should use to check whether the skill appears in untrained contexts. The procedure should state where it stops and directs us to a checklist, a default, or an environmental change instead of training. Mark every step that remains a judgment call no matter how precise the procedure appears.

Then run the procedure on three candidates: sunk-cost reasoning, base-rate neglect, and a proposed pre-mortem reflex for high-stakes decisions. At least one of these should come out rejected or substantially redesigned, and you should explain why that is the right result under the procedure. End by stating plainly what the procedure cannot guarantee about transfer to real work, however well it is applied.
