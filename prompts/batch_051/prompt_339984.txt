Last night a friend and I got into it about Pascal’s wager—he kept saying it’s a sleazy trick because it tells people to fake belief until it sticks, I said that’s not fair to Pascal, he actually treats the “act as if” stuff as a way of retraining your passions rather than lying to yourself. Anyway it made me realize I need this for work.

I volunteer as a peer spiritual counselor at a large public university’s multifaith center. Students come in for 20-minute walk-in chats, mostly 18–22, often anxious, from the whole range: devout, ex-, never-affiliated, “spiritual but not religious.” We aren’t clergy and we’re told not to push any tradition. But sometimes a student is in an agonizing halfway state: they don’t believe the familiy faith, or they’ve half-converted to something new, and they ask a version of “what if I’m wrong?” That’s where Pascal-type reasoning tends to come up—maybe from me, maybe from them. I’ve seen it go well and also go badly: one student ended up staying in a religion mainly out of fear of the expected loss, and a year later felt he’d been hedging his whole personality.

Can you write me a decision procedure I could actually use in a walk-in chat to decide when it’s appropriate to bring in a Pascal’s-wager-style expected-payout argument, and how to run it when I do? I want it grounded in what Pascal actually does and doesn’t claim, and in the standard objections (multiple gods, infinite utilities you can’t compute, belief not chosen by a switch, self-deception, the moral risk of worshipping a tyrant). The procedure should be something I can keep in my head: questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer, where I stop with a no, where I have to hand it to a licensed counselor rather than me. Also include the cases your procedure can’t settle. Then run it on two specific cases: (1) a Muslim student who no longer believes but is terrified of family exile and talks about the expected costs of leaving; (2) a student who has become convinced by an online argument that they should join a very demanding, small splinter religious group because the downside of not joining is infinite. I’m open to the procedure telling me I shouldn’t do it at all in some cases I’d have done it in.
