Over the past year I have come around to the view that, in the closing days of the Second World War, Truman was not forced into a tragic but permissible choice when he authorized the atomic bombing of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. On my view, deliberately killing civilian populations as a means of compelling surrender is an act that no expected outcome — avoiding an invasion, ending the war sooner, saving soldiers’ lives — can justify. The position is not just that the decision was wrong on balance, but that it falls under an absolute prohibition and should be called by the ordinary name for such killing: murder. I want to know what would count against this. What would actually have to be true for the position to be false or significantly weakened? Please give me the considerations — historical, argumentative, or intuitive — that should move me, ranked by how decisive they would be rather than how familiar they are, marking which ones a defender of the view would have to concede genuinely threaten it. Then tell me whether the view can be revised to survive every one of those threats, and whether that resilience would be a sign of integrity or of the view no longer sticking its neck out.
