Consider Fitch's paradox of knowability. Let Kφ mean that φ is known by some agent at some time, and let ◇φ mean that φ is possible. An anti-realist about truth maintains the knowability principle: for every proposition p, if p is true then p is knowable, formally p → ◇Kp. A realist denies this and affirms that at least some truths are unknowable in principle.

Both sides accept a short derivation at the heart of the paradox. Take any true proposition p that is never actually known, p ∧ ¬Kp. By the knowability principle, this conjunction is knowable: ◇K(p ∧ ¬Kp). But knowing the conjunction would require, by closure of knowledge under conjunction elimination, knowing both p and the fact that p is unknown. Since knowledge is factive, knowing that p is unknown entails that p is unknown. Thus K(p ∧ ¬Kp) entails Kp ∧ ¬Kp, a contradiction. Under the shared modal assumption that whatever entails a contradiction is impossible, K(p ∧ ¬Kp) is impossible, so the conjunction cannot be knowable after all. By the knowability principle, no true proposition can be unknown; classically, every truth is actually known. Both parties reject that consequence, and each treats the derivation as a reductio, but of a different assumption.

Draft the joint statement that a committed anti-realist knowabilist and a committed realist who affirms unknowable truths could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Do not adjudicate the dispute; produce the document the collaboration would issue.

The statement should include:

1. A numbered agreed-claims section. It should state the derivation as both sides accept it, the exact assumptions they agree generate the collapse, and which inference steps are shared rather than contested.

2. A disagreements table. Rows should cover at least: the exact formulation of the knowability principle, or the closest defensible descendant the anti-realist actually wishes to preserve; the assumption each side proposes to reject, restrict, or revise; the distinction between an independently motivated restriction and an ad hoc carve-out; and each side's treatment of a concrete candidate unknowable, such as the exact trajectory of a single unobserved dust grain through a particular abandoned room.

3. For each genuine crux, the argument, formal constraint, or case both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance.

Write every formulation so that its own side would sign it, and avoid wording that either side would disown.
