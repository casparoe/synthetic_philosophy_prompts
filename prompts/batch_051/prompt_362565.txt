I've been going back and forth with a friend about a bunch of concrete moral fights — whether opposition to GMOs is mostly disgust dressed up as principle, whether the revulsion people report at consensual incest is evidence of something morally real or just the moral dumbfounding effect doing its thing, whether IAT scores tell you anything about a person's character or just about the cultural water they swim in. We hit the same wall every time: each of us treats his own gut reactions as carrying moral information while writing off the other side's as bias, and I realized neither of us actually has a criterion for when that move is legitimate and when its question-begging. Before I go back into any of those arguments I want the full spec first.

What would a decent theory of the preference/bias distinction in morality actually have to get right? Lay it out as a desiderata list: each requirement, the case or consideration that forces it, and why it has to be a requirement. The list should say something about at least these: reactions that track a real harm versus reactions that just track unfamiliarity or change; whether a bias has to be irrational or can be a perfectly coherent value someone could defend; whether the same judgment can count as a preference in one context and a bias in another; how much weight to put on the process that produced the judgment rather than its content; whether the theory has to treat general human machinery like status quo bias or loss aversion differently from local, acquired biases; and whether the theory is allowed to conclude there is no fact of the matter and every accusation of bias is just rhetoric. Flag each requirement as negotiable or non-negotiable, assuming the point of the theory is to give people a non-circular way to argue across serious moral disagreements. Then tell me which single requirement knocks out the most popular accounts of bias that people implicitly use in these debates, and why. A table for the requirements is fine.
