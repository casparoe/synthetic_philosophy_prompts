I'm putting together a lecture on positive, negative, and republican liberty, and I want a worked example I can walk through with undergrads. I've been going back and forth on a case that I think seperates the three views nicely. Imagine two middle-sized, wealthy democracies, A and B, with essentially identical formal rights to speech, press, assembly, and religion, and in both countries the state hasn't actually punished anyone for exercising these rights in the last 25 years. On paper, and in the record of actual non-interference, they look the same. The one difference is this: in A, the constitution still gives the head of government a standing emergency power to suspend any civil liberty by decree whenever they judge that public order requires it. The power has never been used, and the current leader insists it would be unthinkable to use it, but everyone knows it's there and that a future leader could use it. In B, the equivalent power was abolished a generation ago, so no suspension is legally possible without a constitutional amendment requiring the agreement of several independent bodies.

I want you to reason through, as an expert political philosopher, how A and B should be expected to diverge over the next 30 years: in how newspapers pick stories, in how willing sources are to leak, in how civil-society groups behave, in whether people invest in long-term political projects, in who enters politics and activism, or anywhere else you think the difference would show up. I'm less interested in a yes-or-no answer than in the chain of reasoning: at each link, say which effects are robust, which are guesses, and what would have to hold for you to be wrong. Then say which concept of liberty — negative, positive, or republican — best captures the difference between the two countries, and whether you think they are, in fact, equally free. Before you commit to an answer, brainstorm the candidate effects and then tell me which ones you're willing to defend. Please think carefully; don't just give me talking points.
