In my decision theory reading group, a conversation about Kavka's toxin puzzle took a turn I have not been able to undo. The setup, as we have been running it: a reliable billionaire will deposit a million dollars tomorrow morning if, at midnight tonight, you genuinely intend to drink a toxin tomorrow afternoon; the toxin will make you ill for a day, but the money is already secured by the time you would drink, and everyone involved understands that you need not actually drink in order to be paid. One member of the group built an argument from this that ends somewhere I do not want to be. (1) A rational agent cannot intend to do what they believe they will not do, because intention is a settled commitment to act, and one cannot be settled on an action while believing one will not perform it. (2) At midnight, a rational agent who understands the puzzle will believe they will not drink tomorrow: the money will already have been paid, the drink brings only sickness and no further benefit, and there is no remaining reason to drink. (3) Therefore a rational agent cannot intend at midnight to drink, and the prize cannot be won by bare rationality; one would need self-deception, a binding device, or some other trick. (4) But the principle in (1) is perfectly general, so it implies that whenever someone predicts they will not follow through—the smoker resolving to quit, the writer resolving to finish the manuscript, the government threatening retaliation it would rather not carry out—they cannot really intend the thing they are resolving or threatening to do. (5) That seems to prove too much: it would make a huge share of ordinary resolutions and strategic threats into mere hopes or performances, and it would make the very phenomenon of failing to act on an intention close to incoherent. Yet I cannot locate the step where the argument overreaches. I would most like to keep the ordinary cases of genuine but likely-to-fail intention, and to say that something specific to the timing or the certainty in the toxin case is doing the work, but I want to know what rejecting that costs. Give me a committed diagnosis: name the step you reject, explain why it looked fine, and state plainly what the repair costs, rather than laying out a menu of options. Please write it as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter—no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
