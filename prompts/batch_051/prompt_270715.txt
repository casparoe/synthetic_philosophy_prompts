I have been trying to work out what I actually think about what makes pain bad, and my reactions to a series of cases have left me with a principle I cannot quite make fit. Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. Ordinary toothache. A person has a throbbing, intense, aching toothache that makes it hard to think about anything else. I say it is bad for them, and its badness seems to consist entirely in how unpleasant it feels — not in the tissue damage itself.

2. Pain asymbolia. A patient with a rare condition can identify exactly where a pinprick is and rate its intensity accurately, but shows no distress, does not withdraw or protect the area, and reports that the sensation does not bother them; they may even smile while it is applied. I find myself saying that they are in pain, but that the pain is no longer bad for them — or at least has lost the kind of badness that normally matters.

3. Morphine analgesia. A patient given morphine after surgery says the pain is still there, at the same intensity, but the hurt has gone out of it. I have the same reaction as in (2): the pain has stopped being bad, even though it can still be located and rated.

4. The masochist. A person in a consensual sexual context seeks out sensations that they describe as genuinely painful — they hurt — and says the pain is part of what makes the experience good. My first reaction is that the pain is still bad in a narrow, qualified sense: a bitter component inside a larger good. But then I waver, because it seems odd to call a pain bad for a person who knowingly sought it and would choose it again. This is the case I keep going back and forth on.

5. Depression. A person in a severe depressive episode has no localised bodily pain, but feels an awful, heavy, anguished state. I say it is bad for them, and the badness seems to be exactly the same kind of thing as the badness of pain — unpleasantness, but without the sensory component. This pushes me towards thinking that unpleasantness, not tissue damage or nociception, is doing the real work.

6. The harmless shock. A research participant receives a mild electric shock that stings, while knowing it is completely safe and carries no information about injury. I say the pain is still bad for them, but only a little, and exactly to the extent that it stings.

7. The false alarm. A person feels pain in their chest and is convinced they are having a heart attack; the next minutes are terrifying and awful. The cause turns out to be harmless indigestion. I still say the pain was bad for them while it lasted, and its badness does not depend on whether the fear was well-founded.

8. Congenital insensitivity. A person who cannot feel pain walks on a damaged ankle and injures it further. I say there is no pain-badness here at all, only bodily harm; pain and harm are not the same thing.

My tentative principle has been that the badness of a pain is exhausted by its unpleasantness — by how aversively it feels — and does not depend on any non-experiential fact, such as tissue damage or objective threat, except insofar as such facts help explain why the pain feels the way it does. Cases (1) to (3) and (5) to (7) point towards it, case (8) tells me to keep pain and injury apart, and case (4) is the one that resists it. I am also unsure how to handle the relationship between the badness of pain and the broader unpleasantness of states like grief or nausea.

Can you tell me whether there is a coherent principle that captures all of these verdicts, or nearly all of them? Please state the most defensible principle or small set of candidate principles, explain which of my cases motivates each clause, say what the principle predicts about at least two further cases I have not listed, and be explicit about which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate, if any, and which I should consider overturning.

Please begin by stating your bottom line in the first sentence, then give your reasoning, with no preamble and no restating of the question. Write in British English.
