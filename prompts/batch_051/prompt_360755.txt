Right, here's the situation. I've somehow ended up on my town's transport scrutiny committee because the council is about to choose between three road schemes and one of them would put a bus gate at the end of my road, so its become a bit personal. The council has just announced a pilot where the final choice will be made by a prediction market rather than a vote, and reading up on futarchy has made me wonder what happens if you scale the whole thing up.

So suppose that from 2030, every English transport capital project over £50 million has to be selected by a conditional prediction market. The relevant council or regional authority defines a single ten-year success metric (average journey time in the affected area, or air quality near schools, that sort of thing); markets trade contracts that pay out according to the measured performance of each scheme if it were implemented; the scheme with the highest implementation price gets built; and traders are paid out on the measured outcome a decade later, with a penalty if the metric is missed by more then 10%. Councils can only override in a declared emergency.

Predict, with reasoning, how English transport policy looks after fifteen years. I want the mechanism-level argument rather than a broad 'markets are good or bad' take: which kinds of schemes would systematically win and why (do the markets favour quick, legible, congestion-busting projects over messy long-term ones?), how fast consultants, developers, and councils learn to game the metric, whether a few wealthy traders end up effectively choosing schemes and whether that matters in practice, what happens to maintenance and to projects the metric undervalues, and whether the rule survives politically or gets quietly abandoned and circumvented. For each link in the chain, say how confident you are and what would have to be true for that part of your prediction to be wrong.
