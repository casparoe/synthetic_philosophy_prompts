I've been rereading Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" for a reading group, and there's a passage I can't get past. He writes:

"Once the theory of meaning is sharply separated from the theory of reference, it is a short step to recognizing as the business of the theory of meaning simply the synonymy of linguistic forms and the analyticity of statements; meanings themselves, as obscure intermediary entities, may well be abandoned."

The argument that follows — definitions just report pre-existing synonymies, interchangeability salva veritate already presupposes something like sameness of meaning, and semantical rules only legislate analyticity for an artificial language — is supposed to show that analyticity can't be given a non-circular footing. Fine, that's the stuff everyone remembers. But what I cant figure out is whether that argument survives distributional semantics. We now have word embeddings and large language models that seem to supply exactly the sort of thing Quine said was missing: an empirical, corpus-based measure of when two expressions mean the same. Word2vec and BERT assign vectors so that expressions with similar patterns of surrounding text end up close to each other, and people routinely treat that closeness as a graded stand-in for meaning similarity — no appeals to "meanings" as obscure entities needed. So my question is straightforward: does Quine's argument against the analytic-synthetic distinction survive this development, or does the vector-space picture force a repair? I'd like to know which specific premise in Quine's argument actually gets hit, whether distributional similarity is even a candidate for the synonymy he said analyticity requires (or whether it's just measuring co-occurrence and missing the very thing he meant), and whether a repaired version would still be Quine's position or something much weaker. If your answer is that it doesn't touch him at all, tell me what a distributional model would have to do for it to count.
