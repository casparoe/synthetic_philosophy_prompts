You are a philosopher who works at the boundary of population ethics, animal welfare science, and theoretical ecology, and who is comfortable assigning precise credences and defending them. I want your help settling a running argument I have been having with two people: an ecologist who treats habitat destruction as an unambiguous disaster, and an effective-altruist friend who insists that, from the standpoint of total animal welfare, many habitat conversions might be net positive because rich ecosystems harbor enormous numbers of short-lived, high-mortality animals — and if most wild animal lives are closer to net negative than net positive, then preventing those lives from existing could be a benefit rather than a cost. I am not asking who is right about conservation policy. I am asking for your credence in one precisely stated proposition.

Proposition P: Consider a single site of 100 square kilometers of primary tropical rainforest, completely cleared and converted to low-intensity cattle pasture. Counting all sentient nonhuman animals — vertebrates, and invertebrates if you think it appropriate — over a 100-year time horizon, and including ecological spillover effects on immediately surrounding areas, but deliberately excluding any effects on human welfare and any value from species existing as such, the expected change in total animal welfare is positive. That is, the converted landscape and its spillover zone contain less net suffering than they would have contained if the forest had remained intact.

Think carefully, sketch your major considerations before committing to numbers, and do not hide behind “it depends.” Give me a single number: your credence that P is true. Then decompose that number into at least five sub-questions, including at least these: (i) that nonhuman animal welfare counts morally for its own sake in this calculation; (ii) that the average life in an intact tropical forest ecosystem is net negative rather than net positive; (iii) that the pasture ecosystem supports a substantially lower density of net suffering per hectare than the forest did; (iv) that the transition process itself — clearing, fire, fragmentation, injury, displacement, the suffering of animals killed or deprived of habitat — is not so bad that it swamps any later gain; and (v) that indirect effects on immediately surrounding habitat do not reverse the local verdict. For each sub-question, give a rough probability, one or two lines of justification, and a brief note on what evidence would move that number. Then show how the sub-probabilities combine into your overall credence, rather than simply asserting a weighted average. Finally, tell me which sub-question your final number is most sensitive to, and identify one specific kind of empirical finding or observation that would move your credence by at least ten points in one direction or the other and what it would have to show. For calibration: I am somewhere around 0.3, the ecologist is around 0.02, and my effective-altruist friend is around 0.7; after you have finished, briefly say which of us your reasoning comes closest to and where the disagreement among us most likely lives.
