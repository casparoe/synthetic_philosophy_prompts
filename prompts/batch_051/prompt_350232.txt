I'm doing a presentation for my school's philosophy club on the prisoner's dilemma, and I got kind of obsessed with Hofstadter's idea of superrationality. Just so we're on the same page: in a one-shot prisoner's dilemma with two players in exactly symmetric situations, superrationality says each should cooperate, because each can reason that the other, being in the identical situation and running the identical reasoning, will land on the same choice — and mutual cooperation beats mutual defection. My friend thinks this is obviously right, so I'm trying to steelman the other side by collecting the best objections to it.

So far I have these: (1) it's just evidential reasoning in disguise, because your cooperating is evidence about what the other player will do, but it doesn't cause them to do it; (2) it only works in perfectly symmetric cases and falls apart as soon as the players have even slightly different beliefs or payoffs, so it isn't a general theory of rational choice; (3) it secretly assumes a joint decision procedure or an agreement without communication, and once you allow that you're not really playing the one-shot PD anymore; (4) it tells you to pass up a strictly dominating option, which is exactly the kind of thing individual rationality is supposed to forbid; (5) it confuses what's good for the pair with what's rational for each individual, like sneaking utilitarianism into game theory.

I feel like that's all the standard ones, and I definately don't want a variant of any of them. Give me one more objection that has a different structure from everything on my list — not just a rewording, a special case, or a combination — and then say honestly whether a superrationality fan could shrug it off or whether it actually stings.
