Here's a case I think is weirdly perfect for the conceptual-engineering-versus-conceptual-analysis debate, and I want you to run it in both directions. The episode: in 2006 the IAU voted to adopt a formal defintion of planet — a planet has to orbit the Sun, be massive enough that its own gravity pulls it into a roughly round shape, and have cleared its orbital neighbourhood — and Pluto fails the third test, so it got demoted to dwarf planet. I'm not interested in whether Pluto is really a planet. I want you to treat the IAU as an institutional conceptual engineer, and use the case to stress-test the fix-versus-analyse distinction itself.

First, what was the concept planet actually for before 2006 — for working astronomers, for educators, for the general public — and which of those uses, if any, was the old loose usage actually failing at? Second, is the 2006 definition best read as an analysis of a concept that was already there, a repair of a flawed concept, or a replacement with a new concept, and does the case suggest those are really three points on a spectrum rather than clean alternatives? Third, grade the definition as engineering: where does it succeed on its own terms, and where does it look ad hoc — the cleared-the-neighbourhood criterion is doing a huge amount of work, the definition is framed for our Solar System specifically so exoplanets never got a vote, and the whole thing quietly optimizes for one set of practitioners over everyone else. Finally, run it in reverse: does this episode show that conceptual engineering just is conceptual analysis with better PR, or does it expose something analysis couldn't capture — for instance, that the planet concept was never value-neutral, and that what makes a good concept depends on whose purposes the concept is being optimized for?
