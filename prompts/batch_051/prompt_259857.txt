I spent my working life in physical chemistry, mostly statistical mechanics, and have only recently started reading moral philosophy properly. The bit that keeps pulling me in is the universalizability tradition after Kant: R. M. Hare's universal prescriptivism and Marcus Singer's generalization argument (Marcus, not Peter). The move from a particular act to what if everyone did that looks to me a lot like what we do in statistical mechanics: write down a local interaction between a pair of molecules, then ask what the bulk system does when that same local rule is applied across all N particles. In physics the collective behavior is not just the local rule multiplied. Sometimes mean-field theory is fine; sometimes it badly misses because correlations and fluctuations dominate. You get phase transitions and critical phenomena, and sometimes the order in which you take limits changes the answer.

I want to read the universalization test as the same kind of scale-up. Cases like stealing look mean-field simple: if everyone stole, there would be no property to steal. Other cases look like they depend on how finely you describe the act (is the maxim taking a walk, or taking a walk at 2 p.m. on a Tuesday?) and on whether others would actually follow you, which sounds like correlations rather than a mean field.

So my question is whether this parallel is deep or just physics vocabulary draped over a loose analogy. Is there a way to make it precise? For instance, would Hare-style logical universalizability be something like an exact symmetry constraint, while Singer's generalization argument is more like a mean-field approximation? What would a phase transition in the ethics case even be, an act that flips from permissible to impermissable because some fraction of the population is already doing it? And where does the analogy cleanly break?

1. The mapping (about 150 words): state the analogy precisely, with each side spelled out.
2. Where it holds (about 200 words): the respects in which the two structures really match.
3. Where it breaks (about 250 words): the respects in which ethics and statistical mechanics come apart, including what the parallel would predict that turns out false on the ethics side.
4. Verdict (about 150 words): whether this is deep, superficial, or a confusion, and what would need to be added to make it deep.
