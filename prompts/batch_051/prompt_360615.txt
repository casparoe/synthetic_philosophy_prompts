I have a set of verdicts about when someone who has committed the outward act but lacked fault may nevertheless be punished or formally sanctioned, and I want to know whether a defensible principle lies behind them or whether some of my verdicts must go.

1. A pharmacist dispenses a prescription from a sealed, correctly labelled manufacturer's bottle; the manufacturer had in fact filled the bottle with the wrong tablets, and no reasonable inspection by the pharmacist would have discovered it. A patient is injured. I would not criminally punish the pharmacist.

2. A driver misjudges a yellow light and enters the intersection just after it turns red. I think a fixed fine is acceptable even though the driver was neither reckless nor negligent; she simply made a bad timing judgment.

3. A bar owner checks a patron's ID with both a scanner and a black light. The ID is a forgery that no ordinary check would catch, and the patron is twenty. I would impose a fine on the bar, and perhaps a short suspension, but I am uneasy about treating it as a crime.

4. A twenty-one-year-old has sex with a sixteen-year-old who showed a convincing fake ID, told him she was nineteen, and did not look underage; he reasonably believed she was an adult. I think he may be punished, but materially less than if he had known her true age.

5. A getaway driver waits outside during a planned robbery of a convenience store. He does not know his co-defendant is carrying a gun; the co-defendant shoots and kills the clerk during the robbery. I would not punish the driver as a murderer—only for the robbery he joined.

6. A guest borrows a coat at a party and is later stopped by police; the coat's pocket contains a small quantity of a controlled substance that the owner had left there. The guest had no idea it was present. I would not punish him for possession.

7. A plant manager signs a safety certificate required by regulation, relying on a subordinate who has falsified the inspection log. A reasonable division of labor assigned that verification to the subordinate, and the manager had no realistic way to check each entry. I would not personally punish the manager, though the company may be fined.

There is at least one fault line running through these verdicts. In (2), (3), and (4) I accept sanctioning defendants who did not know the fact that made their conduct a violation; in (1), (6), and (7) I insist on fault. Is there one principle—about the kind of offense, the severity of the penalty, the defendant's opportunity to avoid the violation, the protective purpose of the law, the stigma attached, or the difference between regulatory strict liability and true crimes—that delivers all seven verdicts? If not, give me the strongest one or two candidate principles, state each precisely, show how it handles the cases, and tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon. Then apply the account you think best to two further cases of your own construction: one involving an autonomous vehicle that runs a red light for a reason no engineer can reproduce, and one involving a hiker who crosses into a restricted government area because the boundary signs have blown down.
