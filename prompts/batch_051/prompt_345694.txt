The political obligation debate has been stuck for as long as I have been reading it, and my discussion group now reruns the same moves with no one changing position. Consent theorists hold that we owe obedience to the law because we have consented, whether by voting, accepting public benefits, using state services, or failing to emigrate. Fair-play theorists say the obligation arises instead from receiving the benefits of a cooperative scheme: once we accept the goods the state provides, we owe our fair share of doing what it says, whether or not we asked for those goods. Associative theorists say that membership itself does the work, that being born into and continuing to live within a political community binds us in something like the way family membership does. Against all three, philosophical anarchists rejoin that no actual state secures meaningful consent, that unsolicited benefits cannot generate debts (the old neighbors-with-a-loudspeaker move), and that appeals to membership either sneak consent back in or beg the question by treating the bare fact of membership as though it were already a normative status. The defenders reply that the anarchist's standard for "meaningful consent" is so demanding it would rule out obligations almost everywhere, and the anarchists reply that this is precisely the point. The stakes are not merely academic: if political obligation is an illusion, then civil disobedience ceases to be a genuine duty that is outweighed and becomes simply the exercise of a liberty the state had no right to constrain, which reshapes all our downstream arguments.

What makes the impasse feel tractable is that everyone involved agrees on the descriptive facts. Almost nobody consents in any robustly voluntary sense; states do supply genuine benefits; most people nonetheless feel bound; and everyone also agrees that not all obligations arise from consent, since we have duties not to harm others whatever we have agreed to. Yet nobody moves. My working hypothesis, offered only as a hypothesis, is that this is less a substantive moral disagreement than a disagreement about defaults: one side treats obligation as the normal condition that needs a defeater, while the other treats no-obligation as the normal condition that needs a positive ground, so each side's arguments are persuasive only once its own default has already been assumed. But it could equally be a verbal disagreement about the word "obligation," a methodological dispute about whether exotic hypotheticals or ordinary political life should count as the data, or two groups answering different questions under one heading, something like "what obligations would a legitimate state generate?" versus "what obligations do actually existing states manage to generate?" How would you diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is? And under that diagnosis, what would count as progress, or is this a case where we should stop expecting movement?
