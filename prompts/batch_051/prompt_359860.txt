I have a friend who insists that natural selection explains nothing at all. We argued about it for weeks, and I eventually wrote out what I take to be his argument:

(1) To call an organism fitter than another is just to say that it has a greater tendency to survive and leave offspring in its environment.
(2) The only way to tell whether an organism has a greater tendency to survive and reproduce is to see whether it actually survives and reproduces more.
(3) So the claim that the fittest organisms survive and reproduce more amounts to the claim that the organisms that survive and reproduce more are the ones that survive and reproduce more — a claim true by definition.
(4) Natural selection is supposed to explain why organisms are adapted to their environments, and it does so by saying the fittest survive and leave more offspring. If (3) is right, that explanation is a definition dressed up as an explanation.
(5) Natural selection is therefore not a testable empirical claim and cannot explain adaptation.

I cannot accept that conclusion, but I cannot see where the argument breaks. When I answer that fitness is a hidden tendency to survive, not the same thing as actually surviving, my friend replies that I could never test that hidden tendency except by observing which organisms actually survive, and I am stuck again. My question is: is the argument valid, and if not, which step is the mistake — and is my attempted reply about hidden tendencies wrong, or is my friend's rejoinder to it wrong? Or is the argument valid after all, so that the conclusion follows?
