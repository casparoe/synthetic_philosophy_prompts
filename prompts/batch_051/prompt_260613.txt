The doux commerce thesis holds that market exchange does more than make people materially better off; it acts on their character. Because trade requires keeping promises, meeting obligations, seeing matters from the other party's point of view, and restraining the impulse to deceive or seize, commerce is said to function as a school of manners, rewarding trustworthiness, punctuality, cooperativeness, and reciprocity. The reading of the thesis I want to examine is the reputational one: the morally formative mechanism is repeated, face-to-face dealing among people who expect to meet again and who have reputations to protect.

I am attracted to this argument but unsure it survives the shift to platform-mediated exchange. A large share of trade now runs through marketplaces that assign anonymous counterparties, set prices algorithmically, prohibit haggling, resolve disputes through automated procedures, and replace open-ended local reputation with a quantified rating score. The old mechanism was something like this: I keep my word because I want this person, or people who know this person, to trust me next time. The new mechanism looks more like this: I comply because the rating system will otherwise penalize me, or because the interface leaves me no room to behave otherwise.

Does the doux commerce argument still go through in that setting, does it need to be repaired, or does it cease to be a claim about character and become a claim about something thinner, such as rule-following under supervision? Which premise is actually under pressure, and is there a weakened or reformulated version that preserves what made the original argument interesting? I am asking about the fate of the argument, not for a policy verdict on platforms.
