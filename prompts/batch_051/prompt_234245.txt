I'm on my university's general education committee, and we have to decide whether to approve a proposed core course called Commerce and Character. The professor who designed it, Marcus, keeps grounding the whole course in a passage from Adam Smith's The Wealth of Nations, Book III, Chapter IV, where Smith argues that the gradual spread of commerce and manufactures brought order and good government, and with them liberty and security, to country people who had previously lived in something close to a state of war with their neighbors and servile dependence on their superiors. He calls that effect the least observed but by far the most important of all it's effects. Marcus reads the passage as saying market exchange is a kind of moral schoolhouse: it teaches people to keep promises, defer gratification, respect strangers' rights, and settle disputes without violence. Two committee members think the course is effectively market apologetics dressed up as intellectual history. Their objection is not that Smith misreads the facts but that the syllabus treats the civilizing story as the whole story about markets and character. They want to assign, alongside Smith, the older line that markets corrupt virtue: they train us to be flattering, calculating, and manipulative with our reputations, and to see every relationship as potentially a trade. I need to circulate a short decision memo before our meeting next Thursday. Please draft it: recommend approving the course, rejecting it, or approving with conditions, with a concrete timetable, and engage the committee skeptics' strongest arguments honestly. Also, if you think the Smith passage can be reasonably read either way, say which reading you'd build the course around and why.
