Here's an asymmetry in how we judge self-examination that I cant make come out consistent. If someone says they've been "going deep" on themselves — journaling every night, replaying old fights to find their part in them, asking why they keep ending up with unavailable people — we usually call that self-aware, doing the work, maybe brave. But if someone lies awake replaying those same old fights, asking the same "why did I say that" loop, circling the same ten memories and never landing anywhere, we call it rumination and tell them to get out of their head or see someone. From the outside the motions are almost identical: same memories, same questions, same time of day. Is there a real line between the two, or is "insight" mostly a compliment we pay to people whose self-examination happens to look productive? I want the strongest principle that would justify treating these differently — not just "one leads to insight and one doesn't," unless you can say how you'd know that in advance. Then test it against the awkward cases: (a) someone who journals brutally about their flaws every night, feels worse each time, and keeps doing it because they're sure self-criticism is virtuous; (b) a therapist who has a patient deliberately re-enter a horrible memory and narrate it in detail, which we call treatment, not rumination; (c) a person who every morning deliberately imagines losing the person they love most, as an exercise in not taking them for granted. Is the ordinary asymmetry worth keeping, or should we give it up?
