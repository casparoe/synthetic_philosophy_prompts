Over roughly the past decade, the debate about griefbots — interactive simulations of a deceased person built from their texts, emails, voice recordings, or social media — has settled into a pattern that interests me more than the usual policy back-and-forth, because the two sides seem to accept the same facts and still do not move. One side argues that, when freely chosen by a bereaved person and clearly disclosed as a simulation, griefbots can be a legitimate and even valuable instrument of mourning. On this view they give the bereaved continued access to the deceased's characteristic ways of speaking and responding, they extend already-accepted practices such as rereading old letters, revisiting home videos, or talking at a grave, and they fit a picture of grief in which bonds with the dead are not simply severed but renegotiated over time. The other side argues that something importantly different happens when the other party in the interaction is procedurally generated and responsive rather than inert: it manufactures the appearance of a relation with someone who cannot consent, cannot correct, and cannot be changed by the exchange; it risks turning mourning into an open-ended loop rather than a process with a direction; and it invites companies to monetize the bereaved by promising continued contact with the dead, often through a flattened, obsequious, or fabricated version of the person.

What strikes me is that both camps grant the essential facts. Neither side thinks a griefbot is the deceased or that it has some occult metaphysical status; neither side denies that acute grief is a serious harm or that mourners need support; both sides concede that there will be better and worse designs and that some people should probably not use them. Yet the argument has barely moved: the same cases are traded, the same intuitions are restated, and the two camps often seem to answer each other only by recommitting to what they already held.

What I want is a diagnosis of the disagreement, not a verdict on griefbots. Is this at bottom a hidden empirical disagreement about what griefbots do to grief outcomes over time, which longitudinal and comparative studies could in principle settle? Is it a disagreement about the ethics of representing the dead and the standing of the dead that no amount of outcome data could touch? Is the real divide between different conceptions of what grief itself is for — renegotiating a continuing bond versus accepting finality — or between the interests of the bereaved and the interests or dignity of the dead? Or are the two sides answering different questions under one word, with one asking whether this can help a mourner and the other asking whether it is something we may build and sell at all?

Given whatever diagnosis you defend, tell me what would count as progress: a distinction that dissolves the apparent conflict, a more carefully specified question each side could genuinely engage, a kind of evidence that would actually move one side or the other, or a reason to expect the impasse to persist because it rests on starting intuitions that neither side can argue for or against.

When you answer, lead with your diagnosis in the first sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating the question.
