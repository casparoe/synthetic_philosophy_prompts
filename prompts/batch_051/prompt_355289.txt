I have just joined the Orphan Works and Abandoned Software Advisory Panel at a university library. The panel reviews requests involving materials whose rights holders are dead, defunct, unknown, or unreachable, and it decides whether the library may preserve them, provide access to them, or authorize adaptations of them. I have been studying the panel's past decisions in preparation for next week's meeting. In a case from the 1920s, the panel approved full public digitization of a novelist's manuscripts after a search found that the author had died without heirs and the work had no commercial life; it relied on the author's absence and the availability of a takedown notice. In a case involving 1983 oral-history recordings, the panel approved preservation and listening only at supervised terminals and refused online streaming, citing the privacy of living, identifiable interviewees rather than the dead interviewer's rights. In a 1956 botany textbook case, it approved open download after concluding that the publisher had dissolved and the author's heirs could not be found. In a 1970 industrial-film case, it authorized preservation and classroom screenings but declined to stream the film online because it contained commercially licensed music with no documentation, which the panel treated as an unresolved layer of rights distinct from the film itself. In a 1966 industrial-catalog case, it denied a company's request to sell reproductions but approved noncommercial digitization for teaching, saying it will not commercialize material it holds only as custodian. And in a 1980s database-software case, it refused online distribution and authorized only preservation copies and on-site terminal access, because the publisher's corporate successor, though unresponsive, was not an absent owner.

Next week the panel must decide a request from a nonprofit computing-history group. The group wants to host the complete installer for Spirea Geometry, an educational program published in 1994 by a company that dissolved in 1997 with no successor. It also wants to distribute a compatibility patch it wrote that modifies the code so the program will run on current systems. A former developer has been located and states that he was an employee, that the code was created as work-for-hire, and that he does not claim any ownership interest. The software has not been sold for decades, but copies circulate informally on user forums. The proposed use is noncommercial, and the group would label the download as an archival copy and invite any rights holder to request removal.

I am less interested in the bottom line than in the pattern. What are the strongest candidate principles that would make sense of the panel's precedents, and which past decision is the closest analog to Spirea Geometry under each principle? For each candidate principle, say what it would require for (a) hosting the installer and (b) distributing the compatibility patch. Then give the recommendation that best coheres with the panel's own precedents, identify the principle the panel would be adopting with each possible outcome if the precedents underdetermine the case, and specify the further request, if any, that would most cleanly settle the matter.
