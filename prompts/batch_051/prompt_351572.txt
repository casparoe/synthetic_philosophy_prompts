I work as a software engineer on a platform that aggregates user-submitted ratings and short analyst-style forecasts, and I keep running into a question at the border of our content policy and defamation law that nobody on the team can state precisely. A merchant recently objected to a review that gave their business a "2 out of 10" for creditworthiness and added, "I would be surprised if this place is still solvent in a year." Our trust-and-safety lead dismissed the complaint by saying that "opinions can't be defamation — only false facts can." That was meant to settle the matter, but it felt too clean to me, because the rating is numeric, the forecast is specific, and both are attached to a named business that could lose customers if they circulate. I don't have a background in philosophy or law, and I suspect that one sentence is really compressing several distinct claims.

I would like you to take the maxim "opinions can't be defamatory; only false facts can" and lay out the different precise principles it could be expressing. I can already imagine a few: that purely evaluative language never asserts anything true or false; that adding "I think" or "in my opinion" automatically shields a statement; that predictions and ratings concern the future or a matter of judgment and therefore cannot be proved false; and that the law requires a provably false assertion of fact before anything can count as defamation. For each candidate reading, tell me whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or simply false, and say whether that reading would actually settle the case of the "2 out of 10" creditworthiness rating and the one-year solvency forecast. I would like the response as continuous prose only — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables — the way you would write an email, and I would appreciate a closing bottom line on whether the slogan does any real work here or only sounds as though it does.
