I'm a doctoral student in political philosophy, and next month I'm giving a seminar paper that offers a distinctive reconstruction of Locke's labour theory of acquisition and the Lockean proviso. My thesis is that labour-mixing should not be read as literally infusing the self into an object and thereby transferring full title to the labourer; instead, labour generates an exclusive claim only over the value the labourer adds, while the unimproved natural resource remains subject to a continuing common claim. On my reading, the proviso is therefore not a one-off test applied only at the moment of first appropriation. It is a standing constraint on holdings, so that later acquisitions or transfers can reopen entitlements whenever they leave some people below the baseline of what they could have drawn from the commons. I conclude that Locke's view, properly reconstructed, is more demanding than usually supposed: even after legitimate original acquisitions, persistent inequalities that block others' access to the earth and its productive capacities trigger a repair obligation.

The workshop audience is mixed, and I know roughly who will be there. One group is made up of classical liberal Locke scholars who think the proviso is essentially satisfied under commercial society, because almost everyone is materially better off than they would have been in a pre-property state of nature; they will regard my standing-constraint reading as smuggling distributive egalitarianism into a theory of original acquisition. A second group is sympathetic to left-libertarian readings of Locke: they share my egalitarian interpretation of the proviso but think my demotion of labour from a full title to a value-added claim severs the self-ownership foundations of property and will weaken the very rights they want to defend against taxation and redistribution. A third group is represented by an early modern historian who is skeptical of rational reconstruction generally and will argue that applying contemporary distributive concepts to Locke is anachronistic. Prepare me for that question period: the objections in roughly the order I will actually face them, phrased the way these particular people would phrase them, with a note on which objections have real force, what I should concede up front, what I should hold firm on, where the weakest point in my own position is and how I should defend it rather than hope it goes unnoticed, and the question I am hoping nobody asks.
