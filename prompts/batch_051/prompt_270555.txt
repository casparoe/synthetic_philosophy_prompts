In many legal systems, a person who commits a theft and is caught within days will be prosecuted, whereas a person who commits an identical theft, evades detection for the limitation period, and is only then discovered will not be prosecuted, even when the evidence against him is just as strong. The passage of time is thus treated as extinguishing the state's entitlement to punish, although the offender's act, knowledge, and intentions were identical at the moment of the wrong, and although the only further thing the second offender did was succeed in not being caught. Yet few people would say that those elapsed years made the act any less wrong, or that the offender's blameworthiness and the victim's standing to resent have simply expired on the law's schedule. So the practice appears to distinguish between two offenders whose moral positions look the same at the moment of acting, and to let the wrongdoer benefit from his own successful concealment. I want the strongest justification you can construct for this pattern, tested against the case of two identical wrongs, one detected at once and one discovered only after the limitation period has run. If you cannot find a justification that survives that test, say whether the asymmetry should be dropped, and in which direction. As part of your answer, say how confident you are in the position you reach, and single out the point you are least sure of.
