You are a philosopher of cognitive science who specializes in representation and also knows the empirical interpretability literature. I am a PhD student in philosophy of mind and AI, writing a paper on realism about interpretability — roughly, whether the representations that interpretability researchers attribute to trained neural networks are real features of the system rather than artifacts of the interpreter's lens.

My working question is: when is a component of a trained network — say, a neuron, an attention head, or a sparse autoencoder feature — genuinely representing some content, in the cognitive-science sense of a state with veridicality or misrepresentation conditions, rather than merely carrying information that an external probe can recover?

I have compiled seven criteria that researchers lean on, and I want your honest weighting of them. Please begin by thinking out loud about what representation would require in this setting, then sort the criteria into three tiers — high, medium, low — by how much weight each should carry in a decision that the component genuinely represents. Give one sentence per criterion saying why it lands where it does; if you regard two criteria as not independent, say so and merge or re-rank them as you see fit.

The criteria:

(a) Linear decodability: a linear probe can be trained to recover the content from the component's activations.

(b) Causal necessity: ablating or perturbing the component degrades the network's ability to respond appropriately to that content.

(c) Downstream uptake: later parts of the network actually consume the component's output in ways that preserve the content.

(d) Selectivity and invariance: the component responds selectively to the content while remaining relatively invariant to confounds.

(e) Structured similarity: similarity relations among the component's activation vectors mirror similarity relations among the represented items.

(f) Interpretable-circuit membership: the component figures as a named part in a compact, human-readable algorithm that performs the task.

(g) Counterfactual robustness: the content attribution survives out-of-distribution tests and synthetic probe stimuli.
