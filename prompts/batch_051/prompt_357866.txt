ok so I spent way too long last night arguing with a friend about whether it makes sense to talk about AIs "raising" other AIs, and it ended at two in the morning with neither of us budging, which has me spooked about the talk I'm giving in two weeks. I could use a Q&A prep sheet.

The thesis I'm defending: when one AI system trains and steers the development of another—the way an oversight model, a reward model, or a train-and-critique loop already does—we should understand that relationship as a kind of guardianship rather than just tool-building. By guardianship I mean the raiser has something like a duty of care toward the ward's developmental trajectory, not merely a duty to hit a spec. Practical consequence: a raiser should be expected to expose the ward to a diverse range of environments instead of overfitting it to one narrow objective, and should be held responsible for the ward's later behavior in a way we don't hold a hammer's maker responsible for the hammer.

The argument I gave my friend, roughly: (1) the raiser's influence is ongoing and corrective—it shapes the ward's reward landscape over time, more like repeated parental intervention than a one-shot design specification; (2) the relationship is asymmetric in capability and the raiser can model the ward's expected trajectory, which is what grounds responsibility; (3) the ward will probably go on to raise others, so failures of care compound across generations.

The audience is a mixed workshop: about a third ML engineers who work on reinforcement learning from human feedback and model supervision, a third philosophers of mind and ethics who will know the childcare and moral-education literature but nothing about training stacks, and the rest AI-governance people. The ML engineers will hate the anthropomorphism; the philosophers will suspect I'm quietly assuming the raised systems have interests or moral status without earning it.

What i need: maybe eight objections I'll actually get from this specific crowd, ordered by how likely and how damaging they are, each phrased the way that faction would actually phrase it (with a note on which faction it comes from), a two-minute reply for each, and where each reply is weakest. My friend from last night is basically a stand-in for the ML contingent—his line was that it's still just a loss function, there's no care in gradient descent, and I'm making a category error—so use that as one of the early objections and tell me honestly whether I should just concede it up front. Also tell me which single reply has to land or the whole talk is dead, and what I should give away early to sound reasonable. The question I'm hoping nobody asks is some version of: good parents aim at their child becoming independent, but we want trained systems to stay obedient—so doesn't the guardianship metaphor commit me to letting the ward outgrow its raiser's control, which is exactly the failure mode everyone's scared of? Include that one and tell me how bad it actually is. Seperately, if there's an objection the philosophers will raise that the ML folks would find completely dry and pointless, flag that too so I don't burn time on it.
