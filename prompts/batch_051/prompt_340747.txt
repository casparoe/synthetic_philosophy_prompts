I'm a journalist on the science-and-ideas beat, and I've been turning over a resemblance that I can't decide is structural or merely verbal. The Septuagint, the ancient Greek translation of the Hebrew scriptures, comes with a famous legend: seventy-two translators are said to have been shut away from one another and made to render the text independently, yet when their versions were compared they agreed word for word. The tellers of the story read that convergence as the signature of something beyond ordinary human skill, a divine hand guiding the translation. In its barest form, the inference is that independent efforts with no communication between them that nevertheless land on identical output point to a common cause outside any individual worker.

That is, near enough, the logic of independent replication in the sciences. Several laboratories run the same procedure without colluding, and convergence on the same result is taken as evidence that the result tracks something real, a cause outside the labs rather than an artifact of one group's biases. But the two cases appear to point in opposite directions: replication points toward an impersonal fact, while the Septuagint legend points toward a mind. I'm trying to determine whether these are genuine instances of a single principle, something like the rule that agreement across independent and error-prone sources, when not forced, licenses an inference to a shared cause, or whether they merely share a loose shape. One worry is that a measurement is supposed to converge when it tracks its object, whereas a translation is not: two competent translators working independently would normally be expected to diverge, so word-for-word identity may not be the translation-case analogue of replicating a finding. What would have to be true on the translation side for the Septuagint argument to carry the force its tellers took it to have, and does the analogy with replication actually survive that scrutiny? And does any of this illuminate the underlying question whether a translated text can be inspired, or does it just dress an old argument in scientific costume?
