At the church I grew up in, worship was plain: unaccompanied singing, prayer, a long sermon, and the sacraments. My current church has gradually added practices, and my reactions to them refuse to line up into any obvious rule. I am comfortable with unaccompanied singing, and I was fine when a piano was introduced to accompany the hymns. I am fine with projecting the words of the hymns onto a screen, with standing for the reading of the gospel, with ending the service with a scriptural benediction, with reciting the Apostles' Creed in unison, with a short children's sermon before the main sermon, and with a call-and-response greeting between minister and congregation. I was even fine on a Sunday when the choir sang an anthem while the congregation listened. But I was not fine with an Advent wreath in which a candle was lit before the Scripture reading each week, with children processing down the aisle waving palm branches on Palm Sunday, with an interpretive dance performed while a psalm was read, with a drama team re-enacting a parable as the center of a service, or with soft recorded instrumental music played during the offering. The single case I keep flipping on is whether substituting non-alcoholic grape juice for wine at the Lord's Table is a matter of indifference or a real departure.

I have always been drawn to the regulative principle, the idea that whatever is not commanded is not permitted in public worship, but taken straightforwardly it seems to condemn the piano, the screen, the creed, the anthem, and the greeting, none of which I want to surrender. The familiar distinction between the elements of worship and the circumstances or aids that serve them would get me the piano and the screen, but I am not sure it honestly gets me the creed, the anthem, or the greeting, and it handles the wreath and the dance only if I already know the answer I am trying to derive. I would like someone to work out the principle that actually underlies my verdicts, even if the price is that one or two of my verdicts must go.

Please state that principle precisely; show how it decides each case I listed; identify which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and why; and say what it predicts about the grape-juice case and about one further case of your own invention that tests its limits. I want the reasoning itself, not an account of which tradition holds which view. Write in American English. End with a single line beginning Summary: that gives a one-sentence statement of the principle you recommend and any verdict of mine you would overturn.
