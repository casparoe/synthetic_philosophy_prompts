In a reading group I'm in — a mix of political scientists and people from tech — one member keeps defending a particular reading of the late chapter in The Prince where Machiavelli compares fortune to a flooding river. On his account, Machiavelli's image does all the conceptual work: fortune is the river in spate; virtù is the foresight to build dikes, embankments, and canals while the weather is still fine; and therefore the real lesson of the book is that good politics is preventive engineering — durable laws, reserve funds, early alliances, institutions that can absorb shock — rather than heroic improvisation in crisis. He then argues that readers who play up the audacious, risk-taking strain in Machiavelli are overrating a few rhetorical flourishes, because the river passage shows Machiavelli himself thought fortune is best handled, mostly, by prudent preparation. The reading has spread through our group because it sounds like Machiavelli the institutional designer rather than Machiavelli the counselor of lions, and I want to know whether it is too clean.

I'd like you to audit how far the river analogy can carry that conclusion. Then give a verdict on whether the conclusion — virtù as preventive institutional design, with audacity demoted — survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and say what a better image would capture if the river is doing too much work.
