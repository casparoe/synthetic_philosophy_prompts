I have been working through a tension in the philosophy of mathematics, and I would like help working out which side should give way — not a verdict for one camp.

On one side is the constructivist picture behind intuitionistic logic and the Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov interpretation. Its claim is that the meaning of a mathematical statement is fixed by what counts as a proof of it: a proof of A ∨ B is a proof of one disjunct plus an indication of which one; a proof of ∃x P(x) is a constructed object plus a proof that it has P; a proof of A → B is a method for turning any proof of A into a proof of B. This picture has real strengths. It explains why an existence claim in mathematics is not discharged merely by showing that the denial of existence leads to contradiction. It explains why intuitionistic arithmetic has the disjunction and existence properties: whenever it proves a disjunction it proves one disjunct, and whenever it proves an existential it proves a specific instance. It gives logic direct computational content, as in the Curry–Howard correspondence. And it has a built-in account of classical mathematics, because the double-negation translation embeds classical logic in intuitionistic logic, reading a classical theorem roughly as "it is not not the case that..."

On the other side is a conviction I find I cannot drop: that for a precisely posed statement about a definite structure such as the natural numbers, there is a fact of the matter. Either there are infinitely many twin primes or there are not, and this seems true in a sense that has nothing to do with whether anyone ever constructs a proof of one disjunct. Excluded middle presents itself to me as a law of thought rather than a bold hypothesis, and much of ordinary mathematics — including arguments that a purported counterexample would contradict a proof — appears to depend on it. It also seems perfectly coherent to say "one of these alternatives holds, though we may never know which," whereas the constructivist semantics seems to license only the weaker claim that it is not not the case that one holds. I know the standard reconciliation is to read my intuition as exactly that double-negation, but "it is not not the case that there are infinitely many twin primes or there are not" does not feel like the same thought as "there is a fact of the matter."

What I want is not a partisan answer. I want to know what it would take for the realist intuition to be the thing that goes, what it would take for the constructivist semantics to be the thing that goes, and whether there is a reading of "true" on which both survive. Which purported reconciliations — the double-negation reading, a distinction between truth and warranted assertibility, a pluralism about mathematical truth — are substantive, and which merely relabel one side? And what, if anything, could count as evidence that one of these pictures of mathematics is the better one?
