In the novel I am drafting, two enemy powers can travel in time, and each sends one agent to the same moment in 1930 to kill the other's grandfather before the other's parent is conceived. As I tried to write the climax, I realized the setup is a two-agent variant of the grandfather paradox, and I want a precise account of how the standard analyses carry over or break down.

The canonical case is this: a single traveler goes back to kill her own grandfather before her parent is conceived. The familiar responses, as I understand them, are three. First, time travel of this sort is impossible, because it would permit an inconsistent history. Second, the timeline is single and self-consistent, so the attempt has to fail — the gun misfires, the plan miscarries, or the traveler changes her mind — because only globally consistent histories can occur, and a successful self-erasing murder is not one of them. Third, the visited past is not her own past, or it splits off from her own, so she can kill a counterpart of her grandfather while the history she came from remains intact.

Now the variant. Two travelers, A and B, each go back to kill the other's grandfather before the other's parent is conceived. A's existence depends on A's grandfather surviving; B's existence depends on B's grandfather surviving; neither depends on the other's ancestor. Each killing, if it occurs, requires the killer to have been born and to have traveled back, so being present at the scene already carries information about who exists in the future. I want to keep the case clean: the two agents are strangers, their missions are independent, and apart from the assassination plot no causal line connects the two families.

For the single self-consistent timeline view, I want the space of globally consistent histories worked out, with presence at the scene handled explicitly rather than taken for granted. Is a history in which both A and B are present and both kill consistent? Is a history in which A kills while B is present but fails consistent? Is a history in which exactly one kills consistent, and if so, must the other be absent? Is a history in which both are present and both fail consistent? Then state the general rule. My suspicion is that self-consistency stops guaranteeing that any particular attempt fails and starts guaranteeing something relational between the two fates, but I want the exact statement and the reasoning behind it.

Then run the same case through the other two analyses. Does the impossibility argument still bite, given that the two-agent version appears to contain at least one consistent single timeline? On the branching-worlds view, can both assassinations occur in the same encountered past while A and B both continue to exist as visitors from a history in which both grandfathers lived, and is that a strength of the view or a cost? What I ultimately need, as the person who has to finish the plot, is a decision table: if I want both characters to pull triggers in the same room, what does each analysis require me to write next — a jammed gun, a misidentified victim, a man already absent from history, a split in the timeline, or something none of the three familiar options has named?
