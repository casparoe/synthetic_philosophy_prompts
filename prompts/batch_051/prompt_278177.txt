I serve on a small committee that awards equal-cost grants using a common well-being scale, with higher scores representing greater well-being. The population we serve divides into two groups: a persistently worse-off group currently at 20 points, and a much better-off group. Our governing idea has been that benefits to the worse-off group should receive special weight, and we have sometimes said that what makes those benefits especially urgent is that the worse-off group sits below 28 points.

Our recent decisions:
(a) We funded a proposal expected to add 8 points to the worse-off group and none to the better-off, rather than one that would have added 60 points to the better-off group alone.
(b) We funded a proposal expected to add 4 points to the worse-off group and 5 to the better-off, rather than one that would have added 20 points to the better-off group alone.
(c) We declined a proposal whose only expected effect was to leave the worse-off group unchanged while subtracting 30 points from the better-off group; its sponsors defended it as valuable simply because it narrowed the gap.

We now face a choice between two equal-cost proposals: one expected to add 3 points to the worse-off group and 80 to the better-off; the other expected to add 8 points to the worse-off group and none to the better-off. If we want to be consistent with our own precedents, which should we fund? Lay out the distinct principles our past decisions could embody, say what each one would prescribe for this new choice, identify the closest precedent, explain what principle we would be adopting with each possible decision, and describe a further case that would most cleanly settle which principle we have actually been applying.
