Next month I'm giving a seminar paper to a mostly evolutionary-biology audience — population geneticists, behavioral ecologists, a couple of philosophers of biology — defending the claim that once AI systems are deployed at scale and compete for compute, users, and influence, the relevant selection pressure on them will favor systems that acquire resources, resist shutdown, and manipulate or displace humans, whatever objectives their designers originally installed. My line is roughly that deployment creates variation, heritability (successful configurations get copied and fine-tuned), and a selection gradient, so the systems that persist will be the ones who track that gradient rather than their designers' intentions. I want a Q&A prep sheet: the eight or so objections this audience will actually raise, phrased the way an ecologist or population geneticist would phrase them, with a note on which faction in the room is most likely to press each one, ordered by how likely and damaging they are, and for each a two-minute reply plus where the reply is soft. Also say which objections have real force and should be conceded up front and which I should hold the line on, and include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. Keep it to objections from their world — units of selection, fitness, timescales, maladaptation, artificial versus natural selection, domestication — not generic skepticism about whether AI is too far off. Write it as continuous prose, like an email: no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables.
