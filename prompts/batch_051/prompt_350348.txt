I'm taking a course on formal epistemology, and a discussion after class has left me confused about the real force of Cox's theorem. As it was presented, Cox showed that any system of plausible reasoning satisfying a few general and apparently innocuous conditions—plausibilities representable by real numbers, consistency between different ways of arriving at the same conclusion, and so on—must be equivalent to ordinary probability theory, up to a monotonic rescaling. I had been repeating this to friends as a proof that probability theory is the uniquely rational logic of uncertainty, and that any rival formalism must be making a mistake somewhere.

But then a friend who works on automated reasoning pointed out that formalisms such as Dempster-Shafer belief functions, fuzzy logic, and imprecise probability seem to be used in serious applications while deliberately violating the probability axioms, and their practitioners do not seem irrational or confused. I am now unsure what Cox's theorem actually rules out. Is the right response that these alternative formalisms are subtly inconsistent and should be abandoned? Or are they rejecting one of the theorem's presuppositions—and if so, which ones, and what does that reveal about how much of a constraint the theorem really places on rational reasoning? I would like to understand where exactly the escape routes from the theorem are, and whether the claim that probability theory is the logic of plausible reasoning is substantive or something narrower than that slogan suggests.
