I'm leading a session for a reading group of generally literate adults with no formal background in philosophy or cognitive science, and the topic is the thesis of Hofstadter and Sander's Surfaces and Essences: that analogy is not a special, occasional flourish of language but the basic engine underneath word learning, category formation, mathematical insight, and theoretical novelty in science. I don't need the explanation itself; I need a plan for how to teach it. In particular, I want the sequence of ideas arranged so that each move rests only on what has already been secured, and so that the thesis shifts in the listener's mind from sounding absurd to feeling nearly inevitable. I'm inclined to make the word mother the single carrying example — a child's mother, then mother lode, motherboard, the mother of all battles, Mother Nature — but before I commit to it, I want an assessment of what that example has going for it, what any carrying example for this idea must have and must lack, and where this one would begin to mislead. I also want the misconception map: for instance, that analogy is merely decorative comparison, that literal and figurative language are two cleanly different kinds, that real categories are held together by necessary and sufficient conditions, that analogies are too loose to generate new knowledge, and that calling thought analogical makes all meaning subjective. For each, say the point in the sequence where it should be headed off rather than argued against after it has taken hold.

Finally, I want a single closing exercise or question whose answer would show whether the group has actually arrived at the deeper point — that on this view an essence or concept is what stays constant across a family of analogical extensions, not a hidden definition that the extensions merely point to. And tell me what I should consciously refuse to take on at this level: the computational modeling of analogy, the structure-mapping debate, neural evidence, the book's extended case histories — and how to refuse those things without leaving the audience feeling they have been handed a metaphor dressed up as a theory. Keep the answer in American English.
