I teach a course on moral philosophy, and we are about to do a unit on the ethics of risk imposition without consent: the distinctive wrong involved when one party makes a decision that exposes other people to a chance of serious harm without asking them. I want to use Steven Spielberg's Jaws as a case study, and I would like a careful philosophical reading of the film focused on that issue. The central episode is Mayor Vaughn's decision to keep Amity's beaches open, and to downplay the danger, after it becomes clear that a large shark is in the water; he is protecting the town's summer economy. Please separate, as sharply as you can, the wrong of exposing swimmers to a deadly risk without their informed consent from the wrong of deceiving them about that risk. Is the deception the real moral violation, an aggravating factor, or something else? Then consider a counterfactual: if the town had publicly posted the risk and allowed each person to decide whether to enter the water, what exactly would have changed morally, and what would not? Also take seriously the reverse direction of risk: when the authorities do restrict access or close the beaches, they impose costs, and possibly risks of their own, on people who depend on the summer season and never consented to those losses. In your reading, identify which scenes or decisions most clearly dramatize the ethical structure you describe, and where the film's need for a culpable official and a visible shark might distort the harder reality of routine, diffuse, statistically managed risk. Does the film locate the moral failure in individual greed and cowardice in a way that lets the audience off the hook, and would a philosopher of risk regard that as a legitimate dramatic compression or as a distortion?
