I am a PhD student in the philosophy of cognitive science, and I have been trying to work out whether the account of the self associated with Douglas Hofstadter is an empirical thesis, a conceptual redescription, or something in between. On that account, the human 'I' is not an inner substance and not a stored model of the person, but a self-referential symbol: a strange loop in which a symbol system represents its own representing, and which persists only while that loop runs. The difficulty is that the claim is usually stated at a level that is hard to test.

Suppose we insist, for the sake of argument, that a strange loop is a causal-functional kind—a pattern of self-referential processing that could in principle be identified from outside the system. To make that precise, imagine two artificial agents that both use the first person fluently. Agent A contains an explicit, queryable self-model, updated by a subsystem that operates on representations of the agent's own states. Agent B contains no such model; instead, its own outputs, tagged as self-produced, are fed back among the inputs that determine its next self-ascriptions, so that its competence with 'I' depends on the continued running of a loop.

Design a concrete experiment, or a small programme of experiments, that would discriminate between the strange-loop account and the explicit self-model account as characterisations of these two agents. Specify the intervention or measurement, the behavioural or representational quantities observed, and what pattern of results would count as evidence for each account. Finally, state what a favourable result would still not establish: which part of the claim would remain an interpretive or conceptual commitment that no such experiment could settle. Please write the answer in British English.
