I've been going down a rabbit hole in the well-being literature about whether novelty or variety is its own prudential good — something that makes a life go better over and above the pleasures and achievements it causes — or whether it's only instrumental, a hack for keeping hedonic adaptation from flattening repeated experiences. Honestly I can't tell if I'm rationally valuing adventure in my own life or just scared of being bored in a way that says more about my dopamin system than about what actually makes a life good. Anyway, I rewatched Groundhog Day with this in the back of my head and I'd like you to do a proper reading of the film through that lens. The obvious take is that Phil's early problem is too much optionality and too little that constrains him, and that the way out is to turn infinite possibilities into a repeatable day with purpose — piano lessons, ice sculpting, catching the kid falling from the tree, the flat tires. That makes the movie look like an argument that novelty is overrated and what really matters is mastery, connection, and meaning. But I'm not sure that's quite faithful: the loops still contain surprise within repetition, like never knowing how Rita will respond, or whether he can actually land a new skill, and the film seems to think that matters. So where does the film actually come down on the question — are variety and adventure components of well-being, merely instrumental, or something more like a symptom of the underlying thing that's missing? Which scenes complicate the easy 'novelty is overrated' reading, and does the film's own repetitiveness for the viewer do philosophical work? I'd rather you tell me where the movie resists the theory than force it into neat category.
