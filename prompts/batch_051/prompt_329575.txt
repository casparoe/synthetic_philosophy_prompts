I've mostly come around to Williams's integrity objection, or at least a version of it I'd be willing to defend. Start from the drowning-wife case: a man has to choose between saving his wife and saving a stranger, and before he acts he finds himself having to establish that it is at least permissible, from an impartial point of view, to save her. I want to say that if that extra step is doing any real justificatory work, the theory that requires or invites it has already lost something—it can't make room for acting from a relationship in the way the relationship itself calls for. I'll go further: this isn't just about what would be eerie to announce, it's about the reasons themselves, and a well-functioning agent in that situation should not have to pose the question of whether the act is justified, even privately.

Okay, so what counts against it? Not the stock replies—indirect consequentialism, rule consequentialism, the line that deliberative constraints aren't criteria of rightness. I want an actual exposure map. Which cases would make this seem to prove too much (unreflective partiality is clearly sometimes bad, and sometimes the pause to deliberate is exactly what makes an act the right one), and which would make it seem too weak (it only stings act-consequentialism, or only a naive picture of applying a theory)? Which commitments does it stand or fall with—a clean split between reasons and motives, a clean split between first-order action and second-order justification, the irreducibility of relationship-relative reasons? Then the bit I keep tripping over: if a consequentialist says he simply has a very high utility weight on his wife, so there is no detour in deliberation, is that a dodge, a change of subject, or a concession? What would the integrity objection have to be committed to for that move not to count as an answer, and what would have to be true for that commitment to fail? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, including what the position predicts about less dramatic cases that its rivals don't, an honest statement of which ones a committed Williamsian can brush off without cheating, and a verdict on whether the view has been armored into unfalsifiability—and, if it has, whether that is a bug or just the price of doing moral psychology.
