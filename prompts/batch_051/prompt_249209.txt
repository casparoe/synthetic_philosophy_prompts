A column I read summarized the view some political philosophers call "epistocracy" roughly as follows. Democracy, the columnist said, assumes every adult is equally capable of weighing complex policy, but most voters know almost nothing about the parties, the budget, or basic economics. The epistocrat's point is simple: if you would not let the passengers jointly fly the plane or perform the surgery, why let them jointly run the state? Defenders of the view therefore propose that citizens with demonstrated political knowledge — say, the top scorers on a civics test — should receive extra votes, or even the exclusive right to vote. The column added that several epistocrats would restrict the franchise to the college-educated, since education tracks civic competence; the resulting state, it concluded, would be governed by people whose values are more reliably sound than the average voter's. The main obstacle, the column said, is the intuition that all citizens have equal political standing — an intuition epistocrats are willing to set aside because they hold that only the quality of outcomes matters.

I suspect the broad shape of this is right but that a few details have been flattened in the way op-eds flatten philosophy — especially the slide from political knowledge to college education, and the slide from making better decisions to having better values. Please give me an accuracy audit of whether this summary is fair: go piece by piece, and for each piece say what a careful defender of epistocracy would accept, what they would qualify, and what they would reject as a distortion. Throughout, separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. I would like exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
