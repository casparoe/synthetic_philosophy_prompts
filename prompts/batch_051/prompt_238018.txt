I am a junior doctor in an NHS hospital, and I have been turning over a disagreement with my registrar. A patient presented with a sore throat, a low-grade fever, and no concerning signs; the registrar considers a viral cause overwhelmingly likely and would send the patient home without antibiotics. I am less certain, and I am considering a short course of antibiotics, partly because the case is not completely clear-cut and partly because I dislike sending people away with nothing. When I raised the issue of antimicrobial stewardship, the registrar replied that my individual prescribing decisions make no difference to antibiotic resistance. In his words, resistance is driven by massive volumes of use in agriculture and healthcare and by poor infection control, and no resistant strain will ever emerge, or fail to emerge, because one junior doctor writes or withholds one prescription. He concluded that if my only remaining hesitation is stewardship, I should set it aside and decide purely on the clinical needs of the patient in front of me.

I could not identify the flaw at the time, but the argument felt too quick. I would like a rigorous philosophical assessment of whether the registrar's reasoning is sound. In particular, I want to understand whether a very small individual contribution to a very large aggregate harm can give me a reason to act differently even when my action is almost certain not to be the decisive one, and where the registrar's argument goes wrong, if it does. Please answer in British English.
