I've been kicking around a term I call "orphan reasons," and I want an honest read on whether it's a real concept or just a metaphor I got attached to. The idea is that a reason can keep doing normative work — justifying a rule, sustaining an obligation — long after no one actually holds it or can state it. Once a reason gets "launched" by being built into a rule or a practice, it can float free of any particular reasoner; it's a reason with the host gone. Example: a licensing rule was adopted because people thought the credential prevented some specific harm; we later learn it doesn't, but the rule stays on the books and people treat it as still carrying the original rationale, which nobody actually believes anymore. Another example: a family keeps a ritual for "the reason we've always done it," even though no living member can say what the reason was. I know reasons are sometimes supposed to be mind-independent, but I mean something narrower: not just that a reason can exist apart from any one person, but that a reason can outlive everyone who ever grasped it and keep doing the same job through the practice. Is this just institutional inertia plus deference to past people, dressed up in new language? What's the nearest existing philosophical idea, and is mine just a version of it? And where does it break — what would my view count as an orphaned reason that's really just a new, currently-held reason (say, predictability, authority, or habit) doing the work?
