Two people in my research group were arguing at lunch about a friend of one of them, and I left unable to say which of them was right about which claims. Could you go through the exchange move by move and say, for each claim, who has the better of it, who is overstating, and what assumption might be quietly doing the work?

The argument was about Michael, who is four years into a PhD he says he no longer wants and no longer expects to use; he is miserable, and yet he leans toward finishing, partly because he cannot stand the thought of the four years having been for nothing.

Daniel said the case is a textbook sunk-cost fallacy. The four years are gone no matter what Michael decides, so a rational decision compares only the future path of finishing against the future path of quitting from now; backward-looking reasons, like "those years must not be wasted," have no place.

Priya said Daniel had drawn that comparison too coarsely. From now, finishing has about a year of cost left before it yields a credential, a narrative of completion, and an identity as someone who finished; quitting means those same years come to count as failed preparation. Those are all future consequences of the options, and the fact that Michael is four years in is exactly what makes them cheap or costly. So even comparing only the future, the past can enter through the way it shapes future costs and meanings.

Daniel answered: granted, but that is not what Michael says when he defends staying. Michael says he cannot let those years be for nothing, which is a reason aimed at the past rather than at the future. A completed narrative is one thing; grief over an unredeemed investment is another, and only the first is a reason.

Priya said the distinction is too sharp to survive contact with real choice. A completed narrative just is, for that kind of person, what redeems the years. If Michael would experience the same facts as more coherent, less regrettable, and less identity-damaging if he finished, then his preference is a preference about a future state, and Daniel has not shown it is irrational; he has only shown that it is psychological rather than financial.

Daniel said the very fact that the preference is psychological makes it suspicious. Preferring a flattering story about one's past is itself a taste, and tastes formed under loss aversion can be questioned. If someone would spend a fifth unhappy year to buy a less painful account of years one through four, we should ask why the less painful account must be bought at that price rather than achieved by re-narrating the past. Quitting lets Michael retell the four years as a costly way of learning what he does not want; it does not force him to count them as useless.

Priya said that retelling only works up to a point: Michael will know he left an open promise unfinished, and self-respect is built partly on finishing things past the point where they stopped feeling good. If he contracts the habit of quitting whenever the local cost-benefit ledger turns negative, he may buy a better marginal decision now at the cost of becoming a person he trusts less.

Daniel answered that this was the strongest point he had heard, but it is a policy argument and not a reason for this decision. If the worry is becoming a serial quitter, Michael should adopt a forward-looking rule about when persistence is worth it; he should not treat this particular past investment as a reason. Using the four years here to make the decision sticky is just having the sunk-cost fallacy do the work of an unadopted commitment device.

Priya said a commitment device can work without being chosen in advance: the past investment really has changed what he can now walk away from. To call it a fallacy is to pretend he is back at year one with full optionality, and he is not.

I found them close to deadlock. I would like you to work through the exchange in order, saying where each speaker makes a valid point and where each overreaches, including any places where one changes what the other needs to show. At the end, say what the decisive question would be if Michael asked for advice rather than for a ruling on sunk costs. Please write the adjudication as continuous prose, the way you would write an email, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
