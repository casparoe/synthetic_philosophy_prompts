I am a retired physiologist, and I have only recently begun to read academic philosophy. My instinct from a working life in the laboratory is to test a proposed definition against cases before trusting it, and that is what I should like you to do here.

I have been reading William James's article 'What is an Emotion?', published in Mind in 1884, and the following passage has unsettled me:

'I should say first of all that the only emotions I propose expressly to consider here are those that have a distinct bodily expression. That there are feelings of pleasure and displeasure, of interest and excitement, bound up with mental operations, but having no obvious bodily expression for their consequence, would, I suppose, be held true by most readers. Certain arrangements of sounds, of lines, of colours, are agreeable, and others the reverse, without the degree of the feeling being sufficient to quicken the pulse or breathing, or to prompt to movements of either the body or the face.'

That passage is often treated as a preliminary disclaimer, but it may actually be doing the main philosophical work: James appears to restrict the term 'emotion' to episodes with an obvious bodily expression and to set intellectual and aesthetic feelings aside as something else. I cannot tell whether this is a stipulation, an empirical claim about what the ordinary word tracks, or something in between.

I should like a working definition of 'emotion' — yours, not necessarily James's — that has been tested against hard cases. Some of the following are to my mind clearly emotions, some clearly are not, and several I genuinely cannot settle; do not feel obliged to agree with my sorting:

1. Terror when a car swerves across into one's lane.
2. A hot flash of anger at an insult.
3. The quiet satisfaction of finishing a difficult calculation.
4. Being moved by a landscape without any quickened pulse or impulse to move.
5. A startle reflex when a door slams.
6. A weeks-long homesickness with no sharp episodes, only a low persistent ache.
7. Acute pain in an injured knee.
8. A cool intellectual recognition that a policy is unfair, felt with no bodily change and no inclination to act.
9. A stab of envy at a colleague's appointment.
10. An objectless anxious dread on waking, before anything has come to mind.

Give me the definition, run it against each case, say explicitly where it gives the wrong answer or no answer, and then either revise the definition or defend the surprising verdicts. I am especially interested in whether James's line — bodily expression as the mark of emotion proper — should survive contact with these cases, and in the points at which your definition is forced to defer to ordinary usage rather than settle the matter. Please write your answer in American English.
