In David Hume's essay Of the Standard of Taste, Hume observes that the apparent disagreement about beauty is less than the real disagreement:

"As this variety of taste is obvious to the most careless enquirer; so will it be found, on examination, to be still greater in reality than in appearance. The sentiments of men often differ with regard to beauty and deformity of all kinds, even while their general discourse is the same."

I have been struck by a structural parallel between this passage and the situation in contemporary psychometrics when a construct is measured by multiple raters. There, too, different raters assign different scores to the same object even while applying the same verbal labels; the field responds not by treating their disagreement as disqualifying the construct, but by estimating a latent score that the raters are imperfectly measuring and by evaluating raters according to how well their judgments cohere under a specified model. Hume's ideal judges—whom he later characterizes by delicacy, practice, comparison, and absence of prejudice—seem to play the role of high-quality raters whose convergence fixes the standard of taste. Is this parallel deep or only a loose analogy? In particular, can Hume's standard of taste be usefully reconstructed as an early latent-variable model of aesthetic quality, with the ideal judges serving as a calibration panel? If so, does psychometrics give reason to think that such convergence is evidence of an objective aesthetic property, or merely of a socially selected agreement? Or does the analogy fail because Hume's judges are meant to correct defective taste rather than to be fallible indicators whose consensus estimates an independent quality? Take a definite position and defend it, rather than laying out the options.
