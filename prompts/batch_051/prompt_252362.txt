Give me a working definition of a country's happiness — the thing a national well-being ranking would have to track if a government were justified in treating that ranking as a target of policy — that gets the following cases right.

(a) A country where the average answer to the standard 0–10 life-evaluation question is high, and daily affect measures, time-use records, and observer reports all confirm that people mostly enjoy their day-to-day lives.
(b) A country with the same high average life-evaluation score as (a), but where time-use surveys record frequent stress and anger and very little enjoyment.
(c) A country whose average life-evaluation score rises in a given year entirely because its least satisfied residents emigrated in that year.
(d) A country whose citizens, after long repression and economic stagnation, report moderate life satisfaction — apparently because they have lowered their expectations — while daily affect measures show frequent fear and little enthusiasm.
(e) A country with a high average life-evaluation score in which the bottom tenth of the distribution reports extreme misery.
(f) A country whose residents report high life satisfaction and frequent positive emotion, but score low on standard measures of meaning and purpose.
(g) A country where income, health, and physical safety have improved markedly over two decades, yet average life-evaluation scores have not risen, because aspirations and reference points have risen at the same pace.

I am inclined to count (a) as clearly happiness and (c) as clearly not a genuine rise in the country's happiness. I would call (e) happiness, but badly distributed. I have no settled view on (b), (d), (f), or (g). Propose the definition, run it through all seven cases, and say where it gives a surprising verdict or requires overruling one of my judgments. If the best way to fit the cases is to distinguish the happiness of the country from the happiness of the people in it, state which of those two the main international rankings are in fact measuring.
