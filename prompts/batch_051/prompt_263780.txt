I recently read an essay in a general-interest magazine about why certain moral practices disappear. The author presented what she called the honor theory of moral revolution, attributed mainly to Kwame Anthony Appiah, with Bernard Williams invoked as philosophical backup. Her summary, as I remember it, ran roughly as follows.

Practices like dueling and chattel slavery were not brought to an end by new or better moral arguments. The intellectual case against both had been available for centuries and had done next to nothing. What changed, in each case, was a shift in what counted as honorable and shameful. Dueling collapsed when it lost its aristocratic cachet: once honor attached to declining or ridiculing the duel rather than fighting one, dueling came to look like low-status brawling, and gentlemen abandoned it within a generation. Slavery, in Appiah's account of the British case, ended the same way: abolition became a point of national and personal honor rather than merely a moral demand, so that support for the slave trade became shameful in a way philosophical argument had never made it. Williams's role, the essayist said, is to show that the modern rejection of slavery was not the discovery of an argument that Aristotle and his contemporaries somehow missed, but a historically produced change in ethical outlook. From this the essayist concluded, presenting it as the joint view of the two authors, that the engine of moral change is honor rather than reason: principles and arguments trail behind status, and moral argument by itself is causally inert.

I find the general idea attractive, but I suspect this packs several distinct claims together, and the last sentence in particular sounds like an overstatement. I don't know enough about what Appiah and Williams actually say to be sure which parts of this are fair to them, which parts are the essayist's own gloss, and which parts a defender of either author would reject. Could you go through the summary claim by claim and give me an accuracy audit: what is faithful to the honor-theoretic account of moral revolutions, what is exaggerated or conflated, and whether the conclusion that moral argument is causally inert actually follows from anything accurate in the summary? I want to know which claims I can safely repeat and where the essay has overshot its sources.
