For the past year I have been reading philosophy of probability in retirement, after a career in physical chemistry, and the problem that has most unsettled my old statistical habits is the reference class problem: every person belongs to indefinitely many groups, and the probability we assign to some event for that person changes depending on which group we select as the comparison class. I now volunteer two days a week on the clinical risk committee of a mid-sized hospital, where our analysts estimate individual patient risks—a given patient's chance of a postoperative infection, of readmission within thirty days, or of a serious drug interaction. These numbers are not academic; they inform preoperative counseling, prophylaxis choices, and follow-up intensity. We review two or three such estimates each week, and the most common failure is that the analyst takes the largest cohort that shares some obvious feature, without defending why that cohort is the right one.

I would like you to design a decision procedure for choosing a reference class that our analysts, who have no formal philosophy background, can actually follow. It should take the form of an ordered checklist or set of tests suitable for pasting into our estimation template. For each step, say what question is being asked, what counts as a sufficient answer to proceed, where the procedure should stop with a no rather than forcing a number, and where the decision must be passed to a person because the remaining choice is one of values or clinical judgment rather than data. Then apply the procedure to two cases we have argued about. First: an eighty-two-year-old patient with mild cognitive impairment and controlled hypertension needs an estimate of the risk of postoperative delirium; the analyst's preferred class is all patients over sixty-five having the same procedure, but our records also contain a smaller class of patients over eighty with cognitive impairment. Second: a twenty-four-year-old first-time offender needs a reoffending-risk estimate, and the two available classes—all persons with that charge statewide, and a demographically matched subset with the same offense history—yield materially different scores. I want at least one application to produce an answer the committee would rather not hear, for example a refusal to issue a single number or an instruction to use a coarser class than we would prefer. Finally, tell me honestly what the procedure cannot settle, and which of its steps are really value choices disguised as empirical determinations.
