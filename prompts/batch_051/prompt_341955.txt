I keep trying to settle my views on whether negative emotions are ever worth having, and I've started collecting my gut reactions to cases because they don't obviously add up.

My friend's teenage daughter died a few years ago, and in the immediate aftermath a doctor offered him a drug that would eliminate the grief completely without touching his memories or making him foggy. He refused. I'm pretty sure he was right, but all I could say was that the sadness "fits the situation." Meanwhile my sister went on antidepressants for a depression that had no obvious trigger and just settled over her, and I thought that was unambigously good — I'd have fought anyone who said she was loosing something valuable. A novelist I know says her work got shallower after a medication flattened her lows; she stopped writing the kinds of stories people used to love, and even though she's more functional day to day, I can't shake the sense that something was lost. Then there's a man I know whose mother died and who reported, without any bravado, that he never felt sad, not once, and saw no reason he should. I find that disturbing in a way I can't justify — he isn't hurting anyone, and he did love her. But I also know someone who deliberately goes on what she calls grief retreats, where she lets herself fall into sorrow about a loss she numbed through at the time, and I think that's admirable even though she's voluntarily courting pain. And when a friend took a mild sedative so he could get through his wife's funeral without collapsing, I had no objection at all — it felt like the same thing as the doctor's drug but at a lower dose, and I can't say why that changes anything. Finally, I think a world where everyone got a childhood implant that blocked sadness forever would be a worse world, not a better one, even if every person in it reported being happy.

My working hunch is something like: a negative emotion is worth having just when it's aimed at something that matters, the way grief is about the person who's gone, while aimless depression isn't about anything. But that doesn't survive contact with the funeral-sedative case, and the novelist case seems to be about more than just aboutness. Is there a principle that captures most of these verdicts? And which ones would I have to abandon, and what does the principle say about a person who could either live the safe life without loss or the riskier life with a great love that ends in devastating grief?
