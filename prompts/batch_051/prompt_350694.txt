Consider the following proposed constitutional protocol for handling secession in a multinational state, of the sort motivated by the recurring impasses in Scotland, Catalonia, and Quebec. A region may initiate the process either by a petition signed by at least eight percent of the regional electorate or by a majority vote of its regional legislature. Initiation opens a fixed eighteen-month negotiation period, during which the central government and the regional government must attempt to reach a separation agreement covering the division of public debt, citizenship and residency rights, currency and central-bank assets, public pensions, borders, and military installations. If no agreement is reached, the central government's last formal offer becomes the default text. A binding referendum is then held in the region, on the question of whether the region should become independent on the terms of the negotiated agreement or, absent one, on the terms of that default text. The regional electorate consists of registered voters who have been resident in the region for at least two years. Approval requires both that at least sixty percent of valid votes cast be in favor and that overall turnout reaches at least fifty-five percent of the regional electorate. If the referendum fails, no further process may be initiated in that region for twelve years. If it passes, the agreement is implemented over a four-year transition period, during which either side may withdraw from the agreement upon its own determination that the other side has materially breached the terms.

Please analyze how this mechanism would actually behave, not whether secession is desirable in the abstract. What does it incentivize for committed secessionists, committed unionists, ambivalent regional voters, and the central government? How might each actor game the petition threshold, the negotiation clock, the default-offer rule, the joint supermajority-and-turnout requirement, the twelve-year cooling-off period, and the transition-withdrawal clause? Where are the likely failure modes — for example, initiation used as a bargaining chip for fiscal concessions, strategic abstention campaigns, deliberately punitive default offers, or manufactured breaches during the transition? Which of these failure modes can be corrected by adjusting the protocol, and which corrections would undermine the mechanism's stated purpose of making secession orderly, deliberate, and legitimate?
