In our social studies unit on measuring well-being, we came up with a working test for whether a survey-based measure is really measuring the thing its label claims: (1) the wording should pick out the concept as people actually use the term, not a close neighbor of it; (2) the results should be reasonably stable across similar questions and across languages; (3) the measure should be hard to improve without changing the underlying reality it is supposed to track; and (4) what gets reported to the public should be the same as what was actually measured, with no slippage in the label.

A case keeps bothering me, and I would like a definite verdict rather than a menu of options. The international ranking that makes the news each year as a happiness ranking is built mainly from a single survey question. Respondents are asked to imagine a ladder numbered from 0 to 10, where 10 is the best possible life they can imagine for themselves and 0 is the worst, and to say which step they stand on. The country averages are then put in order, and the report and the press describe the top countries as the happiest. But the question asks people to evaluate how their life is going overall, not to report how often they feel happy or how happy they are. A person could rate their life a 9 because their circumstances are excellent while feeling cheerful almost never, and another could rate their life a 5 while feeling happy most of the time.

Apply the four criteria one by one to this ladder-based ranking. Is it measuring happiness, or is it measuring something more like life satisfaction, and does that distinction actually show up in my criteria, or does one of the criteria need to be revised? Then give me your committed position: should a government treat climbing this particular ranking as a serious policy goal, or would that be targeting the wrong thing?
