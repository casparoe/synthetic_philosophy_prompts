I keep noticing a pattern in arguments and I'm hoping someone can tell me whether its got a name and a literature. It's this: people will defend a value as if it matters for its own sake, but when you watch what actually does the work in their defense, every reason they give is about what the value is good for, and the value quietly stops mattering to them when it stops being good for that thing.

A couple instances. A friend insists he cares about truth for its own sake, even truths that are useless or painful. But when I push, the examples he reaches for are all truths that helped someone make a better decision, or comforted someone, or exposed a fraud; when I ask about a truth that's completely inert — no decision hangs on it, no identity, no emotion, nobody's behavior changes — he sort of shrugs and stops paying the costs he'd just claimed he would pay. Same with a relative about tradition: she'll say "we do it because it's ours, that's just what tradition means" like that's a stopping point, not a means. But when the practice stops giving her community its yearly excuse to gather and stops giving the family a shared script, she drops it without much anguish; the defenses she accepts from others are all about cohesion and continuity. I've seen the same shape around purity talk: the things people treat as defiling track contamination, health, sex and ingroup boundaries, but arbitrary rules they happen to have grown up with don't get the same sanctity, so the "purity" seems to be doing a lot of quiet means-to-an-end work while being presented as if it mattered by itself.

What I'm trying to pin down isn't the familiar distinction between valuing something for its own sake and valuing it as a means to something else, and it's not just "people lie about their motives." It's the specific case where the value is genuinely felt as self-sufficient, and still the conditions under which it survives and the reasons that actually persuade all look means-ends. Is there an established concept for that? And if there is, how does it sit relative to that familiar distinction, and where are its edges — does it require the person to be unaware of the means-ends structure, can a whole community do it at once, that kind of thing. I won't be satisfied with just a label; I want to know what the thing is, what it's near to but not the same as, and how you'd tell a real case from a fake one.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — say whether there's an established concept and what you'd call it — then give the reasoning. No preamble, don't restate the question.
