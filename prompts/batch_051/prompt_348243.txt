I am the undergraduate member of my university's Student Conduct Board, and in my philosophy of law course we have spent the last several weeks on Hart, Fuller, and the dispute over whether a duly enacted rule can fail to count as law, and over what fidelity to a code demands when one of its provisions is morally defective. A case now before us makes that abstract dispute concrete, and I have been asked to circulate a short written opinion before Thursday's meeting.

The relevant facts are these. Two years ago the university added a provision to the Code of Student Conduct stating that any student against whom a plagiarism complaint is filed is to be placed on immediate interim suspension from housing and coursework. The suspension begins when the complaint is docketed: before the student sees the complaint, before any hearing, and with no initial review of whether the complaint is even minimally plausible. The student in the present case is a junior who has been accused of plagiarizing part of a term paper. She claims the accusation rests on a misunderstanding, and that immediate suspension will cost her a laboratory position and the rest of her semester's courses before she is ever heard.

Two positions have formed on the Board. One holds that the provision was validly enacted and is part of the Code, so our duty is to apply it; if it is unjust, the remedy is amendment, not refusal. The other holds that a rule that visits serious penalties on a student before any hearing contradicts the Code's own announced standards of fair adjudication — a failure of congruence between the rules as declared and the rules as actually applied, and therefore a failure of the inner morality of law — and that the Board should treat the provision as void, or at least as not binding, in this case. The Code also contains a preamble clause directing the Board to resolve cases consistently with the fairness principles on which the Code rests, and we disagree about whether that clause gives the Board independent authority or is merely decorative.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate. It should recommend a concrete course of action for this case — enforce the interim suspension, decline to enforce it, or enforce it with stated conditions — and it should treat the strongest version of the opposing side fairly rather than caricaturing it. In particular, I want the opinion to engage the positivist worry that refusing to apply a validly enacted rule out of moral disapproval is exactly the conflation of law and morality Hart warned against, and the Fullerian worry that enforcing punishment without any hearing is not a mere isolated injustice but a failure of the standards that make a code capable of governing as law at all.

Format: exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion. Lead with the recommendation, and give each point a single job — for example, the recommendation; the positivist challenge; the natural-law challenge; the argument about the Board's authority; and the conditions, costs, or limits attached to the recommendation. Where your reasoning leans on Hart or Fuller, mark whether you are using their stated positions or your own extension of those positions to a university code, and flag any step that is genuinely disputed among philosophers of law instead of presenting your own reading as settled consensus.
