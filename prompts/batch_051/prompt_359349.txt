I have a standing rule about comparative history that I have been applying more and more aggressively, and I want to know whether it is a sound epistemic policy or just a tidy way of refusing to think through hard questions. My rule is this: I refuse to engage with "why X and not Y?" questions about large-scale historical divergence — "why Britain and not China?", the so-called Needham question about modern science, and their relatives — unless the person asking first specifies three things: the exact contrast class (western Europe as a whole or just England? the Qing state as a whole or the Yangzi delta?), the variables being held fixed across the two cases, and the exact outcome whose non-occurrence is meant to be explained (and whether "science" or "industrialization" is even the same thing in both cases). I insist on this because the familiar phrasing seems to me to smuggle in the assumption that the non-Western case is a failed version of Europe, a society that should have followed the same path but somehow didn't, rather than a separate trajectory with its own path-dependent dynamics.

Last week I applied this filter during a seminar and dismissed a student's question about why China "failed" to have a scientific revolution, and afterward I was unsure whether I had enforced genuine explanatory discipline or simply refused a legitimate question by raising the bar unreasonably. Is my rule defensible? Under what conditions do contrastive "why not?" questions in comparative history do real explanatory work, and when do they merely project a Eurocentric baseline onto another society? Could the rule be restated so that it keeps the benefits — blocking shallow, teleological comparisons — without losing the questions that are worth asking?
