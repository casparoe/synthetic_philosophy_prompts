Design a 90-minute activity for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the limits of explanation. The goal is for participants to feel the force of computational irreducibility: for some systems, knowing the rule and the starting state is not enough to jump ahead -- the only way to learn the state at step N is to grind through all N steps, because no shorter computation is available (or, more carefully, no shortcut is known and the system is believed to be irreducible). The key constraint: participants should run into this themselves through the rules, incentives, or roles, and the facilitator should not say anything like "shortcut" or "irreducibility" until the debrief.

My rough idea, which you should push on or improve: a two-phase prediction contest around a human-run one-dimensional cellular automaton. Most of the class acts as cells and computes row after row on a big grid; a smaller team of predictors, who cannot see or hear the grid, gets points or candy for forecasting a specified distant cell or row. Phase 1 uses a rule where a cheap shortcut exists (say Rule 90 starting from a single live cell, so the rows are Sierpinski's triangle mod 2), so predictors who find the trick can outrun the simulators. Phase 2 switches to a rule like Rule 30, where at least the center column has no known shortcut. Dont just take my setup as given -- improve it if you can, but keep the core contrast between a rule that can be predicted without running it and one that apparently can't.
