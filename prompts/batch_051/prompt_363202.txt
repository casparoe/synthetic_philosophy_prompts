I am drafting a paper on the dilemma for physicalism usually called Hempel's dilemma. I can see that the argument ought to be resistible somewhere, but I cannot decide where the resistance should be located, and I want to be told exactly what is wrong with the following reconstruction of it. Please first say whether the numbered argument is valid. If it is invalid, identify the precise step that fails and the reading of the term or claim on which the failure depends; if it is valid, say which premises are true and which a determined opponent should reject, including step (1) if you think it misstates the target thesis. I particularly want to know whether step (2) states a genuine exclusive alternative, or whether there is a third way of fixing the extension of 'physical' that the dilemma ignores — for example an account that characterises the physical by structural or compositional features rather than by a list supplied by physics, or some version of the via negativa on which the physical is simply the non-mental. If you judge step (4) to be the weakest step, give the strongest reading on which it is true and check whether the argument still goes through on that reading. Conclude by stating how confident you are in your verdict and singling out the point you are least sure of. Please write the response in British English.

(1) Physicalism is the thesis that everything that exists is either itself physical or is exhaustively determined by physical things and physical properties.

(2) To give 'physical' a determinate extension one must defer either (a) to current physics, taking the physical to be whatever entities and properties current physics posits, or (b) to a future, completed or ideal physics, taking the physical to be whatever such a physics would posit.

(3) If (a), physicalism is almost certainly false. Current physics is incomplete — it has no agreed theory of quantum gravity, for one — and the history of physics is a history of abandoned or reconceived posits such as phlogiston, caloric, and the luminiferous ether, so there is no good reason to expect today's ontology to survive.

(4) If (b), physicalism is either contentless or trivially true. We cannot now say what a completed physics will posit; if a completed physics posited irreducible phenomenal or proto-phenomenal properties, physicalism would come out compatible with panpsychism or strong emergentism, and if it did not, the claim that everything is physical would reduce to the claim that everything is whatever the completed science of the world says there is.

(5) Therefore physicalism is either almost certainly false or contentless, and cannot serve as a substantive and likely true account of the place of consciousness in nature.
