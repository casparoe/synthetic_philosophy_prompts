I have spent a long time reading the animal-sentience literature alongside the effective-altruist and rationalist discussions of farmed-fish welfare, and I keep returning to an argument that used to function as a conversation-stopper. The argument ran roughly as follows: fish and the decapod crustaceans farmed in aquaculture do not have a neocortex, or any structure agreed to play an equivalent role; conscious pain requires that kind of cortical machinery; therefore the writhing, escape responses and avoidance learning these animals display are best explained as unconscious reflexes, and extending welfare protections to them rests on a category error. On that view, the fact that a farmed salmon or a prawn reacts to injury tells us no more about felt suffering than a thermostat's reaction tells us about felt temperature.

The development that seems to have unsettled the argument is empirical. Over the past two decades researchers have identified nociceptors in teleost fish, shown that noxious stimulation changes attention, appetite, social behaviour and antipredator behaviour in ways that can outlast the acute stimulus, and found that some of these changes are attenuated by morphine. A smaller but real body of work now points in the same direction for some decapod crustaceans: shore crabs and hermit crabs show avoidance and motivational trade-offs that appear sensitive to electric shock rather than fully reflexive. Yet the original anatomical premise has not been withdrawn, because fish and crustaceans still lack the cortical structures the old argument invoked.

I would like an adjudication of whether this classic argument survives the new evidence, needs repair, or is dead. When a defender of the old view now says that the animal still lacks a neocortex, which premise is doing the actual work, and is that move still available without becoming an unfalsifiable or question-begging stipulation? Is there a repaired version that concedes the new data while still denying that farmed fish and decapod crustaceans fall within the scope of welfare concern, and how much weaker is it? I want an answer that distinguishes what has been shown empirically about nociception and pain-like behaviour from what remains unresolved about consciousness, and that says whether the disagreement is ultimately empirical, conceptual, or terminological. Please write the response in British English.
