A colleague has been pressing me to accept an argument he says shows, on purely logical grounds, that no machine could reproduce a human mathematician's arithmetical ability. The conclusion seems too strong, so I want to locate exactly where the reasoning fails, if it does. I will set the argument out as he gave it, as carefully as I can.

(1) Suppose, for reductio, that there is a Turing machine M that enumerates exactly the arithmetical sentences I would accept on reflection, and let T be the formal system whose theorems are precisely those sentences.
(2) T is consistent. If T proved both a sentence and its negation, then both would be enumerated by M, and by (1) I would accept them both; but I do not accept contradictions.
(3) T is sufficiently strong and effectively presented. Because M enumerates its theorems, T is recursively enumerable; and since my elementary arithmetical competence is at least that strong, T includes enough arithmetic to encode its own syntax and provability relation.
(4) By Gödel's first incompleteness theorem, every consistent formal system satisfying the conditions in (3) has a Gödel sentence: a sentence G that is true under the standard interpretation of arithmetic but not provable in the system.
(5) I can see that the Gödel sentence for T, call it G, is true. By construction, G says of itself that it is not provable in T, and (4) tells us that T does not prove G; hence what G says is the case. Therefore G is among the arithmetical sentences I would accept on reflection.
(6) But G is not a theorem of T, so M never enumerates it, contradicting (1).
(7) Since M was arbitrary, no Turing machine can enumerate exactly my arithmetical judgments, and no mechanical account of mathematical reasoning can be correct.

I want a diagnosis, not a survey of the literature. Which premise or step should be rejected, and why did that step look safe on a first pass? What does rejecting it commit one to? If you think the argument is valid, then which premise is the least costly to abandon, and can a mechanist consistently grant anything like the reflective ability described in (5) while still denying the conclusion?
