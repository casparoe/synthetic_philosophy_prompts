I'm a novelist, and for a chapter where a startup founder thinks he can bottle whatever Mary is missing, write me a parody of an investor memo, around 500–700 words, from a company called QualiaCube. The memo pitches a device that lets people who've never had a particular quale buy the experience on demand. The company's founding myth is the Mary's-room thought experiment, but they get it all entrepreneurially wrong. Here's the passage I want the memo to lean on, paraphrased from Frank Jackson's 1982 paper Epiphenomenal Qualia: Mary has been trapped in a black-and-white room her whole life, learns all the physical information about color vision through black-and-white media, and the question is whether she learns something new when she finally sees a red tomato. Use that as the backstory for the pitch, but don't lecture about it — keep the memo deadpan and internally consistent, so a reader who knows the knowledge argument recognizes the jokes at its expense and a reader who doesn't can still follow. The satire should land these real features without naming them: the anti-physicalist conclusion that there is more than the physical facts, the ability/acquaintance reply that it's just know-how or acquaintance, and the weird tension in the idea that if the missing something is nonphysical it shouldn't be sellable, but they're selling it anyway. Last line should be a one-sentence summary of the memo's actual philosophical bet.
