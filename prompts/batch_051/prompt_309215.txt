Consider the following tension in the epistemology of financial markets. A canonical result — the no-trade theorem — says that if risk-averse traders share common priors and their rationality is common knowledge, then the very fact that a trade is mutually acceptable reveals information, with the result that purely informational differences should not generate trade: each party, seeing the other's willingness to trade, should update and withdraw. Yet real financial markets produce enormous trading volume, and the same tradition also holds that prices aggregate dispersed information with remarkable efficiency. The two ideas are in obvious tension: if prices are so informative, why does anyone find it worthwhile to trade on information?

Below are six candidate ways of easing this tension. Sort them into three tiers — first-line explanations, useful but secondary, and mostly beside the point — according to how well each explains sustained, large-scale trading volume while preserving the core idea that prices largely aggregate information. Then give a one-sentence justification for the placement of each item. You may leave a tier empty only if you judge that no item belongs there.

1. Heterogeneous priors: traders simply disagree about how to interpret public and private information, so they are willing to bet against one another.
2. Noise and liquidity trading: hedging, rebalancing, and liquidity shocks create a steady background of trades behind which informed trades can hide.
3. Overconfidence and bounded rationality: traders overweight the precision of their private signals and underweight the information conveyed by the counterparty's willingness to trade.
4. Costly information with imperfectly informative prices: prices are informative enough to reward careful analysis but never fully revealing, because a fully revealing price would destroy the incentive to gather information.
5. Genuine gains from risk-sharing: much observed volume reflects differences in endowments, horizons, and risk preferences — insurance and hedging rather than speculation — and thus lies outside the no-trade theorem's scope.
6. Violations of common knowledge: actual traders are unsure of the counterparty's motives and reasoning, so the structure of mutual knowledge the theorem presupposes never obtains.
