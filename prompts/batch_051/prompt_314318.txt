I have no formal background in law or philosophy, but I have spent a lot of time thinking on my own about whether ordinary people should be paid for the information companies collect about them, and I keep running into the same move.

The move is an analogy. People I argue with—mostly people who work in technology and some commentators I read—say that information about you belongs to you in roughly the way your car or your house belongs to you. From that they conclude something strong: a company that collects that information without meaningful consent has taken something of yours, and if it later profits from it, you are owed a share. In these conversations, 'data is property' is not a throwaway slogan; it is the load-bearing premise. I want to know how far that analogy actually holds.

Please audit the analogy. Begin with an explicit correspondence table: in the data case, what is supposed to play the role of the owned object, the owner, the act of acquisition, the boundary-crossing, the right to exclude others, the right to sell or license, and the harm done when the thing is taken? Then identify the places where the mapping breaks down.

After that, do the part I cannot do myself: sort the disanalogies into those that matter for the conclusion the analogy is being used to support, and those that are genuine differences but carry no argumentative weight. I already suspect several disanalogies, but I do not know which are load-bearing: data can be used by many people at once without anyone having less of it; most of 'my' data exists only as a byproduct of interactions with other people or with the service itself; and the boundaries of one person's information are hard to draw—my location on a street also records something about the person I met there. I also half-understand a point I have seen made, that property is not one thing but a bundle of rights—to possess, use, exclude, transfer, and draw income—and I want to know whether the analogy quietly depends on property being a single, well-bounded object with one clear owner.

The conclusion the analogy is being made to carry is not only that I have a privacy-style veto over data about me; it is the fuller ownership package, including a right to sell and a right to be paid whenever value is extracted from that data. Once the disanalogies are on the table, tell me which parts of that package survive, which are weakened, and whether the conclusion was being carried by the analogy all along. If some other analogy would do the work more honestly, give it and say which parts of the conclusion it can actually support.

End your answer with a single line in exactly this form: Verdict: <one sentence stating which parts, if any, of the ownership package survive the audit>.
