I'm a PhD student in comparative historical political economy, and I keep hearing "path dependence" used in seminar to mean everything from "history matters" to "the outcome was basically arbitrary." Before I read any more about QWERTY, railway gauges, and the like, I want the spec: what would a theory of path dependence/lock-in actually have to get right to be worth taking seriously? Write the desiderata list — the cases it must distinguish (increasing returns, switching costs, sequencing effects, punctuated change...), the distinctions it can't collapse (sensitivity to initial conditions vs. determinism vs. mere persistence; lock-in vs. path-dependent-but-reversible; an outcome being suboptimal vs. being inexplicable without the path), and the phenomena from adjacent fields it can't just wave away (e.g., the fact that evolution and markets sometimes produce convergence on similar solutions and sometimes don't). For each requirement, say why it's a requirement, and tell me which single one does the most to exclude the sloppy usages. Also flag any pair of desiderata that look jointly unsatisfiable — if you can't both identify the relevant early choices and remain usable before we see how things turned out, I want to know. And if the whole notion is too baggy to deserve a spec and I should just be talking about specific mechanisms instead, say so rather than humouring me.
