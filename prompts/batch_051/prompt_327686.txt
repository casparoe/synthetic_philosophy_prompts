I've been going back and forth with a friend about Quine's picture of moral values and whether it actually survives the fact that today's systems end up with something like moral language without ever having been in the kind of contact with the world Quine thought did the work. As I understand Quine, his whole move was to deny any clean split between science and ethics: moral judgments are just more nodes in the same web of belief we use to predict and get around in the world, only farther from direct sensory checking than, say, "there's a racoon in the garage." They connect to observation through a long chain of increasingly soft sentences, and that chain is supposed to be what gives them their empirical point; there is no first philosophy underneath them and no special moral faculty either.

The disagreement is whether this picture can handle LLMs. A model trained on an enormous pile of human text will happily produce recognizably moral judgments, and it does so without observation sentences, without a sensory surface, without bumping into a world, and without anything we would normally call inculcation. Its "moral" output looks like a statistical artifact of a training distribution, not a node in a web held together by evidence. That seems like it should cut pretty hard against Quine. But I can also imagine the Quinean replying that the corpus just is the accumulated sensory and social history of humans doing the inculcating, and gradient descent is a very diffuse way of absorbing it. I genuinely cant tell whether the original argument goes through, needs to be repaired into something weaker, or is just dead. Walk me through it.
