My cousin just started at a food-security NGO, and at a family dinner she wouldn't stop talking about a proposal floating around her office for changing how famines get officially declared. Right now, the way she explains it, declarations mostly follow crop forecasts, rainfall data, and satellite vegetation maps: if the harvest looks bad enough in aggregate, the aid machine gets switched on. The reform proposal wants to stop looking at how much food a region produces and instead use what its backers call an entitlement trigger: a famine would be declared when the price of the main staple rises past some fixed multiple of rural daily wages, or when casual employment and other ways of earning exchange income collapse for the poorest decile, even if the harvest itself was fine. The idea is that people don't starve because there is no food around; they starve because they lose the ability to command food, and that can happen in a year when the granaries are full. I grew up somewhere my grandparents still talk about the bad years, so this isn't abstract for me.

I'm trying to work out what the rule would actually do, treating it as a mechanism rather than as a moral upgrade. What would it incentivize among national governments — both ones trying to hide a famine and ones that would happily be declared famine-struck in a decent year — among local traders and larger farmers, among the aid agencies that administer the thresholds, and among rural households once everyone understands how the trigger works? How would each group game it: governments fiddling wage or price data, traders hoarding to nudge the price-to-wage ratio over the line, agencies keeping thresholds soft so their caseloads look manageable, households changing their work patterns to stay legible as "worst decile"? And what are the second-order effects — for example, a big aid shipment has it's own price effects and might crash the very prices and wages that the next year's declaration depends on; and does the threshold create an incentive to keep people just poor enough to trip it? I guess the deeper question is whether changing the definition of a famine changes who actually goes hungry, or just who shows up in the statistics. Could the failure modes be patched without quietly drifting back to the crop-forecast system?
