An antitrust authority has fined two firms on the ground that their pricing algorithms colluded. The firms do not deny that the systems' prices remained elevated and closely aligned; they deny that this was collusion. Their account is that the two systems were trained independently on the same observable market, with no shared objective and no channel between them, and that each system's policy is simply a learned best response to the other's published prices. On that view the observed outcome is a mutually reinforcing equilibrium of two unilateral learners, not an agreement. The authority's counter-evidence is that, over a long run of interactions, one system repeatedly made small moves that were weakly profitable on their own but that appeared tailored to the other system's next update, anticipating its responses in a way too systematic to look like two agents merely treating each other as an external environment.

The conceptual claim on which the case turns is therefore not "were the prices high?" but "is there something agreement-like between the two systems — a joint policy, the analogue of a meeting of the minds — over and above two individual policies that happen to lock together?"
