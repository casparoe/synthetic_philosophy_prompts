I help edit a small philosophy journal—really a website with delusions of grandeur—and I keep seeing the same thing: an argument's conclusion only follows if you grant some inference step the author treats as too obious to mention. Sometimes it's modus ponens, sometimes contraposition, sometimes double-negation elimination, sometimes an informal principle they treat as obviously valid. I'm considering a submission rule to drag all this into the open.

The rule: every paper must include a derivation ledger. Each numbered inferential step must name the rule it uses and cite a separate section of the ledger where that rule itself is derived from other rules, whose own derivations also appear. The ledger must be finite, must bottom out in rules explicitly designated as self-evident or universally accepted, and no rule may appear in its own ancestry. Referees would check ledgers as part of review, not just skim them.

Before I announce this, I want someone to treat it as a mechanism and walk me through how it would actually run, not whether it's noble. What kinds of papers get through, and what do authors start doing within the first month? How does the self-evident category get gamed, and by whom—authors, referees, me? Does the no-circularity ban stay enforceable once chains get forty or fifty steps long, or does everyone just learn to hide the circle inside a definition? And here's my real worry: this seems like it might just be Carroll's tortoise turned into a submission policy. In that story the tortoise takes each premise, and even takes the rule of inference as a further premise, but still withholds the conclusion until yet another rule is added, and then another. If the ledger demands that rules be derived and that the chain terminate, does terminate actually solve the tortoise, or does it just move the point where the whole thing stalls? What would a journal that genuinely enforces this look like after a year? And if I patch the first obvious holes, what breaks next?
