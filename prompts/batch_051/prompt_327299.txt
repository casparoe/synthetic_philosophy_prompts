Quick setup so we're on the same page: a driver is on a highway and will pass two identical exits. If he exits at the first, he gets 0. If he continues and exits at the second, he gets 4. If he drives past both, he gets 1. Key feature: whenever he is at an exit, he can't tell whether it's the first or the second (he's absent-minded). The expected value of continuing at each exit with probability p is p[p(1) + (1-p)(4)] = 4p - 3p^2, which is maximized at p = 2/3, giving 4/3, which beats both pure options. My officemate instead says the rational thing is just to always continue, and he gives this argument:

(1) At either exit the driver's information is exactly the same: he can't distinguish first from second.
(2) So any plan he can actually execute must prescribe the same action at both exits.
(3) That leaves only two executable plans: exit at both exits, or continue at both.
(4) Exiting at both pays 0; continuing at both pays 1.
(5) A rational agent picks the executable plan with the higher payoff.
(6) So the rational driver continues at every exit.

He also says the 2/3 answer is cheating because a plan that says continue with probability 2/3 at each exit doesn't tell the driver what to do when he's actually sitting at an exit—he has to make a definite choice there and then, not roll dice. I half-suspect step (3) is the problem because it just ignores mixing, but I cant quite articulate why mixing is a real plan rather than a dodge. Is the argument valid? If not, which step fails? I want a committed verdict, not a "both sides have a point" answer: tell me whether the 2/3 answer survives, and exactly where his argument goes wrong (or why I'm wrong to doubt it).
