Notice the curve. In ordinary practice, the more someone can know and can do, the stricter the standard we hold them to: a pediatrician who waves off a fever gets destroyed, while a neighbor who shrugs at the same fever gets a pass; "I didn't know better" becomes a worse excuse the more you know. But in a lot of theodicy the very same knob gets turned the other way. God's vastly greater knowledge and power are given as reasons to hold God to a lower standard, or none: we can't see the whole picture, there may be goods we can't detect, who are we to second-guess. We would never accept who-are-we-to-second-guess from the pediatrician, even though the pediatrician also knows more than the parents do. So is there a principled reason the blame curve should rise with knowledge and power for ordinary agents and then reverse for God — or is it just a graph we drew backwards around the one defendant we want to acquit? Give me the strongest non-circular argument for a real discontinuity, something better than saying God is different. Then run it on a being who is finite but vastly smarter and more powerful than us, a superintelligent alien, say. If the principle clears the alien too, tell me whether that's a price worth paying; if it doesn't clear the alien, tell me what exactly God has that the alien lacks and how that difference does the moral work.
