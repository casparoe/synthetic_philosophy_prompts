I keep going back and forth on this, and I want someone to actually work through the options instead of just telling me to bite a bullet. I'm attracted to expected utility theory because it's the only decision rule I know that follows from a clean set of axioms, gives a definite verdict in every finite case, and matchs up with intuitive arbitrage and dominance reasoning. But I recently got shown a pair of games and I can't shake the intuitive verdict, even though the theory goes silent.

Here's the setup. The Pasadena game: you toss a fair coin until it lands heads for the first time. If the first heads is on toss n, you get (-1)^(n-1) times 2^n / n dollars, so the payouts alternate — win $2, lose $2, win $8/3, lose $4, win $32/5, and so on — with each later payout larger in size but also much less likely. Its expected value is the series 1 - 1/2 + 1/3 - 1/4 + ..., which converges to ln 2, but only conditionally: reorder the terms and you can get any finite number, or make it diverge. So by the standard definition, the game has no well-defined expected value.

The Altadena game is exactly the same coin-toss process, but every payout is one dollar higher — whatever the Pasadena game would give, plus $1. Its expectation series is the Pasadena series with the extra terms 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + ... folded in, so it is still only conditionally convergent. Standard expected value says: undefined. Both games, no verdict.

But the intuition seems as firm as anything in decision theory: the Altadena game is better than the Pasadena game, and the price difference is exactly one dollar. It dominates state by state; every possible sequence of coin flips leaves you a dollar richer. If a theory of rational choice can't say "take the dominating one, and it's worth $1 more," that feels like a failure of the theory, not a misleading intuition.

So here's what I want. On one side: expected utility theory with its axioms and representation theorems. On the other: a statewise-dominating improvement with no well-defined expectation. Walk me through which one should give. What would it cost to keep the theory and declare the intuition a mistake; what would it cost to amend the theory so it can pronounce on these games, and where would the amended theory have to stop to avoid nonsense; and is there a reading on which the theory and the intuition both survive? Don't just pick a side — tell me what the negotiated verdict looks like and what each side has to sacrifice.
