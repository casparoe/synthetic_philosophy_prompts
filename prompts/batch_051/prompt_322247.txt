A colleague in my research group has been defending the singleton hypothesis—roughly, the claim that a durable future for advanced intelligence will be organized under a single decision-making agency at the highest level, such as a world government or a superintelligent system with decisive control. He takes himself to be defending only the modest thesis that such an outcome is the most likely long-run result. But the argument he keeps giving seems to prove far more than that: it seems to show that any durable non-singleton order, including a stable balance of power, a lasting federation of equals, or an equilibrium maintained by mutual deterrence, is impossible in principle for any kind of advanced agents at any level of technology. That is much stronger than the singleton hypothesis needs to be, and I find it hard to accept. Yet when I press on the individual steps, each one looks plausible. I have reconstructed the argument below and would like a diagnosis of where it goes wrong.

(1) Consider any durable future containing a population of advanced agents—artificial intelligences, states armed with such systems, or coalitions of either. In any such arrangement, either some agent has a decisive advantage in its capacity to shape the world, or no agent does and the agents stand in rough parity.

(2) Suppose some agent has a decisive advantage—an advantage sufficient to overwhelm any rival or coalition of rivals. Then the only thing standing between that agent and the establishment of a singleton is its own decision to refrain. But an arrangement whose persistence depends on the incumbent's continuing forbearance is not durable: a later incumbent, a shift in policy, or a change in the agent's internal constitution could reverse the forbearance at any time. A decisive advantage, then, either already amounts to a singleton or is a temporary arrangement on the way to one.

(3) Suppose instead that no agent has a decisive advantage, so the agents remain in rough parity. Parity is not self-maintaining. Rates of technological, economic, and military growth differ across agents, and small differences compound, eventually producing a decisive gap unless some agency acts continuously to equalize capabilities. But continuous equalization would require a standing authority with the power to monitor every agent's capabilities and transfer resources from the stronger to the weaker. An authority with that power and scope is itself a singleton in all but name. So parity either collapses into a decisive advantage or is preserved by something that already is a singleton.

(4) Therefore, any durable arrangement of advanced agents either is a singleton or is on an inevitable trajectory toward one; every durable non-extinction future is a singleton future.

(5) But this conclusion is too strong. It does not merely make singletons likely, or even overwhelmingly likely; it makes lasting non-singleton orders impossible in principle, ruling out arrangements that have persisted for long stretches historically and that remain coherent descriptions of possible futures for advanced artificial systems.

I cannot tell whether the error is a false premise or a slide in the reasoning from one step to the next. Which step should be rejected, and what exactly does rejecting it cost? In particular, what remains of the singleton hypothesis once the argument is repaired, and what is the strongest defensible version of the claim?
