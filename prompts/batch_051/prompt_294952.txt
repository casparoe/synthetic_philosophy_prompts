Last night I sat with two friends, Emre and Priya, while a small disagreement between them turned into a long argument I mostly just listened to. It began with a mundane grievance — Emre had sent Priya an anxious message and she had gone silent for several days, and he had been hurt at having to guess what her silence meant — but it quickly became a general dispute about whether we owe it to other people to make ourselves legible: to put our reasons, feelings, and expectations into words so that the people close to us are not forced to read our silences. I have my own leanings, but I will leave them out in reporting what was said.

(1) Emre said that, in any relationship deep enough that someone must act on the other person's state of mind, you owe them the work of being readable. If they have to infer what you need from silences and curt replies, and can be blamed for guessing wrong, you have handed them a map-reading job without a map; you have offloaded your own self-interpretation onto them. There is a defeasible duty of legibility toward people whose lives are entangled with yours. (2) Priya replied that much inner life is not legible even to the person living it; people often do not know what they feel or why until later. A standing duty to be legible would therefore require either fabricating a coherent account or being counted as having failed, which rewards the articulate and the self-assured and punishes the confused. (3) Emre answered that she was attacking a stronger principle than his. His duty is not a duty to be clear but a duty to try, and it gives way when clarity is impossible. There is a difference between "I cannot put this into words yet, and I am not asking you to act on it" and "I will not help you read me, but I still expect you to respond correctly"; the first honors the duty, the second uses opacity as a form of power. (4) Priya said that even a duty to try is too strong as a standing demand. Why should another person's wish for a map of me give them a claim on my self-narration? People hold a legitimate interest in the privacy of their own minds even from those they love, and not every relationship creates a right to the other person's self-interpretation. At most there is a narrower duty: do not punish someone for failing to read you when you refused to give them the means. (5) Emre replied that this narrower principle gets her most of the way to his view. If you will not be read, then you cannot later treat the other person as though they failed to reach you. Legibility is not a duty to confess; it is a duty to either make yourself readable or stop holding others answerable for a reading they could not have performed. (6) Priya answered that even this cleaner version treats opacity as something requiring a justification, when sometimes opacity is simply how a boundary announces itself. A person who will not explain may be saying — to themselves as much as to the other — "not here, not to you, not yet," and giving a legible account of that refusal may be exactly what would dissolve the boundary it protects.

Please adjudicate the argument move by move. Rate each of the six moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification for each rating, and then say which party is right about which specific claims, and what both of them are missing.
