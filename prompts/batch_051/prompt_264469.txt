I am a software engineer, and I sit on the product safety review board at a company that builds developer tools. We have to decide this week whether a new agentic feature may launch on schedule. The feature lets the assistant plan, execute, and debug multi-step changes inside a sandbox with limited shell access; the engineering team considers it ready to ship, but our safety team wants roughly six to eight more weeks of red-teaming and a runtime monitor before general release. A well-funded competitor is widely expected to ship a comparable feature within the month, and delaying our launch would probably cost us a meaningful slice of the developer market. One faction on the board argues that delay would be self-defeating: the competitor would release a less carefully tested version, customers would use it instead, and we would have made the safer option uncompetitive while doing nothing to reduce the overall risk the board exists to manage. The other faction argues that approving the launch would amount to letting a competitor's release date set our safety standard, and that the extra cost of mitigation is precisely the kind of investment the board exists to defend. My own training is in engineering rather than philosophy, but colleagues have described this standoff in terms of the competitiveness constraint and the alignment tax, and I suspect the real issue is structural rather than merely a scheduling dispute. Please draft the decision memo I will circulate before the board meets. It should state a clear recommendation near the start among these options: approve the launch as planned, delay the launch until the requested mitigations are complete, or approve with specified conditions and a timeline. It should then give the reasoning in a way the engineers and the safety team can both recognise as their actual positions. In particular, present the strongest version of the case for the course you do not recommend, and show why that case does not win on balance. I would rather have a few sharp considerations than a broad list of arguments. Please aim for around 900 words, write the memo entirely in British English, and format it as continuous prose, like a formal letter or a long email, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
