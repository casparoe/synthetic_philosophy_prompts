Hey, so I'm giving a talk at a small decision-theory workshop next month and I want a Q&A prep sheet rather than a defense. The talk defends Hofstadter's superrational take on the Platonia dilemma, and then uses the actual Luring Lottery that Scientific American ran in the 1980s as a case study. His setup: 20 people, no communication, and if exactly one of them sends a telegram by noon the next day, that person gets a billion dollars; if zero or more than one send, nobody gets anything. Causal decision theory says to send with probability 1: for any chance the other 19 might all stay quiet, sending has positive expected value and not sending has zero, and your choice doesn't cause theirs. But if everyone reasons like that, everybody sends and we all get nothing. The superrational move is to treat yourself as one of a class of identical reasoners who will land on the same answer, and then maximize over the common mixed strategy everybody uses, which gives p = 1/20. I want to argue that's a genuinely distinct norm. EDT half-sees this—if you run the symmetry premise far enough, EDT basically turns into superrationality—but only by quietly importing the common-policy constraint that causal decision theory conspicuously lacks, so superrationality isn't reducible to either of them. I'm defending the premise.

Then I want to argue the actual Luring Lottery isn't the refutation people think. In the real contest you could send in a postcard with a number of entries; the prize was a million dollars divided by the total number of entries, randomly allocated, so the individually best response to everyone else's N entries is to submit N yourself, which spirals into everyone submitting enormous numbers. The magazine's superrational advice was to roll a die with as many sides as the number of expected responders and send '1' only if you rolled a '1'. In fact people sent in googolplex-sized numbers, the prize became a tiny fraction of a cent, and the contest basically broke. I want to say that's what happens when you drop the key assumptions—symmetric reasoners, a common model, common priors—and run it on a huge heterogeneous public. Its evidence about real-world coordination failure, not about the internal coherence of superrationality.

The audience is maybe half philosophers in formal epistemology or decision theory, a third economists/game theorists, and a handful of grad students, two of whom definitely read the rationalist blogs. So the LW-shaped objections (something about UDT, acausal trade, timeless decision theory) will be in the room but they're not the ones I'm scared of. I need the objections this specific crowd will actually raise, ordered by likelihood-times-damage, phrased the way they'd phrase them (unilateral deviations, common priors, trembling hands, equilibrium selection; probably not 'acausal trade'). For each objection: a two-minute reply and a note on where that reply is soft. Be honest about what I should concede up front. Include the question I'm praying nobody asks, and why it's the one that actually sinks me. And commit—rank them, tell me what to hold and what to give away, don't just give me a menu of possible objections.
