I am a novelist, and in the book I am drafting a character promises her dying father that she will keep the family house in the family, then sells it some years later when keeping it becomes ruinous. In a discussion about the scene, a fellow writer kept telling me that ‘every promise is a debt’ and that the character is simply a promise-breaker; when I objected that the promise was made under pressure and no longer corresponds to anything she wills, he cited a passage from David Hume’s A Treatise of Human Nature (Book III, Part II, Section V, ‘Of the obligation of promises’):

“The act of the mind, exprest by a promise, is not a resolution to perform any thing: For that alone never imposes any obligation. Nor is it a desire of such a performance: For we may bind ourselves without such a desire, or even with an aversion, declar’d and avow’d. Neither is it the willing of that action, which we promise to perform: For a promise always regards some future time, and the will has an influence only on present actions.”

I can see that the passage is meant to close off my appeal to her changed will, but I cannot tell exactly what claim ‘every promise is a debt’ is being used to assert, or how much of it the passage actually establishes. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims that proverb might be making—about what creates the obligation, when it takes hold, to whom it is owed, and how strong it is—and say for each whether it is trivial, defensible, or false, and which of them the Hume passage genuinely bears on? Please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and do not present a contested view as the consensus. I would be grateful if the answer were written in British English.
