I'm giving a colloquium next month defending the claim that mathematics is, at least in part, quasi-empirical in Lakatos's sense: mathematical concepts grow through a back-and-forth of conjectures, proof-attempts, counterexamples, and renegotiated definitions, rather than being fixed by axioms and secured once and for all by deduction. My main ammunition is the V - E + F = 2 story from Proofs and Refutations: the theorem gets proved, then the hollow cube, the picture frame, nested cubes, and the star-polyhedron show up as counterexamples, and instead of the theorem just being falsified the concepts 'polyhedron' and 'face' get stretched until the theorem survives. I want to argue this isn't merely history but a live fact about how mathematics actually works, and I'm going to use the Lakatosian vocabulary -- monster-barring, exception-barring, lemma-incorporation -- even though i know it will annoy parts of the room.

The audience is a departmental colloquium: mostly philosophers of mathematics who lean platonist, two formalists who basically hold Hilbert's line that mathematics is a game with uninterpreted signs and hate talk of counterexamples "forcing" conceptual change, and half a dozen working mathematicians from the topology group who think philosophy of math is a spectator sport but will get prickly if i sound like i'm saying proofs arent proofs.
