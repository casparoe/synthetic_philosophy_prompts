I have been trying to determine what counts as genuine agreement when someone accepts online terms of service, rather than merely legally effective assent. By genuine agreement I mean an act through which a person knowingly and voluntarily changes her own normative situation, in a way that is attributable to her as a deliberate performance rather than as something that merely happens to her. My working criteria are:

(1) Knowledge: the person either understands the salient new obligations, or had reasonable notice of their existence and a fair opportunity to learn what they were.

(2) Intent: the act is performed in order to undertake the obligations, not merely to dismiss an obstruction, continue a routine, or reach a further screen.

(3) Voluntariness: the person has a real option of refusing without suffering losses disproportionate to what is at stake, and there is no unfair surprise or dependence that forces her hand.

(4) Communicative character: the act must conventionally count as an acceptance, so that a reasonable counterpart is entitled to treat it as acceptance, and the actor, when her own behavior is described back to her, can recognize it as acceptance rather than as something that merely occurred.

Now the case. A paid note-taking application updates overnight. A user who has been a subscriber for two years and keeps her work documents in the app opens it the next morning to a full-screen dialog headed Terms updated. The only prominent control is a button labeled Continue. Below the button, in smaller type, is a sentence explaining that by continuing the user accepts the revised terms, which are available through a link; the revisions include a new arbitration clause with a class-action waiver. The only alternative to Continue is a small Cancel control that logs the user out and cancels the account immediately, forfeiting the remainder of the prepaid subscription. The user needs her notes for work that morning and has no practical substitute for them. The terms also contain a clause of this kind: the company may change the terms by posting new ones on its website, and continued use after thirty days counts as acceptance of the new terms. The user clicks Continue without opening the terms. Assume, arguendo, that a court would be likely to enforce the terms; the question is whether the click counts as genuine agreement under my four criteria, and whether any gap between enforceability and genuine agreement should trouble a theory of contract.

Work through the criteria one by one, saying for each whether the act satisfies it, fails it, or leaves it indeterminate, and why. Then give your overall verdict on whether this is genuine agreement in my sense, and identify which criterion or criteria your verdict most depends on. Then address two further questions. First, if the button had been labeled I agree to the updated terms, would the analysis change, and under which criterion does that difference, if any, fall? Second, under my criteria, would a future revision imposed through the thirty-day continued-use clause be genuinely agreed to by that same user? Finally, say whether the case exposes a weakness in any of the four criteria and, if so, how you would revise them.

Your final answer should end with a machine-readable line in exactly this form: \answer{...}, where ... is replaced by one of: qualifies as agreement, does not qualify as agreement, or criteria leave it indeterminate. There should be no text after that line.
