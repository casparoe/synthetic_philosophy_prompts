I'm a software engineer with no econ or philosophy background, so go easy on the jargon but don't dumb it down. Lately I keep running into three camps arguing about climate policy: the carbon-tax people, the regulation people, and the degrowth people. I want to understand whether the carbon-tax camp's standard efficiency argument actually survives an objection the other two camps keep gesturing at.

The standard argument, as I understand it, goes like this. Set a tax equal to the social cost of carbon. Then every emitter sees a price on each ton, so anyone who can cut a ton for less than the tax cuts it, and anyone who can't pays. Total cuts happen wherever they're cheapest, and no central planner has to know anyone's abatement costs. The picture underneath is alot of emitters making alot of small, continuous, reversible decisions at the margin.

The development that seems like it might break the picture: in the sectors where the remaining emissions are hardest to cut — steel, cement, chemicals, long-haul shipping, the grid itself — decarbonization doesnt look like a million little tweaks. It looks like a small number of lumpy, bet-the-company decisions: retire this blast furnace now or run it fifteen more years, build a green-hydrogen plant or not, put in a transmission line or not. These have huge fixed costs and long lifetimes, and they only pay off if a bunch of other actors (suppliers, buyers, government, the new pipeline or grid connection) make compatible choices around the same time. The "marginal ton" is kind of an abstraction when the actual choice is between zero and two million tons depending on whether one plant gets built or scrapped. I can't tell whether this is just a practical wrinkle or whether it kills the argument.

So: does the standard efficiency argument for a carbon tax survive this? Which specific premise does the lumpy-infrastructure picture put pressure on, and is there an honest repaired version that still says something important but weaker? And if it mostly doesn't survive, what's the remaining case for a carbon tax — is it a signaling device for long-term expectations, a distributional tool, or what? Also tell me whether this pushes toward regulation, toward degrowth-style demand reduction, or toward some mix.
