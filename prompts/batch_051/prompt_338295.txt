Next month I have to pitch our engineering org on switching product localization from a human translation agency to doing almost everything with our LLM-powered translation tool, with human post-editing only for legal copy and a few high-risk strings (payment messages, destructive actions). My position is that the system doesnt actually understand what it's translating, and that's fine — understanding is the wrong bar. What we need is that a user in Spanish or Japanese completes the right action, and on that metric the machine is good enough and much cheaper.

The room will be: our head of localization, who used to be a working translator and genuinely believes translation is about carrying meaning over, not swapping words; our one computational linguist, who has real eval numbers and will push back on my "nobody can tell the difference" claim; two engineering leads who care mostly about velocity and cost and will probably love the pitch; and a product marketer who cares about brand voice.

I know the standard objection: a model can output a perfectly fluent sentence that's quietly wrong — wrong pronoun, wrong sense of an ambiguous word, dropped negation — because it never had the meaning in view. The scary version is a string that reads like clean UI copy but tells the user to do the opposite of what they need to do.

Prepare me for the questions they'll actually ask, ranked by how likely and how damaging they are, phrased the way each of them would phrase it. Which objections have real force and which are mostly people defending their professional territory? What should I conceed up front, and what should I hold firm on? What is the one reply that has to land for the proposal to survive? And tell me the question I should be praying nobody asks, and where my answer to it would be weakest.
