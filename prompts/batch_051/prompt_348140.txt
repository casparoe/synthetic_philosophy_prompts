I recently read an essay by a science writer that summarized, in passing, the idea that mathematical beauty is evidence for the truth of a physical theory. The passage said something very close to this:

"Many of the greatest physicists have treated elegance as more than a matter of taste: between two theories that fit the available data, the mathematically beautiful one is more likely to be true. The history of physics seems to support them. General relativity and Maxwell's equations were accepted in large part because they were beautiful, and Dirac's prediction of the positron is often presented as a case in which trusting beauty paid off before experiment could catch up. Conversely, when a theory is ugly—full of arbitrary parameters, asymmetries, or ad hoc patches—physicists treat that ugliness as a strong reason to reject the theory. The same principle is supposed to operate in mathematics, where an elegant proof is more likely to be sound than a clumsy one. And it is offered as the explanation for why string theory continues to be taken seriously after decades without direct experimental confirmation: its elegance is treated as a standing reason to keep working on it, even as experiment fails to catch up."

I am inclined to think this smuggles an aesthetic preference into what should be an empirical matter, but the writer is well informed and I do not want to be uncharitable. Could you give me an accuracy audit: is this a fair summary of the position, or does it distort it in ways that matter? Go through it sentence by sentence and separate what a careful defender of elegance as a theoretical virtue would accept, what is a common simplification, and what is overstated or wrong. In particular, does anything true in the summary support the concluding claim that elegance gives us a positive reason to believe a physical theory is true, or that an elegant mathematical proof is more likely to be correct?
