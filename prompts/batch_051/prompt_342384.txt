Hey — I'm a retired physical chemist, and I've spent the last few years getting into philosophy of science, mostly by reading on my own and auditing a seminar at the local university. The thing I keep coming back to is the Kuhn/Popper debate about whether science stays rational during a scientific revolution. I know the summary version: Popper thinks you can in principle falsify a theory and abandon it, while Kuhn thinks that during a revolution the very standards for what counts as a good theory are part of what's contested, so there is no neutral scorecard to apply. But I can't make this vivid for myself or for my reading group, and honestly most discussions we have just end with people nodding at definitions.

Here's what I want. Design a 90-minute activity for my reading group — about 14 of us, mostly retired scientists plus a couple of patient philosophy grad students — that makes participants feel the force of the idea that theory choice in a revolution can't be settled by a neutral set of shared rules, and also the force of the worry that if there really are no shared rules, rational choice starts to look like a polite word for conversion. I don't want any of this announced up front. I want them to run into the problem themselves, from inside the roles or incentives, and only afterwards have it named.

The activity can be a structured deliberation, a role play, a game with a scoring or payoff system, whatever you think works, but I need:

- clear roles or rules and any materials,
- a timeline for the 90 minutes,
- a debrief that maps specific moments in the activity onto the Kuhn/Popper disagreement, with Popper not treated as a strawman,
- an honest note on what the activity can't reproduce, where the faciltator has to supply the missing piece.

I'd also like there to be a forced joint decision built in — say a grant committee that has to rank projects from both research programs — so the groups can't just agree to dissagree. Don't base the activity on a real historical episode like phlogiston and oxygen; invent a fictional but recognizable field with two rival research programs, so people don't burn their energy fact-checking the history instead of feeling the structure. No reading beforehand either; the group is there to do something, not to have read something.

Tell me too what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — for example one group might just game the scoring instead of genuinely trying to argue, or someone will collapse the two sides into a compromise too early — and what I should do in the moment when that happens.
