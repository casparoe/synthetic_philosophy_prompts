Two people in my department got into it about Malthus and I keep turning it over — can you referee? Dana said Malthus was basically right but early: population grows like compound interest while the food supply grows at best linearly, so sooner or later something has to give, and every time we've dodged a crisis it's been through a technology like synthetic fertilizer or the Green Revolution that pushes the limit back. Pushing the limit back, she said, is not the same as showing there's no limit. Owen said that's exactly why the theory is unfalsifiable: the prediction is "eventually, unless something changes," and every time something changes you say "see, just postponed." A prediction with no date and an open-ended escape clause isn't a prediction. Dana came back that he was demanding a date from a theory that never gave one; the claim is structural, that population pressure has to be relieved by some check — famine, disease, war, or deliberate restraint — and a tech fix just raises the level of subsistence. Owen said fine, but look at the demographic transition: in rich countries people got wealthier and had fewer kids, with no famine or war, just changed preferences, so the "grows like compound interest" premise is false in exactly the countries that escaped. Dana said the transition is itself one of the checks in modern form, because kids get expensive and the opportunity cost of raising them rises with income — pressure working through incentives rather than starvation. Owen threw up his hands: now food supply rising counts as postponement and fertility falling counts as a check; what observation would convince her Malthus was wrong? Dana said plenty: centuries of growth with no resource pressure, no falling fertility, no rising land or energy prices, no resource conflict — or output per person growing with no additional resource input at all. Owen said she'd set the bar at "centuries of data" for a theory thats supposed to be testable now, and a theory compatible with famine and abundance, high fertility and low fertility, is astrology, not science. Dana said astrology doesn't specify any indicators, while Malthus does specify a family of them — prices, fertility, conflict — and that's the difference between a structural prediction and a prophecy. Owen said if the theory doesn't say which indicator, where, or when, then after the fact anything counts. I genuinely dont know who's right about what. Can you go through it move by move and say who's right on each specific point, and what, if anything, both of them are missing? You're a philosopher of science who also knows some economic history.
