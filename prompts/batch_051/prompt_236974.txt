Something's been bugging me about AI race talk, and I could use help getting it straight. I read Fearon's rationalist account of war back in grad school and one move stuck with me: if both sides are rational and fighting is costly, there ought to be some bargain both sides prefer to actually fighting, so the interesting question is always which specific thing made the bargain fail. His menu was: private information with incentives to misrepresent, commitment problems, and indivisible goods.

Now look at what labs and governments actually do around frontier AI. They call it a race and a winner-take-all thing. They pass export controls on chips, refuse to release model weights, and say "pause AI" proposals are naive because the other side will just pocket our restraint. But the same people sign non-binding safety pledges and talk like more transparency between labs would fix the deep problem. Those feel like cures for different diseases: transparency treats private information, export controls treat a power shift, and "pausing is naive" treats a commitment problem. Maybe I'm conflating things, but it seems like the practice doesn't know which disease it thinks it has.

So my question: what does the AI-race framing, and the concrete policies that go with it, presuppose about the nature of the conflict between labs or between states? Does the practice consistently presuppose one of Fearon's mechanisms, or does it slide between them depending on whats convenient? Which rival views of the situation does it quietly rule out — like that this is a coordination game with an enforceable treaty, or that one side's preferences are being misread? And if you replaced the presupposed view with one of those rivals, what concretely would have to change first: verification, inspection, costlier signals, open-weight norms, actual enforcement?
