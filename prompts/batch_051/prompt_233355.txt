I teach value theory, and I've been going in circles on the question whether an achievement can be good when nobody benefits from it—not the achiever, not an audience, not anyone downstream. I have strong-ish gut reactions to a pile of cases, and I'd like you to do the reflective-equilibrium work: tell me what principle, if any, captures my verdicts, and which of the verdicts I have to give up.

Here are the cases.

1. The burned proof. A mathematician spends twenty years proving a hard conjecture. He writes up a correct, elegant proof. The night before he submits it, a fire destoys the only hard copy and all the backups, and he has a stroke and never reconstructs it. I think something genuinely good happened, not just something sad.

2. The unwitnessed record. A runner spends a decade training, then runs a marathon alone on a certified course at 3 a.m. in a time that would shatter the official world record. No one observes it, the race doesn't count for the record books, and she never races again. I'd say she achieved something real and it was good for her—but I don't feel the same value leaking out into the world that I feel in Case 1, and i'm not sure I can defend the asymmetry.

3. The con man. A man spends ten years becoming the best confidence man and cold reader alive, and uses the skill only to defraud elderly people of their savings. I don't want to count his excellence as good at all. The world is worse because he's excellent.

4. The hot-dog champion. Someone trains with insane discipline and becomes the greatest competitive eater in history. I don't deny it's hard and that he's excellent at it, but I balk at calling it a valuable achievement. If pushed, I'd say it's trivial in a way that the con man's skill isn't, even though the con man's skill is morally bad—which makes me worry my idea of "trivial" is doing something I can't state.

5. The prodigy. A twelve-year-old understands graduate-level mathematics with almost no effort or study. I'd say her ability is excellent, but it isn't an achievement, and the excellence doesn't reflect credit on her in the same way earned excellence does.

6. The hermit. A woman lives alone on a mountain and cultivates courage, honesty, and generosity to a very high degree. She rarely sees another person, by choice, so nobody benefits. I want to say her virtue is good anyway and her self-cultivation is a real achievement.

7. The posthumous book. A philosopher finishes an important book and dies the day before it's published. Thousands read it and it changes how they think. Clearly good for the readers. Whether it's good for her, or an achievement on her ledger, I can't settle.

My rough hunch is something like: excellence is good when it's achieved through effort in a domain whose ends are worth caring about. But that's vague, and it seems to handle the con man by condemning the ends, not by saying anything about the skill. I'd like the principle to do better.

Please write back in exactly these sections:

(1) "Candidate principle" — state your best principle, or at most two competing candidates, as precisely as you can. (~100 words)

(2) "The walkthrough" — run it through all seven cases, flag which clauses do the work in each, and mark each verdict as saved or overturned. (~400 words)

(3) "What has to go" — say which of my verdicts can't survive and why. Don't be polite; if you'd overturn Case 1 or 6, say so. (~150 words)

(4) "New cases" — apply the principle to the case I keep going back and forth on, and then test it on one or two cases of your own that you think expose its weak spots. The case I wobble on: a research team discovers, with solid methods, that a remote planet exists in another galaxy; the result is known only to them and does nothing for anyone's life. Is that a good achievement? (~250 words)

(5) "Confidence" — how confident are you in the principle and the walkthrough, and which single point are you least sure of? (~100 words)
