Spinoza opens Part V of the Ethics, “Of the Power of the Understanding, or of Human Freedom,” as follows:

> At length I pass to the remaining portion of my Ethics, which is concerned with the way leading to freedom. I shall therefore treat therein of the power of the reason, showing how far the reason can control the emotions, and what is the nature of Mental Freedom or Blessedness ; we shall then be able to see, how much more powerful the wise man is than the ignorant. It is no part of my design to point out the method and means whereby the understanding may be perfected, nor to show the skill whereby the body may be so tended, as to be capable of the due performance of its functions. The latter question lies in the province of Medicine, the former in the province of Logic. Here, therefore, I repeat, I shall treat only of the power of the mind, or of reason ; and I shall mainly show the extent and nature of its dominion over the emotions, for their control and moderation.

Earlier in the Ethics Spinoza denies that the will is a free cause, treating every mental act as a determinate link in the causal order that follows from God’s nature. How, then, should we understand the “way leading to freedom” that this passage announces? Offer an interpretation of Spinoza’s conception of “Mental Freedom or Blessedness” that reconciles it with his necessitarianism. In particular, explain how the mind’s “dominion over the emotions” can be a real power even if it is not a power to have done otherwise, and address whether the intellectual love of God should be understood as the culmination of this freedom rather than a separate religious doctrine.
