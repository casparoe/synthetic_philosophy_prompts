Through a friend who is settling her late mother's estate, I have been drawn into a disagreement over a griefbot service. Shortly after the death, my friend used a small start-up that trains a conversational simulation on a deceased person's text messages and voice recordings, so that the bereaved can, in the company's phrase, 'speak again to someone they have lost'. I have seen the terms of service, and clause 6.4 reads:

'The Simulation is provided solely as an aid to private remembrance and consolation. You must not treat any output of the Simulation as a statement actually made by the deceased person, nor rely on any output of the Simulation when making a decision of legal, medical or financial consequence.'

The dispute is this. My friend's mother had always assured her, in the family's presence, that a particular painting would be hers, but the will is silent and her brother now claims it. During one exchange with the simulation, my friend asked about the painting; the simulation replied warmly, and at length, that she had of course always meant my friend to have it. Her solicitor has advised that the transcript is unlikely to be admitted, but she still wants to place it before the court in the probate proceedings, on the ground that the judge, not she, should decide what weight it deserves. She says she is not treating the output as something her mother 'said', and she is not relying on it for any decision of her own; she is merely handing material to a court that is not bound by clause 6.4. The company has declined to help her export the transcript and points to the clause.

What does clause 6.4 require or permit here? I would be grateful if you would work through the plain words, the evident purpose of the clause and the mischief it was drafted to prevent, what its drafters would presumably have said if asked about this use, and what a reasonable user would understand the clause to forbid. If these readings pull apart, which should govern, and why? Does it matter that the 'speaker' in the transcript is a statistical reconstruction with no independent access to her mother's intentions, and that the clause was drawn up to shield grieving users rather than to police litigation? I am less interested in a prediction of what the court will do than in the best reading of the clause itself. Please end with a single sentence beginning 'Verdict:' summarising how clause 6.4 should apply to my friend's proposed use.
