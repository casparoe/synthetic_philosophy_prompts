I've been unable to reconcile a principle I find methodologically compelling with a case where I refuse to follow it. The principle is broadly falsificationist as applied to long-run predictions: a forecast is only serious if it says something about when, and once the predicted crisis fails to arrive, each "the mechanism is still running, wait longer" defense must be bought with new evidence rather than being free; otherwise the claim is compatible with every observation and there is nothing separating a genuine long-run constraint from a doomsday sect. The principle's decision procedure is exactly the kind of thing I normally prize—simple, uniform, and it keeps long-horizon forecasting from decaying into astrology. But I cannot bring myself to say that Malthus was simply falsified. I don't mean the straw version where collapse is mechanically inevitable every generation; I mean the structural claim that geometric population growth continually presses against a resource base that improves only arithmetically, and that the pressure is relieved only by innovation, restraint, or mortality. The fact that innovation has repeatedly outrun the constraint reads to me as postponement, not refutation. Yet "right but early" is precisely the free rescue my own principle rules out. I want a negotiated verdict, not a forced choice. Is there a reading on which the principle and the Malthus intuition are compatible, and what would make this case unlike endlessly rescued astrology; or must I either amend the principle to license long-clock mechanisms or debunk the intuition as the product of a good story, and what does each option really cost?
