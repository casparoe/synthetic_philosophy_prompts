In my law and society class last week the professor put a hypothetical on the board and then just let two students argue it out. The hypo: a small town has an old bylaw from 1901 banning fortune-telling, with a licensing scheme nobody has used since the 1940s. No one has been prosecuted under it for generations, no official so much as mentions it, and the local paper referred to it as a "dead law" back in the 1960s. Then a fortune-teller opens a shop, and the new district attorney announces he'll prosecute her under the 1901 bylaw, saying he doesn't get to pick and choose which laws count. Both students thought the DA was wrong, but for opposite reasons, and neither could finish the other off.

Maya said the DA is wrong because the law simply isn't law anymore. Law is a practice, not a pile of paper, and a rule that the whole community and every relevant official has ignored for eighty years has lapsed. She said this is what the old doctrine of desuetude was for: a statute can die through long non-use plus a shared understanding that it's spent. Her worry was notice. If the bylaw is still valid-but-dormant, then some lady with a storefront sign is supposed to have discovered a 120-year-old ordinance no one enforces, which makes the actual rules of the town unknowable — and that's the opposite of what the rule of law is for. Dev said the DA is wrong, but for the opposite reason: he's wrong because not enforcing a law isn't the same as repealing it. The town council could have repealed the bylaw any year since 1901 and didn't, and as long as it's validly enacted and unrepealed, it stays law; its age and lack of use don't touch its validity. If a prosecutor can kill a law by ignoring it, he said, the law changes silently through official neglect, with no public act, no deliberation, no accountability. The rule of law, Dev said, obliges you to repeal in the open, not to wink laws out of existence.

Then it got good. Maya: "On your view, any dead law can be revived on a whim, and the first person prosecuted is the one who finds out the town's real rules. That's permenent latent liability — rules you can't know by living in the town." Dev: "And on your view, a majority can quietly nullify a law it doesn't like by refusing to enforce it. Imagine a fair-housing ordinance that everyone stops obeying and no official enforces for thirty years; by the time someone complains, desuetude says the law is gone, and a minority is told the community has spoken. That's mob rule with a Latin name." Maya admitted that was a real hard case, but said the alternative is worse: a town run partly by rules nobody knew still counted, revivable whenever a new DA wants a headline. I walked out not knowing who wins which point. Could you go through the exchange move by move, say what's sound and what's overstated in each person's argument, and whether there's a principled way to tell a law that lapsed through desuetude from one that's merely under-enforced? Who, if anyone, is more right overall?
