I am a novelist, and I am writing to ask whether a structure I keep using in my fiction—and keep recognizing in the design of real institutions—has an established name and a precise analysis.

The structure is this. Someone, in a state they regard as especially clear-headed, arranges matters so that a later version of themselves will be unable, or at least severely impeded, to act on a desire the clear-headed version foresees. A mild instance: a writer who knows she will check her messages instead of finishing a chapter places her phone in a timed lockbox to which neither she nor her family has the key until evening. A sharper instance: a man with a history of gambling signs himself onto a casino exclusion list, so that even on the nights when he most wants to enter, the house is bound to turn him away. In fiction I tend to press the structure further, toward a character who, convinced that his grief will later make him do something ruinous, signs an instrument granting a trusted person durable authority to refuse his instructions.

The same silhouette appears at the level of institutions. A constitution may require two successive legislatures, or a supermajority, before certain settled provisions can be altered; a founding document may make some clauses unrevisable altogether. A present body arranges that a future body, with a future majority and future information, cannot easily undo what it has inherited. I find myself wanting to know whether the personal case and the constitutional case are genuinely one phenomenon or merely resemble each other in shape.

What I am after is the exact anatomy of this configuration. Does it have a name, and does that name mark a single kind of act or a family of related acts? Where does it pass from prudent self-restraint into an unjustified refusal to let one's future self exercise judgment, particularly when the later desire is not a lapse but an honest change of values? And in the political case, is there a principled difference between a present electorate binding its own future members and a present electorate binding people who did not yet exist when the decision was made? I am not looking for a label alone; I want to know how the concept is bounded, which cases lie inside it and which only mimic it from the outside, and which distinctions do the real work.
