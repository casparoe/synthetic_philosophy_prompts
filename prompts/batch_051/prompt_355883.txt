Two friends of mine got into it the other night about the "designated survivor" thing — the practice of picking one cabinet member to skip the State of the Union and sit in a secure location, so that if the Capitol is wiped out someone in the line of succession survives. I'm usually the one asked to settle these arguments, but I honestly couldn't, and neither of them was being glib. Could you go through their exchange and say who's right about what at each step — not just who wins overall, but which specific moves are sound, which don't quite follow, and what, if anything, both of them are missing?

Here's roughly how it went.

Priya: "The whole practice is grotesque. You tell one person 'you get to live' and everyone else in that chamber is left as a potential body count. That's not a policy, it's a lottery where the prize is being a spare part for the state. And the secretary didn't sign up to be the designated survivor — they signed up to run an agency."

Marcus: "But they already sit in the line of succession by virtue of the office the Senate confirmed them to. Continuity of government is a real moral good: if the whole elected leadership dies, the alternative isn't a clean democratic process, it's chaos, or generals picking a president, or whoever grabs the phone. The designated survivor just makes sure the constitutional line actually works."

Priya: "There's a difference between the line of succession existing for accidents and deliberately hiding one official so that the line works exactly as planned when everyone else is killed. One is a contingency; the other is a decision about who becomes president. It also means the people in the room are part of a plan they never consented to, with their deaths priced in."

Marcus: "Every emergency scheme distributes risk unevenly. Triage does that. And the legitimacy objection cuts too wide: whenever a president dies and the VP steps up, we get someone the voters never elected as president. Nobody says that office is illegitimate. We accept it because it's lawful and continuity matters more than rerunning the election."

Priya: "The VP ran on the ticket, though — voters at least weighed them as a possible successor. Nobody votes for Secretary of Agriculture thinking 'this person might be president mid-term because they got picked out of a hat.' And there's a moral burden specific to being the one hidden away while your colleagues all sit together. Being in the line is one thing; being told you're the one who'll live if the rest are vaporized is another."

Marcus: "Leaving survival of the government to chance isn't neutral, it's more arbitrary, not less. And public officials consent to a package of duties that includes continuity; a heavy moral burden doesn't make the arrangement illegitimate. I do want an actual answer, though — what's the better policy that doesn't just wish the problem away?"

Could I get a careful, step-by-step verdict on the specific points each one made?
