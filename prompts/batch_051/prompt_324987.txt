At a reading group I attend, two members, Mara and David, got into a dispute about whether it makes sense to call a particular finite sequence of coin flips random, and I have been turning it over since without being able to sort out who was right, because each of them seemed right while speaking and then wrong the moment the other replied. As I remember it, the exchange went like this. (1) Mara said that if a fair coin is flipped twenty times, every single sequence of heads and tails has exactly the same probability, so it is a mistake to look at twenty heads in a row and call it non-random: randomness is a property of the generating process, not of the outcome. (2) David replied that this is exactly what algorithmic randomness denies. A sequence of twenty heads is compressible, since a program saying "print heads twenty times" is far shorter than the sequence itself, while a typical twenty-flip sequence has no description shorter than itself; incompressibility is what makes a sequence random. (3) Mara answered that "incompressible" is only defined relative to a choice of programming language, and one can always build a language in which whichever "typical" string you please is a built-in constant that a one-character command prints. Kolmogorov complexity is language-independent only up to an additive constant, and for a twenty-bit string that constant can be larger than the string itself; so at that length, judgments of randomness track the choice of reference machine, not the string. (4) David conceded that twenty bits is a special case, but said the theory is asymptotic and nobody rests anything on sequences that short. Once the string gets long enough, the compressible strings form a vanishingly small minority, and that is why a million heads in a row from a supposedly fair coin is treated as evidence against the fairness of the coin while a typical million-flip sequence is not. So the intuition Mara dismissed, he said, does real work at the only scale where the notion matters.

I am not asking for a simple verdict that one of them wins. Then give what you think the correct overall position is, and in particular address whether the sentence "this finite sequence is random" is best understood as a claim about the sequence itself, about the process that produced it, or about our available descriptions of it. Please write your answer in continuous prose, as you would in an email or a letter, with no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables.
