I have been attending a philosophy discussion circle since leaving my laboratory, and last week two of its most thoughtful members, Marta and Alan, became entangled in an argument that I could not resolve, though I have turned it over for days.

The case under discussion was this. A colleague of mine from my years in research retired with a half-finished monograph he always intended to complete. Every January he lays out a writing schedule for the coming year and says, with apparent sincerity, that finishing the book is what he most wants, all things considered; he regards the daily satisfactions that compete with it as trivial by comparison. Yet every week he chooses the trivial satisfactions—long lunches, day trips, small repairs and errands—and defers the book. Each Sunday he reproaches himself, reaffirms the schedule, and believes the next week will be different.

Marta called this weakness of will in the strict sense: the man's own settled, all-things-considered judgment remains in place, and week after week he acts against it. His regret and his renewed resolutions are evidence, she said, that the Sunday judgment, and not the Saturday choice, expresses the self he identifies with.

Alan objected that this is not a failure of will at all but a schedule of discounting of the sort studied in behavioral economics. People like this man discount the near future more steeply than the distant future: from January, the book's completion and the Saturday pleasures are both far off, so the book wins; on Saturday morning, the pleasure is immediate and the book's payoff still distant, so the pleasure wins. He offered the familiar illustration of a person who prefers \$120 in thirteen months to \$100 in twelve months, yet prefers \$100 now to \$120 in one month once the delay is upon him. At each moment, Alan insisted, the man does what he most prefers. What Marta calls "his own judgment" is merely the preference of the temporally distant self, and it has no privilege over the preference of the present self.

Marta answered that on Alan's view the term "weakness of will" would apply to nothing: the gambler who bets the rent money, the addict who uses again, even the person who breaks a promise when it becomes costly—all would be redescribed as preferences that shift with proximity. If no temporal perspective is privileged, she said, we cannot even say the man is failing by his own standards.

Alan replied that perhaps we often should not say that. What looks like self-condemnation is a later self siding with an earlier self against an intermediate self. And notice, he added, that the man can act on his distant preference if he really wants to: he can precommit—remove temptations, make promises with penalties, arrange his week so the choice never arises. That is a matter of strategy, not willpower.

Marta returned that precommitment makes no sense on Alan's own premises. To bind one's future self is to treat the earlier self's preference as having authority over the later self's—but that is precisely what Alan denied in calling the January perspective unprivileged. If the Saturday self's preference is just as valid, then precommitment is not self-discipline but the past tyrannizing the present.

Alan said that precommitment need not assume one authoritative self; it can be understood as a bargain among a series of time-slices, like a treaty between neighboring states. The earlier self does not claim authority over the later one; it merely acts to protect its interests against a foreseeable defection, just as the present self is free to scheme back.

At this point Marta made the remark I have been turning over since. If the self is really a series of neighboring time-slices with no privileged standpoint, she said, then "its interests" in the treaty metaphor does no work, because there is no asset the present self and future self jointly own. Alan's view fragments the person into time-slices and then quietly helps itself to the very thing—a life whose good extends across time—that it set out to eliminate. Either there is one person with a standing plan, in which case Saturday's choice is akratic, or there are only time-slices, in which case the January preference has no more claim than the Saturday one and precommitment is just partiality toward one temporal region.

The discussion ended there, with Alan saying he needed to think and Marta conceding that her final point, while it felt right, was not as precise as she would have liked. Neither claimed victory, and I honestly could not referee the exchange myself. I would be grateful if you would adjudicate it move by move: say which parts of each person's argument are correct, which overreach, and what both of them are missing. I am especially interested in whether "weakness of will" does any work over and above a description in terms of hyperbolic discounting, and if so, whether that work is merely rhetorical or genuinely normative.
