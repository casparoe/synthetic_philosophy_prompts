I'm giving a paper next month to a reading group that's jointly run by the philosophy department and the machine learning lab, and I need to be ready for the Q&A. My thesis is that the child-machine section near the end of Turing's Computing Machinery and Intelligence isn't a minor afterthough but the actual heart of the paper. On my reading, Turing's real proposal is that you shouldn't try to program an adult mind's abilities directly; you should build something more like an educable child — relatively little initial mechanism, lots of blank pages, to use his notebook image — and then educate it, with rewards, punishment, ordered lessons, the whole thing. The imitation game, on my account, is less a definition of intelligence than a way of checking whether the education has worked. My one-line argument is that Turing treats the direct adult-simulation route as basically hopeless and deliberately swaps it for an educational problem, so anyone who reads him as mostly the inventor of a chat benchmark has inherited the wrong half of the paper.

The audience: four or five people from the ML side who work on large language models and will think of Turing as a historical mascot at best, two philosophers of cognitive science who know the paper but one of whom thinks the whole project is behaviorist and will push me on when I'm allowed to say the machine actually learned something, and a historian of computing who will care a lot about whether I'm reading Turing in context or just projecting modern deep learning back onto him.
