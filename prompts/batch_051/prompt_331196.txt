After jury duty a while back I've held onto a rule I'm not sure I can defend. The case was civil, so the standard was only "more likely than not" — not beyond a reasonable doubt. A cyclist had been hit by a bus, and the only evidence against one company was that they ran something like 80% of the buses on that route at that hour. No witness, no paint transfer, nothing tying this particular bus to the incident. 80% clears "more likely than not," but I couldn't bring myself to find them liable, and I was the holdout.

I've since generalised it into a policy: I won't base a judgment that someone did something on purely statistical evidence, no matter how high the probability, unless there's at least some individualized evidence linking the specific person to the specific act. My brother says this is incoherant: if I really thought it was 80% likely the company's bus did it, I should find for the plaintiff. I happily act on 80% probabilities everywhere else in my life, so my reluctance in court must just be squeamishness about numbers or about blame. Is my rule defensible? If it is, what exactly is it tracking, and is there a cleaner version of it I could state that keeps whatever is good about it without the parts he's right to call irrational?
