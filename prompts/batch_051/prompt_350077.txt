Hey — I'm a PhD student in philosophy working on interpretations of probability, and I've realized my gut keeps switching depending on the case. I'm hoping you can help me see whether there's one principle behind all of it or whether the right move is a principled pluralism.

Here's where I land.

A coin about to be tossed: P(heads) = 1/2. Fine, frequentism seems build for this.

A fair die that was just manufactured, verified fair, and sealed in a box where it will never be thrown: P(6) = 1/6 still feels true, not undefined or merely someone's fair betting rate. But there are zero trials, forever. This is the one that makes me want to reject strict frequentism.

A particular radium-226 atom: P(decays within one half-life) = 1/2. This feels like a physical tendancy of the atom itself, not a fact about my evidence or a reference class. But when I try to say what that tendency is without leaning on long-run frequencies, I come up empty.

That it rained last night, given only that the sidewalk is wet this morning. No repeatable trial, no mechanism being queried, just my information. Epistemic probability seems exactly right here.

That a particular candidate wins the next presidential election. I don't think there's a hidden true probability out there. But if another grad student and I have seen the same polls and I say 0.3 and she says 0.8, it doesn't feel like a mere difference of taste. One of us seems wrong, and I don't know how subjectivism explains that.

So: is there a single interpretation of probability that gets all of these, or close? If pluralism, what principle tells us which notion is in play in a given case, and what do they have in common such that we call them all probability? And what does the account say about some further case I haven't listed, where the interpretations pull in different directions? Which of my verdicts, if any, does it force me to give up?
