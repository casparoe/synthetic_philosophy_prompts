I read a long piece in a health-policy magazine about why so many depression trials fail, and at one point the author summarized the ethics of randomization like this: "Before a doctor can ethically enroll you in a randomized trial, clinical equipoise requires that she be completely undecided about which treatment is better. If she has even a mild hunch that one arm is better, enrolling you would betray her duty to give you the best care, because she would knowingly be withholding the treatment she thinks is superior. Some people try to fix this by saying the uncertainty can sit at the level of the community instead of the individual doctor: the trial is fine as long as at least one expert somewhere still isnt sure which arm is better. But that is obviously hopeless, since you can always find a dissenter, and doctors always have hunches anyway. So clinical equipoise collapses, and randomized trials are basically impossible to justify ethically."

The author was using this to argue that current trial ethics rests on shaky ground, and I can't tell whether this is a fair statement of the position or a slow-burn strawman. Could you go through it sentence by sentence and tell me which parts are accurate, which parts are common simplifications, which parts are outright wrong, and whether the final skeptical conclusion survives once the summary is corrected? I'm especially suspicious of the move from "the community is uncertain" to "one dissenter is enough," but I don't know the position well enough to say that with confidence. I'm not after a reading list or citations, just the reasoning.
