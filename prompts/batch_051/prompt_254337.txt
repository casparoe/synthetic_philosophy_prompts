I am preparing a seminar on Hume's discussion of causation and have found myself unable to give a precise gloss of a short methodological passage in Section VII, "Of the Idea of Necessary Connexion," of An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding. The passage runs:

Complex ideas may, perhaps, be well known by definition, which is nothing but an enumeration of those parts or simple ideas, that compose them. But when we have pushed up definitions to the most simple ideas, and find still some ambiguity and obscurity; what resource are we then possessed of? By what invention can we throw light upon these ideas, and render them altogether precise and determinate to our intellectual view? Produce the impressions or original sentiments, from which the ideas are copied.

My difficulty is not with Hume's eventual conclusion—that the idea of necessary connexion is traced to a customary transition of the mind—but with the structure of the test he announces here. I can see at least three ways to read it: as a semantic criterion, where a term has content only if its simple ideas can be traced to impressions; as an empiricist method of analysis, where obscure philosophical terms are replaced by their original sensory materials; or as a dialectical challenge to anyone who insists that "power" or "necessary connexion" has an intelligible meaning. Which reading, if any, best fits the paragraph and its subsequent use in the section? In particular, what exactly would count as passing or failing the search for the impression of necessary connexion—must one produce a single discrete impression answering to the whole idea, or is it enough that its component simple ideas can each be traced to impressions? I would value a close reading that separates the copy principle, definitional analysis, and introspection, and shows how much work each strand is doing in the argument.
