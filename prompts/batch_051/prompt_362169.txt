I spend most of my working life writing automation and control logic, and I have found myself thinking about a piece of animal behavior a colleague described to me: the digger wasp of the genus Sphex drags a paralyzed cricket to the entrance of its burrow, sets it down on the threshold, goes inside to inspect the tunnel, comes back out, and then pulls the cricket in. If the cricket is nudged a few inches away while the wasp is inside, the wasp drags it back to the threshold and runs the inspection routine again; repeat the nudge several times and the wasp will repeat the loop indefinitely, apparently unable to notice that the situation no longer requires it. A colleague called the pattern sphexishness. What bothers me is that I can see the same shape in systems I have built—a fixed retry loop, a job that re-queues itself on a schedule even after the conditions that justified it have passed—and, if I am honest, in my own habits.

The position I keep getting pulled toward is this. In engineering terms, flexibility is not the absence of a sequence of operations; it is a longer sequence, with more branches, more state, better sensors, or a learned policy that selects among far more subroutines. Whatever responsiveness I enjoy is, on this view, another such program running on a finite physical machine, and the only difference between the wasp and me is how many internal states the program has. Saying that I am not mechanical because I can notice a routine and deliberately break it just moves the subroutine up a level: the noticing and the breaking must themselves be implemented somehow, so they must be more machinery. Free will, on this account, would be the name I give to execution paths whose conditions I have not yet traced. The theory is attractive to me because it is parsimonious, leaves no causal gaps, and matches exactly how I would actually build adaptability into a system.

At the same time, I have an intuition I cannot quiet. When I catch myself inside a fixed pattern, recognize it as a pattern, and then choose to do something off-script—even something pointless, just to demonstrate to myself that I can—the experience feels different in kind from the wasp's loop or from any code path I have ever written. I seem to have the whole situation in view, not just the next condition in an internal table. The theory replies that this feeling of standing back is itself one more branch in a larger program, and that is precisely the reply that bothers me: it is built to absorb every possible objection in advance, which may mean it is deep or may mean it has stopped making contact with what it claims to explain.

I am trying to decide which of these should give way, and I would like you to reason with me rather than resolve it by fiat. Give me the strongest version of each side—the best case that the intuition tracks a real difference in kind that a finite, programmatic picture of my behavior leaves out, and the best case that the intuition is an artifact of sitting inside a sufficiently large rule table and should be discounted. Then give me your considered verdict. Should I debunk the intuition, abandon or qualify the theory, or is there a reading on which they are compatible? In each case, say what would justify that move rather than simply restating it.
