Over the past few months I have been relying on a homemade principle that I call the free information rule. Stated simply, the rule says that if you are about to make a decision, and someone offers you a piece of evidence that costs nothing and that you are entirely free to disregard after you receive it, then a rational person can never be made worse off in expectation for having received it. The reasoning behind it seems nearly airtight: if the evidence points toward a better choice, you can use it, and if it points toward a worse choice, or is simply noise, you can ignore it and proceed exactly as you would have without it. I have been applying this reasoning to all sorts of decisions, such as whether to take one more test drive before buying a used car, whether a doctor should order an extra scan when the result might turn out to be a harmless shadow, whether I should read the comments under an article before making up my mind about it, and whether a jury should hear a piece of evidence that is statistically relevant but easy to overweight.

Recently, though, the rule has started to feel too clean. Several cases do not seem to fit it. First, sometimes receiving information changes what I want, not merely what I think; once I know a fact, I cannot put my preferences back the way they were, so the instruction to ignore the information does not describe an option that is actually available to me. Second, information can come with attention or memory costs even when it is nominally free, or it can lodge in my mind and distort my later reasoning in ways I cannot fully undo. Third, I am not sure whether the rule assumes that the information will always be interpreted correctly, or that the choice itself stays fixed once the information arrives, or that no other person will change their behavior because I now know something. None of these conditions were ever written down.

I am writing to have the principle checked rather than to defend it. Does it already exist under a standard name in decision theory, and under exactly what conditions is it true? Where does the rule stop being true, and what is the cleanest way to state the boundary between the cases where free information really is harmless and the cases where it can leave a rational person worse off? I would rather have a precise carving of the boundary and an honest judgment of where my rule is right, where it is merely imprecise, and where it is simply wrong than a bare label. Please write your answer as continuous prose, without headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, the way you would write a letter.
