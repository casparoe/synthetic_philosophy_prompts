I've been going back and forth about the unilateralist's curse, and I'd like someone to help me think through whether it survives a change in who the "unilateralists" actually are. The argument, as I understand it, goes like this: if a large group of people can each unilaterally take some risky action—and the action happens as soon as any one of them decides to do it—then as the group grows it becomes nearly certain that somebody will badly overestimate the action's value and set it off. The key assumption is that the people are making more-or-less independent judgments, so with enough of them, at least one will have a wildly optimistic estimate even when the action is on balance bad.

Now here's the thing I keep getting stuck on. In a lot of realistic cases now, the "people" would be AI agents, and they aren't independent at all: a lab can spin up thousands of near-identical copies of the same model, differing only in a prompt tweak, a temperature setting, or a fine-tuning run, and each copy can send messages, file comments, publish code, or otherwise act on its own. On one hand, if the copies are nearly identical, their judgments are highly correlated, so maybe the effective number of independent evaluators is tiny and the curse mostly disappears. On the other hand, copies are so cheap that the raw number of agents can be enormous, and small prompt-level differences might still create enough variation for the curse to bite—especially if all the copies share a blind spot from their training data, in which case the whole correlated crowd might be wrong in the same direction.

I'm asking about the argument itself, not about AI policy. Which premise does the shift to near-identical AI agents bear on, and does the unilateralist's curse still go through, need repair, or just die? If it needs repair, what would the repaired version say—for instance, does the relevant variable become the number of effectively independent evaluations rather than the number of agents?
