I've been trying to get my head around the boundary problem in democratic theory—the question of who ought to count as a member of the demos when a group is about to make a binding collective decision—and I keep coming back to the 2014 Scottish independence referendum as a way to pressure-test it. The broad facts as I understand them: the vote was in September 2014, on whether Scotland should become an independent country. The franchise included UK citizens resident in Scotland, EU citizens resident in Scotland, and 16- and 17-year-olds, but it excluded Scots-born people who were living elsewhere in the UK or abroad at the time. Turnout was around 85%, and the result was 55–45 against independence. I want to analyze the referendum through the lens of the all-affected-interests principle: roughly, that everyone whose interests are significantly affected by a decision should have some say in it, not just those who happen to be enrolled in the relevant polity.

What I'm after is a careful mapping rather than a summary of the politics, which I already know. Who counts as 'affected' here, and how would you even operationalize that—is the rUK voter affected because the pound and borders might change, is the London Scottish diaspora voter affected enough that exclusion is unjust, are 16-year-olds more affected because they live with the consequences longest? Then I want the reverse direction: what does the episode do to the theory? Does it confirm it, show that 'significantly affected' has no non-arbitrary cutoff, or expose that secession is a case the principle was never built to handle, since the decision to hold a referendum at all already presupposes a demos? And where does the principle fall silent—say, on how to weight the interests of a Glasgow Yes voter against a Newcastle No voter, or on how a 'no' result and a 'yes' result would affect people asymetrically and at different distances? If the all-subjected principle (everyone who'll be bound by the laws gets a say) does better on some of this, say so; if it does worse on the diaspora and the 16-year-olds, I want to see that too. I dont need citations or readings, just a disciplined analysis that runs both ways.
