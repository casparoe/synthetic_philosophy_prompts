I'm a novelist, and at my writing group the other night two friends of mine got into it over a third friend who edits a small lit mag. She'd just accepted a story by a close friend of hers that, by most people's lights in the room, was a lot weaker then a stranger's submission she'd turned down. I kept thinking I could use the argument in a novel, but first I'd need to know who's actually right. They asked me to referee and I honestly couldn't. Here's the back-and-forth, as close as I can remember it.

Ben: She edits the magazine for everyone who sends work in, not just her friends. Her job is to pick the best thing she can, and once friendship tilts the call, that's bias — a small betrayal of the stranger who submitted in good faith.

Ana: Where exactly is friendship supposed to show up, if not in decisions like this? If loyalty can only steer the harmless trimmings and never a real choice, it's not a virtue, it's just a feeling. Calling it bias already assumes the impartial rules of the game are the right rules, and that's the very thing I'm disputing.

Ben: So would you defend a judge who ruled for a friend? If not, you agree at least some roles demand impartiality. The only question is whether an editor at an open-submission magazine has that kind of role — and I say she does, because she's made a standing offer to strangers that their work will be considered on its merits.

Ana: A judge isn't parallel. She has state power and is bound by explicit rules of impartiality; our friend runs a tiny magazine and can champion whoever she wants — that's a lot of what little magazines are for. And "best story" isn't a settled fact in fiction anyway. Every aesthetic judgement is partial, so your "clearly better" is just your taste wearing a lab coat.

Could you go through the exchange and rate each of those four moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification each, and then use the fifth point to say who's more right overall? Exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no introduction or conclusion.
