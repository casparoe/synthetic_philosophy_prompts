Suppose an engineering cooperative builds and maintains shared digital infrastructure for its member organizations, and it adopts the following practice, organized around a Heideggerian distinction: when equipment works it is ready-to-hand—transparently absorbed into the task; when it fails it becomes present-at-hand—a conspicuous object with properties. The cooperative's rule: when any piece of equipment—a server, an API, an authentication layer, an internal dashboard—breaks down, the team using it may not immediately propose a fix. It must first produce a breakdown log: a written reconstruction of the whole network of tasks, expectations, dependencies, and adjacent tools in which the broken thing had been silently doing its work while it worked. The entry must state which parts of that network were invisible to the team before the failure, and it must close by naming one thing the failure revealed that was not already present in any specification, test, or status page. Only after the entry is accepted may repairs be proposed. Teams are evaluated for promotion not by uptime or failure rates but by the depth and quality of their breakdown logs.

Analyze how this mechanism would actually behave over time, not whether it is a good idea in the abstract. Focus on incentives: what would teams, managers, and clients be driven to do differently? How would the rule be gamed, and what second-order effects would it have on how systems are designed and on how the organization remembers its own history? Where would it predictably fail, and which failure modes are fixable by adjusting the rule? In particular: can a practice built around documenting the passage from readiness-to-hand to presence-at-hand ever capture that passage, or would the demand for documentation turn breakdowns into just another deliverable and systematically edit out the phenomenon it was meant to record?
