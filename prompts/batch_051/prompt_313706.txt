I'm a criminology PhD student, and I keep turning over a mechanism for my chapter on error in capital punishment; I can't decide whether its a clever way to price in wrongful-conviction risk or just a way to stall every execution forever. Suppose a US state passes a rule that no execution may be carried out unless the county that prosecuted the case first lodges a bond, equal to something like 20% of that county's annual law-enforcement budget, with the state supreme court. The bond is held for thirty years after the execution: if the person is posthumously exonerated in that window, it pays out to their estate or named family; if not, it reverts to the county. I'm not asking whether this is just. What I want is a cold analysis of how it would play out: what it does to charging and plea decisions, to jury behaviour, to clemency, to the way courts treat post-conviction evidence long after the person is dead, and to how innocence projects spend their efforts. Where does it get gamed first? My worry is that the definition of 'posthumously exonerated' becomes the whole game, or that counties just externalise the bond somehow. Are those failure modes fixable without scrapping the mechanism?
