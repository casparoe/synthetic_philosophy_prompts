I'm a retired astronomer and I've been reading around in philosophy, and I keep tripping over the sunk cost question. My working criteria for when it's OK to keep pouring resources into something are: (i) expected future payoff has to exceed expected future cost, ignoring what's already in the hole; (ii) it can still be rational to finish if quitting would break a commitment or wreck your credibility; (iii) it can still be rational if the activity has turned into something you value for its own sake rather than the original goal; (iv) it can still be rational if you can recover or repurpose some of the sunk cost. Now the case. For six years I've been building a small radio telescope in my yard to monitor a set of pulsars for a long-shot timing anomaly. I'm maybe 80% done: dish, mount, and receiver work, but the data pipeline and calibration are half-built. Last year a satellite survey pretty much settled the question I was chasing, so the original science is dead. I promised my old collaborator, who died two years ago, that I'd get the thing running. I could finish it over a summer for a few thousand dollars. The finished telescope would be a nice demo for the local high school astronomy club, but it won't produce publishable results, and if I'm honest the fun has mostly gone out of it. Am I rational to finish it, or is that just the sunk cost fallacy with a eulogy attached? Go through my criteria one by one, say where they underdetermine the verdict, and tell me whether my list needs a fifth criteria or whether one of mine should go. Give me exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no introduction or conclusion.
