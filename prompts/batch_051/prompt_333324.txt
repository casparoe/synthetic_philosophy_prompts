After a career in behavioral ecology, where I studied cooperative systems and the organisms that exploit them—pollinators that take nectar without transferring pollen, cleaner fish that bite rather than clean, helpers that slip into a nest to lay eggs—I have recently turned to moral philosophy. In the field I was content to call such organisms cheaters or social parasites as neutral descriptions: a cheater was simply a strategy that reaped a benefit from a cooperative arrangement without paying the cost. I find I cannot make the same move in the human cases. My verdicts on those cases are as follows, and I want to know what principle, if any, they trace.

1. A hiker walks a volunteer-maintained trail every weekend, has never joined a work party, and never donates. I consider this wrong, though minor: they free ride on the labor that makes their activity possible.

2. A patient has received blood transfusions and later declines to donate blood, citing inconvenience. I do not think they act wrongly. Blood donation seems to me a gift, not a contribution owed.

3. A citizen never votes, yet expects police, courts, and fire protection and holds strong views about them. I count this a civic failing, not a moral wrong, and plainly not the same kind of failure as the hiker's.

4. A scientist builds a career on data from public repositories but refuses to deposit their own data because curation is costly. I think they wrong their community: they take from an explicit practice of reciprocity on which their own work depends.

5. A farmer's orchard is pollinated by the neighbor's bees because the hives happen to be nearby; the farmer pays nothing and keeps none of their own. I do not think they wrong the neighbor. The benefit is an unavoidable spillover, not something they extract.

6. A medically eligible adult declines a safe, effective vaccination during a serious outbreak while relying on the reduced transmission produced by everyone else's vaccination. I think this is seriously wrong, even in the variant where the refusal makes no measurable difference to the outbreak.

7. A healthy adult lives on inherited wealth, does not work or volunteer, and contributes nothing to any collective enterprise while using roads, utilities, and the law. My instinct is that they are not a free rider in the same sense, because they are not appropriating another person's contribution; but I cannot yet say why.

I have a tentative principle. Taking a benefit without contributing is wrong just when one exploits a practice whose persistence depends on most participants being willing to contribute, while claiming for oneself an exemption one could not will as a general policy. I already see the strain: as stated, it seems to condemn the non-voter and the transfusion recipient, whom I do not want to condemn, and it might acquit the hiker if their individual shirking harms no one.

Please take my seven verdicts and extract the best principle you can. State the principle precisely, apply it to each case, and tell me which of my verdicts it preserves and which it forces me to revise. Then apply it to a few cases I have not listed: the turnstile-jumper on a well-funded metro; the person who benefits from others' costly political activism but never risks anything; and the company that builds a product on open-source software while contributing nothing back. Do not survey the options; commit to one account of what is wrong with free riding, and be explicit about the price that account pays.
