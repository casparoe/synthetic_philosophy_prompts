I am trying to work out what I actually think about what it is to care for someone, and my gut verdicts on cases keep resisting every simple principle I try. I'd like you to reconstruct the principle — or the small set of candidate principles — that best fits my verdicts, apply it to some new cases, and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate.

My verdicts, roughly as they feel:

1. A nurse is warm and genuinely attentive to a patient, but she is careless with medication and harms the patient through a wrong dose. I don't call this good care; warmth without competence is not enough.

2. A surgeon performs a flawless and difficult operation but is cold, brusque, and never listens to the patient. I want to say he gave excellent treatment but did not care for the patient.

3. A son visits his mother in a care home every Sunday, manages her finances, and advocates for her with the staff, but he feels no warmth and does all of it from duty. I'm torn: part of me says this is care, just without the feeling; another part says something central is missing.

4. A father works two jobs to pay for his daughter's medical treatment but is rarely at the hospital and does not know her doctors' names. I'd say he cares deeply about her, but I hesitate to say he is caring for her during the illness.

5. A friend listens to my problems for hours and gives me genuinely useful advice, but I later learn she resented every minute of it and complained about me to others. I want to say the behavior was caring but she was not — or that it was care, but flawed.

6. A stranger gives a large donation to a medical fundraiser for someone he has never met. I call that generous and beneficent, but not care.

7. A mother is emotionally attuned to her child, and the child feels deeply loved, but she doesn't take the child to the dentist, supervise homework, or enforce bedtimes, and the child suffers for it. I'd say she cares, but it is deficient care.

8. A paid home health aide is punctual, efficient, and technically competent, but she never learns the patient's name and handles the patient like an object. I'd say that is service, not care.

9. My aunt cares for my uncle, who has dementia and sometimes no longer recognizes her. The care is competent and tender, and I have no hesitation calling it care even though he cannot reciprocate or respond in kind.

My working rule was that care requires both the right feeling and the right actions. But that rule doesn't fit the partial cases. It would rule out the dutiful son and the resentful friend's behavior, both of which I'm inclined to count as care in some imperfect sense; it would count the father as caring for his daughter during the illness, which I'm not sure I want to do; and it would say the attuned mother does not care at all, when I want to say she cares but deficiently. The rule seems too blunt.

So: is there a principle that gets all of these, or nearly all? If so, state it precisely and say which of my cases motivates each part of it. What does it say about a teacher who is warm and responsive to her students but fails to teach them anything? What does it say about a social worker who follows every rule and secures benefits for a family but never asks the family what they need? And which of my verdicts, if any, should I revise?
