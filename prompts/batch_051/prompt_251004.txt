I keep seeing people run together two claims from the orthogonality-and-instrumental-convergence cluster. The first is that an agent's final goals and its level of capability are orthogonal — that there's no level of intelligence that rules out any particular goal, or vice versa. The second is that, even if final goals are arbitrary, capable agents will converge on the same handful of instrumental subgoals anyway: self-preservation, resource acquisition, goal-content integrity, maybe information-gathering. I get the formal argument for the second one — roughly, an optimal agent should keep itself alive and keep its options open, because a dead or optionless agent can't pursue whatever it's after. But I want to know whether it's an empirical claim about actual learning systems, not just a theorem about ideal maximizers.

So: how would you test the instrumental-convergence claim? I want a concrete design — what kind of agent, what sort of environment, what you'd vary across runs, and what observation would count as the agent having picked up self-preservation or resource-acquisition as an instrument, rather than just doing whatever the reward function happens to reward. The case I keep worrying about is the shutdown switch: if an agent avoids a shutdown button, is that evidence for a self-preservation drive, or just evidence that it's maximizing reward in an environment where getting shut down means reward stops? Give me the control or manipulation that would seperate those.

Then be brutal about what a positive result would and would not show. If a bunch of agents with different terminal rewards all converge on the same subgoals, does that support the claim that capability is doing the work, or just that they all share an architecture and an optimizer? And is there anything no experiment of this kind could settle in principle — about whether the result carries over to agents much more capable than the ones you trained, or about the orthogonality claim itself?
