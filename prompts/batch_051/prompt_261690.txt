I'm a novelist, and I've gotten myself stuck on a detail for a book about a village tribunal. I'd read enough about the Condorcet jury theorem to know the standard story: if each voter is independently better than a coin flip at getting a yes/no question right, the majority gets more reliable as the group grows. So I assumed a person who is wrong more than half the time would always drag the group down. But a friend in political science told me there's a wrinkle I should put in the novel: a below-chance juror can actually make the majority more reliable, by being wrong in a useful pattern. I love the idea — the fool who saves the jury — but I need the mechanics to be believable enough to hang character motivation on.

So here's what I want. Construct an ordinary-life case, nothing exotic and no hidden information or mind-reading, where a new member whose individual accuracy on a class of yes/no decisions is strictly below fifty percent raises the committee's majority accuracy once they join. Keep it small and scrutable — two or three or five voters, or whatever size you need, but if the group is even just say how ties are resolved. Then do the check: lay out the pattern of who's right and wrong in which kinds of cases, explain why the below-chance member doesn't drag the majority down, and name the specific feature doing the work (I have a hunch its about the pattern of errors being correlated with the others, but I want it shown, not asserted). Then tell me what happens to the effect if you remove that feature, and how someone who thinks the Condorcet independence assumptions hold would attack the case. The numbers have to come out of a believable error pattern, not just be stipulated. Keep it under 600 words.
