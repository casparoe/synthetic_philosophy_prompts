In my seminar on truth and paradox, we have been contrasting Curry's paradox with Yablo's liar. Curry seems to derive an arbitrary proposition Q from a single sentence that says of itself that if it is true then Q, and the usual candidates for the bad step are the truth schema, the logic of the conditional, and the structural rules of the deduction. Yablo is introduced as a pressure test for the idea that self-reference is the culprit, because an infinite sequence of sentences, each saying that all later sentences in the sequence are false, is inconsistent even though no sentence mentions itself. I have been trying to turn Yablo's construction into a Curry-style proof of an arbitrary Q, and I am not sure how the standard diagnoses carry over.

Fix Q and let S_0, S_1, S_2, ... be an infinite sequence, where each S_n says that if every later sentence in the sequence is true — that is, if S_m is true for every m > n — then Q. Assume a two-valued semantics with a truth-functional conditional and the disquotational truth schema, and suppose for reductio that Q is false. If S_0 is false, then its antecedent is true and its consequent false, so every later sentence is true; but then S_1 is true even though its own antecedent is true and Q is false, which is impossible for a truth-functional conditional. If S_0 is true, then, since Q is false, its antecedent must be false, so some later sentence S_k is false; but the falsity of S_k forces all sentences after k to be true, and each of those sentences would then have a true antecedent and a false consequent, contradicting their being true. It looks as if Q cannot be false, and the sequence would force Q while every S_n comes out true.

I want to know whether this construction really gives a non-self-referential Curry-style proof of an arbitrary Q. If the reasoning is valid, where should someone who wants to block it push, and is that the same point at which the standard responses to Curry or to Yablo already push? If it fails, what exactly is the first invalid step? Would the argument go through, fail, or behave differently if the antecedent were instead "some later sentence is true" or "all later sentences are false"?
