I'm currently in a seminar on historical forecasting and the use of base rates to estimate the life expectancy of great powers, and I've arrived at an argument that seems to show the outside view undermines itself. I can't tell whether the argument is valid but leads somewhere uncomfortable, or whether one of the steps slides. Here it is, with a concrete case in mind.

Consider a rising continental power that is, at the same time, a multi-ethnic land empire, a nuclear-armed great power, and a centrally planned economy. We want to estimate the probability that it survives essentially intact for the next fifty years.

(1) The outside view instructs us to begin by identifying the reference class of past powers relevantly similar to this one, take the base rate of survival in that class over a fifty-year horizon, and adopt it as our prior, adjusting only for diagnostic features of the particular case.

(2) But no reference class is forced by the method itself. The case belongs to many classes at once: land empires, multi-ethnic autocracies, nuclear-armed great powers, centrally planned economies, rising challengers to an established hegemon, and so on. Each class is defined by a real feature of the case, and each can plausibly be presented as the relevant comparison.

(3) These classes have different historical survival records. Large land empires have often broken up within a few generations of their peak; nuclear-armed great powers have generally remained intact over comparable spans; centrally planned economies show a mixed record of reform, stagnation, and collapse. The base rates of the classes therefore diverge, and depending on which class is chosen, the prior probability of fifty-year survival ranges from quite high to quite low.

(4) Since the method does not itself determine which class to use, it does not determine a unique prior. A forecaster who reports the base rate is reporting the base rate of a class she selected, not a class the method selected.

(5) The only available basis for the selection is a case-specific judgment about which dimension of similarity is causally relevant to survival — that territory and internal diversity matter more than nuclear status, or that the logic of deterrence matters more than imperial overstretch, or the like. But that is precisely the kind of inside, theory-laden judgment the outside view was meant to discipline.

(6) Thus the outside view faces a dilemma. If it declines to choose among classes, it yields no determinate forecast. If it chooses, it imports the inside view it claimed to correct. In the first case, it cannot rule out much: for almost any forecast, some defensible class can be supplied whose base rate supports it. In the second case, it is not an alternative to the inside view but a disguised continuation of it.

(7) Yet this conclusion proves too much. The principle behind the outside view is simply that base-rate information from the past should constrain predictions about a particular case. If the multiplicity of reference classes made that principle empty, the same problem would discredit every frequency-based prediction — medical prognosis, recidivism risk, cost overruns, insurance pricing, and any other inference from a statistical class to a singular case. Since we are presumably not prepared to conclude that all statistical forecasting is rationally inert, something in steps (1) through (6) must be rejected.

Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? In particular, can the outside view be saved by saying that it properly outputs a range or a weighted family of priors rather than a single number, or does that just concede that it cannot constrain forecasts in the way it promised? If I had to choose, I would rather give up (6) than (7), but I don't see how to reject (6) without admitting that the outside view is only as strong as the inside knowledge used to select its reference class.
