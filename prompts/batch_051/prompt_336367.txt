I am taking a university course on the ethics of clinical research, and the more I think about randomized controlled trials the more I notice an asymmetry in how we judge the very same act depending on the context in which it is performed. Suppose a physician is treating a single patient and must choose between two accepted therapies. If the physician honestly faces genuine uncertainty — the evidence does not say which therapy is better — and resolves the choice by flipping a coin, most of us would react badly. We would say the physician has abandoned the patient, failed to exercise judgment, perhaps committed malpractice; certainly we would not call the coin flip an ethical achievement. Now suppose the same physician, with the same patient and the same two therapies, is acting as a trialist in a randomized controlled trial. The treatment the patient receives will still be selected by a randomizer; from the patient's point of view, the allocation is still a matter of chance rather than of anyone's judgment about what is best for them. Yet in this setting we do not condemn the randomization. We treat it as the gold standard of rigorous evaluation, and we regard a physician who refuses to randomize as obstructing the very knowledge that could help future patients. The same physician, in the same corridor, can step from the clinic to the trial ward and find that randomization has changed from a vice into a virtue.

I can rehearse the usual explanations: the trial participant consents to random allocation, whereas the clinic patient has consented to treatment chosen for them; the trial pursues a different end, namely knowledge for future patients rather than care for this one; and the trial is ethical only under equipoise, when there is genuine uncertainty about which arm is better, so the participant is not being denied anything known to be better. But I am not sure any of these differences does the necessary work. The trialist is typically still the participant's physician, the participant is still a patient in need, and genuine uncertainty also describes the clinic case in which the coin was flipped. What I want to know is whether there is a principle that generates both judgments — that condemns randomization for the individual patient while celebrating it in the trial — or whether the asymmetry is merely the product of two different institutional labels for the same thing. Please give me the strongest principle you can find, test it against the following cases, and then say whether the asymmetry should be kept, reversed, or given up: a physician outside any trial who, facing genuine uncertainty, tells a single patient as much and randomizes with consent; an emergency trial in which randomization occurs while the patient is unconscious and there is no opportunity to consent; a patient who enrolls in a trial only because the trial offers a chance at a drug they could not otherwise afford; and a study comparing two treatments when the enrolling physician privately believes one of them is superior but lacks proof.
