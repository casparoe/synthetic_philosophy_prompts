I'm a novelist, and the thing I'm trying to work out at the moment is the toppling of Edward Colston's statue in Bristol in 2020, because two of my characters are a retired history teacher who thinks is was just vandalism and a young activist who thinks it was a long-overdue act of disrespect to someone who never deserved any. I keep circling a distinction from ethics between recognition respect and appraisal respect: roughly, a baseline respect owed to any person just because they're a person, and a further respect that has to be earned and can be lost, which tracks someone's qualities or deeds. Treat that as an analytical lens. What exactly was the object of respect or disrespect when that bronze went into the harbour? The man, the statue, the city, the institutions that kept the plinth, the descendants of the enslaved? When someone says the toppling was 'disrespectful', which sense are they using, and is that a category mistake? When someone says Colston didn't deserve respect, is that consistent with still owing something to the statue or to fellow citizens? Then run the case back against the lens: what does Bristol 2020 show about the limits of the recognition/appraisal distinction—does it stretch awkwardly over dead people, public sculpture, and groups, and if so, is the problem the distinction or that people were really arguing about something else, like standing or speech? Keep the mapping disciplined. I'd rather you tell me where the distinction goes quiet than force it to speak on everything.
