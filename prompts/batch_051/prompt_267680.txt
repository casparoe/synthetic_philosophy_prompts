I sit on the credentialing committee of a large teaching hospital. We are deciding whether to take action against a surgeon whose risk-adjusted complication rate over the past three years is nearly twice the department average for the same procedures, a difference that is statistically very unlikely to be due to chance. An outside review examined every flagged case and could identify no error in any individual operation: each chart, read on its own, records what appears to be defensible surgical judgment and technique, with no common mechanism or pattern of negligence linking the complications. Some committee members argue the aggregate pattern is enough, by a preponderance of the evidence, to find that this particular surgeon's practice poses an avoidable risk to patients, even if we cannot locate the mechanism. Others reply that this is naked statistical evidence rather than evidence about this surgeon's conduct at all. They compare it to holding a bus company liable merely because most buses on the route were blue, or penalizing a spectator merely because gatecrashers made up most of the crowd, when no witness can tie this individual to any specific harmful act. They insist we should not single out this surgeon on the basis of an aggregate regularity alone. The bylaws permit restriction of privileges when the committee finds, by a preponderance of the evidence, that a practitioner poses a continuing avoidable risk to patients, and they authorize a range of interventions from a period of intensified monitoring, to mandatory proctoring, to restriction or suspension. I need to circulate a short written opinion before Thursday's meeting. Please draft the decision memo: it should recommend a concrete course of action with a time frame, explain the reasoning, and give an honest treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side.
