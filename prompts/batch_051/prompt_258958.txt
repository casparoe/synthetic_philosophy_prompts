You are an expert in philosophy of language and semantics who has also thought carefully about how to teach context-sensitivity. I am preparing a fifty-minute discussion section for first-year undergraduates in an introductory philosophy of language course. Next week I need to design the section around a question: Could a human language entirely lack pronouns or indexicals—or could it have one but not the other? I do not want you to explain the issue to me; I want a plan for how I should run the section.

The students have already been introduced, informally, to the difference between expressions whose reference is fixed independently of the occasion of utterance (their working picture of proper names) and expressions whose reference shifts with who utters them, where, and when (I, you, here, now, this). They have noticed that Spanish often omits subject pronouns, and a few have heard that some languages use demonstratives or kinship terms where English would use a pronoun. Their immediate reaction to next week's question is that the answer is obviously yes: a language could just use proper names, definite descriptions, explicit times, and explicit places instead. I want the section to make them see that this obvious yes is less settled than it looks, without pretending the section will settle a debate that professionals find subtle.

Before you write the final plan, spend a few paragraphs in rough notes brainstorming candidate examples and the load-bearing distinctions—for instance: pronouns versus indexicals; lacking a form versus making it optional; paraphrasing a sentence versus communicating the same thing to someone who must act on it. Then present the plan itself.

The plan must specify:

1. The order in which the ideas have to be introduced, with a one-sentence justification for each step that identifies what it is a prerequisite for. I want the dependency structure, not just a list of topics.
2. The single concrete example or scenario that should carry the whole section, and what this example must have and must not smuggle in. If it helps, explain why you rejected one natural alternative example.
3. The misconceptions the students will bring or form, in the order they are likely to surface, and the exact point where each should be headed off rather than left until the end.
4. A final question or short exercise that would show me whether the students have understood the question itself—and not merely learned to say that a language can or cannot do without indexicals.
5. What I should deliberately leave out or flag as a simplification at this level, and why including it would derail the section.

Please do not answer with a bibliography or reading list; the section is self-contained, and I need pedagogical decisions rather than sources. Write in American English. End with a final line beginning "Summary:" followed by a one-sentence summary of the teaching plan.
