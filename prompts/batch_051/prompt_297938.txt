I write about virtual worlds and the companies that run them for a magazine, and I keep hitting the same editorial call a few times a month: when is it right to say someone's virtual sword was 'stolen,' that a VR groping was an 'assault,' or that a player 'destroyed' another player's digital artwork — and when should I hedge and call it harassment, deletion, or a terms-of-service violation instead? I don't want to just follow what the platform calls it, and I don't want to go by vibes. Write me a reusable procedure for deciding wether an event inside a virtual world should be reported as a real harm to a real person or a real loss of a real object, grounded in whatever the philosophy of virtual objects and fictional entities actually establishes (not the fun speculation). It has to be usable by me on deadline — say 20 minutes a case — with no assumption that I've done the reading, and it has to be defensible to my editor and to readers who will either say I'm dismissing victims or that I'm turning a game incident into a crime. Include a step-by-step checklist with only the distinctions that do real work, then two or three worked cases that pull in different directions, including one where the procedure makes me write something I'd rather not. Format the answer in four sections, in this order: 'The checklist' (roughly 300 words), 'Worked cases' (roughly 450 words), 'What the procedure can't settle' (roughly 150 words), and 'The principle underneath, in one paragraph' (roughly 120 words).
