I find myself pulled in two directions on what makes consent valid, and I want a negotiated verdict rather than a winner.

On one side, I hold a clean, deflationary account. I treat someone as validly consenting to another person's action—touch, sex, medical treatment, participation in research, use of personal data—just in case, at the moment of agreement, the person (i) has the relevant decision-making competence, (ii) is not relevantly misled about what is being agreed to or about its significant risks, and (iii) agrees voluntarily. I take voluntariness narrowly: an agreement is involuntary only when it is produced by a threat that worsens the person's expected position relative to a relevant baseline, as when compliance is extracted by the threat of harm. This account is simple, unified across domains, and yields reasonably determinate verdicts. In particular, it lets me say that hard choices and background poverty, however terrible, do not by themselves invalidate consent: they may make a person's options bad, but they do not make the choice something someone else did to them.

On the other side, I have a firm intuition that this is too narrow. Consider a tenant who agrees to sex with a landlord because the landlord has offered to forgive rent arrears and the alternative is eviction; or a worker who accepts a grossly unsafe job because the alternative is destitution; or a patient who enrolls in a risky drug trial because no one will otherwise treat their condition. In each case the inducement is an offer rather than a threat, and in each case the option chosen may be no worse than the person's pre-existing baseline. Yet it seems wrong to treat these as fully valid consent; the powerful party is positioned to set the terms of another person's body or labor, and that position itself feels coercion-like. But I can also see the slippery slope: all choices occur under scarcity, and treating every unattractive alternative as coercion seems to erase the difference between being coerced and merely having bad options.

Then walk me through the possible resolutions. What would it take for the intuition to be debunked, or reassigned elsewhere—say, to the unfairness of background distributions or to the exploitative terms of the offer, rather than to the validity of consent? What would it take for the voluntariness condition to be amended to include coercive offers, unconscionable terms, or structural domination, and how would the amended account avoid the slippery slope? Is there a reading of my original account on which both the theory and the intuition survive, perhaps by distinguishing what invalidates consent from what makes the surrounding situation unjust?
