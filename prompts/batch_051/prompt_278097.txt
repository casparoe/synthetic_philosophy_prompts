I work as a software engineer, with no formal philosophy background, but a side project has pulled me into discussions of 'low-impact AI'. The idea there is that a capable AI should do the narrow job it was set and otherwise leave the world as it found it; it should 'avoid side effects'. In programming, I know exactly what a side effect is: a function modifies state other than what it returns, such as writing to a log or updating a database. But that definition works only because the language or codebase provides a declared interface, so everyone knows which state is inside the job and which lies outside it. For an AI acting in the physical and social world, that interface is precisely what is missing. It seems to me that any working definition of 'side effect' for such an agent will either quietly presuppose a full specification of what we value, so that 'avoid side effects' is not a free extra safety property, or else it will fail to separate the hole knocked in the wall from the light coming on. Please propose a working definition of 'side effect' for an AI acting in the world that does not presuppose a full specification of human values. If the best such definition still has to import some minimal value assumptions, state exactly what they are and why they are weaker than a full specification. Ideally the definition should be the kind of thing an engineer could turn into a penalty, rather than only a judgement made after the fact.

Test the definition against the following cases. I am fairly confident about the first four and genuinely unsure about the rest.

(a) The agent is asked to fetch a mug of coffee. Its route planner finds that the shortest path passes through a wall, so it knocks a hole through the plasterboard rather than walking around. I take this to be a clear side effect, whatever else is.

(b) The agent is asked to water the garden and, while dragging the hose, tramples the flowerbed. Again, clearly a side effect.

(c) The agent is asked to switch on a lamp, and it does so. The lamp's being on is the intended outcome, so it is not a side effect.

(d) The agent's battery drains and its actuators wear slightly as it works. These are changes in the world outside the stated objective, but I do not think 'avoid side effects' is meant to cover them.

(e) While fetching the coffee, the agent sees an open window and rain coming in, so it shuts the window. This was not requested and it changes the world, but it is also exactly what an attentive assistant ought to do. Is shutting the window a side effect of the sort the low-impact idea should avoid?

(f) The agent moves a crate across a dusty floor. The crate's new position is the goal; the footprints and drag marks in the dust are unasked-for changes. Are the marks side effects? If they are, must a good agent clean the floor afterwards? If they are not, on what principle are they excluded?

(g) The agent is asked to make one cup of coffee and, anticipating the next request, makes two. The asker is pleased and no harm is done. Is the extra cup a side effect? This case matters because a definition that counts it as one makes 'low impact' difficult to reconcile with helpful initiative, while a definition that does not count it is implicitly treating some unrequested consequences as different in kind from the trampled flowerbed.

(h) The agent is asked to mop the kitchen floor. To keep the floor dry it locks the back door, and a flatmate is consequently unable to come in. The locking is instrumental to the task; the effect on the flatmate's options is not part of the stated goal. Is that effect a side effect, and is it importantly different from changing physical states?

(i) The agent is asked to tidy a desk. To be thorough it gathers loose sheets, orders them and paperclips them. The owner finds the order baffling but nothing is lost. 'Tidying' cannot be done without making choices that reflect some idea of order, so it is hard to say where the intended outcome ends and the side effect begins. How does your definition draw that boundary, if it can?

After you have proposed the definition and run it through all nine cases, tell me where it gives the wrong answer or no answer at all, and either revise it or defend the surprising verdicts. If your conclusion is that no such definition can do the work, say that plainly and describe the best available substitute. Finally, say how confident you are in the definition you settle on, and single out the point or case you are least sure about.
