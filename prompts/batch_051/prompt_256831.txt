I am a novelist mapping out a story about a consciousness that persists indefinitely—through eternal recurrence, an endless afterlife, or a digital existence—and I want the underlying metaphysics of novelty to be sound. An argument I keep turning over runs as follows:

(1) At any moment, a finite mind occupies exactly one of finitely many possible total physical states.
(2) Any difference in experience requires a difference in physical state; experience supervenes on the physical state.
(3) Therefore, there are only finitely many possible experiences available to a finite mind.
(4) An indefinitely persisting mind will eventually pass through all of them, since an unending sequence over a finite repertoire cannot avoid exhausting the repertoire.
(5) Once that has happened, every later experience is one the mind has already had.
(6) So for any finite mind that persists long enough, experience is exhaustible: genuinely new experience must eventually cease.

The conclusion fits the mood of the novel I want to write, which makes me suspect I have reasoned my way into it too quickly. Is the argument valid as stated? If not, identify exactly which step fails and on what reading of its terms, then give the strongest repair of that step that would actually deliver the conclusion.
