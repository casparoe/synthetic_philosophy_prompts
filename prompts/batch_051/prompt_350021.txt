I'm a PhD student in political theory, and in a fortnight I'm presenting at a departmental seminar that mixes political philosophers with international lawyers. My paper defends a remedial-only account of the right to secede, but with a specific twist: I argue the bar is usually set too high, and that a sustained, deliberate refusal to let a group govern itself in the areas it was promised, together with a refusal to recognise it as a political people rather than a private cultural association, can count as the serious injustice that grounds a secession right even in a broadly democratic state and even without mass atrocities. On my test, Scotland and Catalonia wouldn't currently have the right, but a state that formally entrenched a devolved settlement, promised it was permanent, and then gutted it would be much closer to the line. I talked this over with a friend from Edinburgh the other night and she said I'd basically made the threshold so flexible that any well-organised nationalist movement could walk through it, which I think is wrong but I couldn't pin down exactly where.

The crowd matters here. Two or three people in the room hold a primary, plebiscitary position: any distinct, territorially concentrated political community that wins a fair independence referendum gets the right, full stop. There's an international lawyer who thinks the whole discussion floats free of the law, since international law itself only ever really recognised a remedial, decolonisation-flavoured self-determination and treats moral talk about secession as a category mistake. And there's a Quebec specialist who has heard every Scotland/Catalonia analogy before and visibly checks out the moment either is mentioned. Then a handful of agnostics. I need a prep sheet, not a polished defence: I want the objections in the order they'll actually come, phrased as those people would phrase them, with how damaging each one is, what I should concede on the spot, what I should hold, and where the reply goes soft. Please also include the question I'm hoping nobody asks, and a closing note on what would change my mind: which objection, if it really landed, should push me to abandon or seriously revise the thesis rather than patch it.

Sections, in order: (1) "The objections, in order" — for each one give "How they'll put it" (a sentence or two in their voice), "Damage" (out of 10), "What to concede", "Reply" (roughly two minutes' worth), and "Soft spot". (2) "The question I'm hoping nobody asks" — half a page. (3) "What would change my mind" — a solid paragraph.
