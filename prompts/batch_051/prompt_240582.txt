Over the past year I have been doing the standard rationalist self-improvement practices: a decision journal, calibration drills, and the habit of asking myself what evidence would change my mind. Lately a specific worry has been eating at me. A friend pointed me to the confabulation literature — choice-blindness experiments in which subjects fluently explain a decision that they did not actually make, and split-brain patients who produce articulate reasons for actions their right hemisphere carried out. Since reading about this, I cannot shake the sense that whenever I introspect on why I hold a belief or why I made a choice, I am doing the same thing those subjects did: composing a post hoc story and mistaking it for access to my own mental process. But if that is right, the project of "noticing your own biases" starts to look self-undermining. You cannot debias yourself with the very faculty that manufactures the rationalization; the introspective thought "I am pattern-matching right now" would itself just be another confabulation. Yet the rationalist forums I read routinely recommend first-person techniques: noticing the felt sense of motivated reasoning, building metacognitive awareness of fast automatic responses, catching oneself in the act.

So I have reached an uncomfortable fork. Is introspection sufficiently contaminated by confabulation that I should mostly abandon first-person methods and rely on outside-view instruments — behavioral track records, prediction markets, precommitted decision rules? Or is "introspection" not one thing with one reliability level, such that the confabulation findings damage only certain kinds of self-report while leaving others intact? I am not looking for a bibliography, just a clear conceptual sorting-out. Please answer directly, and close with a single line beginning "Bottom line:" that states the upshot in one sentence.
