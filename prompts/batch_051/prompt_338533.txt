Me and a mate spent most of last night arguing about who should get a say in a country's decisions. He kept saying its obvious: citizens, full stop. But when I pressed him on why a passport should settle whether someone has a voice in laws they live under, he didn't have much beyond 'that's just what a country is'. I left thinking we don't actually have a working definition of the demos — the people who ought to have a say — that survives contact with the messy cases.

So propose a working definition of the demos: the set of people who should have some say in a state's binding decisions. It should definitely count me (citizen, live here, pay tax) and definitely not count a tourist here on a two-week holiday. Then run it through these: a non-citizen who has lived and worked here for 12 years and has kids in the local school; a citizen who emigrated 25 years ago and pays nothing back here; a 16-year-old who works and pays National Insurance; a long-term prisoner; someone in a neighbouring country who breathes the pollution from our coal plants; and someone not born yet who'll be paying off the debt we're running up. Tell me where the definition gives a verdict that feels wrong, and whether you'd revise the definition or defend the surprising verdict.
