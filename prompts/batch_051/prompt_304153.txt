I am a professor of philosophy with a research background in the philosophy of biology, and I serve on the standing biosignature-review panel of an astrobiology consortium. Several times a year the panel receives a dossier claiming that a newly detected system is, or may be, life: a possible extraterrestrial microbe, a prebiotic protocell from a laboratory origin-of-life study, a virus-like particle from a returned sample, or something similar. The panel must place each case in one of four categories: life, candidate life-detection, life-like but non-living process, or non-living chemistry. Our findings are used in press releases and in recommendations about which investigations to fund, so both kinds of error are costly. An overeager finding of life would damage the field's credibility; an overly dismissive finding could quietly kill a promising line of research. The other members of the panel are geochemists, microbiologists, and instrument engineers. They are skilled at interpreting data, but they have no background in the definition-of-life debate, and our decisions currently vary from meeting to meeting depending on which criterion happens to dominate the room.

Please write a reusable decision procedure that the panel could run in a single one-day meeting. Begin by saying what the procedure cannot do, so we do not mistake it for a solution to the definition of life itself. Then translate the principal criteria in the philosophical and scientific debates into an ordered sequence of questions a non-philosopher can apply: boundedness; self-maintenance or metabolism; reproduction with heritable variation; and evolution by natural selection. For each question, specify what counts as a sufficient answer, where the procedure stops with a classification rather than moving on, and where it must hand the case to a human decision because the remaining question is conceptual or evaluative rather than empirical. Be explicit about whether these criteria are being treated as a definition of life, as diagnostic symptoms of a definition, or as family-resemblance criteria with no single essence or necessary-and-sufficient condition, and mark which distinctions cannot survive the simplification and where the procedure will predictably misfire.

Then run your procedure on these three cases: (1) a lipid vesicle from an Enceladus-analog sample that maintains an internal pH gradient and grows by taking up lipids from the medium, but shows no heritable variation or replication; (2) a self-replicating RNA system from a hydrothermal simulation that undergoes selection for faster replication but depends on a continuous external supply of activated nucleotides it cannot synthesize; and (3) a virus-like particle that carries genes and evolves under selection but can reproduce only inside a host cell and has no independent metabolism. For each case, state the classification the procedure yields and flag any step at which the procedure forces a result the panel is likely to dislike, explaining whether that marks a defect in the procedure or a reason to accept the result.
