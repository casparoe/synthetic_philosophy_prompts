I am preparing a short position statement for a graduate seminar on animal minds and moral status, and I have just come out of a long argument with a friend about insects. My friend, who is more skeptical of insect moral status than I am, pressed me on Darwin. He quoted from the preface to the second edition of the *Descent of Man*, where Darwin writes:

“I may take this opportunity of remarking that my critics frequently assume that I attribute all changes of corporeal structure and mental power exclusively to the natural selection of such variations as are often called spontaneous; whereas, even in the first edition of the ‘Origin of Species,’ I distinctly stated that great weight must be attributed to the inherited effects of use and disuse, with respect both to the body and mind.”

My friend took the passage to show that Darwin himself thought mental powers arise through several distinct mechanisms—including the inherited effects of use and disuse and what he calls “correlated” growth—so one cannot move straightforwardly from evolutionary continuity to the conclusion that insect minds are sentient in any morally significant way. I suspect he is overreading the passage, but it made me wonder whether my own position hangs together. You are a professional philosopher working in philosophy of mind and animal ethics, and you know Darwin’s text well. Please think carefully: before giving your audit, spend a paragraph or two laying out how each commitment below could be given its most charitable precisification, then identify which commitments are in genuine tension, which only look as if they are in tension, and rank the genuine tensions by severity, saying in each case what would have to give and what the cheapest revision would be.

Here is where I have landed:

1. Sentience is a matter of degree, not a binary property; organisms can have more or less of it, and having less does not mean having none.
2. Honeybees and ants have weak but nonzero sentience; their behavioral flexibility, learning, and nociceptive responses are enough to warrant that attribution, even if their subjective lives are nothing like ours.
3. Moral status tracks degree of sentience, but not linearly: very weak interests can be outweighed by almost any human convenience, though not by the mere fact that the being is small or alien.
4. A thermostat or a current large language model has zero sentience, despite exhibiting goal-directed processing and being able to optimize for outcomes.
5. If Darwin is right in the passage above, then evolutionary continuity and the plurality of mechanisms by which mental power develops leave substantial uncertainty about exactly where sentience begins; this uncertainty should incline us toward a precautionary attitude toward insects.
6. But precaution has limits: for insects, minor human convenience can justify harms only when the harms are small, diffuse, and not reasonably avoidable at low cost.

Go through these carefully and tell me which single revision would clean up the most tension, and which tension is the deepest.
