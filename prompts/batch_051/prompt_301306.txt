You're a philosopher of mathematics who also designs interactive teaching games, and I want you to think carefully before committing: start by brainstorming the mechanics, then lock in the schedule. If some part of my sketched design seems off, change it.

I'm teaching an upper-level seminar called "What counts as a legitimate mathematical object?" Fourteen students, mixed math and philosophy majors, 90 minutes. The running theme is that "constructible" and even "legitimate" are relative to an allowed toolkit, and that the old Greek disputes about what it means to solve a problem never went away — they just moved: compass-and-straightedge exactness, then approximation, then transcendental curves being admitted by some and not others, then computational methods. I want a runnable 90-minute activity where the students feel the force of that idea before I name it in lecture.

Make it a game with real rules, roles, and point incentives, not a discussion with props. Here's a rough skeleton: split the room into four or five "schools of construction," each with a license box and a different physical toolkit. School A gets a straightedge and compass. School B gets the same plus a physical angle trisector, a tool that can divide any angle into three equal parts. School C gets a marked straightedge plus a compass and is allowed the neusis move: slide the marked ruler until it passes through a given point and lands on two given lines at the marked distance apart. School D only gets paper and a folding protocol, with folds as moves; the wrinkle is that origami folding can genuinely do some things compass and straightedge cannot, like trisect an angle and double a cube, so that school isnt just doing approximations. Maybe a fifth school gets a "limit machine" allowance: they can specify an infinite sequence of compass–straightedge constructions and count its imagined endpoint as the result, which quietly lets them reach numbers no finite compass-and-straightedge construction could ever produce, and should force a fight about whether the limit is the same kind of object at all. Adjust the schools if you like, but keep the asymmetry real.

All schools get the same task list: a few easy warm-ups (bisect an angle, square on a given segment, a regular hexagon), then the three classical problems — trisect a given 60° angle so nobody games it by trisecting a right angle, double the cube, square the circle — plus two or three extra tasks you choose so they are trivial for some licenses and impossible for the straightedge-and-compass school. A regular heptagon is a good one, since compass and straightedge cannot do it while the origami school can. Points for each completed construction. A "Council of Construction" rotates every 20 minutes and audits results: anyone can challenge another group's construction as illegitimate, the council rules by majority after a short statement of reasons, and points get clawed back if a construction is ruled illegitimate. Add a purity bonus: more points for getting a result with a weaker license. Crucially, no one — including the facilitator — announces in advance which tasks are impossible under which license. The straightedge-and-compass group should hit the wall and start suspecting that its failure isnt incompetence but impossibility.

Write up the full activity for me: a materials list, the exact task list with point values, the rule sheet for each school's license, the audit procedure with examples of what counts as a legitimate challenge, and a 90-minute timeline. Then give me the debrief mapped to the concepts: tool-relative legitimacy, exact versus approximate, the difference between "we cant do it" and "it's impossible in this toolkit," and how enlarging the toolkit can change what the problem means rather than just making the old problem easier. End by telling me what will predictably go wrong when this is run and how to handle it — groups getting stuck, the council turning into an endless debate, someone smuggling in an unlicensed move, the origami group breaking the whole economy. And be honest about which feature of the idea the activity cannot reproduce. My guess is it can't reproduce the actual 19th-century impossibility proofs, so the facilitator has to supply those after the students have become hungry for them; say plainly what it can and cant do.
