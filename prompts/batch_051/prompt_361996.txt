I'm putting together a handout for a small philosophy of technology class (a mix of undergrads and postgrads) on a question that keeps coming up: if we ever build AI systems that are much better than humans at changing minds, how should those systems be allowed to talk to us? The group keeps argueing past each other, so I want a sorting instrument — a decision tree, or a short quiz with a scoring key if you think that works better — that starts from people's reactions to concrete cases and leads to a named position. The positions I definitely want on there, described neutrally, are something like: (i) a strong anti-persuasion view where the system may answer questions but shouldn't try to move people; (ii) a truth-plus-no-exploitation view where honest argument is fine but tailoring to a person's known weaknesses is not; (iii) an autonomy-respecting persuasion view where the system may try to bring people round to their own considered values, including through Socratic questioning; (iv) a paternalist view where it may persuade people for their own good even against their current preferences; and (v) a liberal view where any truthful persuasion short of fraud is permissible because competent adults own their choices. Feel free to add another leaf or two if there's a consistent combination I've missed, and mark combinations nobody defends as 'unnamed' with a sentence saying whether the view looks incoherent, merely unattractive, or just unexplored.

Please build the tree from the logical space rather than starting from the labels, and phrase every branch node as a question someone could answer without knowing which position it leads to. I'd like the cases to be where the disagreement actually bites: an AI assistant that knows mentioning a patient's daughter would make them quit smoking whilst neutral health information wouldn't; a commercial assistant that could be superhumanly good at closing sales with micro-tailored framing; an AI that knows a user holds a politically important factual mistake and is considering a Socratic campaign to loosen it; an AI that nudges a depressed person to see a doctor; an AI that tailors a pitch to a gambling addict's triggers; and maybe an AI talking to a child. Each node should force a trade-off rather than just asking 'how much do you dislike persuasion'. Also, if two leaves can't be seperated by the tree, say so rather than papering over it. Keep it to about two pages, and describe the leaves with phrases a student would recognise rather than only the short labels I gave you.
