I have been circling an old problem about promising, and I would like a careful negotiation rather than a settlement from on high. Please adopt the role of a professional moral philosopher, take the question slowly, and begin by spelling out the strongest version of each side in your own words before you compare them; resist the urge to declare a winner early.

On reflection I find a broadly expectation-based account of promissory obligation compelling. On this view, the wrong in breaking a promise is not an exercise of a mysterious normative power by which saying certain words binds the will, but a more familiar wrong: a promise knowingly invites another person to form expectations about what I will do, expectations that enter into their planning, their sense of security, and their understanding of how their future will go, and breaking the promise lets them down through the very expectations I cultivated. The account has genuine systematic virtues. It makes promissory obligation continuous with our duties not to mislead, manipulate, or exploit; it explains why promises function as instruments of assurance; it explains why an insincere promise can still obligate, because the relevant expectations are created regardless of my private state of mind at the time; it explains why a promise extracted by coercion need not bind, since what is extracted under duress does not ground the relevant sort of expectation; and it explains why a promisee may release me from a promise, because the release withdraws the expectation on which the obligation rested. That is a lot of explanatory work done without needing to postulate a special annexe to morality.

And yet. Suppose a dying friend asks me to promise to destroy a set of private letters without reading them, and I promise with every intention of complying. No one else knows the letters exist; the friend will not know whether I keep the promise; and no third party can be harmed, disappointed, or exposed if I break it. Later I read and keep the letters. The expectation-based view seems forced to say that by then there is no living subject of the expectation and no one whose plans I have upset, so whatever self-reproach I feel, I have not wronged anyone. I cannot make myself believe that. It seems to me that I would be wronging my friend, not merely failing to honour a useful convention or to live up to my own self-image. I recognise that a defender of the theory might reply that this intuition is grief, or socialisation, or an overgeneralisation of ordinary cases dressed up as moral insight, and I do not want to ignore that possibility.

I want you to help me determine which side, if either, should give way, and what each concession would cost. More precisely: what would a successful debunking of the deathbed intuition need to explain about it, and what would it show; what would amending or abandoning the expectation-based account require, and would the amended account still earn the assurance-based explanation that made it attractive; and is there a reading of 'binding' or 'wronging' on which the theory and the intuition are not, after all, in conflict? Reason outward from the case and from the structure of the views themselves rather than from any scholarly apparatus. Please write your answer in American English.
