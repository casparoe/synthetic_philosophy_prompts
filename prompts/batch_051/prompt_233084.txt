I've been defending a mechanistic account of biological explanation in a philosophy of science seminar, and I want to stress-test it rather than just collect rival labels. The view I find myself committed to is roughly this: explaining a biological phenomenon is a matter of describing the mechanism that produces it, where a mechanism is a set of entities and activities organized in space and time such that their orchestrated operation gives rise to the phenomenon. On this picture, explanations that leave out the relevant entities and activities, or that merely state mathematical regularities, are at best incomplete and often not explanatory until they are anchored in a mechanism.

Lately, however, I have been unsettled by examples from systems and theoretical biology that do not obviously fit: explanations that appeal to network topology, robustness, bistability, optimality, thermodynamic constraints, or abstract mathematical structure, and that seem to predict or unify phenomena without identifying the parts and operations that produce them. My question is not whether rival accounts exist, but what would actually count against the mechanistic thesis if I treat it as a substantive claim rather than a tautology. Which actual or possible scientific developments would force a defender of the entities-and-activities account to abandon it, and which would merely be awkward? I would like the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely uncomfortable, with a note on which ones a committed mechanist should concede as genuine counterpressure, which ones they can responsibly redescribe in mechanistic terms, and what evidence or argument would settle each case. In particular, I want to know whether the view has been so qualified that it risks nothing, and if so, whether that insulation is itself a reason to reject it.
