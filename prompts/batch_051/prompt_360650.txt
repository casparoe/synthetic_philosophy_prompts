I'm preparing a unit for a first-year philosophy of mind course on the problem of other minds, and I want to pair a close reading of Descartes's Second Meditation with a screening of Peter Weir's The Truman Show. Early in the meditation, having subjected everything to hyperbolic doubt, Descartes writes that "I am, I exist, is necessarily true each time it is expressed by me, or conceived in my mind"; later he looks out his window and observes that what is strictly available to sight is just hats and coats moving along, which might cover specters or automata, and that it is a judgment rather than the eyes that tells him these are people. I'm struck by the asymmetry the film inherits from these two moments: Truman Burbank's own experience is indisputably real to him, while everyone else in his world really is playing a part—they are, in effect, automata hidden under the everyday appearance of friends and strangers.

I want a critical reading of the film through this Cartesian problem of other minds. Where does the film genuinely dramatize the idea that other minds are reached by judgment or projection rather than directly perceived? Where does it push back against the Cartesian worry—for example, by showing that Truman's error is not excessive doubt but a failure to attend to the many clues around him? And does the film treat his emerging suspicion that he is the only real person as a rational inference from evidence, or as something closer to a paranoid break that the narrative happens to vindicate? The reading should stay faithful in both directions: do not reduce the film to an illustration of Descartes, and do not turn Descartes into a generic everything-is-a-simulation thesis. Anchor the reading in specific scenes, but avoid plot summary. End by saying what the film adds to the philosophical debate that the argument alone cannot—perhaps something about the moral weight of continuing to treat others as full persons when their inner lives are not given, or about the difference between philosophical skepticism and the mediated world the film depicts.
