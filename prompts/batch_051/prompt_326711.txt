A weird argument broke out at lunch between two people on my team, and I keep turning it over. Neither of them would budge, so I'm hoping someone can go through it point by point and rule on the actual claims.

Kyle had this toy case. Two roommates, Reed and Imani, own one guitar. Reed hates the noise: his ideal is nobody plays, but if somebody has to play, he'd rather it be him, because then he can keep it short and isnt stuck listening to Imani. Imani loves the thing: best is hearing Reed, next best is playing it herself, worst is nobody playing. So there are three states (ignore the both-play case): N = nobody, R = Reed plays, I = Imani plays. Reed's ranking: N > R > I. Imani's ranking: R > I > N.

Kyle said this simple setup breaks a pair of rules everyone seems to accept. Rule 1, unanimity: if literally everyone prefers one state to another, that one should win. Rule 2, self-determination: people get to decide what they themselves do. But the rules collide. Unanimity says R beats I, since both rank R above I. Self-determination says N beats R, because whether Reed plays is Reed's call and he ranks N above R. It also says I beats N, because whether Imani plays is Imani's call and she ranks I above N. Now the ranking cycles: R beats I, I beats N, N beats R. Kyle's conclusion was that the two rules cant both be right, and one of them has to give.

Nadia said the toy case is rigged. The whole problem comes from nosy preferences, like Reed ranking R over I because he'd be annoyed to hear Imani play. A real principle of self-determination should only protect preferences about your own life, not preferences about what someone else does. If you screen those out, the cycle goes away.

Kyle came back that the nosy stuff only shows up in the unanimity step, R over I. The self-determination steps use Reed's N over R, which is just "I'd rather not play," and Imani's I over N, which is just "I'd rather play than not." So Nadia is stuck rejecting unanimity, not self-determination. And if unanimity only counts when everyone's reasons are pure, then it barely counts anywhere, because people's actual preferences are full of other-regarding stuff. He asked her for a principled line that keeps unanimity in ordinary cases but excludes this one, and he said she never gave one.

Nadia said that's looking at it wrong. Rights are not preferences, and freedom is not a ranking you plug into a big social ordering. A right is a constraint on what can be imposed in the first place. You don't first rank N, R, and I by people's wants and then apply freedom as a filter. You rule out forcing Reed to play if he declines, and you rule out stopping Imani if she wants to. So there is no single global ranking that has to be consistent, hence no paradox. She admitted she didn't have a clean rule for which constraints count, but said every theory has that problem.

At that point they started going in circles about whether unanimity was even a good rule and I lost the thread. Please adjudicate. Go through their actual claims in order and say for each whether you buy it, buy it with qualifications, or think it's wrong, and give an overall verdict on who is more right. Also tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing.
