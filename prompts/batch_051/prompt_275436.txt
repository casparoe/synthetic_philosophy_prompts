I've been trying to isolate the strategic structure underneath Marx's claim that competition compels capitalist firms to adopt labor-saving machinery even when doing so is collectively self-defeating. Suppose we model an industry as a multiplayer prisoner's dilemma. There are many firms, N, and each must choose once between two strategies: Automate (install labor-saving machinery) and Hold Back (continue with the current, labor-intensive method). For any given number of rivals who Automate, a firm earns a strictly higher payoff by Automating than by Holding Back, but every firm's payoff decreases as more rivals Automate. In the two corner cases: if a firm Automates while all or most of its rivals Hold Back, it underprices them and captures market share, earning the temptation payoff T, the best individual outcome. If all Hold Back, each enjoys a comfortable and stable rate of profit, the reward payoff R. If all Automate, the technique becomes the industry norm, capital costs rise, and price competition leaves every firm worse off than under universal restraint, the punishment payoff P. If one firm Holds Back while the others Automate, it cannot compete on cost and earns the sucker payoff S. With T > R > P > S, Automate is a dominant strategy, and the unique Nash equilibrium is Pareto-inferior to universal Hold Back.

Marx's account of machinery and the falling rate of profit seems to have roughly this shape: the first capitalist to introduce a new machine pockets an extra profit, but once rivals copy it the temporary advantage disappears, the ratio of outlay on machinery and materials to outlay on wages rises, and the general rate of profit tends to fall. No individual capitalist can opt out of the race without being undersold.

I want to know how far that analogy can be pushed, and where it breaks. Work through what the standard analysis says when the classic two-person, one-shot prisoner's dilemma is modified along three dimensions at once: the number of players becomes large; the interaction is repeated indefinitely among the same firms; and the set of players is open to exit and entry, so firms that try to sustain restraint can be displaced by entrants willing to Automate from the outset. In particular, would the folk theorem imply that sufficiently patient firms could sustain the Hold Back outcome through tacit collusion or reputation, thereby blocking the tendency Marx describes, or would free entry, imperfect monitoring, and replacement by aggressive entrants destroy those equilibria? And does the analogy stop applying once the number of Automating firms changes the general rate of profit itself, rather than merely redistributing a fixed surplus among players?
