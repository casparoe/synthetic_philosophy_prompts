I'm a maths PhD student who got roped into teaching a one-off fifty-minute session for first-year undergrads (mostly maths, a few philosophy) on the question of whether 2 is an element of 4. I know it sounds like a nonsense question; my plan is to use it as a trapdoor into mathematical structuralism without naming structuralism until the last ten minutes. The students know basic set notation and can follow a definition, but they've never seen the finite ordinal constructions, so I can introduce just enough set theory. My current hunch is to show that on the von Neumann construction 2 = {0,1} and 4 = {0,1,2,3}, so 2 ∈ 4 comes out literally true, while on a Zermelo-style construction it comes out false; then the real point is that neither answer is wrong, and the fact that membership facts flip when you switch between two good reductions is the problem. I tried it on a housemate and he just kept asking yes but which sets are 2 and 4 really, so I'm expecting that too.

But I don't want it to become a set theory lecture, and I'm not sure what order to put the pieces in. Plan the teaching sequence for me, not the explanation itself: what idea has to be in place before the question bites; what one running example should carry the whole session and what features it has to have; which misconceptions to expect at each stage — my guesses are it's just a convention, obviously no because 2 is smaller than 4, and which set is 2 really — and where exactly in the sequence to head each one off; what to leave out at this level and say so; and one final question or exercise I can use to tell whether they've actually made the structuralist move rather than just repeating a slogan.
