So a bunch of engineers I work with keep saying that choosing an electoral system is basically choosing a codec. Votes in, seats out. First-past-the-post is lossy compression with gross artifacts: shift turnout a few points and the seat totals swing wildly, and a party can get 20% of the vote and end up with no seats at all, like a codec that just deletes a whole frequency band. Proportional representation, they say, is near-lossless: the seat totals reconstruct the vote shares with only a little quantization error from rounding to whole seats. And from this they conclude PR is obviously better the way FLAC is better then a 96 kbps mp3, and that any gap between vote share and seat share is a defect in the system, not a design choice.

I'm trying to figure out how far this actually holds. Give me the correspondence table: what plays the role of the source signal, the encoder, the reconstructed signal, the loss function, the artifacts. Then the places the mapping breaks. My main suspicions: is there even a fixed "signal" sitting out there before the system encodes it, or does the system itself change what gets measured (tactical voting, two parties absorbing everything, people staying home)? And is reconstruction fidelity the metric a voting system should optimize, or does that quietly import that a parliament is just a faithful playback of voter preferences, when it also has to form governments, represent places, and actually take decisions? Then tell me whether their conclusion—PR is strictly better and any disproportionality is a bug—survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was doing all the work. If the codec picture is bad, is there a better one from signal processing or CS that actually fits?
