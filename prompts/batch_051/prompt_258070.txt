Ian Hacking's looping-effect argument says that classifying people in the human sciences is unlike classifying things in the natural sciences: once a psychiatric label is in circulation, the people it labels become aware of it, reshape their self-understanding and behavior around it, and thereby change the very kind the label was meant to capture, so the classification must be revised in turn. Now suppose a large and growing share of this process runs through machine-learning systems rather than clinicians applying a diagnostic manual. Recommender systems surface disorder-related content to users who would not otherwise have encountered it; users adopt the vocabulary and perform the category in the posts and searches they leave behind, which become training data for the next generation of models; those models then mirror or refine the label back to new users in symptom checkers, therapy apps, and chatbots. There is no single authority updating the category against fixed criteria, and the classifier is trained partly on the very population it classifies. Does Hacking's argument survive this development, or is one of its premises undercut? Say which premise the change bears on, whether the looping effect still occurs and whether it is accelerated, dampened, or fundamentally altered, and what a repaired version of the argument would claim. Also tell me what would change your verdict: which fact about these systems, if it turned out otherwise, would reverse your assessment, and where is your reasoning most likely to fail?
