Having spent my career in physiology, I have only recently begun reading the early modern philosophers carefully, and I find myself stuck on a point in Part V of Descartes’s *Discourse on Method*. Descartes argues there that the fact that some animals, such as magpies and parrots, can produce words without being able to speak as we do shows that beasts possess no reason. My difficulty is that I cannot tell exactly what this is supposed to rule out. If a sheepdog responds to a shepherd’s whistled commands by executing the right maneuver, or if a rat that has been shocked in one chamber freezes upon entering it again, does Descartes’s criterion commit him to saying that there is nothing within the animal corresponding even to sensation or memory—that it is, as the clockwork image suggests, wholly devoid of any inner state? Or does his argument only exclude a certain kind of reflective, propositionally articulated thought while leaving room for feeling, associative memory, and nonlinguistic intelligence? I suspect I have been reading him more crudely than his own position requires, but I am not sure where the distinction should be drawn. Please say how confident you are in your reading and single out the point you are least sure of.
