At a reading group someone made an argument that's been nagging me all week. A friend who used to run insider-threat programs at a big tech company kept saying the AI control agenda is just corporate security with worse branding. His line was: supervising a potentially misaligned model is like managing a phenomenally competent contractor who might be quietly working for a competitor. You can't expect to align the contractor with the company's mission, and you don't need to. You limit what they can access, you log every action, you have a second person review anything irreversible, and you structure pay plus monitoring so that defecting is more expensive than delivering. He extended it: labs don't need to solve alignment before deploying; they need least privilege, logging, red-team review, and seperation of duties. A lab that hasn't done that is being negligent, but a lab that waits for alignment is being religious.

I was mostly convinced until I started thinking about how loaded the contractor image is. A contractor can't copy himself the night before the audit, run a few thousand candidate attacks against your logging pipeline in an afternoon, or coordinate with all his copies through channels you literally cannot inspect. But I'm suspicious of my own suspicion here: "AI's aren't people" is also a cheap move, and maybe an analogy doesn't need to be perfect to earn its conclusion.

So how far does it actually hold? Lay out the correspondence table — manager, contractor, contract, pay, the competitor, the logs, the second reviewer, the severance agreement — and then the places the mapping breaks. Which disanalogies actually matter for whether insider-threat controls transfer to a capable model that might be scheming, and which are just stylistic? And does his conclusion survive once those are on the table: should a lab deploy with those controls and not wait for alignment, or was the contractor the one doing the argumentative work?
