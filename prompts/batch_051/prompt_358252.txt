I'm a journalist covering the ideas beat, working on a piece about the old intuition that pain and pleasure are asymmetric — that pain is something real and positive that happens to you, while pleasure is mostly just pain leaving or a need being satisfied. I'd like someone to pressure-test that intuition against a variant of a standard thought experiment, and to show their work.

First, the springboard. In Arthur Schopenhauer's essay "On the Sufferings of the World," from his Studies in Pessimism, he writes:

"It is the good which is negative; in other words, happiness and satisfaction always imply some desire fulfilled, some state of pain brought to an end."

Now the thought experiment. Nozick's experience machine, you'll remember: a device that feeds your brain a lifetime of whatever experiences you want while you float in a tank. Nozick's claim was that many of us still wouldn't plug in, because we want to actually do things, to actually be a certain kind of person, and to be in touch with a reality that isn't man-made.

Here's the variant I want worked through. Instead of a pleasure machine, suppose it's a quiet machine. It can keep you in a clearheaded waking state with exactly zero felt suffering — no pain, no fear, no grief, no boredom, no unsatisfied craving — but the only way it can get you there is by flattening every felt positive state too: no pleasure, no desire, no excitement, no striving. The state is untroubled but colorless. Real life, for the person choosing, has the usual mix; to keep things concrete, say real life comes out at about +80 on a pleasure scale and −20 on a pain scale, while the quiet machine sits at 0 and 0. My question is whether the standard anti-machine case survives this change. Does someone who refuses the original pleasure machine have the same reason to refuse the quiet machine, or does Schopenhauer's claim that the good is negative change which reasons carry the weight? Please work through what these three views would say: a classical hedonistic utilitarian, a suffering-focused (negative) utilitarian who treats avoiding pain as lexically prior — in other words, no amount of pleasure can buy off a given amount of pain — and a Nozick-style defender of real over simulated life. Then tell me where the three views come apart, and whether there's any way to make them converge.

One last ask, since I have to file something accurate. Please seperate what's actually settled here from what's genuinely contested among people who know the area, and don't present one camp's contested position as the consensus.
