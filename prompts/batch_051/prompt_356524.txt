I am drafting a novel in which a senior statesman must decide whether to authorize the use of a new weapon against a city, knowing that it will kill tens of thousands of noncombatants but believing that it will end the war sooner and thereby save more lives overall. I want this character to reason honestly rather than serve as a mouthpiece for either jingoism or pacifism. The character keeps returning to G. E. M. Anscombe's pamphlet Mr Truman's Degree, and in particular to her claim that deliberately killing the innocent as a means to one's ends collapses whatever principled difference there is between war and murder. Before I write the argument scenes, I would like the full specification of what a moral theory of killing noncombatants in war must get right: which cases it must handle, which distinctions it cannot collapse, which facts about deliberation under conditions of war it cannot contradict. For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are particular commitments of Anscombe's own absolutism or religious outlook. Then tell me which single requirement knocks out the most candidate theories, and whether any pair of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable. A brief indication of how a consequentialist, a double-effect theorist, and a pure just-war theorist would do against the list would help me calibrate the protagonist's reasoning, but keep the weight on the criteria themselves.
