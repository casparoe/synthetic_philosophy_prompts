My cousin's a philosophy major and we've been arguing for months about whether compatriots have special claims on each other. I started writing down her verdicts on cases:

- She said if she could only pull one person out of a burning car, her immigrant neighbor or a random American stranger, she'd pull the neighbor, not even close.
- She defended a hospital policy giving the last ventilator to a citizen over a non-citizen permanent resident with the same survival odds; she didn't think it was cruel, she said the state can stick up for its own members when it's running something like that.
- She gives to a malaria charity abroad instead of a less effective local one, because the lives saved per dollar are way higher, and she said a gap that big swamps partiality.
- She said if her best friend and a stranger both needed a dose of some medicine she happened to have, and both would benefit equally, she'd give it to her friend and not feel bad.

New case: we're abroad and come across a crash. Two other tourists are badly hurt, one American (like us) and one not; we've never met either of them. There's one spot left in an ambulance, a medic asks us to choose, and both will die without it with equal odds.

Predict what she says. Is she running something like "the state may be partial but individuals may not," or "compatriots always have some special claim," or what? Which old case is the closest analogy, and what further case would settle which principal she's actually using?
