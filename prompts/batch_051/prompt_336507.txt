I have been working through Sidgwick's dualism of practical reason, and I find myself wanting a verdict on whether a particular recent development breaks the argument, rather than a summary of it. As I understand him, Sidgwick held that practical reason seems to yield two ultimate principles that can come apart. One is rational egoism: it is rational for each person to aim at the greatest balance of their own good. The other is universal benevolence, which might be thought of as a requirement to aim at the good of all sentient beings, taken impartially. Sidgwick thought that each principle, when subjected to the strictest intuitive scrutiny, appears self-evident; that neither can be derived from the other; and that no further principle is available to adjudicate conflicts between them. The result is the dualism: practical reason is left in a kind of permanent tension between the personal and the impartial point of view.

Now bring in a development from the last few decades that Sidgwick did not have available: evolutionary explanations of our moral and prudential intuitions. On reflection, the two poles of the dualism do not look equally vulnerable to this kind of explanation. The strong intuition that one's own interests, and the interests of close kin, have a special weight is exactly the sort of disposition one would expect natural selection to install, since it tracks biological fitness. The intuition that the good of all sentient beings makes an equal claim on one's action is much harder to explain in the same reductive way; it looks more like an expansion or correction of parochial intuition than a straightforward adaptive product. A debunking line of argument therefore suggests that the apparent self-evidence of rational egoism is suspect, while the apparent self-evidence of universal benevolence may survive the same scrutiny, which would break the symmetry on which the dualism rests.

My question is whether Sidgwick's dualism survives this development, and I would like the answer to operate at the level of the argument rather than its conclusions. Exactly which premise or methodological commitment is under pressure: his test for self-evidence, the assumed parity between the two intuitions, or the claim that debunking explanations are normatively irrelevant? If the dualism collapses, what takes its place: full impartialism, the demotion of egoism to a powerful but non-rational motive, or a repaired dualism of some other sort? If it does not collapse, show precisely why an evolutionary origin story for an intuition fails to undermine its authority in practical reasoning, and whether the argument can be restored in some weaker form.
