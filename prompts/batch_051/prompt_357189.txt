I spent most of my career as an experimental scientist, and since retiring I have been reading my way through the foundations of economics. One question I keep returning to concerns what exactly the utility functions of revealed-preference theory are about. On one reading, a utility function fitted to an agent's choices is like temperature or mass: an unobservable magnitude that we measure, however imperfectly, through one of its observable effects, namely choice. On the other reading, it is closer to bookkeeping: once we have established that the agent's choices satisfy the relevant consistency conditions, saying that the agent maximizes a utility function adds nothing but a compact re-description of the choices themselves.

Consider the following claim, which I have encountered in both forms and which I would like to put under empirical pressure:

The utility reconstructed from choice behavior is a real, stable property of the choosing agent, one that exists independently of the particular consistency axioms we happen to impose, and not merely an artifact of those axioms.

Design the strongest concrete test of this claim that you can, spelled out operationally. I want the test to be one that could genuinely separate the two readings rather than re-run their definitions: specify what would be measured, in what contexts, with what kind of agent or system, and what pattern in the data would count for and against. In particular, address at least the following:

(1) Whether cross-context prediction can do the work. If a utility function fitted from choices under risk predicts choices over time or effort without refitting, does that favor the real-magnitude reading, or is that already guaranteed by the background theory once the appropriate consistency axioms hold?

(2) Whether convergence among independent measurement procedures matters. In my own science, unobservables earned their keep when distinct instruments converged on the same value. Is there an analogue for utility — choice-based scaling, self-reports, response times, neural or physiological correlates, or willingness to accept money to alter one's own option set — and would convergence among several such measures constitute evidence for a real magnitude, or only evidence that several noisy measures of demand share a common cause?

(3) What a null result would show. If the fitted utility fails to generalize from one context to another, should we conclude that utility is only a local bookkeeping device, or merely that the standard theory is misspecified? How would one tell those two apart?

Finally, be explicit about what even a clean result could not settle: whether the residual disagreement is an experimental limitation or a defect in the claim itself, and whether the test can distinguish a real but purely ordinal utility from one that carries some cardinal or cross-context structure.

After the main analysis, I would like a concise structured summary as a JSON object with the following keys and no others:

- "operationalized_claim": your restatement of the claim in testable form.
- "strongest_test": a compact description of the design, specifying measures, contexts, and agents.
- "positive_result": the data pattern that would favor the real-magnitude reading, and what it would establish.
- "negative_result": the data pattern that would favor the bookkeeping reading, and what it would establish.
- "what_neither_result_settles": an array of the residual questions the test cannot decide.
- "verdict": "supported", "undermined", or "underdetermined", with a one-sentence justification.
