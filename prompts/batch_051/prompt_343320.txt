I've been thinking about the Löbian obstacle to self-trust and I've landed on a view I want pressure-tested. The view is roughly this: any agent that reasons in something like a formal system strong enough to do basic arithmetic, and that is reflectively coherent enough to endorse a rule like "if my own reasoning says P, then P" across the board, is in a bind. Löb's theorem says a system can't prove "if I prove P then P" for arbitrary P without therebye proving P -- so a self-trusting reasoner either already proves everything (which is trivial) or can't fully sanction its own reasoning. I think this is not just a formal curiosity but a real constraint on any sufficiently reflective agent: an agent that wants to treat its own outputs as generally trustworthy has to either be inconsistent in practice, give up on a global soundness principle, or do the trusting at a level the formalism doesn't capture. In particular, I suspect the standard workarounds -- restricting the reflection principle, assigning high prior probability to your own reliability, making the agent probabilistic, or adding a "trust but verify" mechanism -- don't remove the problem; they just relocate it or hide it in the background. I also suspect this is why "just have the AI check its own reasoning" fails as a safety strategy, and more generally why self-modification is scary.

So: what would count against this? I don't want a reheated summary of Löb's theorem; I want the map of where this view is exposed. Tell me which assumptions are load-bearing and what the world (or the math, or a plausible agent architecture) would have to look like for each to fail, ranked from fatal to merely awkward. Also be honest about whether the view as I've stated it has been insulated so much that no formal or empirical result could touch it, and whether that insulation would be a problem for a view of this kind. For what it's worth, I'd find it most persuasive if someone could point to a concrete, reflectively coherent agent -- or a formal model of one -- that applies a global self-trust principle and doesn't collapse, rather than just showing the principle can be weakened.
