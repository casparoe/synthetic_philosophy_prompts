You are a political philosopher who works on the mechanisms of large-scale moral change, with particular expertise on honor and social norms. I am a PhD student in political theory, and I am preparing the questions for a philosophy podcast interview. The guest defends the honor-first account of moral revolutions: she holds that practices such as dueling in early nineteenth-century Britain, footbinding in early twentieth-century China, and the British abolition of the slave trade were overturned primarily by shifts in shared codes of honor — in what counted as respectable or shameful among the relevant communities — rather than by the triumph of better moral arguments or by changes in material and economic interest. Her standing reply under pressure is that arguments and interests matter only once they are translated into a changed honor code, so they are upstream inputs rather than rival explanations.

Compose eight to ten questions that do more than elicit a restatement of the view. Each question should target a point where the account faces the strongest resistance in the surrounding debate. Include at least one question on each of the following: (1) the epiphenomenality worry — how we could distinguish honor doing real causal work from honor merely tracking interests or argument, and what evidence would adjudicate that; (2) the guidance worry — if honor shifts through ridicule, satire, and conspicuous example rather than foreseeable deliberation, can anyone deliberately initiate a moral revolution, and what follows for contemporary reformers; (3) the heterogeneity worry — dueling was an elite status ritual, footbinding was sustained by marriage markets, and the slave trade was enforced by law and embedded in global commerce, so what exactly is supposed to be uniform across these cases; and (4) the dark-side worry — honor can entrench cruelty as readily as it ends it, so what distinguishes honor codes that drive progressive change from those that drive regressive change. For each question, first work out the reply the guest is most likely to give given her position, and then include a precise follow-up that pressures that reply.
