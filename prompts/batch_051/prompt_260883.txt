An argument I have been formalizing for our interpretability lab seems to prove that no interpretation produced by a neural network can ever be warranted, and I cannot bring myself to accept that conclusion. The argument overreaches because some automated mechanistic descriptions have survived targeted ablations, transferred across checkpoints, and guided successful interventions; that looks like genuine evidence, not fiction. But I cannot locate the step that goes wrong, so here is the argument as I have it:

1. An interpretation of a model's internals is not just a predictive summary of the model's input–output mapping; it is a claim about the model's actual internal computational structure.
2. We are warranted in accepting such a claim only if we are warranted in believing that the interpreting procedure reliably tracks that structure, rather than confabulating fluent descriptions on the basis of superficial regularities in its own training data.
3. When the interpreting procedure is itself a trained neural network, our only way to determine that it tracks structure rather than confabulates is to interpret the interpreter's internal computation; external checks of its outputs alone cannot separate a description that mirrors the target's causal structure from one that merely reproduces the same regularities those checks are sensitive to.
4. But interpreting the interpreter is the same kind of task as interpreting the original target. If the second interpretation is also produced by a neural network, the same requirement arises again, and again, without end.
5. A belief whose warrant can be completed only by an infinite ascending chain of further beliefs is never warranted; if every supporting floor passes the load to the floor above it and the building has no top floor, no floor is actually supported.
6. Therefore, no interpretation produced by a neural network is ever warranted.

Which premise or step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? If forced to choose, I would rather preserve the practice of treating at least some automated interpretations as warranted than accept the conclusion, but I want to know what that choice commits me to. Please answer as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no separate introduction or concluding paragraph. The five points should be: the precise step or inference that fails; why that step looked compelling; what giving it up costs for automated interpretability as a whole; the strongest alternative diagnosis and what that alternative would surrender instead; and the resulting picture of how far a network can take us in interpreting another network.
