I am giving a paper next month at my institute's interdisciplinary governance seminar, and I need a Q&A prep sheet. The thesis I am defending is that incorruptibility is best understood not as an especially strong will or a fixed trait of character, but as a structural property of an agent's motivational economy: roughly, a condition in which considerations of private gain, personal loyalty, or advantage are absent, inert, or systematically excluded from deliberation in favour of role-given and public obligations. On this view, incorruptibility can come about through many routes — habituation into the virtues, institutional design, self-binding, or even a motivational deficit — and these routes have different normative standings. I argue that incorruptibility is therefore not always good: some ways of being uncorruptible involve insensibility, abdication of judgement, or dependence on a corrupt role.

The audience is not composed of metaethicists. It consists of: three neo-Aristotelian moral philosophers who work on virtue and practical wisdom; four political scientists and economists who model corruption as a principal–agent problem and treat incentives and monitoring as central; a compliance director and two investigators from a national integrity commission, who think in terms of controls, disclosure, and sanctions; and two empirically minded moral psychologists who study self-control and motivated reasoning. They are broadly hostile to the idea that a structural account can replace talk of character, but they are also hostile to naive "just hire honest people" views.

My argument in brief, for the objections to target: I start from the observation that both the character account and the incentive account fail, in opposite directions, to explain cases of officials who remain clean while their character is otherwise unremarkable, or who fall despite believing themselves honest. I then define corruption as the betrayal of a role-given trust for a private-regarding consideration, and define incorruptibility as robustness of that role-given trust against the activation of corrupting considerations. I claim this robustness is a structural feature of the agent's deliberative situation — which considerations are live for them — rather than sheer countervailing strength. Finally I argue that a structurally uncorruptible agent may be blameworthy, if the structure is maintained by culpable indifference, or pitiable, if it is maintained by an incapacity, and that some institutions should prefer a measure of ordinary corruptibility over some forms of incorruptibility.

Please draft the prep sheet, not a defence of the paper. Order the objections by a combination of how likely they are to come up and how much damage they would do if they land. Phrase each objection the way a member of this specific audience would actually phrase it, in their disciplinary vocabulary. For each, give a reply I could give in about two minutes, say what I should concede and what I should hold, and note where the reply is weakest. End with the question I am hoping nobody asks, and explain why it is the most dangerous. Use British English throughout.
