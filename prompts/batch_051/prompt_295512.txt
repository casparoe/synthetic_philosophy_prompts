I teach a small upper-level seminar on practical rationality — sixteen students, 90-minute sessions — and I want to run a session on whether 'better than' is transitive, and on spectrum arguments that need transitivity to get going. Most of the class can recite the transitivity axiom without flinching, but they have no feeling for what it would be like to give it up, or how quickly pairwise choices stop looking transitive once options differ along more than one dimension. I keep imagining a trade game: everyone starts with a bundle of two cheap goods (say, wrapped chocolates and points toward a small prize), and I offer a chain of one-for-one swaps where each swap is attractive by itself — a little more of one good, a little less of the other — but the chain is arranged in a loop, so if you accept every individually attractive trade you end up strictly worse of than where you started, and a patient 'pump' can keep milking you. I half-expect this to blow up in my face: some students will just refuse to keep trading once they smell a trick, others might have perfectly transitive preferences all the way around, and then nothing happens for ninety minutes.

Could you design the full activity properly? I want the problem to come out of what they themselves do — their own trades, or their votes on proposed trades — before anyone says the word 'transitivity.' Give me the setup and materials, the exact rules (including whether trading is private or public and how payoffs work without real money), a minute-by-minute timeline for 90 minutes, and a debrief that maps what happened back to the idea: how pairwise 'better than' judgments can cycle, how a spectrum argument strings together small steps to force a conclusion people then reject, and what that pressure means for whether we should treat 'better than' as transitive or instead blame something about pairwise comparisons. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time — students gaming it to look consistent, no one cycling, the winner being whoever refused to trade — and how to handle each. And be honest about which part of the idea the game can't reproduce and I'll need to supply in the debrief.
