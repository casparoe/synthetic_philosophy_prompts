I've been reading Thomas Paine's *Agrarian Justice*, where he makes the argument that the earth itself was originally common property and that only the improvements made by cultivation are properly individual. He says every landowner therefore owes the community a ground-rent, and he wants to use that rent to pay every person a lump sum at adulthood as compensation for being shut out of their natural inheritance by landed property.

Here's a modern mechanism I've been thinking about and I want to know how it would actually play out, not just whether it's fair.

Say we introduce an inheritance tax with a twist. Instead of taxing the whole estate at death, we tax only the portion of any real property in the estate that represents land appreciation the decedent themself inherited. For each parcel, compute the difference between its current land value and its land value at the time the decedent received it by inheritance. That difference is taxed at 100% into a national fund. The fund pays a flat £20k 'natural inheritance' payment to every citizen at 25. Earnings, buildings, and self-purchased land pass untaxed; only inherited land-price appreciation is clawed back.

What does this do to who ends up owning land, to family strategies around inheritance, to the timing of gifts, sales, and death? Where does it get gamed first — below-market sales to relatives, holding companies, moving land offshore, or something else I'm not seeing? Can any fixes close those gaps without gutting the distinction between 'self-made' and 'inherited' land value? And I'd like you to say what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would lead you to a different verdict about whether this mechanism actually curbs dynastic wealth, or just relabels it.
