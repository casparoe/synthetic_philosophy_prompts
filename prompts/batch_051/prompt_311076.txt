My AP Biology class recently read a popular-science account of molecular genetics, and one paragraph has been bothering me. The author's point, as I understood it, was this: when biologists call DNA a code, they are not speaking loosely. A gene is a sequence of the letters A, C, G, and T; that sequence is transcribed into RNA and then translated by the cell's machinery into the order of amino acids in a protein. Because the mapping from nucleotide triplets to amino acids is arbitrary — any triplet could in principle have been paired with any amino acid — and because the sequence carries exactly the information that determines a protein's structure, the author concluded that the genome literally contains instructions, much as a blueprint or computer program does, for building the organism. The author added that refusing to call DNA a code is like refusing to call a book a source of information: information is physically real, and DNA is genuinely read by the cell.

I suspect the step from an arbitrary triplet mapping to a literal language or program is where this goes wrong, but I cannot say exactly how, and most of the individual claims sound true when I check them against our textbook. I would like to know whether this paragraph is a fair summary of what molecular biology actually licenses. Going clause by clause, which parts are accurate, which are metaphors being presented as literal, and which are false or overstated? In particular, does the author's conclusion that the genome literally contains a program-like set of instructions for building an organism follow from the accurate parts about transcription, translation, and triplet assignments?
