In my ancient philosophy class we're reading Parmenides and I keep nodding along to the broad summary — he denies that reality can be many or changing — but the moment I try to trace it to the actual text I loose the thread. We spent a whole seminar on this passage from his poem, the one usually called On Nature, in the part editors call fragment 8:

"There is left but this single path to tell thee of: namely, that being is. And on this path there are many proofs that being is without beginning and indestructible; it is universal, existing alone, immovable and without end; nor ever was it nor will it be, since it now is, all together, one, and continuous. For what generating of it wilt thou seek out? From what did it grow, and how? I will not permit thee to say or to think that it came from not-being; for it is impossible to think or to say that not-being is."

What I can't figure out is how this is supposed to get you to the wild conclusions (no change, no plurality, no motion) instead of just the fairly boring claim that things don't pop into existence out of nothing. Can you walk me through the actual inference? In particular: what assumption about not-being is doing the work, why does that rule out generation and destruction first, and why does Parmenides think it then rules out any internal difference or movement too, once he gets to the all-alike and full-of-being moves? And where should I be looking if I want to see the misstep — is it the slide from not-being being unthinkable and unsayable to the conclusion that there is no not-being at all, or something in the way he uses is? Is there a reading where the whole thing is a point about the limits of thought and reference rather than a piece of cosmology, and if so does that rescue the argument or just relocate the problem? I'm after the underlying logic, not the historical background.
