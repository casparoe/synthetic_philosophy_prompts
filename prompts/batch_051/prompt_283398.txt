Write a villanelle (nineteen lines, with two refrains in the usual arrangement) about Berkeley's master argument for idealism. The first refrain should be the commonsense objection he is answering: that one can easily conceive of a tree in a wood, or a room with objects in it, existing with no one there to perceive them. The second refrain should be Berkeley's reply: that the one doing the conceiving is already there, supplying the perceiver the scene was meant to exclude. Let the form do the philosophical work. Each tercet should make a fresh attempt to state the unperceived tree, and each return of the first refrain should be answered by the second, so that the idealist's riposte keeps reasserting itself in the very act of trying to get clear of it. The two refrains should finally meet in the closing couplet, and that meeting should state precisely where the dispute lives: the distance between the tree as represented in thought and the scene the thought represents. The poem should work for a reader who has never read Berkeley, but a reader who knows the master argument and its standard criticism should recognise the move rather than a mere name-drop. Write the poem in British English.
