I am a retired paleoclimatologist; for four decades I worked on abrupt climate change, volcanic forcing, and threshold behavior in coupled natural systems. In retirement I have recently taken up the philosophy of history, and I keep encountering arguments that germs, climate, and volcanoes should be treated as "historical actors" — genuine participants in history rather than inert scenery. I want you to restate that debate in my native vocabulary, and then tell me what does not survive the restatement.

The target vocabulary is Earth-system science as I practiced it: boundary conditions versus forcings; perturbation magnitude, duration, and seasonality; sensitivity and state dependence of the responding system; damped and amplifying feedbacks; thresholds, nonlinear response, and hysteresis; stochastic variability and the distinction between a deterministic driver and a probabilistic constraint. For pathogens, you may also use reservoir, transmission, and host-population dynamics. Treat me as fully fluent in all of this; do not explain it, use it.

Here are four claims I keep finding in the literature, in my own plain wording:

(1) Nonhuman processes — an introduced pathogen, the Little Ice Age, the 1815 Tambora eruption — were causes of large historical changes, and they deserve to be treated as something closer to participants than to background conditions.

(2) Histories that classify such events merely as "natural disasters" or exogenous shocks misdescribe them, because their course runs through human institutions, subsistence technology, and social inequality, which determine exposure and shape the social response.

(3) Calling them "actors" is not animism; it is a deliberate correction to anthropocentric history. Properly understood, agency is distributed across networks of human and nonhuman elements rather than located in intending persons.

(4) The danger is environmental determinism — the slide from "climate and disease matter" to "climate and disease decide" — which erases human choice, culture, and politics.

Restate each of the four in my Earth-system vocabulary, one by one. For each, mark what translates cleanly, what has to be distorted to fit, and what has no expression at all in the vocabulary. Then give me the residue report: is what fails to translate a defect of my vocabulary, or a sign that the actor language is doing less causal work than it appears to? Finally, address my uneasiness directly: my vocabulary is built on forcings and responses, which sounds suspiciously close to determinism. Does translating these claims into my terms quietly commit me to the very position (4) warns against, and if so, what would a fair translation have to do to avoid that?
