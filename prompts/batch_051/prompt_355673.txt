I am a novelist finishing a manuscript, and an argument with my copy editor has stalled the final pass. The novel's central point-of-view character is a retired long-haul trucker from east Texas, and I have written his dialogue and much of his free indirect discourse in the English he grew up speaking: "might could," "ain't," double negatives, "we was," and similar forms. The copy editor is not hostile to dialect in principle, but she has flagged three kinds of passage. In some, the character slips into the standard forms ("I'm not going") where the established voice would lead a reader to expect "I ain't goin." In others, she thinks a particular nonstandard locution will be read by reviewers as a typo or an authorial error rather than as characterization. In a third group, she claims the sentence is ungrammatical under any description, not merely nonstandard. When I described the dispute to my writing group, another novelist waved away the editor's notes with the slogan "There's no such thing as bad grammar." Her fuller argument was that every living variety of speech has its own rules, that the character's constructions are grammatical within his variety, and that correcting them is just the enforcement of class-linked prestige forms.

That slogan is carrying a great deal of weight for a single sentence, and I want to know precisely what it would commit me to before I go back to the editor. Could you lay out, as a numbered list of exactly five items with no introduction or conclusion, the distinct claims that "There's no such thing as bad grammar" might be expressing, ordered from the most trivial to the most radical? Make each item no more than two sentences. In each item, state the claim in plain terms, say whether it is a platitude, a defensible but debatable claim, or something false, and then say whether accepting it would actually require leaving my character's language untouched—and, where relevant, which of the three kinds of flagged sentence it does and does not cover.
