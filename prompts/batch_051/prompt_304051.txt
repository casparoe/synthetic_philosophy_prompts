I'm preparing a philosophy class on the one-shot and iterated prisoner's dilemma and on how evolutionary accounts of cooperation explain, or fail to explain, restraint and mutual trust. I want to use the two-ferries scene from The Dark Knight, but not as the usual game-theory illustration; I suspect the standard filing of the scene under prisoner's dilemma is too quick, and I want a reading that tests the models rather than decorating them with a film clip. As the scene runs, the Joker has rigged two evacuation boats—one carrying ordinary civilians, the other carrying prisoners with their guards—and has given each boat a detonator for the other boat's bomb, announcing that if neither boat detonates by the deadline, he will destroy both. After anguished deliberation, the passengers and prisoners each refuse to detonate. Read the scene through the structure of the one-shot prisoner's dilemma, the iterated prisoner's dilemma, and evolutionary explanations of cooperation, and work through where each model genuinely captures the scene and where it distorts it. In particular: is the scene better modeled as an assurance game, Chicken, or something else, and does the correct model change what the film is saying; can the restraint be explained by shadow of the future, reputation, direct or indirect reciprocity, or group selection, or do those mechanisms need repeated interaction and therefore fail to explain a one-shot encounter; and does the film itself seem aware of the difference between one-shot interaction and the repeated interactions on which cooperative equilibria are usually built? Conclude by saying what the scene, read this way, contributes to the philosophical debate that a payoff matrix alone would miss, and what an honest critic should concede the film gets wrong about cooperation. Please also say how confident you are in the overall reading, and identify the single point in it you are least sure of.
