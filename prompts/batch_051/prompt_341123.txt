So I've been reading metaphysics since I retired (I did physical chemistry, mostly spectroscopy and later ultracold atoms), and I keep getting stuck on Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles. The way I understand it: no two distinct things can be exactly alike in every property; if two descriptions pick out different things, there has to be some qualitative difference between them. Black's two iron spheres are the standard challenge — a universe with nothing in it but two identical spheres, same size, same temperature, same composition — and the usual reply is that they still differ relationally: this one is two miles from that one, and so forth. Fair enough, as far as thought experiments go.

But in my lab days we did something that feels like a real version of Black's universe, and I don't know what it does to the principle. Cool a gas of identical bosons enough and you can arrange for any number of them to occupy exactly the same single-particle state. There's no observable that picks out 'this atom' rather than 'that atom'; the wavefunction is symmetric under exchange. By the physicist's way of counting there are ten thousand atoms, but there's no property — position, momentum, spin, no label of any kind — that distinguishes one from another. That's either Leibniz's principle failing in the lab, or its a sign that counting 'ten thousand atoms' was the mistake.

I'd like you to treat this as a testability question. Design something you'd actually measure — a coincidence count in a Hanbury Brown–Twiss setup, a measurement of the second-order correlation function, a precise position measurement, whatever you think is the strongest candidate — and say what result would count for the identity of indiscernibles and what result would count against it. Then tell me what even a clean result would leave unsettled, because my hunch is that the real hangup is in the step from 'indistinguishable particles' to 'two distinct things.' Is that step science, or is it just how we've chosen to talk?
