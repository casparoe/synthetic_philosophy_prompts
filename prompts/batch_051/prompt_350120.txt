The canonical version of Buridan's ass runs as follows. An ass stands exactly midway between two piles of hay that are identical in every respect that matters to it. Since no consideration favors the left pile over the right, the ass cannot choose, and it starves. One standard diagnosis is that the case presupposes a false account of rational choice: rationality does not require that one option be favored by a reason; where the options are tied, either is permissible, and a rational ass simply picks one. Call this the permissibility diagnosis.

Now modify the case. The two piles are not identical. The left pile is larger; the right pile is fresher. The ass has stable preferences along each dimension—more hay is better than less, fresher is better than staler—but it has no rate of exchange between size and freshness: it cannot say whether any gain in size compensates any loss in freshness, and it is not indifferent between the piles in the ordinary sense, because indifference would mean regarding them as equal in overall value, which it does not. The piles are, by stipulation, incomparable for the ass rather than exactly equal.

Does the permissibility diagnosis transfer unchanged to this version? In particular, is the claim that either choice is permissible and that the ass should simply choose arbitrarily the same solution here as it was under exact equality, or does the modified case present a different problem—choice under incomparability rather than choice without preference—that the permissibility diagnosis does not by itself settle? If the two cases are relevantly different, say precisely what the ass's error would be if it starved in the modified version, and state whether the original diagnosis needs to be supplemented, revised, or replaced.
