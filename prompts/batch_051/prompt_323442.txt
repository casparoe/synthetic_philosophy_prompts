In its 2003 report *Beyond Therapy: Biotechnology and the Pursuit of Happiness*, the President's Council on Bioethics writes:

"Like any other technology, the goals it serves are supplied neither by the techniques themselves nor by the powers they make available, but by their human users."

Consider the following asymmetry in ordinary biomedical judgment. Growth hormone given to a child with growth hormone deficiency is classified as therapy: it is reimbursed, taught in pediatric endocrinology as standard care, and attracts little moral controversy. Growth hormone given to an otherwise healthy but very short child to make him taller is classified as enhancement: it is often excluded from insurance, treated as discretionary, and subjected to extra ethical scrutiny. Similar asymmetries recur for methylphenidate used to treat ADHD versus to improve normal concentration, for erythropoietin used to treat anemia versus to raise an athlete's hematocrit, and for antidepressants used to treat major depression versus to blunt ordinary grief. In each case, the same intervention acts through the same biological pathway, but the therapeutic use is considered morally unproblematic and socially subsidized while the enhancing use is considered optional or suspect.

Does the passage from *Beyond Therapy* give us reason to think this asymmetry is poorly grounded? If the technology itself does not contain its own purpose, then perhaps the therapy/enhancement line is not a fact about the intervention but a way we mark which desires for improvement we are willing to endorse. But if that is right, on what basis do we mark it? Is there a defensible justification for treating restoration of species-typical function as categorically different from increasing function beyond the normal range, or is the asymmetry a social convention that should be given up or revised? Defend your answer against hard cases, such as a future memory-enhancing drug that reduces the normal forgetting of healthy adults.
