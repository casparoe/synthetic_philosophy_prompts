Two friends of mine got into it at a reading group over this sentence from Descartes's Sixth Meditation, in the Veitch translation online:

"For there can be no doubt that God possesses the power of producing all the objects I am able distinctly to conceive, and I never considered anything impossible to him, unless when I experienced a contradiction in the attempt to conceive it aright."

One guy said this is basically conceivability-as-possibility: if Descartes can distinctly conceive some object, God could produce it, so there's no room for another empirical or material constraint. The other said the quote is doing no real work, because the only limit Descartes notices is contradiction, but "I experienced a contradiction" is exactly the unreliable part — plenty of things people found distinctly conceivable turned out impossible, so he's just packing certainty into the test. The first replied that for Descartes, God's omnipotence just means the only limit is logical, so a distinct conception is a guarantee of possibility, and objecting to that is sneaking in a hidden constraint on what God could do. The second said that's circular: you'd need to already know your distinct conception isn't secretly contradictory, and Descartes gives no independent method for that. The first said Meditation Six then applies the test to mind and body, so Descartes clearly thinks he does have a method. The second said that just shows the test is doing to much, since it yields a conclusion that's still controversial. They asked me to referee and I couldn't. Please go through their exchange move by move: who's right about what, and what would a more defensible statement of the conceivability-to-possibility principle look like?
