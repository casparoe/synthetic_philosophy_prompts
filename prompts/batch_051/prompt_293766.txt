I serve as chair of the Academic Misconduct Appeals Panel at a research university, and I am preparing for an appeal in which a doctoral student found responsible for fabricating research data asks us to set aside the finding on what are essentially procedural grounds. The student does not deny the underlying fabrication, which has since been independently corroborated. Instead, the student argues that the finding should be vacated because the person who first reported the fabrication—a postdoctoral researcher in the same lab—later turned out to have committed a very similar fabrication at another institution. The student contends that the postdoc lacked the standing to report the misconduct, that the complaint is hypocritical, and that the panel should either dismiss it or formally discount it on that basis. The postdoc is not the victim of the student's conduct and, beyond filing the initial report, has no role in the disciplinary process.

Our panel has never addressed in writing the question this case raises: does a complainant's own comparable misconduct matter to our adjudication of an independently established violation, and if so, in what way? Could you draft the written opinion I would circulate before Thursday's meeting? It should recommend a concrete disposition with reasons—uphold the finding, vacate it, or uphold it with some formal statement about the provenance of the complaint—and it should take seriously both sides. In particular, engage the strongest version of the student's argument: that one who has committed a wrong loses the standing to blame or report another person for the same wrong, and that an institution compromises its authority when it receives such reports without comment. Also engage the strongest opposing argument: that the proceeding's purpose is to establish what happened, not to certify the reporter's character, and that a true report about misconduct is not made false by the reporter's own record.

I should say why I am asking for help with this one. The issue has recently become personal for me: my sister was disciplined by her professional body after a longtime rival reported her for an infraction, and the rival was later found to have committed a similar infraction himself. I noticed in myself how strongly the rival's hypocrisy colored my reaction, even though it did nothing to change what my sister had done, and I want to make sure that reaction is not silently doing the reasoning in the memo I sign.

The memo should be about 800–1,000 words, formal in tone but readable by non-philosophers, and actionable: panel members should know what we would decide and why. Please do not include footnotes or suggestions for further reading. Finally, at the end of the memo, include a short paragraph for the panel's internal use that distinguishes the points that are widely settled among people who study the ethics of blame from those that are genuinely contested—for instance, whether a hypocrite's blame is empty, merely less authoritative, or valid but selectively applied—and avoid presenting a contested position as the consensus.
