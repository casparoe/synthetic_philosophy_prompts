You are a senior evolutionary theorist and philosopher of biology. I want your help diagnosing a specific, long-running dispute that still shapes the evolutionary-psychology literature. The dispute is over whether the field’s characteristic explanations — e.g., modules for cheater detection, mate preference, status seeking, or sex differences — count as genuine science or as unfalsifiable, hindsight-laden “just-so stories.” This debate has been running since roughly the 1970s and 1980s, and by now the two sides mostly talk past each other. I’d like you to set aside the question of who is right and, instead, diagnose the structure of the disagreement itself: Is it a factual dispute about how much evidence suffices? A methodological dispute about what counts as a proper evolutionary explanation? A difference over the relevant null hypotheses and baseline models? Is it driven by incompatible philosophical assumptions about inference to the best explanation, about modularity, or about the relevant timescales for ancestral environments? Or is it, as some have hinted, a dispute over what it would even mean to “test” a claim about how the mind evolved over hundreds of thousands of years in environments that left no detailed behavioral record?

I’m particularly interested in the possibility that the impasse is caused by the two sides implicitly asking different questions under the same label — for example, one side asking whether a given cognitive trait was *shaped by* natural selection for its current function, while the other side asks whether there is *good evidence* that it was, with "good evidence" meaning something different to a paleontologist, a population geneticist, and a cognitive psychologist. My working hypothesis is that the debate has stalled because the two camps have different de facto stopping rules for when an adaptive story is "supported enough" and different views about whether post-hoc adaptiveness can ever provide real confirmation. In your diagnosis, what would actually move the debate? Would it be better formal models, new comparative phylogenetic methods, sharper dissociation between ultimate and proximate claims, a moratorium on "just-so" rhetoric, or something else? Or should we expect no progress at all because the real subject of the dispute is not the science but the standards of evidence themselves — and standards disputes are not empirically decidable? Frame your answer as a structural diagnosis of a long-running impasse rather than as a verdict for one side. Think of yourself as a conflict anatomist in this literature, working through what kind of disagreement this is, where it lives, and what kind of evidence or argument, if any, could destabilize it. Take your time and reason through the issue step by step.
