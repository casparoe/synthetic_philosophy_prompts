I'm a retired physical chemist, and over the last year or so I've gotten into the philosophy of infinity—supertasks, Zeno, the lot. My old scientific reflex is to ask what the limit of a process is, and for a while I thought that single rule would settle every one of these puzzles. It doesn't, or at least I can't get it to fit my gut on the standard cases. Here's where I've landed on each one.

The Zeno runner: before finishing a distance he has to cover half of what's left, then half of that, and so on. I say he just finishes; the position converges to the goal and the infinite sum 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + ... is exactly 1. No problem.

Thomson's lamp: a lamp is on, and at 1/2 minute you flip it off, at 3/4 of a minute you flip it on, at 7/8 you flip it off, and so on, doing the flips twice as fast each time. At the end of the minute, is it on or off? I say there's just no fact of the matter, because the sequence on, off, on, off never settles. My limit reflex handles that case too: no limit, so no determinate final state.

Now the urn case—I think it goes by the name Ross–Littlewood, but I'll describe it in case I've got that wrong. At step 1 you put balls numbered 1 through 10 into an urn and remove ball 1. At step 2 you put in balls 11 through 20 and remove ball 2. In general, at step n you put in balls 10n−9 through 10n and remove ball n. Do the steps at the same accelerating pace as the lamp, finishing by the end of the minute. How many balls are in the urn at the end? My gut answer is zero, because name any numbered ball and I can tell you exactly when it got removed. But the thing is, after step n there are 9n balls in the urn, and 9n grows without bound. So if I'm right, the limit of the sizes is not the size of the limit, and my "just take the limit" rule has a hole in it.

Hilbert's Hotel maybe belongs with these: a fully occupied hotel with rooms 1, 2, 3, ... moves the guest in room n to room 2n, which frees up all the odd-numbered rooms. My verdict there is that it's coherent but strange—it's a fact about infinite sets, not a contradiction. But I don't see a limit process in it, so I'm not sure it's the same kind of case as the others.

Is there one principle about supertasks that gets all of these, or close? State it as precisely as you can, say wich of my verdicts it keeps and which (if any) have to go, and apply it to a case I genuinely haven't made up my mind about: the same urn procedure, except at step n you remove the highest-numbered ball currently in the urn, rather than ball n. My old size-limit rule just says "infinity" there and that's no help.
