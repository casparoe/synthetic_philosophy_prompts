I've been asked to organize a session at a small philosophy-of-psychology grad conference, and I want the centerpiece to be an adversarial collaboration rather than yet another debate. The two people I have in mind have argued for years about what the cross-cultural variation findings in experimental philosophy actually show. One — let's call her the armchair defender — thinks the documented variation is mostly surface noise: translation problems, sloppy vignettes, or the unreliability of fast, unreflective folk answers, so the standard method of relying on considered verdicts about cases survives more or less intact. The other — call him the variationist — thinks the variation is too robust and too patterned to explain away, and that philosophers can't keep treating their own verdicts about hypothetical cases as evidence unless the claim that the relevant intuitions are actually shared has been checked, and usually it hasn't been. They've agreed to sit down and produce a joint statement. Could you draft the document they should end up with? Please write it as a professional philosopher whose own view is not in the room, and think carefully before you write; start by brainstorming what they could actually both sign without it becoming mush. The final document should have: (1) a numbered list of substantive agreed claims (about what the studies do and don't show, about how intuitions function in philosophy, about what would count as explaining variation away) that both would sign; (2) a disagreements table where each row states the neutral question and then each side's answer in wording that side would accept, not a caricature; (3) a list of cruxes — sub-questions such that a decisive answer would make at least one of them shift; and (4) for each crux, one concrete test, case, or argument both sides agree would bear on it, with each side's prediction recorded beforehand. I suspect one of the cruxes will be whether professional philosophers across cultures show less variation than laypeople, and another will be whether a small stable core of cross-cultural intuitions is enough to rebuild the method on. Seperate the disagreements that are substantive from ones that are just verbal as you go, and make sure the joint statement reads like a document two stubborn, good-faith people could actually both sign.
