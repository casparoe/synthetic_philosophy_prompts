Draft the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between two camps in decision theory. Call them the Strict Expected-Utility Camp and the Coherence-Skeptic Camp.

The Strict EU Camp holds that rationality requires an agent's preferences among gambles to satisfy the standard axioms — completeness, transitivity, continuity/Archimedean, and independence/sure-thing — because these are exactly the conditions under which preferences can be represented as expected-utility maximization, and because violations leave an agent open to Dutch books or money pumps. On this view, patterns such as Ellsberg-style ambiguity aversion are mistakes that a rational agent should correct, and the representation theorems associated with von Neumann–Morgenstern and Savage are best read as establishing a standard to which practical reasoning ought to conform, not merely as a compact description of behavior.

The Coherence-Skeptic Camp holds that the representation theorems are theorems about representation, not prescriptions. They grant that if preferences meet the axioms, a utility/probability representation follows, but they deny that rationality requires meeting the axioms in the first place. In particular, the skeptics hold that incomplete preferences over ambiguous acts, imprecise credences, and stable ambiguity aversion can be fully rational, and that money-pump arguments fail against a sophisticated agent who treats her choices across time as one plan rather than as a sequence of independent local maximizations.

Write the document this collaboration should have produced. It should contain:

1. A numbered list of agreed claims — about the mathematical content of the representation theorems, about what the theorems do and do not establish on their own, about the structure of Dutch book and money-pump arguments, and about what procedure would settle the remaining disputes. The claims should be substantive rather than vacuous, and each should be worded so that both camps would sign it without adding small caveats.

2. A disagreements table. For each residual disagreement, state the issue in neutral terms, then give the one or two sentences each side accepts as the exact statement of its own view, then classify the disagreement as substantive or at least partly verbal, with a note explaining where any verbal element lies. A likely candidate: whether the word coherent is merely a mathematical condition a preference relation either meets or fails, or is already an honorific that smuggles in a rational requirement.

3. A section on cruxes. Identify at least three sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one side. For each, give the exact sub-question; a concrete case, formal result, or experimental design that both sides agree bears on it; and what each side predicts it would show, together with how each side would revise its position if the result came out the other way.

One crux should be the pump that is supposed to extract a fee from an agent whose preferences are incomplete and who declines to trade between two acts in either direction. Both camps may agree about what happens to a naive agent; the dispute concerns the sophisticated agent. Record exactly where the two camps part ways about that agent — whether the pump can be made to run regardless of the agent's sophistication, whether the sophisticated agent's refusal to be pumped amounts to adopting a completion of her preferences after all, and whether diachronic consistency of a plan is a separate requirement from synchronic coherence of the preference relation. Another likely crux is whether ambiguity aversion on the Ellsberg choices is best described as a feature of a single preference relation, as a policy for selecting among many permissible completions of an incomplete preference relation, or as a sign of an unmodeled higher-order preference about the source of uncertainty.

Do not adjudicate. The point is to produce a constructive text both parties could sign, with the remaining disagreement made exact.
