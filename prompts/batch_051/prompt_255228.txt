I keep using a distinction in my moral epistemology seminar that I want someone to try to break before I build anything around it. I call it the difference between a signed moral belief and a seeded one. A signed belief is one whose justification bottoms out in someone else's say-so: you hold it because she said so, and that's the whole story—a committee member who votes against a candidate because the chair says it would be unfair and the chair is the one who knows these things. A seeded belief also starts with someone else's say-so—maybe you wouldn't even have noticed the thing was wrong until somebody said, wait, that's not just awkward, it's cruel—but by the time you hold it, the justification you'd actually offer is yours. You can point to the features of the case, draw the comparisons, maybe improve on the original speaker's reasons. The other person got you to see it; they didn't hand you the finished verdict.

My claim, and I'm fairly convinced of it, is that the literature on moral testimony keeps attacking signed beliefs, but the cases people actually use as examples of moral testimony in real life are almost all seeded. The standard anti-testimony thought—that you can't just take someone's word on a moral matter, since then you'd lack understanding or be alienated from your own agency, whatever version you prefer—goes through fine against signed deference, but it proves way too little, becuase the seeded case is the normal one and it looks untouched. The optimists about moral testimony, meanwhile, defend the seeded case but don't seem to notice they've switched targets.

So the honest assessment I want: is the signed/seeded distinction a real one, or does it collapse under pressure—because, say, seeded beliefs are just signed beliefs plus confabulation, which would be worse, not better? Is this already in the literature under some name I'm forgetting? And where does it break? Give me the cleanest case you can where a belief counts as seeded by my own definition and still is exactly the kind of thing the anti-testimony people are right to be worried about.
