Last night I had a long argument with a friend who works as a hospital administrator. He was defending a proposal to publish surgeon-specific mortality and complication rates and to tie each surgeon's recredentialing to those figures. I objected with the familiar one-liner, "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." When he pressed me to say exactly what I meant, I realized I had been running together several different thoughts: that surgeons would falsify records, that they would select healthier patients, that they would neglect whatever the metric does not capture, and that "quality of surgical care" would itself gradually come to mean whatever the metric counts. Before I see him again I want these sorted out. Lay out the distinct precise claims this one-liner can express; for each, say whether it is trivial, substantive but defensible, or false, and what mechanism it identifies (falsification, selection of cases, shifting of effort, a change in the target concept itself, or something else). Then tell me which of these readings would actually support my objection to tying recredentialing to a published metric, and which reading is usually carrying the weight when the same one-liner is invoked about an AI system that games the letter of its stated reward function.
