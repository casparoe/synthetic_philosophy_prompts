Act as an experienced philosophy teacher who designs interactive seminars. I run an undergraduate PPE seminar, about 18 students, 75 minutes, and next week I want them to actually feel Sen's liberal paradox rather than just watch me write it on the board. The paradox, roughly: if everyone prefers x to y the group should too; each person gets to be decisive over at least one purely personal matter; add a barely-demanding consistency condition, and with the right preference profile you get a cycle. Design a runnable activity with concrete rules, index cards or a one-page handout and no software, where the students first build a mini-constitution and thier preference profile and then run smack into the cyclic outcome themselves, before anyone names the paradox or says impossibility. Then give me the debrief: map what happened onto the Pareto principle, minimal liberalism, and the consistency condition; lay out the escape routes and what each one costs; say what predictably goes wrong when this is actually run and how I should handle it; and be straight about which part of the formal theorem the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to just tell them. By the end of the debrief they should be able to say, for each escape route, which principle gets sacrificed and why that stings. Don't lecture up front — let the idea show up in what the students do, not what I say afterward. And think through what they are likely to try first, so the rules channel them instead of fighting them.
