I sit on the ethics committee of a mid-sized hospice network, and we're voting next week on a pilot the palliative care team wants to run. The idea is that terminal patients could opt in before they die to have a "griefbot" built from their voice notes, texts, and journals. After they die, a spouse or adult child could keep talking with it for a limited window, and the bot would answer in something like the patient's voice and style, using only the patient's own material, with a clear on-screen label that it's a simulation. The vendor can enforce a 30-day wait after death, a 90-day cap on use, saved transcripts for the family, and a grief counselor check-in. The committee is genuinely split: half see it as the modern version of rereading letters and watching home videos, the other half see it as a machine for avoiding grief, and one chaplain argued we'd be letting a copy speak for someone who can no longer correct it. I also have a harder request on the same agenda: a man whose wife died in our care without ever being asked for consent, but who has her voicemails and years of text messages, wants permission to build one himself. We keep talking past each other because we're not sure whether the real issue is the dead person's dignity, the survivor's wellbeing, the consent problem, or the insitution's responsibility.

Draft the short written opinion I'd circulate before the vote. I need a clear recommendation — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — with the actual conditions and time frames we could implement, an honest treatment of the strongest objection to whichever way you lean, and a specific answer on what to do about the widower.
