I cover the places where formal models of rational choice run into the messier facts of memory and agency, and I am preparing a long essay about how people try to bind their own future selves when they cannot trust the later self to know where it stands. I want the essay to center on a close reading of Christopher Nolan's Memento against the absent-minded driver problem from decision theory. In that problem, a driver wants to take the second of two identical exits to get home; taking the first exit leaves him badly off, and missing both leaves him merely mediocre. But when he reaches an exit he cannot tell whether it is the first or the second, so any plan must be carried out by a later self who does not know where in the plan he stands. The question is whether the perspective of the planner and the perspective of the person at the exit can be made to speak with one voice; on the standard treatments they sit uneasily together, and deciding which standpoint is authoritative is the heart of the puzzle. Leonard Shelby's condition gives that structure a body and a plot: he writes commitments onto his skin and his photographs precisely because he knows his later selves will not know where they stand in his plan, and the film's broken chronology repeatedly puts the viewer in the epistemic position of one of those later selves. Read Memento through this problem. Where does the film genuinely dramatize the gap between ex ante planning and in-the-moment choice under imperfect recall, where does it distort that gap or exploit it for suspense, and where must an honest critic concede that Leonard's case points to something the formal problem leaves out, such as identity, grief, regret, or self-deception? I want the reading to be faithful in both directions: neither reducing the film to an illustration of a decision-theoretic puzzle nor bending the puzzle until it is only a metaphor. What does Nolan's film add to the debate that the bare highway example cannot?
