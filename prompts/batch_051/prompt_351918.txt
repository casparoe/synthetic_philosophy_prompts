In Bayesian confirmation theory, old evidence—facts already known, and therefore assigned probability one, before a theory is introduced—poses a familiar puzzle: conditionalizing on such evidence cannot shift any rational credence, so the formalism seems unable to say that the evidence confirms the theory. A colleague draws a stronger moral from this. She claims that old evidence never genuinely confirms a new theory: when a theory elegantly explains facts we already knew, we learn something about the theory’s fit to our existing records, but we acquire no reason to think the theory true. I suspect this stronger claim is either untestable or collapses into a merely terminological dispute, but I might be wrong. Design the strongest test you can for it—a formal criterion within confirmation theory, a historical comparative study, or some combination of the two—and be specific about what result would favor each side. Then be honest about what even a clean positive result would leave unresolved.
