A state declares that it will rise ten places in the World Happiness Report within a decade, and it writes the goal into law. Every proposed regulation must now carry a life-evaluation impact statement from a new Office of Life Evaluation: agencies must project the regulation's effect on the national average answer to the 0–10 Cantril ladder question (the survey item behind the rankings) over a five-year horizon. The Office's budget grows in years when the country's ranking rises and is cut when it falls; regulations with a negative projection may still proceed only if a minister signs a public waiver. Trace how this system would behave once agencies and politicians adapt to it. Which kinds of policy would be flattered by such a metric, and which genuinely beneficial changes would be starved of support? Where is gaming most likely to enter first—in the projection models, in the timing or targeting of policies, in the composition of who is surveyed, or elsewhere? Would the failure modes be correctable by reform, or do they follow from the decision to steer a country by a self-reported ranking?
