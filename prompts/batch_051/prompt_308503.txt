I'm giving a talk next month to the alignment team at a small lab — nine people, all ML, no philosophers — and I'm defending a view I've been nursing since the PRM threads on the forum. The short version: the field's shift toward process-based supervision (reward models trained to score each intermediate step, PRMs) and away from outcome-based supervision (reward models trained to score only the final answer, ORMs) rests on a conflation of label budget with signal. My actual thesis isn't just that ORMs are cheaper. It's that at matched test-time compute the reported win for process supervision on math reasoning benchmarks is mostly that label-budget confound, and once you train an outcome reward model on many rollout-level outcomes and then do weighted majority voting or best-of-n, the gap shrinks to where it shouldn't drive infrastructure decisions. Separately I'll claim PRMs smuggle a trusted process into the step labels themselves, so they can't be the backbone of scalable oversight once we're worried about models gaming the process checker, or once we genuinely don't know what correct intermediate reasoning looks like.

The room: the lab lead is an ex-RL engineer who got burned by reward hacking on an early product model and is quietly sympathethic to the supervise-the-process-not-the-outcome line. Two of the researchers spent last summer implementing PRMs, and one of them still basically thinks step-level credit assignment is the whole game. The rest have read the big process-supervision paper and a couple of critical follow-ups but havn't committed.
