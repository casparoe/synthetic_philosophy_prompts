Set out the desiderata that an account of mathematical proof must meet if it is to be adequate to a mathematical practice in which some accepted proofs are produced by computers and are too long for any single human being to survey. The account is supposed to explain how such results can put the mathematical community in a position to know the theorems they establish, and to underwrite decisions about which such results to accept.

For each desideratum, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark it as either a non-negotiable constraint on any acceptable account or a preference of a particular camp (for example, the working mathematician's demand that a proof be surveyable; the formalist's demand that it reduce to a finite, mechanically checkable derivation; the constructivist's demand for an executable construction; the demand that independent implementations agree).

The cases the account must handle include the following. (1) Appel and Haken's computer-assisted proof of the four-colour theorem, whose exhaustive case analysis has never been checked in full by any human reader. (2) The proof of the Kepler conjecture, which originally depended on large computations and was later replaced by a fully formal, machine-checked proof in the Flyspeck project. (3) A hypothetical: an automated prover that includes a neural-network search component whose operation no one can inspect produces a proof of a major open conjecture, far too long for any human to read; a separately designed, independently implemented proof checker verifies every inference. Does this count as a proof, and which desideratum does the most work in forcing the answer?

Finally, say whether the desiderata are jointly satisfiable. If some of them conflict — for instance, if the demand for human surveyability cannot be met by any account that licenses acceptance of the machine-checked results — say so explicitly and state which requirement must give way. Keep the weight on the requirements and the cases that motivate them; do not spend the answer scoring named philosophies of mathematics.
