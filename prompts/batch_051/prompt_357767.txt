I'm a retired population biologist, and the last few years Ive fallen hard into philosophy—mostly methodology, reflective equilibrium, the role of intuitions, simplicity as a theoretical virtue. Since retiring I also handle papers at a small journal of theoretical ecology and evolutionary biology. And I keep hitting the same wall: an author builds a clean principle that handles ninety-nine cases, a referee says one awkward case refutes it, and the authors reply that the referee's case intuition is just wrong and that their principle's simplicity should count for something. I need a reusable procedure, runnable in half an hour, for deciding as editor when a case intuition is strong enough to force a revision, and when a simple principle may override it. The procedure should include a transitivity check—does the principle commit the authors to rankings or verdicts they refuse to accept?—and a simplicity check—is simplicity doing evidential work or just looking tidy?—and it should say explicitly where the philosophy hands the decision back to me as editor, and where the procedure itself is likely to misfire. Then run it on two cases I'm currently sitting on: (1) a referee rejects a paper's definition of tool use because it excludes an ant species that uses leaf fragments to carry liquid; (2) a paper defines the environmental niche in terms of other species' behaviors rather than resource distributions, a referee calls this circular, and the authors answer that the circularity is the price of a simpler theory. I already know which side I'd take in each, and I want the procedure to tell me where that's just bias, including if it means overruling the side I like.
