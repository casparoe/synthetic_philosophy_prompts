I'm in a senior epistemology seminar on disagreement this term, and the professor keeps a running file of "disagreement cases" with her one-sentence ruling about how far the more-confident person should move. She let me copy the file, and I've been trying to reverse-engineer the principle behind it because she won't state it outright. Here's the file:

1. You and a stranger both mentally figure an 18% tip on $47.30; you get $8.51, she gets $8.10, and there's no calculator or time to redo it. Ruling: "Split the difference and hold at half confidence."
2. You and an equally trained philosophy colleague read the same draft of a paper and disagree about whether the main argument is valid; after a half-hour of exchanging reasons, neither of you can find a flaw in the other's reasoning. Ruling: "Suspend judgment."
3. You're arguing with a friend about what time a restaurant closed, and you find out he's had three drinks. Ruling: "You may hold your ground; the disagreement gives you little reason to move."
4. You're arguing with a very smart relative about whether a proposed development will raise nearby property values, and the relative owns property next to the site. Ruling: "No significant pressure to move."
5. Your intro student disagrees with you about whether a particular substitution in a modal logic proof is licensed. Ruling: "Lower your confidence only a hair."

The new case for next week is this. You and a collaborator you've worked with for a decade — same training, same track record of catching each other's mistakes, roughly half and half — independently audit a small nonprofit's books and arrive at different totals. Each of you rechecks the other's spreadsheet. You find a specific line item where she appears to have carried a March expense into April. You point it out. She rechecks the same line and says it belongs in April, and adds that she can't find anything wrong with your work. No third accountant is available until the bank records arrive Monday. How far does she say you should move, and under what rule?

Please answer in American English, in this order and with rough lengths: "Predicted verdict" (a sentence or two, under 60 words), "Candidate rules" (two or three rules that fit the old cases but give different verdicts on the new one), "My best guess" (which rule she's likely running and why), and "The deciding case" (the further disagreement case you'd feed her to settle between the top two). The last three sections can be a solid paragraph each.
