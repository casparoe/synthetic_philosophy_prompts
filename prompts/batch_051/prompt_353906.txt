I'm a retired natural scientist, recently gotten serious about philosophy, and next month I'm presenting a paper at our philosophy of language and AI reading group. My thesis is that what the AI labs call "hallucinations" — confident, fluent false outputs — are best classified with Harry Frankfurt's category of bullshit rather than as lying or as straightforward error, because the fault that matters is neither an intent to deceive nor a failed attempt at truth but a systematic lack of concern for whether what is said is true. I want to defend this without assuming the model has mental states: the lack of concern is a property of the system's training regime and deployment, not a proposed inner life. The audience is mainstream epistemologists and philosophers of language who know Frankfurt mostly through On Bullshit, one NLP engineer who thinks "hallucination" and "bullshit" are both hopelessly anthropomorphic, and a couple of graduate students working on testimony. Draft my Q&A prep sheet — not another defense of the thesis: the objections I'll actually get from this room, not the general AI-skeptic ones, ordered by likelihood and how much damage they'd do, phrased the way this audience would phrase them, and for each a short reply, what to concede, what to hold, and where the reply is weakest. Include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. Separate what is settled in the literature from what is genuinely contested among people who know the topic, and don't present a contested reading of Frankfurt as the consensus.
