You are advising an organization that receives a large volume of unsolicited first-person reports of striking coincidences: two strangers who share an uncanny number of biographical details, a dream that appears to match the next day's disaster, a lost object recovered immediately after an improbable mental request, and the like. The organization must allocate scarce investigative effort across reports before any corroboration has been done, and it wants an explicit procedure for assigning each report a prior evidential weight.

A useful benchmark is the birthday paradox. In a room of only 23 people, the probability that some pair shares a birthday is already above one half, even though the probability that a particular pair shares a birthday is roughly 1 in 365. The paradox turns on the difference between the probability that a specified event happens to a specified observer and the probability that something of that general kind happens to someone within a large field of people, days, comparisons, and pairings.

Here are six proposed rules for weighting a coincidence report. Rank them from most defensible to least defensible as guides for allocating investigative effort. For each rule, also give a score from 0 to 10 indicating how much weight it deserves in a combined allocation formula, and explain in a sentence or two why it lands where it does. Group rules that you consider tied, and say what additional information would break the tie. If some rule deserves to be discarded entirely, put it in a never-use tier with a brief justification.

Rule 1. An event whose unconditional probability is below 1 in 10,000 should automatically receive high priority, because an event that improbable is unlikely to occur by chance in an ordinary lifetime.

Rule 2. A report should be discounted in proportion to the size of the reporter's opportunity field: how many people, days, comparisons, or pairings could have produced a match of comparable emotional force and been noticed.

Rule 3. A report should be discounted whenever the matching pattern was settled after the fact, because a flexible pattern can be fitted to many past events, and the relevant probability is not the probability of the final match but the probability of some match that would have felt equally significant.

Rule 4. A report should receive substantial weight when it comes from a sober, motivated, and apparently honest witness whose honesty you have no independent reason to doubt, almost regardless of the event's base rate.

Rule 5. A report should be evaluated as a population-level claim: how likely is it that something at least this striking would have been noticed and reported by anyone in the relevant population within the relevant time window? Only if that population-level probability remains low should the report move up in priority.

Rule 6. A report should begin from a near-even prior over the principal explanatory templates — chance, hidden common cause, selective memory and reporting, and fabrication — and the prior should move only as far as the specificity of the report and the independence of any corroboration warrant.

Finally, state in one sentence the single largest error an analyst of such reports can make in moving from an anecdote to a conclusion.
