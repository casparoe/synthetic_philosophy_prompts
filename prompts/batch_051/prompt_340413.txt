The Westminster Confession of Faith (1647), Chapter XXI, Section 1, reads:

“The light of nature showeth that there is a God, who hath lordship and sovereignty over all; is good, and doeth good unto all; and is therefore to be feared, loved, praised, called upon, trusted in, and served with all the heart, and with all the soul, and with all the might. But the acceptable way of worshipping the true God is instituted by himself, and so limited to his own revealed will, that he may not be worshipped according to the imaginations and devices of men, or the suggestions of Satan, under any visible representations or any other way not prescribed in the Holy Scripture.”

Suppose a reformed denomination decides to operationalize this principle as a formal worship-approval procedure. Each local session (the governing body of elders) must classify every item in public worship — song texts, instruments, images, gestures, vows, lighting, seasonal decorations, order of service, dramatizations, recorded music — under one of three categories: A, an element of worship expressly commanded in Scripture or necessarily deduced from it; B, a circumstance common to human actions and societies, ordered by Christian prudence according to the general rules of Scripture; or C, an innovation that is neither A nor B. Any five communicant members may challenge a classification. When a challenge is filed, the session has ninety days to produce a written argument showing the specific command or deduction for A, or showing why the item is genuinely common to human societies and does not alter the substance of worship for B. If the session cannot meet this burden, the practice is suspended. Long-standing usage, edification as measured by the congregation, the absence of an explicit biblical prohibition, and analogy to Old Testament practice are all expressly inadmissible as grounds for assigning A.

Treat this as a mechanism, not as a doctrinal view to attack or defend. Walk through how it would operate in practice. What happens to the element/circumstance distinction once it becomes the border litigated by written challenges? Which contested practices will most often be relabeled as circumstances rather than defended as elements, and what incentives does this create for the church’s theological vocabulary? Where will the system first be gamed — through the definition of “common to human societies,” through the placement of the burden on the challenger or the session, through the suspension of practices as a bargaining tool, or through something else? What second-order effects should the denomination expect after twenty years in how “necessary consequence” is argued and in what kinds of worship practices survive? Which failure modes are internal to the rule itself, and can any be fixed without effectively abandoning the regulative principle?
