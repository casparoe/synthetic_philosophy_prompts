I've been going back and forth on this and could use a slow, criterion-by-criterion answer. I was taught to think of a threat as having four features: (1) a conditional structure — if the target does X, the threatener does Y; (2) Y leaves the target worse off than they would otherwise be; (3) the target's awareness of the conditional is part of what moves them away from X; and (4) the threatener maintains the conditional at least partly in order to move the target.

Now consider a case where two agents, A and B, have no causal contact and never will. B is choosing whether to defect in a one-shot game, and before choosing runs a very good prediction of A's policy. The prediction shows A is committed to punishing defectors-against-A-type agents wherever A actually meets them, even though doing so is costly. In the actual case A never meets B, never finds out B's choice, and never punishes anyone. But B cooperates anyway, because the predicted conditional alone is enough to pull B around. B is moved by a fact about A's disposition, not by anything A did or said.

Is that a threat? If yes, which feature does the real work, and do I have to weaken (4) from aimed-at-this-target to something like a disposition that has the same downstream effect as a threat? If no, which feature fails, and is the right lesson that acausal threats are not threats but something adjacent — commitments, correlates, or B just reasoning about A's algorithm? I'm trying to figure out whether my four features were a decent criterion and this is a genuine boarderline case, or whether they were always too tied to ordinary face-to-face coercion and need redrawing.
