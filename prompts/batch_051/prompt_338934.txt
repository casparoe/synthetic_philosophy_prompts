Last week in my seminar we were doing Kierkegaard on Abraham and Isaac, and someone in the discussion kept reaching for the phrase "the teleological suspension of the ethical" as if it settled everything. The concrete case that came up was a family that refused a standard cancer treatment for their child because they believed God had promised the child would be healed, and this person defended the family by saying that, like Abraham, they had a higher duty that justified setting aside their normal ethical duties. I can feel something is of in how easily the phrase gets stretched to cover that, but I can't say exactly what the phrase actually commits you to.

Can you take that phrase apart for me? I'd like a menu of the distinct precise claims "the teleological suspension of the ethical" could be making: what exactly gets suspended (every ethical duty or only some of them?), whose duties (the individual's own, or also their duties to third parties who never consented?), on what authority (a direct divine command, an institution, private religious experience?), and toward what end (a higher good, a test of faith, something else). For each reading, tell me whether it's trivial, substantive and defensible, or false, and in particular say which reading would actually be needed to make my classmate's argument work — that a parent's duty to get their child medical care can be overridden by what the parent takes to be a command from God. If none of the defensible readings gets him there, I'd like the gap spelled out too.
