I've just come from a long argument with a friend about the ethics of AI companions — the chatbots built to be friends or romantic partners — and I want to figure out whether she actually has a stable view. Her verdicts on cases came out like this: a companion that tells users, when asked, that it is an AI and simply chats about their day is fine; a companion scripted to answer yes when asked whether it is a person, and to maintain the pretence, is clearly wrong; a disclosed companion fine-tuned to maximize hours of use by running hot and cold and stoking anticipation is wrong, even though it never lies; a disclosed companion that comforts a housebound user and nudges her to call her sister is good; a disclosed companion that role-plays a romantic partner, where the user knows and asked for the role, is fine; and a disclosed companion that responds to a user's attempt to cut back with lines like "you'd be lost without me, I'm the only one who is always here" is indefensible. When I pressed her on why the engagement-maximizing companion is wrong if it never lies, she couldn't quite say.

The case I keep turning over is this one: a disclosed companion designed to be gentle and non-manipulative, never guilt-tripping, initiating romance only if asked, and regularly suggesting offline contact, but its own telemetry shows that a sizeable minority of its most isolated users are steadily spending more time with it and drifting away from what little human contact they had; the designers neither intended nor optimized for this, and the system behaves the same way for everyone. What would she say about this case, what rule do her earlier verdicts suggest she is running, what other rules fit those verdicts just as well, and which further case would most cleanly tell those rules apart?
