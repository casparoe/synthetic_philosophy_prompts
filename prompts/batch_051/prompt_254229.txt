Earlier this week I spent a long evening arguing with a friend about Quinn's self-torturer, and the discussion kept stalling on one distinction. To fix the case: a subject can advance a device one setting at a time through a long series of settings; the lowest setting is painless, the highest is very painful, and the difference in pain between any two adjacent settings is too small for him to notice, while each advance pays a fixed sum of money. He willingly advances step by step, preferring each setting to the one just below it, yet ends up in a state he would not have accepted in a single direct choice. My friend's position was that these stepwise choices are ordinary preferences, so the example shows that rational preferences can be intransitive. My instinct was that the adjacent comparisons are not full preferences at all but pairwise judgments made under a threshold of indiscriminability, and that the example is a warning about treating each local comparison as decisive rather than a counterexample to transitivity. I would like a clear explanation of the conceptual difference between those two diagnoses, followed by a definite verdict: which diagnosis better captures the force of the example, and why? In particular, address the revealed-preference thought that if he is disposed to choose setting n+1 over setting n at every adjacent pair, then transitivity has simply failed. Please defend one reading rather than surveying the options.
