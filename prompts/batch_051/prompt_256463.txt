Write the joint statement that a committed gene's-eye theorist and a committed multilevel-selection theorist could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration on the units of selection debate. I want the claims they agree on, including about kin selection and the Price equation; the precise formulations of their disagreement, each phrased so its own side would accept the wording; the cruxes; and, for each crux, the case or argument both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a disagreements table.

The joint statement should address, with precision rather than slogan, at least the following: (a) whether "levels of selection" is best understood in terms of replicators or in terms of interactors/vehicles, and whether the replicator/vehicle distinction and the interactor/replicator distinction are competing ontologies or compatible bookkeeping choices; (b) what the Price equation partition actually shows, and whether its covariance terms should be read as causal components of the explanation or only as an algebraic decomposition; (c) whether the trait-group model—in which individuals interact within temporary groups that then dissolve into a global mixing pool before new groups form—establishes group-level adaptation or merely shows individual selection in a structured population, and whether the haystack model changes the matter over repeated generations; and (d) whether every apparent group-level adaptation can be re-expressed, with no explanatory loss, as a gene-frequency effect in a specified environment, or whether some group-level descriptions capture functionally organized group differences that the gene-level description drops.

The gene's-eye side may appeal to replicator/vehicle arguments and to the power of within-group selection to undermine group-level organization; the multilevel-selection side may appeal to trait-group and haystack dynamics and to cases where group-level covariance tracks functionally integrated group differences that are not decomposable into individual effects. Do not resolve the disagreement; the task is to resolve exactly what the disagreement is, to identify which apparent disagreements are merely verbal, and to state the residual differences as precisely as possible.

For the cruxes section, give a concrete case, model, or argument both sides regard as relevant—for example, meiotic drive and other outlaw genes, greenbeards, the long-run stability of group adaptations under within-group selection, or any case where group reproduction and individual reproduction come apart—and record in advance what each side predicts the case will show and what pattern of evidence would move at least one side.
